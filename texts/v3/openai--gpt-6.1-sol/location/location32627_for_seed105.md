The mountain road is still there, though there is no longer any reason for it to keep to the mountain.

It crosses a drowned saddle, turns around a shoulder of black stone, and disappears into a forest whose crowns lean downhill. Above it lies a deep weight of water. Above the water is ice, thick enough that daylight enters only as a faint discoloration: a gray patch moving slowly across the roof of the world. There is no visible sun. At the brightest hour, the farther slopes can just be distinguished from the water between them.

This is a high country. Its valleys lie above the altitude where, elsewhere, trees once stopped growing. The peaks stand beneath the ice like the knuckles of a closed hand. Between them are broad submerged passes, hanging basins, shelves large enough to have held villages. The water has occupied them for so long that there is little left which looks newly drowned. Corners have softened. Soil has settled into the lee of boulders. The old road is recognizable less by its paving than by the unbroken strip of low vegetation that follows it.

The cold is nearly uniform, but not entirely. Water comes up through cracks in the mountain, slightly warmer and carrying dissolved minerals. Around these cracks the forest grows.

From a distance, each grove is a dark stain with a pale center. Nearer, it resolves into trunks rooted in gravel, thick ropes of weed, and leaves so broad and thin that they fold over themselves in the current. Some trunks are living; others are old wood encased in successive sleeves of growth. Nothing here needs to stand by its own strength. A tree can be hollow, split from root to crown, and still carry a luxuriant canopy because the water supports it.

The groves are separated by long expanses of bare stone. You can cross an entire basin without passing anything taller than a hand’s breadth, then enter a green ravine where fronds overlap so densely that there is no clear way through. Growth gathers around warmth and remains close to it. Beyond the last root, the valley opens abruptly into darkness.

There are no fish among the leaves. No shells cling to the stone. Nothing nests in the hollow trunks or grazes the soft mats along the road. The plants shed spores into empty water, and the spores drift until they lodge somewhere suitable or disappear into the deep passes. Fallen leaves decay where they collect. Where the cold slows decay almost to nothing, they accumulate in beds that retain the shape of individual leaves for years.

The lack of animals is most apparent at the edges of the groves. A torn leaf hangs untouched. A drifting seed passes through branch after branch without anything turning toward it. There is movement everywhere, but no pursuit.

Nor is the country quiet.

The ice roof makes a long, low sound when the weather changes above it. It begins beyond one ridge, travels through the water, and arrives in the next basin as a vibration felt in the road stones. Occasionally there is a sharp report, followed several seconds later by another, more distant one. The whole upper darkness seems to shift.

Closer sounds are continuous: the grinding of gravel in a narrow channel, the knocking of loose stones, the scrape of a dead branch drawn back and forth across a wall. On certain slopes, flat leaves strike one another with a thick, rhythmic clapping. A grove can be heard well before it can be seen.

Water still follows the old streams.

This is the first thing about the place that cannot be explained simply by depth or temperature. The mountain is wholly submerged, yet its former watercourses remain active. Along a dry-looking gully, a band of water runs faster than the water beside it. It rounds the same stones, gathers at the same ledges, and pours over the same falls. There is no empty space for it to fall into. The current descends through water, carrying grains of mica and strips of vegetation, its edges visible only when it catches a little light.

At a former waterfall, this produces a pale vertical ribbon. Its impact has kept a bowl open in the sediment below. Everything around the bowl may lie still while the bowl itself rolls with pebbles.

The streams cannot easily be diverted. Fallen rock divides them for a while, but eventually they return to their earlier channels, slipping through rubble or lifting across it in low, clear arches. The oldest channels are the strongest. A recent cut in the slope carries almost nothing.

There are structures beside these currents.

A few are stone huts with low doorways and roofs made from overlapping slabs. Others are little more than retaining walls or platforms projecting over a channel. Their builders used river-rounded stones, fitted patiently together, and wedges of wood that have swollen tight in their sockets. There are no engines, no pipes, no elaborate mechanisms. In one shelter, a row of shallow wooden bowls remains fixed beneath a trickle. In another, a stone trough feeds three beds of pale, narrow-leaved plants.

The beds are still tended by the arrangement of the building. Water enters through a notch, passes over the roots, and leaves through a lower opening. The roof keeps drifting debris away. New shoots have replaced old ones many times without changing the outline of the garden.

Most shelters stand empty. Their doors, where they survive, are tied open with cords now furred with growth. There are worn steps, stone seats, bundles of rods laid beneath ledges. Nothing indicates a sudden departure. The objects seem to have been put down in their usual places.

One house stands alone at the mouth of a pass. Its rear wall is built directly against the mountain, and its front faces a broad black basin. A long branch has rooted in the roof. The branch carries a curtain of leaves that rises whenever the current strengthens, exposing the doorway, then sinks again across it. Inside, silt lies evenly over the floor except for a clear strip leading from the threshold to a niche in the wall. The water keeps that strip swept clean.

Beyond the house, the road divides. One branch descends toward the unseen bottom of the basin. The other climbs to a saddle where the ice presses close to the tallest stones.

During the coldest part of the year, the roof grows downward. Fine needles of ice hang into the upper groves, and the plants bend away from them. During the thaw, dim seams open overhead. For a few days the mountain has color: green along the warm cracks, rust-red mineral fans below the springs, blue in the old compressed ice.

Then the seams close. The groves return to shadow, the streams continue along their remembered beds, and somewhere beyond the next ridge a loose stone begins knocking against another stone.
