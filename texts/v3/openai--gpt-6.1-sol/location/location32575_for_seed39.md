The plateau is high enough that most of the weather passes below it. Through breaks in the cloud, you can see the tops of lower mountains, dark and rounded by distance, protruding from a white layer that hides their valleys. Up here the land has not yet acquired that softness. Its edges are sharp. Its hollows are abrupt. Even under a dim sky, the stone looks newly exposed.

There is no single summit. The country spreads across a broad volcanic shoulder: several shallow basins, a long ridge of black rock, a scatter of low cones, and, beyond them, slopes rising into cloud. The distances are difficult to judge. With no trees, buildings, or roads to give proportion, a ledge that appears close may take half a day to reach. A flock of birds crossing it provides the first reliable measure of its height.

Much of the ground is young lava. It has cooled into slabs, ropes, and plates whose broken edges have scarcely been weathered. In places, a brittle skin lies over an empty space left by molten rock draining away beneath it. The surface can sound hollow under a foot. Elsewhere, a flow has stopped in a bank of angular blocks, each still retaining the shape of its last movement. Pale deposits collect in their joints. Rain has begun to darken narrow paths through the dust, but has not yet cut gullies.

Water is present almost everywhere without forming a great river.

It hangs on the undersides of rock shelves. It beads along cold cracks and trickles through beds of coarse ash. The basin floors hold shallow pools, steel-gray under the clouds, their margins laced with thin ice. A stream may run visibly for twenty paces, disappear into rubble, and emerge much farther downslope as a line of wet stones. There is not yet enough soil to keep the water near the surface, nor enough erosion to give it a settled course.

After rain, the plateau briefly becomes a network of reflections. Every small depression holds a piece of sky. Then the more porous ground drinks them down, leaving water only where fine sediment has sealed the bottom or frost has stopped the drainage.

The air is cold even in the brightest part of the day. Wind comes across the open ground with little to interrupt it, lifting ash in low streaks and flattening the few grasses that have established themselves. Snow survives in the lee of ridges. It is dirty at the edges, peppered with dark grit, and underneath it the meltwater runs in tiny channels that freeze again as soon as the light weakens.

Yet there is warmth below.

At the foot of one ridge, vapor rises from a field of fissures. It does not ascend far. The cold air condenses it into low white clouds that drift over the stones, briefly hiding the ground. Some vents breathe steadily; others fall silent for several minutes before releasing a soft rush. Close to them, yellow and cream-colored minerals crust the rock. Small pools tremble as bubbles rise through their bottoms.

This is where the landscape acquires its industrial character, though nothing has been built. Pressure accumulates underground. Water enters, heats, circulates, and returns. Certain cracks give off a faint hiss, like a distant valve. Beneath a broad slab there is a deeper, irregular knocking, audible only when the wind drops. Warm runoff separates into channels, cools, leaves deposits, and joins the drainage farther out. Across the vent field, the same processes repeat at different temperatures and rates.

From a distance, all of this looks almost still.

Most of the plateau is quiet enough that a wingbeat can be heard. The wind has no branches to shake. There is the dry movement of grit, the occasional click of a pebble slipping from a thawing bank, and water dripping somewhere out of sight. The steam carries sound poorly and hides its sources. You can stand beside a cloud of it and hear a small pool boiling without being able to see the pool.

Vegetation occupies the exceptions.

A green stain follows a warm seep. Moss grows in a sheltered seam where the rock stays wet but does not become hot. Short sedges gather beside the oldest pools, their roots caught in a mixture of ash, bird droppings, and decayed stems. On more exposed slopes, cushion plants sit low and compact, each one surrounded by a wide reach of bare stone.

There is very little that can be called continuous cover. To cross between two patches of green is to cross cold rubble, wet slabs, and dust that has only recently begun to hold together.

The animals make much fuller use of the place.

At first they are easy to miss. The open ground seems empty until something moves at the edge of a pool: a small bird stepping quickly along the waterline, stopping, probing, stepping again. Then there are several. Farther across the basin, a pale strip that looked like lingering snow breaks into dozens of bodies as a flock rises.

Birds gather around the wet margins in great numbers, feeding on insects emerging from the warmer shallows. In sheltered pools, larvae darken the submerged stones. When the wind falls, small flies hang above the water in dense, low columns. Their abundance is local and precise. A few paces beyond the warm margin, the air is almost clear of them.

Waterfowl rest on the larger pools during their passage through the mountains. Shorebirds work the mud exposed by changing water levels. Small ground-nesting birds disappear against the gravel until they move. A falcon crosses the basin on a nearly motionless set of wings, and the feeding flocks contract beneath it.

Higher on the ridge, animals have worn narrow tracks through the ash: mountain sheep, moving between exposed forage and mineral-rich seepage. Their coats are pale with dust. They spend long periods standing in places where there seems to be nothing to eat, then descend to a green patch too small to notice from below. Fox tracks follow the pool margins after fresh snow. At night, something takes eggs from a nest sheltered beneath a lava lip.

The abundance does not fill the space. It concentrates at water, warmth, and shelter, leaving immense intervals between. A basin may be busy with feeding birds while the slope above it remains vacant from end to end.

Changes happen at several speeds. Clouds arrive quickly, closing the view within minutes. Pool edges freeze overnight and loosen by midday. Insects emerge during a brief calm. A flock settles, feeds, and leaves. Beneath these movements, the rock is cooling, contracting, and opening new fractures. A channel that carried water yesterday may be dry today because a crack has widened somewhere upstream.

This is a landscape still establishing its drainage, its soil, its reliable places. A little sediment gathers behind a stone. Moss fixes it there. A bird brings seeds in its feathers or gut. Frost lifts the stone, and part of the small beginning washes away.

Toward evening, the lower clouds rise until they reach the plateau. The distant ridges vanish first. Then the far side of the basin disappears, and the pools lose their reflections. Birds continue feeding close to shore. Vapor from the warm fissures merges with the cloud, and it becomes impossible to tell which whiteness comes from the sky and which comes from the ground.

Only the nearest stones remain visible: black, wet, scarcely weathered. Water collects on their edges and falls into the cracks. Somewhere beyond them, in the obscured basin, a whole flock lifts at once. You hear the brief, soft effort of their wings, then nothing but the dripping and the wind.
