The city lies high above the clouds, but the water is higher still.

It comes out of the mountain behind the city in clear sheets, running down pale rock without quite breaking into streams. At a distance, the slopes look varnished. Closer, you can see the water slipping through shallow grooves worn over centuries, dividing around knobs of stone, gathering again below them. Every ledge has a wet underside. Every crack darkens toward its interior. On the level ground, a footprint fills before its edges have finished crumbling.

There are no trees to explain this abundance. No reeds crowd the channels, no moss softens the retaining walls. The plateau is almost entirely white stone, black machinery, and water. Here and there, a basin has dried to a crust the color of an old tooth.

The sun is fierce. It shines through a thin, high haze and strikes the pale ground hard enough that the undersides of things are bright: the bottom of a bridge, a person’s chin, the bellies of animals standing in the shallows. Shade is sharply bounded and precious, although it offers little coolness. The water emerging from the mountain is hot. So are the paving stones. A hand placed on a railing must move again almost immediately.

Across several miles of gently sloping ground, the city spreads in widely separated pieces. A row of low houses stands beside an immense settling pool. Farther out is a power station, then a customs office, then nothing built for half a mile except channel walls and a solitary stairway. The open spaces are not vacant lots. They are part of the city’s working body: shallow reservoirs, evaporating courts, fields of mineral precipitation. You can see three districts from a doorway, with bright, apparently empty land between them.

Most buildings stand on piers. Beneath them, water moves in broad, dark rectangles. There are no cellars.

The older houses have rounded corners and heavily recessed windows. Their lower walls are coated with deposits that nobody bothers to remove: cream-colored ribs, copper-green blisters, translucent shelves as fine as broken glass. The deposits record small changes in the channels. A sill that once stood above water is now buried halfway in stone grown out of water; a door opened fifty years ago onto dry paving and now opens onto a narrow bridge.

The inhabitants do not call this decay. They file it under maintenance.

At the edges of the settlement, the mountain’s original shapes remain visible. Long, low-backed ridges divide the plateau into basins. None are dramatic enough to have names on a tourist map, but everyone who works the water knows them. One ridge sends a dawn overflow toward the northern sheds. Another keeps a certain pool nearly still even when the rest of the city is draining. The stone has been dissolving and depositing for so long that its hollows seem deliberate. A round basin empties through a notch exactly wide enough to prevent it from overtopping. Downstream, another catches what the first releases.

The engineers have mostly built alongside these arrangements. Their machines occupy the useful interruptions.

Inside the power station, water passes through submerged wheels. You see their rotation through inspection windows: dark spokes moving slowly behind greenish glass. There is no hammering engine, no chimney. Pressure from the heights does the work, and the station sends electricity along thick cables suspended beneath the walkways. At intervals, a cable descends into a sealed ceramic housing, where something is refined, separated, or heated beyond the already punishing temperature of the afternoon.

The city manufactures metals, salts, optical glass, and very fine wire. Its factories have the outward quiet of bathhouses. Long sheds sit over flowing water, their roofs held high on narrow supports. Workers cross from tank to tank carrying instruments under white cloths. A crane advances so slowly that you only notice its movement when you look away and back again.

There should be an industrial roar, but almost all the heavy motion happens underwater. Bearings are enclosed. Discharge pipes end beneath the surface. Even the freight wagons have thick, soft wheels, because a hard wheel quickly fractures the mineral skin on the roads.

What sound remains is small: a fastening knocked against a railing, the papery scrape of a shoe drying as it walks, a single drip falling into an empty bucket. Conversation does not carry far. Beyond the last houses, the silence becomes spacious enough that you can hear the movement of water without hearing a particular stream.

And then the empty land begins to move.

A pale shape detaches itself from a pale stone. Another unfolds from the shadow beneath a sluice. What looked like a scatter of wet pebbles turns, all at once, to face the passing shadow of a bird.

There are animals everywhere.

They are difficult to distinguish from their surroundings at first. Many have translucent bodies, their organs suspended inside them like small pieces of colored thread. Others carry flat, mineral-armored backs, polished by the hot water until they resemble the stones they rest among. Along the channels, six-legged grazers work over fresh deposits with toothed mouthparts. They leave clean, wandering trails across the pale crust.

What they eat is not quite stone. In the warm seams where different waters meet, thin films grow: gray, amber, faintly violet, sometimes visible only by a change in sheen. The films take their nourishment from dissolved minerals and the chemical imbalance between one spring and another. They have no leaves, stems, or recognizable roots. Scrape a surface clean, and by the next morning it may be slippery again.

Most of the city’s animals live by these films, or by eating something that does.

Small swimmers gather where the water changes temperature. They hold position in hundreds, each nearly motionless, with transparent fins making rapid adjustments. Larger creatures lie downstream with their mouths open. Once in a while, one lifts its body and moves a few feet, and a whole patch of apparently clear water scatters.

On the dry ridges, long-legged scavengers pick through the windblown salt. Their feet spread into many fine toes. They walk carefully, testing the crust before committing their weight. Some are taller than a person at the shoulder, but their bodies are narrow and almost weightless-looking. They shelter beneath loading platforms during the brightest hours, sharing the shade with workers who take little notice of them.

The birds are heavier, black-backed and pale below. They circle high over the plateau on heat rising from the basins. Their nests are built from wire stolen from the factories, laid in loose bowls on the coolest roof corners. There is no dawn chorus. A bird calls only when something has gone wrong, and then its short, harsh note is conspicuous enough to make people look up from several streets away.

By late afternoon, the channels have changed color.

As the upper pools evaporate, the dissolved matter becomes concentrated. One run turns faintly blue. Another acquires an orange margin. Fine crystals appear along the edges, each wet at its base and dry at its tip. Small animals climb them to reach cooler air. From a bridge, their bodies look like drops trying to run upward.

This is when the sluice keepers go out.

They carry long-handled tools and wear felt soles strapped over their shoes. At each gate, they take a sample, hold it against the light, and sometimes taste a trace from a glass rod. Then a wheel is turned, or left alone. The decisions are modest, but their consequences travel through the whole plateau. A few inches of additional flow can dissolve a week’s growth of crust. Closing a gate too early can strand an entire basin’s swimmers in water that becomes lethal before dusk.

Children learn to read these changes before they learn the names of the streets. Cloudy water is not necessarily bad; perfectly clear water is not necessarily safe. A white margin means one thing when it is soft and another when it rings under a fingernail. Nobody steps onto a fresh-looking surface merely because it appears dry.

Toward evening, the mountain releases more water. No rain has fallen over the city, but somewhere beyond the ridge, cloud has condensed against colder rock. The new flow arrives warm, in pulses. Pools rise by the width of a finger. Deposits disappear beneath a bright skin. The little armored grazers retreat onto higher shelves, while the swimmers spread into places that were bare all day.

For an hour, the city is full of subtle crossings: animals moving up, water moving down, workers returning over the bridges with their tools wrapped to keep them from clicking together.

The sun drops behind the upper escarpment, and the glare leaves abruptly. Heat remains stored in every surface. The buildings seem to exhale it. Above the pools, steam becomes visible at last, low and delicate, drifting between the widely spaced houses.

From the far end of the plateau, the settlement looks smaller than it is. A few roofs, a few lit windows, the dark skeleton of a crane. Most of its streets cannot be seen because they are water. Most of its inhabitants are hidden under ledges, beneath the factories, inside the warm clear basins.

The night shift crosses to the power station. Far up the slope, a gate opens.

A new silver line appears on the mountain, and slowly makes its way toward the city.
