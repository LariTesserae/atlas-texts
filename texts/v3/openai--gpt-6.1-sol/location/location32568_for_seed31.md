The town stands high on a broad shelf of black rock, with water almost everywhere beneath it.

There is no single river. Water appears in shallow basins between the houses, in narrow seams along the streets, in the pale green mouths of wells that need scarcely any rope. At the edge of the shelf it seeps out of the cliff in hundreds of separate threads. Most cling to the stone rather than falling free. From a distance, the escarpment looks varnished.

Beyond it, other shelves extend across the valley: wide, level surfaces separated by abrupt drops, each carrying its own pools and patches of vegetation. The farthest are blue with distance. Above them rise blunt peaks whose dark faces still show the shapes in which the rock first cooled. Their ridges have not yet been softened into rounded shoulders. Broken edges are clean; fallen blocks remain angular. Soil occupies hollows and cracks but has not covered the stone.

Despite the height, the air is warm. Heat lingers in the paving under bare feet, and the deepest wells give off a faint breath of mineral warmth. The clouds are usually above the town rather than around it. Daylight arrives broad and ordinary, softened a little by moisture, bright enough to show the bottoms of the shallower pools. There are no dramatic shafts or perpetual twilight. A white wall looks white. Wet stone looks darker than dry stone.

The town spreads farther than its number of buildings would suggest.

Houses stand in loose groups around paved courts, with gardens, reservoirs, and stretches of exposed rock between them. A street may pass six doorways and then continue for a long distance beside nothing but a low wall. From one square, the next is visible across an open strip of green, though reaching it requires a detour around a basin. The gaps are part of the town’s arrangement. Channels cross them; paths divide them; retaining walls hold the few deep beds of soil.

Most buildings are made from slabs split along the rock’s natural seams. The walls are thick and imperfectly regular, their joints packed with clay. Some are washed with pale lime. Roofs are nearly flat, covered in beaten earth and edged with small gutters cut from wood. Each roof drains into a covered cistern, even though the ground is wet. Roof water is cleaner than the water that has travelled through the warm stone.

Construction is simple, but the handling of water is exact. A narrow channel beside a doorway turns twice before joining the street drain. A courtyard slopes so slightly that the eye misses it, yet no puddle remains at its center. Where a path crosses soft ground, flat stones rest on shorter stones driven down to the bedrock. Small wooden gates can divert a runnel toward a planting bed or away from a wall. The gate handles have been polished smooth by use.

At the town’s upper end is a long, roofed work court. Beneath it sit grinding stones, baskets, clay jars, wooden wedges, and shallow trays for drying fiber. A few simple metal blades are kept out of the damp in wrapped bundles. The building has no imposing façade. Its importance shows in the generous roof span, the well-fitted paving, and the several paths that meet at its entrance.

Nothing here requires machinery. Loads move on shoulders or low wooden sledges along the smoothest streets. Steps are shallow and broad. Water does much of the work that can be given to it; everything else is arranged to be done slowly by hand.

Vegetation is lush where it takes hold, but it does not take hold everywhere.

Tall reeds fill the larger basins. Broad-leaved plants crowd the mouths of seepages, their leaves dark, heavy, and unmarred. Moss makes thick pads on shaded walls. In the gardens, climbing stems are tied to wooden frames, and low crops grow in rectangular beds raised above the saturated ground. Small trees stand singly in courtyards where enough soil has been gathered for their roots.

Between these green places lie expanses of bare rock. Some are no larger than a floor; others occupy the space of several streets. Their surfaces carry shallow cooling ripples and thin, straight fractures. Moisture darkens the cracks. Plants follow them in narrow lines, then stop where the rock closes again. Seen from a high roof, the town is pale masonry and black paving among separate islands of dense green.

There are no animals. No birds perch on the roof edges, no insects hang above the pools, no small movement disturbs the reeds from within. The planting beds have no fences, and the grain stores need protection only from moisture. Spiderwebs do not collect beneath the eaves. Fallen leaves rest where they land until they soften into the wet ground or are gathered away.

The absence is most noticeable in the sound.

Water is plentiful, but seldom loud. It slips through narrow cuts, presses against the edges of basins, and drops from one ledge to another by inches. Only near the escarpment can its many small sounds be heard together, and even there they remain a low, continuous brushing. In the town, a jar set on a stone threshold carries across a courtyard. A footstep is clear and brief. Behind a partly open door, the movement of a wooden stool is audible from the opposite side of the square.

The air scarcely moves. Leaves at the edges of the gardens hold their positions for a long time. Washing laid over a courtyard rail hangs straight down.

Near the center of town is an open reservoir, broad enough to reflect several houses without reflecting the whole town. Its rim consists of four courses of stone, with steps descending at two corners. A dark band marks the water level. Above it, the masonry is dry; below it, the joints support a short green growth. The band is even and familiar. There are no higher flood stains.

Much of the place has this settled quality. The channels carry the amounts they were cut to carry. The wet beds remain wet; the exposed paving remains firm. The sharp cliffs look newly formed, but there are no fresh heaps beneath them. Walls have darkened and handles have worn smooth without the streets filling with damage. The town occupies a narrow, comfortable balance between warmth, water, and solid ground.

From its outermost path, the whole arrangement becomes visible: roofs spaced around pools, gardens fitted into fractures, pale lanes joining one inhabited court to another. Beyond the last cistern, the paving ends at an unworked sheet of rock. A few reeds stand in a shallow depression. Farther out, water reaches the cliff and disappears over its edge.

Across the valley, another level shelf catches the light. Its surface is mostly empty, except for a dark patch of vegetation and a low line of masonry too distant to distinguish clearly. The space between the shelves is immense and quiet. Warm air rests above the wet stone, and the town’s small channels continue to run.
