You find it above the last occupied roofs, behind a parapet so worn that it looks less like masonry than a fold in the hill. A narrow gap admits one shoulder at a time. Beyond it is a courtyard scarcely wider than a room, tucked between the back wall of an old dwelling and the sandstone into which the dwelling was built.

From the street, nothing suggests it is there. You hear the town’s hand-mills and the knock of someone splitting kindling, then pass through the gap and hear a much closer, busier noise.

The courtyard has no well, no basin, no wet patch beneath a leaking pipe. Its floor is powdered stone. Even in the deepest shade, the dust falls loose from your fingers. A broken earthenware cup beside the entrance holds a dead leaf and the husk of a beetle.

Yet the place is thick with plants.

Succulents crowd the foot of the rock wall: blunt green rosettes, branching stems with leaves like small padded fingers, low mats whose oldest growth has become a felt of dry brown threads. Several have climbed beyond their own support and lie across one another. A woody shrub has rooted in a masonry joint, and its narrow gray leaves fill much of the upper space. You must duck beneath it to enter fully.

Nothing is bright green. The colors are olive, chalk-blue, dusty silver, and the dull purple of leaves exposed to too much heat. Their abundance is in the overlap. There is scarcely a bare edge that has not been occupied by a stem, a root, or a fallen leaf waiting to crumble.

Water arrives rarely enough that there is no structure here for carrying it away. A brief shower, when one comes, soaks into the porous pots and the cracks behind them. The plants hold what they can. Their roots follow joints into cooler stone, far beyond the visible garden. Through the long dry intervals they lose a few lower leaves, draw their surfaces tight, and continue growing slowly at the tips.

The courtyard hangs high on the flank of a broad, old plateau. Through a missing block in the parapet you can see the town descending in close-packed steps, then the pale country beyond it. Distance has little texture: low ridges, gravel flats, an occasional dark seam. The once-sharp uplands have been worn into long backs. Here, too, the sandstone has almost no corners left. Old fractures open with rounded lips. Grains loosen under a thumb.

The building is younger than the rock, but it has been repaired so often that its first arrangement is hard to recover. There are door hinges without a door, a lintel half buried behind a later wall, and shallow holes where roof poles once rested. Someone has made shelves by wedging flat stones into those holes. Someone else has filled broken cooking pots with earth and set them on the shelves.

The garden rises in uneven tiers. A chipped bowl rests in the neck of a large jar. A basket, stiffened with clay, leans against the wall. Three stone troughs are balanced on scraps of timber. Everything can be lifted, patched, or retied by hand. The newest repair is a pale length of fiber cord holding a branch clear of the entrance.

Above, a reed screen stretches beneath a rock overhang. It stops most of the direct sunlight, although thin bars of it travel across the walls during the afternoon. The air is dim and warm. Heat comes through the sandstone at your back and rises from the paving; even without sun on your skin, you can feel how thoroughly the little enclosure has stored the day.

At first, the animals appear as isolated movements.

A lizard slips behind a pot. A beetle crosses the floor with dust clinging to its feet. A small bird drops onto the parapet, tilts its head toward you, and vanishes into the shrub.

Then your eyes adjust.

Ants run along nearly every joint in the wall. They have several routes, some disappearing under the containers, others climbing into the flowering stems. Pale mites gather beneath curled leaves. Wasps inspect the mouths of abandoned burrows in the mortar. A mantis hangs upside down under the reed screen, its color so close to the dry reeds that only a turn of its head reveals it.

The large jar has become a nesting place. Two sparrows enter through a crack near its rim, carrying dry fibers. Their young call from somewhere inside, and the jar gives those thin voices a hollow resonance. When a parent lands, the whole hidden brood starts at once.

There are droppings on the shelves, gnawed seed cases in the corners, shed lizard skins snagged on stems. Fine webs bridge spaces that seemed empty a moment before. A moth disturbed from the wall flutters into the shrub and sets three birds scolding.

The enclosure makes every sound close. Wings strike leaves with papery slaps. Claws scratch the pottery. Wasps pass your ear with a brief, hard buzz. Beyond the parapet, someone draws a wooden shutter across its runners; inside, the sparrows answer as if the noise belonged to them.

No part of the garden remains still for long. The wind above the roofs draws warm air out through the reed screen, and cooler air enters at your ankles through the narrow doorway. Leaves lift. Dust shifts into the same few sheltered corners. Ants carry away pieces of a fallen flower while a beetle works beneath it. Near the hottest strip of paving, a lizard waits with its body raised on straight legs, watching the traffic.

There is just enough room for a person to stand without touching anything, though not enough to turn carelessly. A shoulder can shake a branch; a foot can close an ant route; a sleeve can disturb a nest concealed in the wall.

So you stand quietly. After a while the sparrows return. The wasps resume their inspections. From the street below comes the uneven rhythm of the hand-mill, and beside your shoe a procession of ants divides around a grain of sandstone newly fallen from the wall.
