The sea lies above the mountains.

Not merely over their summits. It occupies the high country as weather once did, filling the passes, resting against the upper slopes, extending from one worn ridge to another without descending into the valleys below. Under the lowest shelf of water there is open air, and far beneath that, another landscape, almost always hidden by cloud. Sometimes a rent opens in the underside of the sea and you can look down through warm green water, through a trembling skin of silver, into several miles of empty sky.

Nothing falls through. A loosened stone sinks until it reaches that skin, then rests there, dimpling it. Over the ages these stones have accumulated into hanging shoals. Roots thread among them. Whole forests grow along the underside, their crowns reaching upward into water, their oldest roots emerging below as dark, wet tassels in the air.

From within, it is difficult to remember which way is high.

The mountains offer a more reliable direction, though even they have been softened almost past recognition. They are immense, pale swells of stone, separated by troughs broad enough to hold their own seasons. No sharp peak remains. The rock has been washed for so long that its layers no longer look fractured or deposited: they resemble the grain of wood polished by hands. Here and there a harder seam survives as a low lip, and the current folds over it in a transparent sheet.

At the crests, the water is only a few body-lengths deep. Much farther along the same ridge, without any visible change in the slope, it may become deep enough to extinguish daylight. Distance does not always keep its agreements here.

The water is warm. Even in the deepest hollows it carries the warmth of a sheltered pool late in the afternoon. Cold exists only in small, wandering pockets, each surrounded by a faint pearly boundary. They move against the currents. Plants close as one passes, and the stone inside it becomes briefly rough, recovering edges it lost long ago. Then the pocket travels on, and the rock is smooth again.

There is no clear sun overhead. The surface, where it exists, is covered by a thick suspension of pollen, minute leaves, and milky mineral dust. Light enters in broad, bruised patches of green and amber. Occasionally an entire basin brightens at once, revealing ridge after ridge behind the nearest ridge, until the terrain seems less like a country than a succession of countries laid over one another.

Then the brightness thins. The far slopes disappear first. The nearby forest remains, softly outlined, every leaf holding a little of the light after the water around it has gone dark.

Vegetation fills the habitable depths, but it does not make a uniform jungle. On the high stone grow low, muscular mats, dark green and coppery, clasping the contours so closely that a bare patch looks like an injury. Their fronds are thick, with blunt tips worn white by contact with grit. Between them rise groves of translucent stalks. Each stalk carries tiers of broad, nearly horizontal leaves, and the tiers of neighboring plants interleave with scarcely any space between.

A current moving through these groves becomes a hundred slower currents. Fine silt settles on the upper leaves. The plants tilt, not all together but in a gradual sequence across the slope, and the silt slides from tier to tier until it reaches the roots. Over centuries, this motion builds terraces of soil on stone too smooth to hold a single grain by itself.

In the troughs the vegetation grows taller. There are branching forms with bark the color of wet ash, though their branches are supple and almost transparent at the ends. Pale ribbons hang from them, taking nourishment directly from the water. Some ribbons extend so far that their lower portions enter a different current from their upper portions. They twist slowly into knots, loosen, twist again. Their shadows travel over the ground with the deliberate complexity of writing.

Most things that appear to be swimming are parts of plants.

A broad leaf releases itself and drifts to another slope, where its torn edge becomes a root. A cluster of seed bladders rises through the forest, stops halfway up, and opens into a small floating meadow. Long filaments withdraw through holes in the rock when the water darkens. Some do so quickly enough to suggest fear, but there is seldom anything nearby to fear.

Animals are scarce.

You might cross an entire basin before seeing one: a white, flat creature moving sideways between the stalks, its outline difficult to separate from the water. Or a single heavy shell traveling over a stone shelf, leaving a clean trail through the green film. The shell’s passage is slow enough that new growth begins at one end of the trail before the animal has reached the other.

High in the dimness there are sometimes enormous silhouettes. They do not gather, and they do not approach. One passes over a ridge, obscuring the light for an hour; months may go by before another follows. Beneath their paths, the floating meadows fold themselves inward and remain folded long after the shadow has gone.

The place is almost silent. There is no surf, no rain striking a surface, no wind in branches. The currents are powerful but broad, and rarely troubled enough to become audible. Near the stone you can hear grains of sand turning in shallow depressions. A leaf loosens with a soft, intimate click. Root fibers under tension occasionally part.

The rare loud sound comes from somewhere very far away: a deep, single knock, felt first in the teeth and then heard. After it, the water pauses. Suspended debris ceases to turn. Leaves hold their positions. Even the falling silt hangs still for several breaths before everything resumes, usually in a slightly different direction.

There are structures, though you may mistake the first one for an accident of erosion.

On an otherwise featureless rise, three stones stand upright around a fourth. They are not tall. Their surfaces have the same polish as the mountain, and roots pass freely through their arrangement. A hundred miles farther on, there is a ring of shallow steps cut into a summit. Each step descends toward the center, but the center contains no hollow, only unbroken rock.

Elsewhere a wall crosses a submerged saddle. It is waist-high, built of loose blocks without mortar, and unfinished at both ends. The plants avoid it by a narrow margin. Clear water occupies that margin even when the surrounding forest is thick with spores.

There are no roads between these remnants, no tools left beside them, no wreckage. Occasionally a doorway stands alone. Looking through one from a distance shows the landscape beyond it. Coming closer may reveal the same landscape in a different season: leaves newly opened where those outside are old, mineral snow descending where the surrounding water is clear. Crossing does not necessarily take you there. Sometimes it only leaves a taste of iron in the mouth.

The changes that matter here seldom announce themselves.

A ridge spends a long season moving east, so slowly that its forest has time to release its roots and fasten them again. The exposed rock behind it is not a scar but a continuation of the neighboring plain. A basin fills with clear water from below, displacing the darker water upward without mixing. Across the whole basin, plants begin to flower. Their blossoms are small and nearly colorless, but so numerous that visibility shrinks to an arm’s length.

Then comes the inland weather.

Currents rise along the mountain flanks and carry pollen into the upper depths. There it gathers in banks, thickens, and rolls over the ridgelines. These submerged clouds develop dark interiors and luminous rims. They shed rain within the water: heavier droplets, oily and gold, falling in straight lines until they touch a leaf or a stone. Where a droplet lands, a ring of new growth spreads outward.

The storm may continue for weeks. Its passage is quiet. Only the forest’s movement makes its strength visible—whole slopes bowing, terraces spilling their stored sediment, floating meadows drawn out into long green islands.

Afterward, the water becomes extraordinarily clear.

For a little while you can see how high this country is. Below the hanging shoals, clouds turn over a distant world. Above, through the thinning pollen, other mountains stand submerged at elevations that ought to be sky. Between them lie warm expanses of water too wide to measure, crowded with forests, ridges, drifting gardens, and the slow work of rearrangement.

Near at hand, a white root tip emerges from a crack in the old stone. It bends into the returning current. Fine grains gather around it, and one by one disappear beneath the widening green.
