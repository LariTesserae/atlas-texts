The high country is green even where it appears bare.

From a distance, the broad slopes look like newly exposed stone: pale shelves, steep escarpments, long aprons of gray material beneath the cliffs. But the gray is the underside of leaves. Each leaf is large enough to carry a watershed. Its upper surface faces the sky; its lower surface folds outward at the rim, showing a tough, waxy skin veined with blue-green cords. Water hangs from those cords in ropes. Where a rope breaks, the fall takes several seconds to reach the next terrace.

There is no rock under any of this.

The country has been assembled from living tissue, layer upon layer, raised above the clouds by columns that continue down out of sight. The columns are not trunks in the usual sense. They are bundles of trunks, and bundles of roots, and the swollen seams where one growing body has accepted another into itself. Some are hollow. Some contain vertical reservoirs. A few are so wide that their outer walls have their own slopes and gullies, their own smaller plants growing from the warm, sweating bark.

Nothing here has been cut to fit. Nothing has been fastened. Yet the land is full of joins.

You can see them along the ridgelines: a lip folded into a groove, a row of knuckles grown through another row, a thick green seam drawing two plateaus together. Fresh joins are cream-colored and tender. Older ones darken, swell, and disappear beneath a rind. The youngest ridges still have abrupt angles, bright torn edges, and narrow clefts where the halves have not quite met. Rain enters these clefts and comes out warm.

The air is hot enough that shade offers only a change in brightness. Moisture gathers on every surface, including surfaces sheltered from rain. Stand beneath a terrace and droplets collect on your hair. Stand out in the sun and the same thing happens, more slowly. The wind is wet. It arrives uphill through the great shafts with the smell of crushed stems and the sourer smell of tissues being taken apart.

For much of the day there is ordinary sunlight: white, strong, interrupted by large, fast clouds. It picks out the faint ribs in the ground and makes the shallow pools look flat and metallic. No strange glow is needed to see how strange the country is. In clear weather, three or four valleys are visible at once, though none rests on earth. They hang at different heights, connected by ramps of folded leaf and long, slack bridges of vine.

These bridges are few. Everything substantial is far apart.

A single pillar may stand alone in a basin several miles across. Between it and the next pillar lies a shallow sheet of water, a green floor, and nothing upright at all. There are no thickets, no scattered trees, no busy foreground of shrubs. Vegetation occupies the entire space without crowding it. The floor itself is vegetable; the walls are vegetable; beneath the water, the soft green lining is a membrane of closely intergrown roots. What would elsewhere be empty space remains empty here: broad air, broad water, room for weather to cross.

The apparent simplicity does not last long underfoot.

The ground has a grain. Walk with it and the surface is firm, faintly resilient. Walk across it and your soles catch on transverse ridges. In places the grain forks around a round pool, then rejoins on the far side. At a steep slope it tightens into corrugations that serve as steps, although their spacing is meant for water rather than feet.

Most paths are dry spillways. They descend from reservoir to reservoir, following the cheapest route for a flood. In the morning they may be walkable. By noon a distant wall can open, and the same path becomes a brown-green torrent carrying folded sheets of skin. There is no dependable distinction between a road and a riverbed.

The reservoirs are held behind thick rolls of tissue. Some resemble low embankments, smooth and rounded. Others have an unfinished look: upright plates standing edge to edge, still separate at their tips. Water leaks through the gaps with a loud, steady hiss. As the plates broaden, the gaps narrow and the water rises. Eventually the weight exceeds what the wall can bear.

A failure begins with a small pale crease.

Then the crease lengthens.

The sound is astonishingly deep: a tearing too large to seem like tearing, followed by the heavy slap of a wall folding outward. Water crosses the basin in a moving ridge. The green floor bows beneath it, flattens, then springs back after the flood has passed. Far downstream, a new pool fills. A dry terrace darkens. Some suspended bridge takes the extra load, twists, and pulls free at one end.

For an hour the entire valley changes its drainage.

By evening, the torn wall has fringed itself with white rootlets.

This is how most of the landscape is made. Growth creates a shape; water tests it; the broken parts become the joints of another shape. The country is young, but not because it has only recently begun. It is young because very little of its exposed surface is allowed to remain old.

Below the ridges there are older strata. They can be seen through openings in the floor: dark compressed layers, fibrous and nearly black, squeezed together under the weight of newer growth. Some openings breathe hot vapor. Others give a view down through tiers of deserted terraces, their reservoirs empty, their bridges hanging in soft loops. Light reaches a few of these lower places at a slant. Beyond them the depth is obscure.

The plants do not climb toward sunlight in a simple way. They climb toward load.

A stretch of tissue carrying water thickens. A bridge supporting another bridge sends out braces. A hollow column begins to divide its interior when the country above it becomes too heavy. Weight draws growth much as light does elsewhere, and fresh pressure can wake a surface that has been still for years. A fallen slab therefore does not simply lie where it fell. Its underside swells against the ground. Its edges curl. After several days, it may have become a low arch.

There are limits. Unsupported tissue dries and becomes brittle in the hot wind. A ridge growing too quickly can outpace its water supply and split from tip to base. Overextended bridges narrow into cords, then break. The wreckage accumulates only briefly. It is soaked, softened, entered by roots, and made useful again.

No animal eats it.

There are no animals at all: no insects around the pools, no birds above the cliffs, no frogs in the hot shallows. The small motions belong to plants. A fringe of fine roots combs through running water. Broad floating leaves turn their edges away from a sudden current. A field of short hollow stalks leans together before a gust reaches it, then straightens in irregular waves.

Pollination, where it occurs, is carried by wind and flood. At certain hours the tall columns open seams high on their flanks and release a powder the color of unbleached flour. It moves across the basins in low clouds. Where it lands on wet tissue, it sticks. Rain washes the rest into channels, and pale streaks collect along the shores.

There is no buzzing beneath the other sounds. No concealed rustle suggesting something has fled. The absence becomes noticeable precisely because the place is so loud.

Water drums on the underside of leaves. Hollow supports boom when their reservoirs fill. Long fibers rub together inside the bridges with a sound like ropes working against a dock. In the heat of midday, a fresh ridge can expand so quickly that its outer skin splits in a series of sharp reports. The echoes pass through the columns and return from somewhere underneath.

Sometimes the whole plateau gives one slow shudder.

It is not usually a collapse. More often a lower tier has shifted, or a support has opened a new chamber, or several square miles of loaded tissue have settled into a slightly better fit. Pools tilt. Water climbs one bank and leaves another exposed. The motion passes, and the land continues growing in its altered position.

On the highest terrace, the atmosphere thins enough for the distance to sharpen. You can see the cloud tops below, white and sunlit, pressed against the outer columns. Beyond them stand other raised countries, each with its own level summits and deep green margins. A storm moves between two of them. Its rain falls into the gap, nowhere near a valley floor.

Here the wind is stronger, but still hot. The ground is pale, nearly yellow, and firm enough to ring faintly under a step. There is little standing water. Instead, moisture beads along thousands of narrow seams and runs inward, away from the edges, toward a depression too broad to recognize immediately as a reservoir.

At its center stands a single unfinished column.

It is short for this place, perhaps the height of a town building, with four blunt walls and an open top. Water pours into it from the surrounding terrace. Its walls rise by small increments, almost imperceptibly. Now and then a new fold emerges at the rim and hangs outward, soft and translucent in the sun.

From the distant slopes, the column will look like another isolated presence in a great empty basin.

Up close, you can see the beginning of a floor in that fold. Its veins already run toward corners that have not yet formed. Rain gathers in its shallow hollows. One edge lifts in the wind, holds for a moment, and settles a little farther out.
