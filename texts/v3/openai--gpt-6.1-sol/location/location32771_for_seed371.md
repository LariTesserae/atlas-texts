The place is a notch just below the crest of a high mountain, small enough that a shouted word could cross it, though tonight no word would survive the wind. Its upper edges are lost in darkness. Below, between the trees, there is a narrow opening onto a much greater darkness: the valley, several thousand feet down.

Nothing here is level. A shelf of earth tilts toward that opening, crowded with low trees, shrubs, and exposed roots. Along its uphill side, a recent break in the mountain has left a wall of pale stone. Even in this light, the newness of it shows. The face has not acquired the dark skin of weathered rock. Its corners are sharp; its seams retain fine powder. Broken pieces lie beneath it with clean surfaces turned upward, some no larger than fingernails, others wedged into the vegetation at waist height.

The fracture has made a clearing without quite making open space. Branches reach into it from every side. Roots hang out of the torn soil above the wall, carrying clots of earth and the undersides of fern beds. One small tree has tipped with the falling ground and now grows almost horizontally across the notch. Its crown is still alive. Leaves cluster at its outer end, where they can catch the little daylight this recess receives.

At this elevation, the trees have no spare height. They grow thickly, bend early, and divide into several trunks. Their crowns overlap so closely that it is difficult to tell where one ends and the next begins. Small, stiff leaves cover the outer branches; larger, thinner ones survive underneath. Ferns occupy the gaps. Dark cushions of moss follow the bark into crotches and hollows, but stop abruptly where the wind can reach them.

From outside, this patch of forest would look like a low hump pressed into a fold of the ridge. Inside, it is several layers deep. There are leaves near the face, branches at shoulder height, another roof just overhead, and a tangled floor that hides the ground. A person could stand here without finding enough room to turn around.

It is cold, though not frozen. The air has the penetrating chill of altitude and moving cloud. Water is present mostly as a surface condition: a sheen on the sheltered sides of leaves, droplets under a root, a darkened patch of rock. There is no stream. The hollow beneath the stone wall holds damp grit rather than a pool.

Much of the mountain beyond this notch is dry. Wind has stripped the exposed slopes to stone and sparse grass. Here, a bend in the ridge catches occasional cloud, and the forest retains what the open slopes lose. Moisture condenses in the foliage, falls a short distance, and disappears into the root mat. Under the ferns, old leaves can remain cool and pliable long after the upper branches have dried. The lushness depends on shelter and accumulation, not on abundant water.

Tonight the shelter is failing in intervals.

A gust passes across the ridge, and the whole canopy lowers. Leaves turn their pale undersides toward the ground. The horizontal tree twists against the root mass that still holds it. For a few seconds, the notch fills with a hard, continuous rushing sound. Then the gust moves on, leaving individual noises behind: a branch scraping stone, loose leaves rattling together, the thick snap of wood somewhere deeper in the tangle.

There are no bird calls, no insects, no feet moving in the litter.

Beneath the wind is another sound, lower and more regular. Far down the valley, out of sight, an industrial installation is running through the night. Distance has removed most of its detail. What reaches the notch is a heavy motor note, interrupted by the faint metallic pulse of something rotating under load. Sometimes the terrain shuts it out. Sometimes the wind turns, and the machinery sounds unexpectedly close.

There is no structure here, no cable, no pipe, no cut path. The forest floor has not been cleared. Yet the mechanical rhythm occupies the place almost as steadily as the rock does. During a lull, its low vibration emerges through the rustling leaves; when the next gust arrives, it is swallowed again.

The darkness is not uniform. Beneath the shrubs it is complete. Against the fresh stone, shapes remain faintly separable. Beyond the opening, clouds occasionally uncover a small patch of sky, just bright enough to show the ridge opposite before covering it again. Each glimpse changes the apparent size of the drop. With the distant slope visible, this is a cramped ledge above a valley. Without it, the outer edge seems to end in nothing at all.

A little rain comes sideways. It ticks sharply on the stiff leaves and makes almost no sound on the moss. For a minute, the air smells of wet mineral dust. Droplets gather on the fallen tree, travel along its slanting branches, and spill from the lowest twig. Then the rain stops. The exposed stones begin drying almost at once.

The loose ground continues to move.

Fine grit skitters from the new fracture and lands in the fern crowns. A pebble drops, strikes a buried slab, and knocks another free. The second fall is louder. It travels through several layers of branches before reaching the edge, where its sound continues downward after the stone itself has disappeared.

Somewhere above, an exposed root tightens with a small creak.

The forest holds much of the broken slope together, but it does not make it stable. Roots grip blocks that may move independently. Moss conceals fresh cracks. A mat of shrubs can remain intact while the earth beneath it slips a hand’s breadth downhill. In the dark, the dense growth gives an impression of solidity that the intermittent falls keep disproving.

Another gust arrives, stronger than the last. The crowns flatten. The hanging roots swing away from the stone wall. Leaves strike one another so rapidly that their separate sounds become a broad, abrasive roar. Under it, the distant machinery continues at the same speed.

When the wind eases, the notch has changed slightly. A branch rests lower. A pale chip of rock lies on a black bed of leaves. There is a new gap beneath the fallen tree, narrow enough to overlook, through which cold air rises from the drop.

The whole place is only a few dozen paces across. Within that small boundary, almost everything is touching something else, and almost nothing is still.
