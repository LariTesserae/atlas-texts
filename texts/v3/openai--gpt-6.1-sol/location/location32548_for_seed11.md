From the low country, the heights look like the worn teeth of something that has almost finished disappearing. They have no sharp summits. Their tops are broad and blunt, their shoulders rounded, their sides stained in long, faint colors that might be mineral seams or the remains of old waterfalls. Between them lie distances that the eye at first mistakes for shadow.

Then a cloud moves through one of those distances, very far below the summits, and its smallness becomes apparent.

The plateau stands above most of the weather. Its cliffs descend into a white brightness through which, on clear mornings, other cliffs can be seen descending farther. Nobody has measured the whole depth. The people who cross the upper country use a knotted cord to measure wells, roof beams, and the width of a field. They do not use it on the edges.

Up here the ground is old almost beyond texture. Stone has been rubbed down to long slopes and shallow dishes. There are no fresh screes, no glittering broken faces, very few loose rocks. Even the gravel is rounded. In places the surface resembles the back of an immense clay pot, fired unevenly, then weathered until all its colors are quiet: plum brown, pale ocher, a greenish gray that darkens under a wet hand.

You can walk across a rise for an hour without seeing a single thing that interrupts its curve.

Yet the plateau is green.

The green lies close to the ground, thick and supple, collected in folds. Ferns with leathery, overlapping fronds fill the hollows. Small-leaved vines knit themselves into cushions deep enough to hide a person lying down. There are forests only a little taller than a knee, their woody stems braided horizontally, their crowns pressed flat by the wind. Beneath those crowns the air is warm and still, and roots descend through generations of black, fibrous humus.

From a distance these growths appear to be islands separated by bare stone. Close to them, they are extravagant. One hollow may hold thirty kinds of leaf: blades as fine as hair, waxy cups, little red fans, soft silver surfaces that turn green when brushed the other way. Moss fills the seams between stems. Flowers open inside the shelter of other flowers. The dead material is eaten, softened, threaded with roots, and used again before much of it can blow away.

There is almost no shade large enough for a standing person.

The few trees grow sideways from sheltered lips of rock. Their trunks travel along the ground, enter a crack, disappear, and emerge some distance away carrying another cluster of leaves. It is difficult to say where one tree ends. Some have polished stones caught inside them, completely enclosed by wood except for a small exposed window. The oldest trunks have become hollow tunnels through which the wind makes a low, hornlike sound.

The wind is the first thing a visitor hears and the last thing they cease trying to hear through.

It rises from the immense valleys in hot, disorderly columns. It pours over one lip of the plateau, races across the top, and encounters air climbing from another side. The collisions are invisible until vegetation lifts, grit leaps, or a cloud is torn into ribbons. Then whole acres seem to turn inside out.

For much of the day the sound is too great for conversation. It has several layers: a broad roar overhead, a hard scraping at ground level, and underneath both a deep vibration carried by the stone. Close to the edges there is also a continuous detonating sound, as though enormous doors are being slammed far down in the white air.

Speech travels badly. A shout may vanish a few paces away. A quiet word spoken into the mouth of a hollow root can emerge distinctly on the other side of a shelter.

People touch one another’s forearms before speaking.

The warmth is surprising. The height ought to make this a cold place, and there are cold moments—especially at dawn, when dew silvers the exposed surfaces—but by midmorning the stone gives off a steady heat. Some of it comes from the sun. Some rises from below. Sit in a shallow basin after sunset and your legs remain warm while the wind chills your shoulders.

No one agrees about the warmth beneath the plateau. At certain fissures, a little breath of heated air comes out smelling of wet iron. Elsewhere, water drawn from a deep crack is warm enough to soften a seed. The ground never trembles in any ordinary way, and there are no smoking mountains. The oldest stories describe something underneath turning in its sleep. The practical people use the warm fissures for drying cord.

Water is scarce in sight but persistent in its habits.

Rain arrives abruptly, often from a cloud that seems too small to contain it. A dark patch forms on the stone. The wind pushes that patch across the plateau, and for a few minutes everything within it is streaming. Then it is gone. On either side, the ground remains dry.

The plants receive most of their moisture at night. Cool air settles against warm rock; vapor gathers on the sheltered undersides of leaves. The smallest fronds are shaped to feed their droplets inward. At dawn, a person can lift a mat of vegetation and find the soil beneath it damp, while the exposed stone beside it is dusty.

There are pools in some of the deeper hollows. They are clear, brown at the bottom, fringed with green so dense it appears padded. Their surfaces remain level while the plants around them writhe. Occasionally a pool empties without warning, draining through a newly opened passage. A few days later, water may appear in a hollow far away. Paths bend around old pool beds; even dry, their mud stays soft beneath its crust.

The most sheltered basins have a smell of bruised leaves, sour fruit, and warm earth. Their luxuriance is startling after the long expanses of stone. A traveler enters one through a narrow gap and finds the vegetation piled shoulder-high, heavy with pale berries and fleshy stalks. The roar diminishes enough to hear water falling from leaf to leaf.

Such places are separated by miles.

Animals are seldom seen. There are small pale moths that stay beneath the foliage and emerge only when the wind drops. A narrow-bodied lizard lives in the warm cracks. Its toes spread into soft, gripping pads; when disturbed, it runs downward into the stone rather than across it.

Once or twice in a season, long-winged birds pass over the plateau. They do not flap. They tilt, disappear into the brightness, and return from an unexpected direction. Finding one of their feathers is an event worth carrying news about.

More commonly, life reveals itself by an absence: a hollowed berry, a clean path beneath the fern mat, a little heap of shells beside a root. Whatever made these things is already elsewhere.

The dwellings are almost as elusive.

A roof may be mistaken for a low outcrop until a hand emerges from beneath it. Houses are sunk into the earth at the sheltered ends of hollows, built of turf, woven roots, and slabs carried from places where the ancient stone still separates into thin sheets. Their entrances face the ground. To enter, one crawls beneath a sloping cover and turns twice.

Inside, the wind becomes a pressure against the roof. Lamps burn seed oil in stone cups. There are baskets, wooden bowls, stone scrapers, and a few dark metal blades sharpened so often that their handles seem too large for them. Damp clothes hang close to a warm wall. The roofs are tied down with living vines whose roots remain in the soil outside.

Far from any house, a traveler sometimes finds a shallow sleeping pit covered with a woven lid, or three upright stones enclosing a hearth. Beside one abandoned shelter, the remains of a clay vessel have worn down almost to the surrounding surface. Its rim is still visible after rain.

No settlement can be seen from another. Across the broad country, smoke is beaten flat as soon as it leaves a vent.

The people know the wind by its changes in weight. They can tell when a crossing is possible by watching a single grass blade near the threshold. They travel crouched, with packs strapped low and loose sleeves bound at the wrist. Over difficult ground they carry a forked staff, pressing its prongs into cracks before shifting their feet.

For all the smoothness of the terrain, nothing on its surface stays still for long. Leaves turn over. Mats of growth bulge and settle. Seeds stream across miles of bare rock, collect in a hollow, and are scattered again. A sheet of rain comes running sideways. Sunlight opens behind it, clear and ordinary, illuminating every wet stem.

Sometimes the wind gets beneath an entire field of low vegetation.

The roots usually hold. The field lifts in sections, showing its dark underside, then drops with a succession of heavy impacts. But once in a while a piece tears free. It tumbles across the stone like a huge, green sail, trailing earth and roots. It may lodge in another basin and live there. It may go over the edge.

Afterward, the bare patch is quiet only in appearance. Roots exposed along its margins begin to dry. Loose soil departs grain by grain. Within days the old, smooth stone is visible again.

From the shelter of a deep hollow, one can watch all this happening across the open heights: the running shadows, the shuddering green, the brief silver of rain. The rock gives the impression of having outlasted every possible catastrophe. Above it, the air is in ceaseless upheaval.

And at one’s feet, protected by a stone no higher than a hand, a small fern slowly uncurls into the warmth.
