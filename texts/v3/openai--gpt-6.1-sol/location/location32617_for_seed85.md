The town occupies the last thirty meters of a mountain, but there is no shore.

Water covers the summit and continues upward into darkness. Below, it fills the valleys to a depth no instrument here has finished measuring. The mountain’s height survives as a fact of distance: beneath the town, the rock falls away for kilometers, and beneath that are the old lowlands. You can feel the altitude in the exposed shape of the place, in the narrowness of its footing. Everything has been built on a crest.

The water is cool enough to make bare fingers ache. It is unusually clear. A light carried through it reveals no sediment, no green suspension, no restless glitter of minute life. The beam simply weakens until the things within it become difficult to distinguish. Beyond that distance, the town could be larger than it is. Only by crossing it do you discover how little there is: a square, seven buildings, a passage beneath the square, and a row of vacant foundations along the northern edge.

The square is scarcely wider than a house.

Its paving consists of pale slabs fitted around the natural summit rock. That rock is black, freshly fractured, with edges that would cut a hand. Thin seams of blue-gray mineral run through it. Nothing has softened the corners. There are no rounded pebbles in the joints, no accumulated silt, no shells fastened to the exposed faces. The mountain looks newly broken, although the town seems to have occupied it for a very long time.

Along one side of the square stands a low rail. Beyond the rail, the paving ends and the mountain drops almost vertically. Looking over it with a lamp, you see several meters of dark stone, a projecting blade of crystal, then the small cone of your own light descending into open water.

The rail has three gates. None leads to a stair.

The buildings are narrow and widely spaced, each with its own patch of unoccupied ground. Their walls are made of a dense, pearl-colored material that shows no joints. Here and there the material becomes transparent, not in window-shaped sections but in irregular areas, as though part of a wall has ceased to obstruct sight. Through one of these places you can see a room containing a table, two shallow recesses in the floor, and a vertical shaft of darkness.

Water fills the room. It fills every room.

Doorways remain open. There are no seals, no pressure locks, no machinery laboring to maintain a pocket of air. The inhabitants, if these are still their buildings, required none. A narrow channel enters each house at floor level and passes out through the opposite wall. All seven channels lead toward the square, where they terminate at a circular stone set flush with the paving.

Nothing flows through them.

The circle is the town’s only source of light. It gives off a dim gray illumination, sufficient to show the channels and the lower portions of nearby walls. The light has no visible grain or flicker. When you cover the circle, the square remains faintly lit. When you move away, the brightness does not follow you.

Beneath the square is a municipal chamber.

Its entrance is a slit between two paving slabs, wide enough for a person to descend sideways. There are steps, though underwater they serve mainly as something to hold. The chamber below is the size of a modest chapel. Along its walls stand empty frames, each supporting a thin surface that resembles a sheet of clear water held upright inside the surrounding water.

Touching one produces a small, precise resistance.

Behind the surfaces are views of the town: the rail, the houses, the northern foundations. They show the place from positions where no observer could stand, including from inside the rock. One view reveals the mountain as a collection of separate, sharp pieces. Fine intervals of darkness divide them, yet nothing has slipped.

The town was built during the summit’s formation. Something interrupted the fracture before it became a collapse.

That interruption is still being maintained.

You begin to recognize its effects above ground. A chip of stone hangs beside the rail, a finger’s breadth from the edge it broke from. In a doorway, three grains of pale material remain separated by equal distances. A long crack crosses the square without widening. The water carries no current, although its depth and temperature should make stillness impossible.

The engineering has no sound you can identify as work. No hum comes from the chamber. No pipes knock in the houses. If you strike the rail, the note travels through the water and fades normally. The town does not absorb your noise, but it contributes almost nothing of its own.

There is one animal near the northern foundations.

It resembles a small ray, almost colorless, with a thick central body and two triangular fins. It rests in open water just above an unfinished threshold. Its mouth moves at long intervals. On the underside are six dark spots, arranged with the regularity of fastenings. Whether they are organs or additions is difficult to tell.

It does not turn toward a light. Passing close to it reveals a faint warmth in the water, confined to the shape of its body. Elsewhere the temperature is uniform.

No algae stain the stone. No growth occupies the empty houses. Even the places sheltered from light and disturbance are clean. The rare animal has no visible food, but perhaps the town provides something other than food. Its position over the threshold looks habitual.

At the farthest foundation, a row of rectangular sockets marks where another building would have stood. Within each socket is a little water, indistinguishable from the water everywhere else. A tool lies across two of them: a smooth handle attached to a loop too thin to see except where your light catches it.

The tool has been put down within reach.

From there, looking back, you can see nearly the entire settlement. Its small illuminated center, its widely separated doors, the pale walls rising from raw mountain stone. Above the roofs is deep water. Beyond the rail is deep water. The streets are laid out for neighbors who could cross them in a few moments.

Everything is ready for the next ordinary action: a gate opened, a tool retrieved, a visitor admitted through a doorway.

The town holds the summit together while it waits.
