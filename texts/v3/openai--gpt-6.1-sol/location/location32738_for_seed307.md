Near the summit, where the mountain has not yet acquired a name or a weathered face, a flake of black stone has lifted away from the rock beneath it. The gap is no wider than a spread hand. From outside, it looks like a wet seam.

There is a landscape inside.

Its entrance is a narrow shelf of pale grit, hot enough that a resting fingertip would begin to hurt before it became accustomed to the shape of the stone. Water creeps across this shelf in shallow, clear sheets. It comes from the back of the fissure, though the back is higher than the entrance, and occasionally it pauses on a slope without collecting or falling. A bead hangs there, round and trembling, until something touches it. Then it remembers to run downhill.

Beyond the shelf, the stone opens into steep little ravines. Their walls are fresh volcanic glass, broken so recently that some edges are still transparent. There are no rounded pebbles. Even the sand consists of tiny blades and hooks, pieces that catch one another instead of settling flat. Beneath the black glass, a softer yellow mineral swells in the heat. It pushes through cracks, forms tender-looking ridges, and hardens while you watch. An hour later the ridges split. New water enters them.

Nothing has had time to become old.

The fissure is almost at the highest point of the mountain. Outside, the air is thin, the wind severe, the neighboring peaks distant and blue. Inside, a drop from one ledge to the next can be much farther than it looks. Small creatures sometimes fall out of sight between two surfaces that appear close enough to touch. When they return, they are cold, and carry on their bodies a dust found nowhere else in the seam.

The heat comes through the floor in uneven patches. In one hollow, water boils without bubbles: its surface simply diminishes, giving off a thick, transparent vapor. In another, bubbles form underneath a crust and press it upward like a chest taking a breath. The crust eventually breaks with a faint tick. The vapor folds along the underside of the lifted flake, condenses, and rains back onto the ravines.

This rain is almost continuous, but very small. A few drops are always falling somewhere. They make dark circles in the sulfur dust, join into threads, fill pits, and spill. Some strike hot stone and disappear before spreading. Others remain cool enough to support animals.

There is no green anywhere. No roots enter from above. No leaf has blown into the opening. The nearest thing to a growing surface is a translucent skin along the cooler margins of the water, so thin that it becomes visible only when light catches it. It draws iron from the stone and leaves a rust-colored stain behind. Where animals graze it away, the stain remains as a map of their feeding.

And there are animals everywhere.

At first they resemble faults in the glass: short white lines, moving dark specks, a roughness along the lip of a pool. Then the roughness separates into bodies. Hundreds of soft, jointed creatures cling shoulder to shoulder beneath each ledge. Their feet are clear. Their backs carry flat mineral scales that darken or brighten according to the heat beneath them. They turn together when a cool drop passes nearby.

In the pools are pale swimmers, all jaw and narrow tail, maneuvering through channels scarcely wider than their bodies. A single drop may contain several, overlapping without appearing crowded until one tries to turn. On the walls, red animals with many fine limbs pick their way between hot patches. Their limb tips whiten where the stone scorches them, then are shed and replaced.

Larger creatures wait under the overhangs. They are only the length of a grain of rice, but in this place they are large. Their folded bodies resemble moist chips of obsidian. They hunt by lifting one edge of themselves and allowing a smaller animal to shelter underneath. The edge closes slowly. Sometimes the smaller animal notices and escapes; sometimes a faint cloud appears in the water, and the hunter unfolds.

There should be a continuous crackle of movement. Instead, the place is quiet. Wet feet find no purchase for a scrape. Bodies bend without rustling. Even crowded fights take place as silent changes of shape. The audible things are few: a mineral shell breaking, one drop landing in a deep cup, the soft hiss from a patch of newly exposed heat.

Light enters through the seam in an ordinary way. At midday it is white; late in the afternoon it becomes warmer. It illuminates the upper ledges and leaves the recesses dim. But shadows sometimes stay behind when their animals move. A creature can cross a pool while its shadow remains on the bottom, attending to something there. Eventually the shadow catches up. Other animals avoid the vacant stretch between them.

The inhabitants have learned some of the stone’s habits. Near the entrance, small six-limbed animals prise loose needles of glass and use them to probe the hottest cracks. They hold the needles in pairs of forelimbs, patiently turning them until a clot of edible mineral skin comes free. There are no stores of tools and no worked shelters. When a needle grows too warm to hold, it is dropped. Another is selected from the innumerable fresh fragments.

They also test the water before crossing it. A shard laid over a stream may sink, float, or stand upright. Each result provokes a different route. Upright water is especially dangerous. It looks much like any other thin thread, but an animal entering it may climb for a long time and emerge on the underside of the roof.

Most survive these mistakes. Some come back changed: an extra joint, a reversed mouth, a body that occupies slightly more space than its outline allows. The others make room for them.

The whole habitat is continually rearranging itself. Heat lifts the floor. Water opens a passage. A crowded pool drains, leaving its swimmers to crawl across wet glass until they find another. The lifted stone above settles by a fraction, and a low chamber becomes a deep one. Eggs hatch along the damp margins, their clear cases peeling open in such numbers that a bare ledge can acquire a moving fringe between one visit and the next.

Outside, clouds pass below the summit.

Their coolness reaches the entrance first. The pale grit darkens. The nearest animals withdraw a little, not far, and condensation thickens on the ceiling until its scattered beads join into a shining sheet. For several seconds the entire roof is wet.

Then one drop falls near the back.

Everything beneath it makes space.
