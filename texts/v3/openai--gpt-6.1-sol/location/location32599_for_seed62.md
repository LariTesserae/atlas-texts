The valley is high enough that sunlight arrives without much softness. Every leaf has a distinct edge. Even on the shaded slopes, the pale stone between roots shines with reflected light, and the undersides of branches are illuminated by the opposite mountain. There are few places where you can stand completely out of the brightness.

Cold settles wherever the light cannot reach directly: under broad leaves, inside the little processing houses, along the bottoms of cracks. You can walk from warm stone into air that feels refrigerated in the space of three steps. Your breath is usually invisible. Sometimes, beside a fissure, it appears for a moment and then disappears again.

The mountains are new.

Their faces have not acquired the long, rounded hollows of older ranges. They rise in slabs, steep and cleanly separated, with corners that look capable of cutting cloth. Across the valley, a whole hillside has lifted above its former position, exposing a band of pale green rock beneath the dark soil. Roots hang down this band in untidy curtains. Some still carry pockets of earth. Others have reached the rubble below and begun again.

The vegetation is older than the terrain it occupies. On the lifted hillside, orchard trees lean at angles that must once have been upright; their highest branches now turn sharply toward the sky.

There is almost no bare ground on the gentler slopes. Small-leaved evergreen trees grow so close together that their crowns form a continuous, uneven surface. Beneath them are glossy shrubs, ferns with stiff black stems, and pale climbing plants that bind trunk to trunk. Growth fills the gaps without concealing all of them. Wherever stone has recently broken, greenery stops at a sharp line, leaving fresh surfaces exposed: white, green-gray, occasionally a dark purple that looks wet even when dry.

Much of this abundance is succulent. Leaves are thick, crowded, slightly cupped. Their waxy surfaces throw back the light. A broken stem smells faintly of cucumber and gives out a bead of clear fluid, then seals itself.

Rain comes infrequently. When it does, it is brief and cold, moving across one part of the valley while the rest remains bright. From a distance you can watch the wet area darken and travel along a slope, too narrow to count as a storm.

Most water arrives by condensation.

At night, moisture collects on the trees and on the metalwork scattered among them. Before sunrise, the leaves tilt toward their stems. Drops run inward through shallow grooves, disappearing into the dark collars where leaf meets branch. A few overflow. Under certain trees the soil is damp to the depth of a hand; a pace away it is powdery enough to preserve the outline of a fallen needle.

There are pools, but you find them by looking down into breaks in the stone. Their surfaces lie several feet below ground level, bright as small windows. Ferns crowd the rims. Roots descend into them without branching until they reach the water. Some pools are clear enough to show the angular fragments on the bottom. Others have a thin, pearly skin that wrinkles when your shadow crosses it.

No stream connects them on the surface.

Across this dense growth stand the works: perhaps twenty buildings over the length of the valley, far enough apart that each seems solitary when approached on foot. They are narrow structures of concrete and glazed brick, with flat roofs and large windows. Green stains mark the walls beneath every pipe joint. Their doors have rounded handles polished by use.

The factories are small; the system joining them is not. Cables pass above the canopy, following long straight lines that disregard the slopes. Pipework emerges from one hillside and enters another. Metal rails run through tunnels just wide enough for a loaded carriage, with strips of sky visible where the roofs have split.

Everything appears maintained. A pane cracked by a root has been replaced with a smaller pane fitted around the root. A stair displaced by the rising ground has acquired three new steps. One building has been lifted bodily onto steel supports, leaving its original foundation half-hidden in ferns below.

The machinery is difficult to hear.

Inside a processing house, wide belts move at walking speed. A bank of electric motors sits behind glass, each motor scarcely larger than a kettle. There are no fans. Heat travels into ribbed pipes that disappear beneath the floor. The shafts turn through dark baths of oil, and heavy felt curtains hang between the workrooms.

Near a loading platform, you might hear the dry contact of a wheel crossing a joint. Then nothing for several minutes.

The carriages bring in thin sheets of mineral taken from the fresh faces of the mountains. These are translucent at the edges and heavy for their size. Under pressure, between chilled rollers, they separate into finer sheets. The finished material is stacked in frames and sent uphill along the cables, where it becomes difficult to distinguish from the bright sky.

Some of it returns as roof panels. Some is fitted inside the lamps, though the lamps are rarely needed. There are stacks of it in locked sheds, each sheet separated from the next by a square of black cloth.

The cold presses make use of a property peculiar to this rock: a newly opened surface continues to separate for years after the first fracture. Left alone, a slab slowly develops parallel seams. The machines keep those seams straight. Along the paths, discarded pieces have begun to fan open like books, with grasses rooting between the pages.

This gradual splitting can be seen throughout the valley. A boulder beside a path may have widened by the thickness of a fingernail since its warning mark was painted. Posts beside several cliffs carry dates and fine horizontal lines. The highest lines are recent.

Plants cover older cracks. Newer ones remain startlingly clean.

Animals are scarce. Once in a while, a small gray creature slips along a cable and vanishes into a junction box. There are a few moths in the shaded buildings, pale enough to be mistaken for scraps of mineral. You may spend a whole day beneath flowering trees without hearing an insect. The flowers are low, fleshy cups, hidden beneath the outer leaves. Pollen gathers on their rims and waits there until two branches touch.

People, when present, move quietly too. Paths have been laid with fine dark grit that gives slightly beneath a shoe. Workers carry tools in padded rolls. On the outside of every factory door is a little brush for removing grit from the soles before entering.

From the ridge, the valley opens fully: a crowded green basin, pale cliffs, scattered roofs, straight cables spanning the irregular land. Beyond it lie other basins at nearly the same height, separated by narrow walls of newly exposed stone. The vegetation continues across them in patches and bands, interrupted by the bright wounds of uplift.

At this distance, even the machinery’s movement disappears. You see only a carriage that was near one tower and is, when you look again, near another.

Toward evening the sunlight becomes more horizontal but scarcely less intense. Leaves on the western slopes close by degrees. The pale cliffs cast long blue shadows over the factory roofs, and the first beads of moisture appear along the cold rails.

For a while, before the roofs lose their light, the rails gleam beneath the trees. A drop forms under a coupling, hangs there, and falls into the dark grit without a sound.
