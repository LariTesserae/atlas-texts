The hollow lies beneath a hill, close enough to the surface that roots enter through its ceiling, but deep enough that the day arrives only in fragments.

Three narrow openings admit the light. They are not overhead in any useful sense: one lies behind a shoulder of stone, another at the end of a sloping throat, and the third is almost closed by a fig whose roots have thickened into a wooden grate. At noon, a little amber light reaches the floor. The rest of the time the hollow has the color of the underside of a leaf.

It is hot here. The hill holds the warmth of each afternoon and releases it slowly through the night. Even the stones feel faintly alive against the hand. There is no broad pool to cool the air, no running stream. Water comes by less dependable routes: along a root, through a seam, as droplets forming on the pale underside of a rock shelf. A brief shower above may produce three days of seepage below. Elsewhere, a wet season can pass without ever moistening a particular ledge.

The hollow is perhaps thirty paces long, though it is difficult to judge across it. Nothing offers a clear view. Rock pillars rise among curtains of roots; fallen slabs make low partitions; vegetation crowds each patch of usable light. You move through a succession of small enclosures, stepping sideways between stone and trunk, ducking under a branch that has grown downward before finding its way back toward the sun. The place seems larger when you are inside it, not because its boundaries recede, but because so much occupies the space between them.

Its floor is old limestone, worn into shallow dishes. Some hold damp leaf matter. Some contain only pale dust and the polished pits of fruit carried down from above. Along the walls, mineral deposits have rounded every projection. What might once have been sharp edges now resemble knuckles, folded cloth, the backs of resting animals. There are hollows smooth enough to fit a shoulder, channels too shallow to carry anything but a thin film of water, and one sloping shelf stained dark by centuries of the same small trickle.

The plants know these distinctions exactly.

Fern growth is thickest beneath the trickle, fronds overlapping until the stone disappears. Farther out, leathery leaves grow from pockets of humus no larger than cupped hands. Their surfaces are dark and waxy, their pale undersides turned toward whatever light the hollow offers. A vine threads through the fig roots, then crosses to a rock pillar on a strand so thin it can be mistaken for a crack in the air. It has leaves near the opening and almost none below; in the darkness it is chiefly a route for small animals.

At floor level, pale fungi open among the fallen leaves. Their caps are broad and shallow, each holding a few beads of condensed moisture. They collapse quickly in the heat. By the following evening, fresh caps have risen through the soft remains of the old ones.

There are structures, though none quite announces itself as a building. A low arc of stones encloses a patch of level ground near the largest opening. Behind it, three flat slabs have been set upright against the wall, leaving a recess just large enough for someone to crouch inside. The roof is a fourth slab, almost hidden beneath roots. No carvings remain, if there ever were any. A shallow depression beside the shelter catches water from a hanging root, and its lip is worn lower on one side.

The stones may have been placed to make a resting place. They may have been arranged around something that is gone. Now a little colony of translucent geckos occupies the recess, their bodies flattened against the warm rock, their toes spread over its faint irregularities. Shed skins cling in the corners like scraps of wet paper.

The hollow is full of animals, but remarkably quiet.

Small bats hang where the ceiling rises into darkness. They shift their feet, groom the membranes of their wings, and occasionally uncover a face to look down. Their movements make no more sound than a fingertip brushing cloth. Beetles pass beneath the leaves. Ants use the roots in two-way traffic, carrying seed husks, white larvae, and fragments of insects that still twitch. A long-legged spider holds itself above a damp seam, waiting where minute flies gather to drink.

There are frogs, too, though they seldom call. Thumb-sized, brown as the wet limestone, they wait inside the folds of fern stems. When an insect passes, a frog is briefly visible as a movement, then once again becomes part of the plant. Their eggs lie in tiny water pockets tucked behind mineral rims. Some of these pockets contain no more than a spoonful of water. The tadpoles turn in them slowly, brushing the bottom with their tails.

Larger creatures come and go through the sloping throat. A narrow-bodied lizard makes a round of the warm ledges every afternoon. A small nocturnal mammal leaves fruit seeds on the same stone near the entrance, carefully avoiding the damp part. Sometimes a snake lies so still along a root that ants climb over it without changing course. Its head is usually pointed toward the geckos’ shelter.

Everything is busy at a scale that asks you to stop moving before you can see it.

A fallen fruit softens. Its skin dimples beneath the feet of beetles. A root tip presses into a seam and, over seasons, lifts a flake of stone. The ferns unfold in the wetter corners while an exposed leaf near the entrance curls tight against the heat. Moisture travels under the leaf litter, leaving a darkened path too narrow to be called a stream. Wherever that path pauses, life gathers.

The quiet has a particular character. There is no wind at ground level, so leaves do not rustle. The birds above are muffled by earth and foliage. Even rain sounds distant: a low, scattered pattering that may stop long before the first drop reaches the hollow. Closest are the dry tick of an insect against stone, the faint scrape of claws, and, at long intervals, a drop striking the little basin beside the shelter.

That drop is surprisingly loud. For a moment, every surface seems to listen.

Certain parts of the hollow remain dry through all weather. One broad stone beneath the fig has a skin of fine dust that never quite washes away. Animals cross it in established lanes. You can find tiny footprints there layered over one another: paired dots, delicate forks, the interrupted line of a tail. In the hottest weeks, pale moths settle along its outer edge. They are not drawn to light. They face inward toward a crack from which a cooler breath occasionally rises.

The crack is too narrow to look into. It releases air for several minutes, then becomes still. Nothing visible enters or leaves it, but the moths wait, opening and closing their wings whenever the faint current returns. A little mineral crust has formed around the opening, unlike the smoother deposits elsewhere: brittle, branching, the color of old teeth.

At dusk, the hollow rearranges itself.

The bats begin to unfold. The geckos leave their slabs. Beetles that spent the day beneath bark climb into the open, and the fern stems acquire new occupants as frogs shift toward their feeding places. Light drains first from the floor, then from the pillars, leaving a pale patch high among the fig roots. For a few minutes the animals moving toward the surface pass through it one by one.

Below them, the heat persists. Leaves give off the faint smell of warmed wax; bruised fruit sweetens the air. The damp limestone smells only where you lean close to it. Water gathers along the roof of the stone shelter until a bead grows heavy enough to slide, following the same groove as the last one.

Long after the openings have gone dark, it reaches the basin. The ripple touches the worn lip, withdraws, and leaves a glistening crescent where a small frog has come down to drink.
