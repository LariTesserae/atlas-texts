The first thing you notice is the knocking beneath the ground.

It travels ahead of you through the paving: three blows, a pause, then a scattering of lighter taps, as though someone under the street has dropped a handful of tools. Farther away, other knocks answer. There are so many that you cannot tell whether they are signals or simply work. Sometimes the whole valley seems to be listening. Then a cart goes over a loose slab nearby, and the racket breaks apart into ordinary noises again.

The valley is shallow, broad, and entirely made.

Its slopes are stacks of earthen blocks, each block about the size of a chest, laid in courses that follow the long bend of the settlement. Some have been fired almost black; others are still the gray of wet clay. They bear thumbprints, scraps of cord, impressions of matting. Here and there a builder has pressed the heel of a hand into a face to flatten it, leaving a hollow large enough to hold rainwater. There are no exposed rocks between the blocks, no older hillside behind them. Where a course is unfinished, you can look into the slope and see more construction: wicker cages packed with rubble, hollow pottery braces, bundles of tiles standing on edge.

Nothing has been built *on* the land. These things are the land.

You can walk for much of a day along the valley’s central depression without reaching its end. It is not a riverbed, though narrow runs of water occupy it after rain. It was laid out as a long trough, with steps and receiving basins, but successive builders have crowded it with kilns, retaining walls, low houses, stairways, workshops, and heaps of material waiting for a use. The water now has to find its own route through this abundance. It slips under thresholds, drips down the inside of ventilation shafts, gathers in the blind bottoms of stairwells. Occasionally a basin fills and spills all at once, sending a cold sheet over a lane that was dry a moment before.

The air smells of soaked brick, lamp soot, and the faint sourness of rooms that have never quite dried.

There is very little light. Overhead, enormous overlapping plates close most of the sky. They are held up by short, thick piers built into the upper terraces, and their edges disappear into the dark. Through a few gaps you see a pale strip—not sunlight exactly, but enough brightness to distinguish a roofline from the space behind it. Rain arrives through these gaps with surprising force. It falls in narrow columns, pounds one patch of paving, then stops, leaving the surrounding lanes untouched.

Elsewhere, moisture collects more quietly. The underside of a plate sweats for hours before releasing a drop. People set bowls beneath reliable drips. Some bowls are old and deeply scored, their rims repaired with little clay staples. Others have been put out that morning. A good drip is guarded without much ceremony: a stool beside it, a sleeping mat, a hand resting on the bowl when someone passes too close.

Water is too scarce to make the place lush, but plentiful enough to keep it uncomfortable. Sleeves brush damp walls. Cold rises through thin soles. In sheltered chambers, condensation slicks the fired surfaces while the unfired ones drink it and darken. Those walls swell almost imperceptibly. Doors that opened yesterday must be shaved today.

The houses stand close enough that many have no outside walls of their own. A bedroom presses against a kiln chamber; a workshop ceiling supports a footpath; someone’s cooking alcove occupies the hollow base of a retaining pier. Where there should be an alley, there is a stack of empty water jars, with just enough room to turn sideways and pass. Where there should be a square, the settlement has left a rough open court full of drying blocks.

From the lower lanes you cannot see far, but climb one of the broad, shallow stairways and the place opens around you. Across the trough are tiers upon tiers of faintly visible buildings. Lamps move among them. At a distance, a man carrying a flame through a screened passage looks like a coal traveling inside the wall.

The lamps burn something dense and dirty. Their light scarcely reaches beyond the person tending them. Work is often done by touch. Block layers feel for the edge of a course, stretch a cord between wooden pegs, then lay the next piece against it. Potters know the thickness of a pipe by the sound it makes beneath a knuckle. Children learn routes by changes underfoot: seven flat slabs, a rounded sill, three steps down, the warm patch beside the communal oven.

There is no complicated machinery. Loads move on sledges, rollers, ropes, shoulders. Water is raised in buckets by people walking backward down an incline. The great overhead plates were lifted a finger’s width at a time, with wedges driven under their edges. Many wedges remain in place. When one loosens, everyone nearby hears it before anything falls.

That is one source of the noise. Another is the constant correction of the landscape.

The valley is still young. Much of it has not settled into its own weight. A wall bows; workers dismantle its upper courses and spread them farther down the slope. A platform tilts; its occupants shift their beds to the high end while clay is packed beneath the low one. At night, somewhere beyond sight, a roof gives a long grinding groan. Voices rise, tools are fetched, and the hammering begins.

Wear has only just started to mark the surfaces. The steps are not yet hollowed by feet. Water has cut no deep channels. Instead, you see the first faint suggestions of them: pale trails below spills, softened corners, a little apron of clay washed from a fresh wall. In one lane, a persistent leak has made a groove no wider than a finger. People step over it carefully. They have begun calling it the stream.

The material for all this work comes up from below.

Every few courts there is a round shaft with a low parapet. Baskets descend empty and return full of cool gray paste. The haul is slow. Two people turn a wooden drum while a third keeps the rope from rubbing against the lip. Down inside, out of reach of the lamps, you hear scooping and the scrape of basket rims.

Nobody has found a natural bottom. The shaft walls are tiled, even below the oldest inhabited levels. When those tiles are removed, behind them are packed vessels, and behind the vessels another wall.

The deepest crews sometimes bring up pieces that do not belong to anything now being made: a curved lintel with sockets for nine doors; a floor tile embossed on both sides; a little stairway whose steps are too narrow for a human foot. These are broken up for aggregate unless somebody wants them. There is no room to keep every peculiar thing the earth provides.

Very few animals live here. Once in a while a pale, low-bodied creature emerges from a drain to lick moisture from the paving. It has soft feet and a hard ridge along its back, the same dull gray as an unfired pot. It stays motionless while carts and people pass, then retreats with a quick, awkward shuffle. The drain workers leave broken dishes in certain corners for it, though whether these contain food or simply water is difficult to see.

More rarely, something large moves inside a wall.

You hear it rather than see it: a dragging, followed by the delicate clatter of loose tiles. Conversations stop until it has passed. Afterward, someone usually checks the nearest supports. There are rules about this. Do not strike the wall while the dragging continues. Do not open a cavity that is breathing warm air. If a new passage is discovered, put a lamp at its entrance and leave it unoccupied for three nights.

The rules are practical and specific. The people who follow them are too busy to explain them at length.

Near the unfinished end of the valley, the lanes become broad again. Buildings stand only waist-high. Fresh floors lie uncovered, their surfaces still soft enough to take an impression. Here the air is colder, and the hammer blows come distinctly, without the confusing echoes of inhabited streets.

Workers kneel along the edge of a new terrace. They pass blocks from hand to hand into darkness. A boy presses a bowl into the floor beneath a newly forming drip. Someone tests a doorway by crawling through it. Beyond them are cords stretched between pegs, showing where tomorrow’s walls will stand.

You wait while a lamp is moved forward.

For a moment it reveals the unbuilt ground: not bare soil, but a close mass of smaller made things—shards, beads, fragments of handles, chipped wedges, all packed together and moist. The workers spread clay over them. They smooth it with boards, leaving a level surface that disappears beyond the lamp’s reach.

Behind you, the occupied valley roars with its evening work. Ahead, a man taps the first block of the next course into place. He feels along its top, shifts it slightly, and taps again.
