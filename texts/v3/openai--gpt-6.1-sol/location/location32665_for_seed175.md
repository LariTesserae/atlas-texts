The hollow lies high on a broad shoulder of the mountain, where the slope briefly forgets to descend. It is small enough that, from its upper lip, you can see nearly all of it: an oval of pale stone, a few dark stands of vegetation, and water occupying the low places between them. Beyond the rim, other slopes fall away into warm haze. Within it, the distances are modest. Nothing is hidden very far from anything else.

The stone has weathered past sharpness. Its shelves are rounded, its cracks widened into shallow seams. Even the steepest face has the softened look of something that has spent a long time under running water. There are no fresh fractures, no loose heaps at the foot of the walls. What could fall has fallen; what remains has settled into a shape it can keep.

In places, the rock is almost white. Elsewhere, moisture brings out a subdued violet, most visible beneath the edges of ledges. A hand laid against it would find it warm, warmer than the air in the shaded parts of the hollow. Heat rises slowly through the mountain. There is no steaming vent or exposed fire, only this steady warmth beneath the wet surface, sufficient to keep the place green while the ridges above it remain bare.

Water is everywhere without forming a single large body.

It lies in long, shallow basins worn across the floor. It darkens the undersides of stones. It gathers beneath leaves and fills the grooves around roots. Along the western wall, it emerges through pores too small to see, making a sheen that thickens until a drop finally travels downward. Several drops may join before reaching the foot of the wall. Most never do. They stop in little depressions and stay there, reflecting a scrap of sky.

The air is damp but clear. Sunlight enters freely through the open top of the hollow and falls across the pale floor. In the afternoon it reaches beneath the overhanging shelves, illuminating green stems from below with light reflected off the water. Only near dawn does condensation briefly soften the view. It gathers close to the ground, around the warm pools, and is gone by the time the sun clears the rim.

There is generous space between the growing things.

Three clusters of broad-leaved plants occupy the deeper pockets of soil. Their leaves are thick, glossy, and loosely arranged, each stem rising far enough from its neighbors to have room. Some leaves are as wide as the pools beneath them. Their edges curl upward, holding rain for days. When one has collected more than it can support, it lowers by an almost imperceptible degree and empties into the leaf below.

Elsewhere, vegetation stays close to the rock. Moss fills a narrow crack without spreading onto either side. Small ferns follow one wet seam halfway around the basin and stop where the seam becomes dry. A low plant with translucent stems grows in isolated cushions, each surrounded by a broad margin of bare stone. The cushions are luxuriant within their boundaries, crowded with new shoots, but those boundaries hardly change.

The hollow could hold much more greenery. It does not.

Roots encounter something in the stone that turns them back. Looking through the clear water, you can see them tracing the same curves as the basin walls, pale cords pressed against the bottom. Where two plants’ roots approach each other, they bend aside before touching. Between the green islands, the water remains open and the rock remains clean.

There are no animals. Nothing drinks from the pools or takes shelter under the leaves. No wings pass overhead. The soft sediment preserves no tracks, and the damp hollows beneath fallen fronds contain only smaller fronds, newly opening.

Decay proceeds slowly. A detached leaf may lie green on the stone for a season, its severed stem still drawing moisture through contact with the surface. Eventually its skin becomes transparent. The veins remain for a little longer, then flatten into a dark stain that the water gradually removes. Soil collects almost entirely beneath living plants. Very little escapes into the open spaces.

The quiet allows small sounds to carry. A drop landing in a pool is audible from the opposite edge of the hollow. Occasionally a leaf releases its stored water in a brief, uneven trickle. Under the largest shelf, a bead of moisture grows on the ceiling and falls into a narrow stone cup. The interval is too long to become a rhythm. After each drop, the place seems silent again.

Near the center is a channel unlike the others. It begins at a pool, runs gently uphill across exposed rock, and ends in a higher basin beside a stand of ferns.

Its water moves uphill.

The movement is slow enough to escape a casual glance. A floating fragment of leaf reveals it, taking most of a day to travel the length of an outstretched arm. There is no surge, no hidden spout. The water adheres to the shallow channel and advances in a thin, continuous skin. At the upper basin it gathers, deepens, and eventually spills over the opposite edge, descending by the ordinary route.

The stone beneath this channel is worn as smoothly as the rest. Whatever permits the ascent has been happening for a very long time.

Other peculiarities become apparent only with patience. The darkest pool casts a wavering reflection onto a wall the sun never reaches. A fern’s new frond opens toward that reflection rather than toward the sky. On the eastern rim, rainwater gathers in a depression that looks nearly level, yet always drains inward, even when wind drives the rain the other way.

Nothing here is built. There are no steps in the slope, no cut branches, no stones placed to make crossing easier. At one margin, flat flakes have separated naturally from the rock. They have edges fine enough to cut a stem, but they lie where they fell, some half covered by moss. The shallow cups, the warm shelves, the ready water remain unused.

From outside, the hollow would be easy to miss. Its rim is low, and its plants do not rise above the surrounding slope. Only a faint brightness of wet stone distinguishes it from the mountain.

Inside, a day has room to pass almost unnoticed. The sun moves from one wall to the other. Leaves gain a little weight. Water climbs its worn channel. Late in the afternoon, the exposed rock dries in patches, while the cracks remain dark and the shaded pools hold their warmth.

By evening, moisture begins gathering again on the western wall. The first drop takes a long time to form.
