The first thing you notice is that the waterfalls do not fall.

They hang beneath the high terraces in thick, white, pleated sheets, reaching all the way to the next shelf of rock. Their surfaces have the roughness of violently moving water: beads, spray, twisting ropes, little hollows where one current ought to be overtaking another. None of these details changes. A drop suspended at the edge of a fern remains there, slightly elongated, full of the small green reflection of its leaf.

The noise is tremendous.

It comes from the waterfalls, but also from the streets, the roots, the open mouths of drains. It has the deep, crowded force of water passing through a hundred stone channels at once. Nothing pulses or recedes. There is no individual splash to listen for. After a while you stop trying to distinguish the sources, though the sound remains strong enough to feel against your teeth.

The city occupies the upper part of a mountain so high that other mountains appear below it as pale irregularities in the haze. It extends across several ridges, filling saddles, climbing buttresses, descending into valleys whose bottoms cannot be seen. Across those valleys stand other districts, green and white and dark red, apparently close until you make out an entire grove occupying what looked like a tuft of moss on one of their ledges.

There is no snow.

The stone is hot underfoot. The air has the dense warmth of a closed greenhouse, and water stands everywhere: in shallow gutters, in the hollows of broad leaves, along seams in the cliffs. Pale, fleshy plants fill the gaps between buildings. Their leaves overlap in layers, lower ones almost black, upper ones translucent at the edges. Roots emerge from masonry and enter it again. Here and there a root crosses a passage at waist height, polished along its upper surface, as though generations of people have climbed over it.

There are no people.

There are no insects either, no birds above the ridges, no small creatures in the pools. The vegetation has all the abundance of a place that ought to be swarming with life. Flowers crowd the wet walls. Huge fruits rest in the forks of trees, their skins swollen and deeply creased. Nothing eats them. Nothing has fallen.

The buildings were not assembled.

Up close, what seemed to be joints between blocks turn out to be growth lines. Each wall rises as one continuous body from the mountain, veined with the same rust-red mineral as the exposed cliffs. Doorways have formed through it. Staircases project from it. Some houses have grown around trees, leaving precise openings for trunks and branches; others have trunks embedded in their walls, and broad crowns spreading out through roof chambers.

There is no clear boundary between plant and stone. A buttress becomes woody near its top. A branch thickens into a lintel. Inside one empty room, the wall bears a line of small, closed buds beneath a ledge that might be a shelf.

Yet the place has the arrangement of a city rather than the accidental resemblance of one. Broad avenues connect terraces. Narrow passages lead away from them to courts and smaller dwellings. There are communal basins, retaining walls, covered ways, flights of steps with generous landings. On the steepest slopes, whole neighborhoods hang from the cliff on enormous rootlike ribs, with streets passing through the ribs’ hollow interiors.

All of it has the worn, settled appearance of something long inhabited. Stair treads are shallow in their centers. Corners are rounded. Thresholds have smooth depressions where feet would have paused.

At first this seems to imply a vanished population. Then you notice that a newly exposed-looking surface has the same wear as an old one. A split in the rock reveals another stair inside the mountain, each buried step already hollowed by use. The city appears to have grown with its wear included.

Light reaches it plainly. The sky is a warm, pale blue, the cloud banks bright but not luminous. Shadows gather beneath leaves and in the covered streets. Across the valley, distance softens the colors in an ordinary way.

Only after crossing several courts do you realize that the shadows remain at exactly the same angle everywhere, including inside rooms with windows facing opposite directions. A narrow strip of sunlight lies across a floor far within the mountain. There is no opening above it. Dust rests in the strip without floating.

The pools are stranger than the light.

Their surfaces are level, even where their beds tilt steeply downhill. But “level” belongs to each pool separately. A basin beside one staircase holds water parallel to the steps. Another, in a wall, presents its still surface vertically, like a dark window. Ferns lean toward these waters, and each fern obeys the gravity of the basin nearest it. In a single courtyard, plants grow upward, sideways, and downward from the same old tree.

You can walk around the vertical pool. Its surface remains a surface. Beyond it are submerged steps and a cluster of pale leaves, all held in the green depth. Along its lower edge, water spills into a gutter on the floor. The spill is fixed in a clear, curved tongue.

Touching it would be like touching water, if water could retain an edge.

The larger channels carry this wetness through the districts. Some run beneath the avenues, announcing themselves through deep slots in the pavement. Others cross overhead in stone troughs furred with moss. Where a channel breaks, its contents extend across the gap without support, a narrow bridge of amber water. At a distant terrace, an entire flooded square stands on its side. Its surrounding buildings turn with it. The square’s trees rise horizontally into the open valley, their canopies interleaved with those of an upright grove below.

The mountain gives way to these arrangements without visible strain. Its cliffs have been rounded by an erosion that seems finished: broad gullies, polished ribs, caves with smooth lips. Nothing looks freshly broken. Even the tremendous overhangs have the reassuring thickness of old tree limbs.

Far above the main city is another ridge, crowded with low buildings and flat-topped vegetation. Above that ridge stands a further cliff. You had taken it for part of the sky until you saw streets along its face.

Looking downward is no easier. The valley opens into terraces, and the terraces into darker terraces, each furnished with its own pools, groves, and dense ranks of dwellings. The haze hides them gradually. There is no visible bottom, but neither does the place feel suspended over an abyss. It feels supported throughout, as if depth has become another kind of foundation.

In a small court away from the broad avenues, a tree shades six empty doorways. Its bark is warm and wet. A stone basin stands among its roots, full to the brim, with a low bulge of water held above the rim. On the wall behind it, a leaf has left a perfect green impression, though the leaf itself is still pressed against the stone.

Here the roar is as loud as it was beside the waterfalls. The enclosed court should soften it; instead, it seems to originate in every surface. Listening closely, you can hear the rough grain of the wall, the hollowness beneath the roots, the weight of water in the basin. Each has a sound, continuous and enormous.

There is no breeze to stir the tree. No drop falls from its leaves. The heat does not deepen. Somewhere beyond the doorway, the vast wet city continues across the ridges, every passage open, every stair worn, every waterfall holding its full force in place.

You stand in the shade for what feels like a long time. Nothing indicates how long.
