The plain is so old that its stones have lost the habit of standing above one another.

From a distance it looks level: a low, dark country under a low, dark sky, water showing wherever the ground has been pressed a little deeper. But close to the surface there are folds, lips, sockets, shallow channels polished almost to a glaze. Nothing has a sharp edge. Even fractures arrive rounded, as though the rock has been worn down in advance of breaking.

Water lies within a finger’s breadth of everything. A footprint fills before the foot is lifted. The undersides of stones are wet; the upper sides sweat. Along the shallow banks, beads gather on bare mineral crust and run uphill to join larger beads. Most of these reverse themselves when watched. Some do not.

There are no trees, no grasses, no reeds marking the boundary between land and pool. The marsh has only water and things that are not quite water. Pale slurries collect in its hollows. Black films flex across its channels. On the higher ground, which is higher by no more than the height of a knee, the exposed rock is crowded with animals.

At first the animals resemble rubble.

They lie packed together in shoals of slate-colored bodies, rounded backs nested against rounded backs, their joints hidden underneath. Some are as small as a thumbnail; others are broad enough to cross a channel without getting their upper surfaces wet. Their shells have the dull finish of old cast iron, though nothing here has been cast. When they move, each leaves a dark, briefly steaming patch on the stone.

They move often.

A ripple passes through a colony and hundreds of backs rise at once. Legs unfold with a clatter. Narrow gaps open between the bodies, showing more bodies below, and below those, clear water trembling over pale sand. The colony is three or four animals deep. In places it is twenty deep. What looked like a bank settles, rearranges itself, and becomes a channel.

The cold is severe, but the water rarely freezes all the way through. Its surface forms a skin that breaks under rain and repairs behind it. Ice grows thickest on moving things: articulated legs, the fringes of swimming mouths, the tips of whiskers that drag across the mud. These fringes chime as the creatures feed. In the dimness, the animals appear to be wearing the only bright objects in the country.

The light comes from the sky and from below, neither source sufficient.

Above, cloud is stretched into long, low bands. Occasionally a paler strip travels through it without revealing a sun. Below, certain seams in the rock glow the dirty orange of an element being brought to temperature. The glow does not warm the air. It illuminates the undersides of pools and gives passing bellies a momentary translucence.

There is machinery here, but no building contains it.

Across the plain, natural depressions draw water down through beds of perforated stone. The water returns elsewhere under pressure. Broad mineral plates rise and fall in their sockets. Long, fibrous veins wind into cables beneath the surface, carrying current between buried masses of iron. Every few minutes a patch of ground shudders, and something vast takes a stroke.

These mechanisms are geological. No one excavated their chambers. No one fitted a shaft to a bearing. Growth, erosion, compression, and an unreasonable amount of time have brought them into alignment. A seam precipitates metal until it is conductive; a channel wears smooth until it seals against the plate above it; pressure finds a rhythm. Throughout the region, the same few processes repeat at enormous scale.

The animals live on their consequences.

Small flat creatures graze the thin electrical discharge where wet stone meets ice. They gather in such numbers that their bodies form a trembling gray felt. Larger animals scrape this felt loose with mouthparts that turn continuously, winding the grazers into their throats. Those larger animals are hunted by long, jointed swimmers that travel beneath the shallow water, visible as ridges moving across the silt.

When one catches prey, the whole channel stops flowing.

For several seconds the water becomes rigid around the struggle. Rain strikes it and remains where it lands. The swimmer folds its victim inward. Then the water loosens, the suspended raindrops fall the remaining inch, and the current carries away little flakes of shell.

Farther out, where the plain opens into a succession of drowned basins, larger creatures stand in the water. Their legs disappear into the bottom. Their bodies are low and elongated, with ranks of vents along their flanks. They take in slurry at one end and expel clean water from the other. They turn slowly, feeding across the basin in parallel paths, leaving bands of pale sediment behind them.

A dozen may be visible at once. Behind them, in the mist, are the darker outlines of several dozen more.

Their passage makes a deep, regular knocking. Each knock travels through the ground before it reaches the ear. On the wet banks, smaller animals brace themselves in anticipation. Some time their leaps to the vibration. Others open their shells only between strokes.

The country is loud enough that an isolated sound has little meaning. Water hammers in buried passages. Ice snaps against moving joints. Colonies grind over rock. From the horizon comes a continuous mechanical roar, occasionally thinning enough to reveal a slower noise underneath: a long scrape, a pause, another scrape.

Silence occurs in sharply bounded patches.

You can step into one without warning. The rain continues to strike your coat, but you hear nothing. Creatures work their mouths beside your feet in perfect quiet. A few paces away, the roar is still visible in the vibration of the pools.

These silent patches migrate against the wind. Animals avoid their leading edges but crowd into their wake, where the stone remains warmer for a little while. Sometimes a patch passes over a feeding basin and the great filter-creatures stop, all facing different directions. They resume as soon as sound returns.

The oldest parts of the plain are difficult to reach.

They are not remote in any ordinary sense. You can see them: broad, whitish flats between the dark basins, almost bare, holding a faint sheen of water. Yet walking toward them repeatedly brings you to the same drainage channel. Its banks are recognizable by a split black stone and a cluster of animals with blue ice on their backs.

Approach from another direction and the stone is on the other bank.

The flats have undergone so much erosion that distance itself has been worn shallow around them. A long journey loses most of its length before it can arrive. Water crosses more readily. Animals cross only when carried. Now and then a current delivers a creature from the white flats whose shell is smooth beyond any ordinary smoothness, without seam or mouth or visible joint. It lies among the other animals until they make room, then sinks through the rock.

Near these flats, rain sometimes falls twice. A drop strikes, splashes, gathers itself from the splash, and strikes again. Pools carry reflections of banks that have already washed away. Beneath one clear stretch of water you may see a steep cliff, towering downward, with ledges full of pale animals. There is no cliff above it, and a stone dropped into the pool reaches the shallow bottom at once.

Still, most of the plain follows dependable routines. Pressure rises through the morning dimness. The basins drain. The exposed colonies shift toward the wet ground, and predators wait in the narrowing channels. Later the buried plates reverse their stroke, water wells up through thousands of small openings, and the colonies spread again.

At the height of the flood there is scarcely any unoccupied surface. Bodies meet bodies. Water fills the joints between stones. Current fills the water. Underneath, the mineral machinery works through its long cycle, bearing the weight of a country that has been polished nearly flat.

Then, across several miles, the ground lifts by a fraction of an inch.

Every pool leans in the same direction. The large animals halt with their vents open. For a moment the roar drops to a heavy ticking.

The water runs. The small creatures unfold their legs. The plain settles, and feeding begins again.
