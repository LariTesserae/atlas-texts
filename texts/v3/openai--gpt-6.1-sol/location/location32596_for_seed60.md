The hollow is scarcely wider than a village square. From its upper lip you can see the whole of it, though you cannot see any ground: only the backs of leaves, the shoulders of root crowns, and three low roofs held at different angles in the green. A narrow channel enters beneath a fringe of reeds and leaves beneath another. Between them, the water is mostly hidden. Its presence shows in the trembling of a leaf where something drips underneath, in the dark sheen of a stem, in the soft collapse of a patch that looked firm.

The descent is warm. Moisture gathers on your wrists and along the seams of your clothes. The air smells of cut stalks, hot rubber, and the sweet brown liquid at the bottom of a vase.

What seems from above to be a single dense thicket separates, at close range, into many kinds of surface. There are broad leaves thick enough to stand on, worn pale in their centers by repeated passage. There are mats of fine roots with the springiness of rope. There are trunks lying horizontally, still alive, their upper sides sprouting fernlike fronds while their lower sides drink from the channel. A mound beside the path consists entirely of old leaf bases. The outer ones have dried into overlapping brown plates; the inner ones are green and warm, and give slightly under a hand.

Nothing here grows out of a visible soil. Plants grow into plants. A seed lodges in the split joint of a larger stem, sends roots through it, and eventually replaces the joint with its own swollen body. The larger plant continues growing, supported by the newcomer. After enough generations, it becomes difficult to say which thing is holding up which.

The path is maintained by cutting rather than paving. Its edges bear the flat, wet scars of yesterday’s work. In places the cuts have already furred over with small white root tips. An iron railing follows the descent, disappearing every few feet into a wooden bulge and emerging farther on with a different tilt. The rail is warm to the touch. Where its paint survives, it is yellow.

Halfway down, the sound becomes distinct.

There is a heavy turning noise, steady but not quite rhythmic: a low churn, a knock, a pause, then another churn. Above it comes the slap of water against broad surfaces. Leaves rub together with a sound like someone slowly sorting an immense stack of stiff paper. Every so often there is a metallic squeal, startlingly loud within the hollow, and all the hanging droplets shake loose at once.

The machinery stands in the middle.

It occupies a brick shed whose walls reach only to waist height. Above them, enormous leaves have been trained across a frame of pipe. Their edges overlap like roof tiles. Rain runs down the midribs and falls into an open gutter, which is itself a living stalk bent into a trough. The stalk has thickened around its brackets; the brackets are still visible as small rusted mouths.

Inside the shed, a flywheel turns through less than a full revolution and settles back. Its shaft disappears into a knotted mass of pale wood. Two belts rise from it to wheels mounted higher in the vegetation. One belt is rubber, patched and polished. The other is a flattened vine, dark with grease, running continuously through a pair of narrow rollers.

The engine draws its motion from the growth around it.

Along the watercourse, rows of swollen stems are clamped inside iron sleeves. As they slowly fill, they press against pistons. Valves release water from one row into the next; the empty stems contract while the full ones take up the load. The movement is small, almost imperceptible at any single clamp. Collected through rods and levers, it is enough to turn the wheel.

The apparatus pumps water to the upper rim, where younger growth would otherwise dry out during the warm afternoons. A thumb-wide pipe delivers a continuous trickle. Beside it, a pressure gauge has a cracked face and a needle that advances so slowly you notice the change only when you return to it.

The plants have grown accustomed to the machinery. Their thickest tissues form against its working surfaces. There is less bark near the piston heads, more fibrous reinforcement beneath the clamps. When a sleeve is loosened for repairs, the stem inside retains the shape of its threads.

Beyond the shed is a small clearing, though it has no bare floor. The surface is a single, broad, densely rooted leaf, perhaps six paces across. Several smaller leaves feed into it along the edges. Its veins rise like shallow ridges underfoot, dividing it into green panels. Water collects in one panel and remains there as a clear, trembling puddle.

A table stands near the center. Its iron legs have sunk into the leaf and been sealed around. On the tabletop lie pruning shears, a tin oilcan, and a ledger in a box with a hinged glass lid. The ledger’s lower corners are swollen. A few lines can be read through the condensation: dates, water levels, the number of strokes completed by the pump. Much of one page is taken up by a careful drawing of a stem that grew around the wrong side of a bearing.

There are other structures, all modest. A narrow locker with a door that can open only a hand’s width. A concrete plinth carrying an electrical switch beneath a hood of leaves. A short bridge whose steel deck has become the interior of a root. You cross it inside a low green passage, hearing your steps ring faintly through the living walls.

The place does not swallow everything at the same rate. Moving parts remain exposed. A rotating shaft keeps a smooth circular opening around itself, while the stationary bolts nearby vanish into thick collars. Warm pipes acquire thin, translucent sheaths. Cold pipes become shaggy with roots. Handles stay clear if they are used often enough. The locker door, apparently neglected, is almost lost.

There is very little animal life. Once, a small snail appears on the underside of a leaf, its shell as pale as a fingernail. It remains there all afternoon. No birds enter the hollow. A single insect crosses the clearing, touches the puddle, and departs without settling.

Most of the small noises belong to the plants: the click of a drying seed case, the trickle from a newly opened seam, the dull report of a stem releasing its grip on a rusted post. In the close air, these sounds carry clearly. Their sources are often hidden inches away.

Toward evening, sunlight reaches farther beneath the roof leaves. The hollow brightens from within. Pale stems shine; water on the ironwork catches in tiny points. Through a gap behind the shed, you can see older layers of the place pressed together: a railing, a shelf of woody roots, a crushed wooden sluice gate, another shelf of roots. All are still carrying weight. Even the splintered gate helps hold open a passage for water.

The pump continues its uneven turn. Somewhere under the clearing, a valve opens with a prolonged groan, and the puddle sinks a little. The floor beneath your feet grows firmer.

At the upper rim, the delivered water spreads through a bed of tightly folded new leaves. Over the course of an hour, one of them eases open. It is a small change, easily missed. By the following morning it will have covered another length of the yellow rail.
