Beneath the lowest root of a drowned tree, there is a hollow small enough to cover with one hand.

It is not an air pocket. Water fills it completely, pressing into the grain of the wood, into the seams of the stone, into the soft folds of everything growing there. Even the roof is wet on both sides. Above it lie silt, tangled roots, and the floor of a cold lake. Below it, only a finger’s depth away, is another roof.

The hollow has been forming for a long time, but its surfaces are new. A pale stone at its center splits a little farther each winter. The split admits water; water admits a root hair; the root hair thickens and forces the stone apart. Fresh edges remain sharp beneath their coatings of slime. Nothing here is worn smooth enough to be called a pebble. The smallest ridges can catch a filament and hold it through several seasons.

There is no daylight. Once, when a storm moved a great quantity of mud overhead, a thread of gray entered along a root and stopped at the first leaf. That leaf still has a gray underside. The others turn toward it very slowly.

Most of the vegetation is dark enough to seem part of the water until touched. Narrow ribbons descend from the ceiling, pass between the fractured stones, and disappear into their own folds. Their surfaces are thick and slippery; along each margin grows a fringe of finer growth, and on each strand of that fringe a still finer one. There is no bare stem. Everything that can carry life carries it.

Some leaves are broad, though there is nowhere for them to spread. They grow folded into packets, cool and firm, with their undersides facing inward. Inside each packet is a little water of its own. When a leaf opens by a fraction, this water escapes and something else takes its place: a mineral taste, the pressure of a remote season, sometimes a faint scent of rain on bark.

The growth feeds chiefly where living tissue touches freshly broken stone. A new fracture draws the roots toward it. They become pale at their tips, divide, and lay themselves against the exposed faces. Elsewhere they remain nearly motionless, spending what the fracture gives them.

The hollow is crowded beyond the point of stillness. There is no space in which anything can drift. A fallen flake of wood does not descend; it rests against a leaf, which rests against a root, which rests against a skin of translucent growth stretched between two splinters. Water moves through this arrangement without making the arrangement visibly move. Pressure passes from surface to surface. A loosening at one end may arrive at the other after a month.

The cold is intimate. It lives inside the wood as much as in the water. Roots are slow to bend, and a torn edge keeps its shape for years. Decay proceeds in thin layers. An exposed splinter may be hard along one side and soft along the other, with a dark seam between them that widens by less than the thickness of a hair.

There are signs that something once arranged this place.

Near the central stone, three rootlets have been bent into an arch. Each was held down beneath a chip of white shell before it thickened. The shell chips are now embedded in the roots. Under the arch lies a level patch of dark grit, edged with short pieces of stem, all cut or broken to almost the same length.

Farther back stands a little enclosure made from overlapping leaf ribs. It has a doorway too narrow for the only animal presently living here. Growth has sealed most of its joints, but the doorway stays clear. Water entering it comes out colder.

No one has seen what made these things. They might be the remains of a nest. They might have grown that way. The roots bend naturally around obstacles, but there is no obstacle beneath the arch.

The animal is a pale, jointed creature no longer than a grain of rice. It spends most of its life wedged behind the white shell chips. Its limbs grip the same two points for so long that growth forms shallow collars around them. Occasionally it releases one foot and places it slightly farther along the root.

Its eyes are present, small and black, but it keeps them pressed against the wood.

When it moves, the leaves nearest it tighten. This happens before its limbs begin to shift. The tightening travels outward, becomes too faint to see, and eventually reaches a place the creature has not yet visited. There, a folded leaf opens enough to expose its tender seam.

The creature may take months to arrive.

Distances are uncertain in the back of the hollow. A root entering a crack can sometimes be felt beneath the opposite wall. Two stones that touch along one edge are separated, along another, by a chamber of cold water too deep to sound. Nothing large can enter that chamber. A filament can, and several have. They extend into it in close parallel lines, retaining their green-black color long after they should have run out of food.

The filament tips occasionally return through the ceiling.

They do not appear to have turned. Their growing ends point upward, while the stems beside them still descend. Between the two lies a length no one could fit inside the hollow. Other roots make room for their return, compressing against the walls until the wood creaks.

That is the commonest sound here: a small, muffled creak, followed by a long silence. Sometimes a grain of stone detaches with a faint tick. Sometimes the animal’s feet rasp against shell. Sounds lose their edges quickly in the packed growth. Only the low pressure of the lake persists, felt everywhere without seeming to come from anywhere.

At irregular intervals, the hollow receives a tide.

The lake above may be perfectly still when this happens. Water enters through the fresh split in the stone and bears down upon the folded leaves. The arch flexes. Grit rises from its level patch but remains gathered beneath it. For a little while the vegetation becomes less dense, each strand lifting just enough to let water pass.

Then the pressure diminishes. Leaves settle into changed positions. A shell chip is turned. A fine root has crossed a gap it could not cross before.

After one such tide, a second pale creature was found behind the arch. It had no room to unfold its limbs. For several winters it remained there, alive or nearly alive, while a leaf grew between it and the first creature. Eventually only its back could be seen. Now the back too is covered.

The hollow continues to widen, though scarcely any more space becomes available. Each new sliver is occupied as soon as the stone yields. Roots enter, leaves fold themselves into it, and the cold water holds them close. Deep in the newest crack, where the broken faces still fit almost perfectly together, a pale tip has begun to branch.
