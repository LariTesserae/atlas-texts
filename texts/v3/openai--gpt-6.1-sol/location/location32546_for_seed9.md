The whole place is smaller than a village square, but it stands above the clouds.

On clear mornings you can see the tops of weather systems passing beneath its southern rail: broad white backs, dark seams of rain, occasional openings through which the distant lowlands appear flat and faintly blue. Up here the sunlight is ordinary sunlight. It warms the handrail, catches on wet leaves, makes you squint. Nothing about the light explains why the ground is warm, or why, at this height, there are breadfruit leaves as wide as serving trays.

The heat comes from below.

Under the garden are six floors of machinery, though you would not know there were six unless you descended the maintenance stair. From the path you hear only a low vibration, most noticeable when you rest a palm against one of the upright pipes. Boilers, condensers, pumps. Their noise has been baffled into chambers lined with cork and felt, and their waste heat rises through the bed in narrow channels. The garden is the roof of something still working.

Its boundaries are close. A glazed wall on the north side, a service house on the east, the southern rail, and to the west a nearly vertical bank of pale, wet stone. Between them, the ground folds into three little terraces. You could walk across the place in two minutes if the paths were straight. They are not. They bend around pools, duck beneath leaves, divide at sumps, then rejoin on opposite sides of a swollen root. From most positions you cannot see more than a third of the enclosure.

Even the stone bank is manufactured.

Behind its surface, perforated pipes feed a thick suspension of mineral powder into knitted wire. The suspension sets slowly, first as a rubbery paste and then as something harder. New sections have the color of milk and the rounded contours of dough pressed against a screen. Older sections are ivory, then faintly gold where iron has reached them through the water. A few corners still show the weave beneath.

There has been almost no time for erosion. The ledges have not broken down into rubble; the gullies have not cut themselves. Every hollow has been held open by a former, every channel guided by a strip of copper or a temporary wax lining. The garden has ridges, but they are young ridges. Their edges are soft because they have not finished setting.

Near the western path, a clear trickle crosses one such edge and leaves it slightly larger.

You have to watch for a while to notice this. A drop gathers, hangs, falls. Behind it remains a grain of pale material. Another drop follows. Along the underside of the ledge, delicate teeth are forming, spaced according to some unevenness in the delivery pipe. Farther down, the same water darkens with root seepage and passes over a strip of black mesh. There it leaves a thin, glossy skin.

The water is doing several kinds of work at once.

It arrives as vapor from the machinery, as pumped meltwater from a cistern elsewhere on the tower, and as rain when a cloud rises high enough to cross the rail. In warm weather the glazed northern wall runs with condensation. Gutters no wider than a finger carry this down into planting beds. Some are exposed, bright metal threads under the foliage; others have disappeared beneath moss.

The paths are always damp. They are made from interlocking plates of porous ceramic, their upper faces rough enough for bare feet, their undersides ridged to collect water. Press on one and it gives a little. Lift your foot and a bead rises through a hole beside your heel. The next person may find it gone, drawn back down by the pump cycle.

In the lowest terrace, water lies almost level with the path. There are square beds of reeds here, planted so densely that the shape of each bed survives only as a difference in height. Between them grow small trees with glossy trunks and crowded crowns. Their roots emerge through purpose-made slots in the floor, turn along shallow channels, and enter other beds. Some have been clipped and grafted to keep them within reach of the gardeners. Others have found gaps intended for cables.

The foliage is lush without being grand. There is very little room for a full-grown tree. Instead there are climbing stems, layered ferns, broad leaves trained almost horizontally, and vines folded back along their own supports. A flowering shrub has been espaliered against a warm exhaust casing. Its pink flowers open close to the metal, in air that smells faintly of hot oil and crushed green stems.

Plants grow quickly here. The night crew cuts basketfuls from the paths; by afternoon, fresh shoots are already leaning into the cleared space. Fallen leaves are fed through a small pulping machine in the service house. Their slurry returns through the soil lines, mixed with minerals and something sour-smelling from the fermentation vats.

There is no buried earth. Beneath the moss and root felt are manufactured granules, ceramic ribs, membranes, and pipes. Even the trees were raised for this roof. They arrived small enough to carry up the maintenance stair, with their roots packed in numbered sleeves.

Still, the arrangements are beginning to slip.

A fern has established itself inside a pressure-gauge housing. Its fronds tremble whenever the needle moves. Roots have lifted one corner of a drain cover, and sediment is collecting there despite the weekly flush. A creeper has crossed the northern glazing and found the hinge of a ventilation panel. When the panel opens, it raises a whole curtain of leaves.

The technicians could remove these things. Sometimes they do. Sometimes they move a pipe instead.

There are few animals. A handful of tiny flies gathers over the fermenting bed near the service house. In the warmest pool lives one dark frog, introduced to eat them. You hear it less often than you see the little spreading rings where it has entered the water. No birds nest here. Once in a while a swift passes above the enclosure, so fast that its shadow seems like a loose leaf blown across the path.

Most of the sound comes from small adjustments: water ticking into a metal gutter, a valve closing softly under the floor, leaves brushing the glazing. At irregular intervals the western bank gives a faint crack. A mineral seam has set, or a support has shifted under the increasing weight. Afterward, a caretaker comes out of the service house, touches the new stone, and makes a mark beside it with a grease pencil.

The marks are everywhere once you begin looking. Dates on ledges. Short lines showing the expected height of a pool. A circled patch of moss. Numbers on leaves that are being measured rather than harvested.

At noon, the roof becomes almost close and sultry. Moisture hangs under the broad foliage while the open path dries in bright strips. Above the southern rail, the air remains sharp and clear. You can step from the warm shade of a banana plant to that rail in four paces and look down at a cloud moving slowly past the tower.

Behind you, a pump changes speed.

Water starts running into a channel that was dry a moment before. A row of leaves lifts as pressure reaches the irrigation sleeves beneath them. On the western bank, a fresh bead of white paste swells through the mesh and settles against the last layer.

There will be a little more ground here by evening.
