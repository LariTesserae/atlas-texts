The plain is the color of unglazed pottery. Under the noon sun it looks almost white, but wherever a little moisture reaches the surface, the earth deepens to orange, then to a dull, warm red. There are no mountains on the horizon. A few long rises interrupt the flatness, so broad and gradual that you can climb one for half a day without noticing until you turn and see the pale ground spread below you.

Everything beneath your feet has been placed there.

The plain is made of earth carried, wetted, pressed, and laid in courses. Its rises are embankments. Its hollows are basins. What first appear to be outcrops are the exposed shoulders of buried walls, built from thick clay blocks with rounded corners. Even the loose dust is the skin of something made.

You can see the work most clearly at the edge of a shallow channel. Beneath a hand’s breadth of dry crumbs, the bank is smooth and compact, interrupted by horizontal seams. Here and there a seam holds the impressions of fingers. Several hands pressed this part flat; someone drew the heel of a palm across it; someone else added the next layer before the first had dried. The marks have not yet softened into anonymous texture.

There is no exposed rock. Digging only reveals another course of earth, another bed of packed grit, another thickness of clay. The landscape has been built deep enough that whatever lies below it has no part in the life of the surface.

And it is immense.

The channels run beyond sight. Some are narrow enough to step across, others wide enough that their opposite banks disappear in the shimmer of afternoon. For most of the year they hold no flowing water. They are slightly darker paths through the brightness, their bottoms damp beneath a thin, brittle crust. The largest basins contain sheets of shallow water that retreat toward their centers through the hot months. Around them, the ground bears faint rings: white salts, red silt, a line of stranded husks.

Rain falls in different parts of the plain at different times. From a rise, you can watch a shower moving across the distance while the air around you remains clear and punishingly hot. The rain appears first as a gray thickening beneath a cloud, then as a dark patch on the ground. Sometimes that patch grows into a band several hours wide. Sometimes it vanishes before reaching the nearest channel.

The heat makes weather out of the plain itself. All morning the pale surfaces warm. By afternoon, air lifts from them in great slow columns. Clouds gather above the damp basins, flatten, pull apart, and gather again. Their shadows cross the earth with a speed that makes the stillness beneath them seem deliberate.

There is little shade. A clay bank offers a narrow strip in the morning and another in the evening; at noon, scarcely anything. The few shrubs are too small and open to shelter more than their own roots. Their leaves are gray-green, held edge-on to the sun. Some have stems swollen near the ground, storing water where the soil stays cool. Others grow as low mats, scarcely distinguishable from fallen straw until you notice the tiny new leaves along their margins.

Vegetation follows the seams.

A crack between two courses catches enough dust to become a rooting place. A sag in the plain holds rain for one additional day. A buried wall turns moisture sideways, and a thin line of grasses appears above it, revealing the wall’s course more accurately than the surface does. You can follow these green lines for miles. They branch at old junctions, stop at compacted thresholds, spread where a filled chamber has settled.

Most of the ground remains bare. Between one patch of plants and the next there may be an hour’s walk through clay, salt, and light.

Nothing moves across these distances on legs. No birds gather at the water. No insects skim its surface or enter the flowers. The plants reproduce by wind, by fallen pieces that root after rain, by seeds carried along the channels in thin washes of silt. Some flowers remain closed, forming seed inside their folded petals. Others open briefly in the early morning, pale and inconspicuous, then curl shut as the ground begins to radiate heat.

The silence is not complete, but each sound has room around it.

Near a drying pool, you hear a small click as a film of clay splits. After several quiet minutes, another answers from farther along the bank. Water sometimes makes a faint swallowing sound beneath a crust, draining into a buried joint. In the reeds there is a dry rubbing, stem against stem. Wind crossing a broad channel changes pitch almost imperceptibly, becoming a lower breath before thinning again over the level ground.

At night, the earth releases the day’s warmth. The air may cool, but sitting on an embankment still feels like sitting beside something that has recently been fired. Dew forms first on the darkest surfaces, then along the edges of leaves. By dawn, beads of water line the finer cracks. They persist for perhaps an hour after sunrise. Small roots occupy precisely those cracks, taking advantage of a daily supply too slight to make a puddle.

The plain is young in the way that a newly plastered wall is young. Its broad shapes are finished, but its materials have not finished adjusting to one another.

Long embankments settle by fractions. Basins widen without visibly breaking. A smooth slope develops a shallow wrinkle where a wet inner layer shifts beneath a dry skin. Here and there the ground opens into a narrow fissure, exposing fresh earth darker than anything around it. The fissures are sharp-edged. There has not been time for water to round them, or for repeated winds to carry their debris away.

After rain, clay swells against clay. Seams that were open in the morning close by evening. Elsewhere, drying pulls two surfaces apart. The ground is continually making small accommodations, although a person standing on it would feel none of them.

Occasionally there is a larger change. An entire bank gives way with a low, brief rush, laying itself into a channel in thick folds. Water arriving later finds this obstruction and spreads sideways into a basin that has never held it before. Within weeks, the basin acquires a dark film. Within a season, a few green shoots appear. The abandoned stretch downstream pales and hardens.

There are no deep gullies yet, no old rounded hills, no valleys patiently widened through long ages. Even where water runs, it mostly obeys the shapes prepared for it. A shallow lip sends it east. A compacted sill holds it back. A sequence of broad shelves slows its descent so much that a distant storm may arrive as a quiet sheet of water days after the clouds have gone.

The arrangements are simple enough to follow on foot, but large enough that their full purpose is difficult to see. One low wall seems pointless until you climb the next rise and find that it separates two expanses of different color. A chain of basins appears irregular from the ground; from an embankment, their rims align toward the far horizon.

There are no machines, pipes, gates, or working buildings. Nothing interrupts the channels except earth. Whatever labor shaped the place used the materials still visible here: clay, grit, reeds, water, hands. Reed impressions survive in some exposed blocks. In others, loose fibers hold the clay together, their broken ends projecting from the surface. There are stretches where the finish is rough, and stretches where someone took care to smooth a slope that no one now sees except in passing.

No builders remain.

In the late afternoon, the plain becomes easier to read. Low sunlight catches the seams. A nearly invisible terrace throws a long shadow. The edges of filled depressions emerge as gentle curves, and the immense level surface resolves into many adjoining pieces, each laid against another.

Then the light withdraws and the pieces merge again.

Far away, rain is falling. You cannot hear it, but the underside of one cloud is dark, and a cool current of air reaches you across the warm clay. In the channel at your feet, the first water is already arriving: a shallow front advancing through dust, gathering into separate threads where the bed is uneven.

It moves slowly past the exposed marks of the builders’ hands. Dry clay darkens. A curled leaf lifts, turns once, and travels on.
