The town occupies a shallow hollow near the top of a limestone ridge. You can walk from one end to the other in a few minutes, although there is no straight street through it. The lanes turn around shoulders of rock that were too large to remove, pass beneath a room built between two houses, and emerge into little open courts where the sky seems unexpectedly wide.

Beyond the last walls, the ridge continues bare for a short distance, then falls away. From inside the town, that drop is mostly invisible. Its presence is felt in the cool air coming through the eastern passages, and in the way distant country sometimes appears between two buildings: a strip of lower hills, blue and indistinct, set far beneath the level of a doorstep.

Almost everything is made from the same pale stone.

It is creamy where freshly chipped, gray on exposed faces, and brown along the bottoms of walls where damp dust has accumulated. The older blocks have rounded corners. Here and there, shells lie embedded in the limestone, revealed by centuries of hands, rain, and the passage of loads. A curved fossil crosses the edge of one threshold. Another, no larger than a thumbnail, is polished smooth beside a door latch.

The buildings are low and heavy, mostly one room deep, their roofs laid with overlapping stone slabs on timber beams. Good timber is scarce at this height. A beam may have a slight bend in it, and the room below follows that bend rather than wasting the wood. Doors are narrow. Windows are smaller still, set well back in the thickness of the walls. The shutters are assembled from short boards, carefully fitted and repaired with wooden pegs.

There are perhaps thirty houses, a storehouse, a covered work court, and a broad chamber where people gather. Together they form a compact piece of town: shared walls, public steps, drains, places assigned for particular kinds of work. Yet there is considerable emptiness within it. One court contains only a stone bench. Another holds a shallow trough and a single iron ring fixed to the wall. Between the houses are patches of bare bedrock that nobody has built over, because they are useful for laying out cloth, sorting grain, or simply crossing.

The lanes are worn rather than paved. At their centers the rock is smooth, with slight hollows where feet have found the easiest route. Along the sides, narrow channels carry rainwater toward the lower court. These channels were cut by hand and have been repaired so often that their edges no longer belong to any one period of work. A fresh notch fits into an old groove; an old groove disappears beneath a doorstep and resumes on the other side.

Water is present in small, definite places.

Under the northern retaining wall, a seam in the rock stays dark. Moisture gathers there, occasionally swelling into a bead that drops onto a flat stone below. There is no stream. The sound, when it comes, is a single soft tick.

The town’s cistern lies beneath the lower court. Its mouth is covered with a fitted slab, pierced by an opening just large enough for a bucket. A short wooden windlass stands over it. The handles have been rubbed darker than the rest of the wood, and the rope runs through a smooth notch in the stone. Rain reaches the cistern from the roofs and lanes after passing through a settling basin where grit collects.

Most of the time the basin is nearly dry. A little moisture remains beneath its removable cover, enough to keep the stone cool and to support a green film along one edge. The exposed courts dry quickly in the high air, but beneath stairs and behind thick walls there is a persistent cellar smell: wet mineral, old lime, a trace of wood.

Vegetation has found these protected places and largely kept to them. Fine grass grows where runoff leaves a little earth. A few low plants spread their tough leaves beneath the retaining wall. Lichen makes faint yellow and charcoal islands on roof slabs, especially where the stone holds the sun.

In the largest court stands a small pear tree. Its trunk leans away from the wall, then bends back toward the open sky. The ground around it is contained by a low ring of stones. Scraps of soil have been brought there in baskets, and the roots have worked down into a crack beneath them. The tree is old without being large. Its lower branches are polished where someone has repeatedly steadied themselves while climbing to reach the fruit.

There are no gardens beyond that court. The land outside offers thin soil, loose chips of limestone, and cushions of dry grass spaced widely apart. Along the ridge, the rock has weathered into shelves and shallow bowls. Nothing looks newly broken. Even the fallen blocks have settled firmly into the ground, with soil packed beneath their sheltered edges.

The daylight is clear and ordinary. It reaches the roofs early, then works its way down the walls. In narrow passages, the difference between sun and shade is enough to make a person pause before stepping across. The shaded stone stays cool even when the open court is comfortable. Toward evening, the limestone takes on a mild honey color, while the hollows in the lanes become blue.

Sounds do not accumulate here. They arrive separately.

A wooden bucket touches the side of the cistern. Someone draws a whetstone along a blade. A shutter closes, and its latch falls into place. From behind a wall comes the patient grinding of grain between stones, then a pause long enough to hear the wind moving around the roof corners.

Work is done with familiar, repairable things: iron-edged tools, wooden wedges, baskets, rope, stone weights. In the covered court, a bench holds a half-finished door beside a bundle of short planks. The floor beneath it is dusted with pale curls of wood. At the far end, a small forge has blackened the wall above its hearth. It is cold more often than lit.

Animals are few. A bird occasionally lands on the cistern frame, looks into the opening, and leaves. Small lizards keep to the warmer faces of the outer walls. There is a patch of fur caught on a rough gatepost, and narrow tracks in the dust beneath a storehouse door. At night, something light may pass along a roof, briefly dislodging grit.

The place has very little sense of becoming. Its houses have reached the shape the hollow can comfortably hold. The drains follow the available fall of the rock. Doorways open where a person can approach them without difficulty, and steps have been added where the ground requires steps. Repairs preserve these arrangements.

Rain darkens the walls and then leaves them pale again. The pear tree loses its leaves and grows them back. A broken roof slab is replaced by another of much the same size. These changes pass through the town without substantially altering it.

At the upper end is a small square with no building on its outward side. A waist-high wall marks the edge, broad enough to sit on. From there, the roofs lie just below eye level: irregular stone plates, a few dark openings, the pear branches rising between them. Farther away, the land descends through empty folds.

The wall’s coping is smooth under the hand. In a shallow depression beside it, a little rainwater has remained after the rest of the square dried. The water is clear enough to show the pale grains at the bottom. It occupies exactly the hollow the stone has made for it.
