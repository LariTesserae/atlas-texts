The hollow is small enough that, from its rim, you can hear someone shifting their weight on the opposite side. You cannot necessarily see them. There are too many things between: blunt fingers of limestone, hanging chains, the backs of low sheds, curtains of water so thin they resemble flaws in the air.

It lies only a little below the surrounding ground. Three worn steps descend into it from a lane of crushed shell. The bottom step is almost always submerged.

There is no grass along the lane, no moss on the steps, no tree leaning over the hollow. Even the sheltered seams between stones are bare. The ground is a mixture of pale grit, dark mud, and fragments of something blue-white that breaks like pottery but softens when held too long in a wet hand. In the hollows of old footprints, tiny animals turn those fragments over.

The place may once have been a quarry. Its walls suggest cutting, but the cuts have been softened by so many years of seepage that the distinction between tool marks and natural bedding has nearly disappeared. Broad horizontal grooves go around the hollow, interrupted by pillars left standing close together. Some are narrow enough to embrace. Others are squat, room-sized masses with passages between them that must be entered sideways.

Everything has a rounded edge. Water has worn shallow saddles into the tops of walls, scooped pockets under overhangs, and polished the lower faces of the pillars to the sheen of a tooth. The limestone is gray when merely damp and nearly black where water runs continuously. Here and there a fresh chip shows its dry interior: unexpectedly warm-looking, the color of bread.

Most of the water comes through the stone.

It beads along the horizontal grooves, gathers at their low points, and falls in patient threads. It rises through the floor in clear, trembling patches. Under a loose slab, it can be heard moving before it can be seen. Rain adds very little to the depth of the pools, but changes their surfaces completely. Every drip becomes harder to distinguish from every other, and the fine suspended dust vanishes from the air.

In dry weather the hollow still smells wet: cold iron, shell interiors, cloth left folded in a cellar.

The pools are separated by ridges only a finger or two above the water. A person walking through can choose a route from one slick crown of stone to another, although the route changes during the day. Water levels do not rise together. One basin may be filling while its neighbor empties, with no visible channel between them. The differences are small—usually less than the height of a boot—but enough to uncover a passage, drown a nesting shelf, or leave a shoal of animals briefly stranded in a shining cup.

At the deepest point stands a pump house.

It is built of dark brick, no larger than a kitchen, with a pitched sheet-metal roof weighted at each corner by a stone. Its door has swollen shut. Through a broken upper pane you can see a flywheel, a leather belt, and a pressure gauge whose glass has turned milky around the edge. The gauge needle moves even when the flywheel does not.

Two pipes leave the building. One crosses the hollow on low iron supports and disappears into a wall. The other goes directly down into a pool. Its open end is visible through the water, fringed with pale, jointed animals.

No one appears to maintain the machinery. Nevertheless, the flywheel makes a few slow revolutions at irregular intervals. The belt tightens. Somewhere under the floor a valve closes with a soft knock. Water shivers in three or four pools, and a smell of old grease comes out through the broken window.

The mechanism never works hard enough to sound like an engine. It has the volume of someone doing a small job in another room.

Other remnants are tucked among the stone: a steel ladder with its lowest rungs dissolved away, a ceramic switch box, two porcelain insulators lying beside a narrow drainage trench. There is a length of insulated cable looped over a pillar. The insulation has split along its underside, and water travels along the copper inside, dripping from each exposed strand separately.

On a ledge behind the pump house sit six square weights, each pierced for a handle. They have become shelters. Small shelled creatures withdraw beneath them when the air dries.

There are many animals here, though at first the hollow seems empty.

The most visible are flat, translucent things that occupy the undersides of dripping shelves. Each is about the size of a hand, with a hard central disc and a fringe of thin legs. They hold still so effectively that they look like patches where the stone has failed to dry. When a drop forms above one, the animal raises two legs to meet it. The water passes down their grooves to its mouth.

In the pools are pale shrimp, black segmented worms, and little armored grazers whose shells have the same pitted texture as the limestone. Their food is a nearly colorless film growing where seepage enters the water. It has no leaves or stems. On some surfaces it is no more than a greasy shine; on others it forms soft white granules that the grazers scrape away, exposing fresh stone.

The densest populations live around the pipe mouths. There, where the water carries the smell of metal, the film is thicker, and the animals climb over one another in slow heaps.

A few larger creatures hunt among them. These are slender, gray-backed swimmers with blunt heads and eyes so small they are easily mistaken for pores. They wait beneath the ledges until a worm extends too far from a crack. Then comes one quick flexion, a brief cloud of silt, and stillness again. Their backs are often marked by rusty crescents from passing through corroded pipework.

Above the water, insects crowd into the limited dry spaces. They are short-winged and heavy-bodied, with waxy coats that shed condensation. They rest in the bolt holes of the pipe supports, in the fold of the pump-house roof, inside the pressure gauge. Occasionally one slips, strikes a stone with a faint tick, and rights itself.

Nothing sings.

The sounds are water touching water, a shell dragged across rock, the delicate grit of feeding. A hanging chain sometimes moves without striking anything. When it finally touches a pillar, the note is low and brief. Beneath these small sounds is a vibration you feel through the soles of your boots, particularly on the ridges nearest the pump house. It strengthens before the flywheel turns.

Light reaches the bottom reluctantly. The hollow is shallow, but its pillars, walls, and low roofs divide the sky into narrow pieces. On an overcast afternoon, the pools look deeper than they are. A bright object dropped into one remains visible after the bottom around it has disappeared.

The water also seems slow to give up an image. Lift your hand away and its reflection stays for a moment, flattened under the surface, then loosens and breaks. The delay is most noticeable in the basins nearest the downward pipe. The pale hunters strike at these fading shapes. Their noses touch empty water where an insect stood a second earlier.

Beside that pipe is a narrow shelf covered with empty shells. Some are fresh and sharp-edged. Others have worn almost into rings. The flat animals settle among them, and their young shelter inside the openings until they grow too broad to fit.

By evening, condensation thickens on the chains. The metal roof darkens one panel at a time. Droplets gather beneath it in closely spaced rows, enlarging until their weight pulls them free. Each fall disturbs an insect, which edges farther into its seam.

The flywheel turns again. A ridge emerges near the steps, shining and bare; across the hollow, water quietly covers the shelf of shells. The animals begin moving before the change is complete. Grazers climb the pipe supports. Worms draw back into the wall. Under the newly exposed ridge, something much larger than a shrimp makes a slow passage through the silt.

For a while, the submerged bottom step becomes visible. Its center is deeply worn, though the two above it are nearly flat. Then clear water wells over it once more, carrying a few white grains out into the nearest pool.
