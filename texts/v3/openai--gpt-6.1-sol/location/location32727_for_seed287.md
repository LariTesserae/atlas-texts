The hollow lies just beneath the ground, shallow enough that roots have found their way through its roof, deep enough that the weather arrives late.

Above it, rain may already have passed and the surface be steaming in the sun. Below, a dark spot is still spreading across the ceiling. It will take several hours to gather weight, and several more to release a drop. Most drops never reach the floor. They strike a leaf, travel along its folded edge, and disappear into the crowded green.

The whole place is no larger than a modest village square. You can see its far wall from the entrance, although the way across is less obvious. The floor descends in broad, worn shelves around a central depression, each shelf thick with vegetation. The plants leave narrow lanes between them, some no wider than a sideways person. In the lanes, the ground is rust-red grit over smooth stone, dry enough to powder under a shoe. Beyond them, beneath the leaves, it is dark and faintly greasy.

The heat is almost unbearable.

It comes from below, not above. Put a hand against the wall and the stone is hotter than the air. The same is true of a buried pipe, a fallen brick, the broad bases of the plants. Only the new leaves are relatively cool. They hang in layers from the roof and rise in layers from the floor, meeting around chest height, so that walking through the hollow means passing through a thin band of tolerable temperature. Kneel and the heat closes over your head. Climb onto a ledge and your face enters air that feels as if it has just escaped an oven.

There is little light to explain so much growth. A narrow fissure high on one wall admits a dirty amber brightness. Elsewhere, small glass tiles remain embedded in the ceiling. Whatever once illuminated them still works intermittently: a tile pales from brown to yellow, holds that color for a while, then darkens. They never brighten together. Under each one, leaves turn by almost imperceptible degrees.

The vegetation is lush in volume rather than softness. There are fans of leathery foliage, overlapping scales on pale stems, trunks that divide and rejoin. Some leaves have the blunt, heavy shape of machine paddles. Others are translucent at their edges, showing networks of black veins. Nothing here looks thirsty, although water is scarce. The oldest growth has learned to keep its moisture behind wax, resin, and sealed seams.

Roots cross the passageways like cables laid without regard for anyone who might need to walk there. A few have grown through ceramic insulators. Others occupy the empty housings of motors, packed so tightly that the iron shells have split along their casting lines.

There are machines still working.

Not many: three, perhaps four, depending on whether the low vibration under the western shelf belongs to the visible equipment or something further down. The largest stands half-concealed beside the central depression. It is a pump, or the surviving upper portion of one, with a flywheel taller than a person and a piston descending through the floor. Its frame is bolted to a foundation that has rounded at the corners until it resembles a natural outcrop.

The flywheel moves slowly. You can watch a chipped spoke travel downward, disappear behind a curtain of leaves, and return some minutes later.

It is far too loud for its speed.

A sustained metallic roar fills the chamber. The piston adds a deeper sound, a dragging concussion that travels through the feet before reaching the ears. Pipework trembles inside the walls. Somewhere out of sight, a loose plate rattles continuously against another loose plate, both polished bright along the narrow line where they meet. Speech is possible, but only close to someone’s ear.

The plants stand almost motionless in this noise. A broad leaf may quiver at its tip. A seed sheath occasionally ticks against a support. There are no birds, no insects, no small bodies disturbing the undergrowth. Fallen leaves remain where they fall. Their exposed surfaces dry to a thin brown shell; underneath, they soften into black layers that gradually become part of the soil.

At the bottom of the hollow is a pool the size of a washbasin.

Everything seems arranged around it, though nothing gives it room. Roots press against its margin. Broken conduits point toward it. The shelves of stone descend to it with the shallow, settled curves of terrain worn over a very long time. Its water is nearly black, and its surface trembles with the pump’s vibration. It gives off no steam.

Once during each slow revolution of the flywheel, the level rises. Only a little—enough to wet a pale line around the rim. Water enters several grooves cut into the stone and travels outward beneath the plants. Then the pool sinks back to its previous depth.

The grooves are ancient. Their edges have worn smooth, and in places roots have diverted them, making small reservoirs inside the hollows of their own thick bodies. You can see how much care went into cutting them, and how long the place has had to revise that work.

Scattered structures survive on the higher shelves. There is a brick enclosure with no roof, a waist-high cabinet whose doors have rusted together, and a stair of seven steps leading to a blank wall. The brick enclosure contains a stand of pale stems. Their leaves unfold horizontally through the window openings, each opening filled almost exactly by one leaf.

Beside the cabinet, a pressure gauge remains legible. Its needle rests beyond the numbered scale. The glass is cloudy, but the dial behind it is clean. A root has entered through the back and grown around the mechanism without displacing it.

Farther along is a small shelter made of riveted sheet metal. It has space for a stool and a shelf. The stool is gone; its four feet remain bolted to the floor. On the shelf sits a shallow ceramic cup, filled to the brim with fine red dust. The threshold is worn down in the middle. A strip of dense foliage now blocks the route to the pump, leaving no sign of the hands or feet that once kept that route open.

Here and there, the growth does something stranger.

A fallen iron bar has acquired a bark-like skin, though both broken ends still show metal. One plant bears, beneath its leaves, a row of small hard shapes like wing nuts. Another has grown around a dangling length of chain; each link is enclosed in a separate green swelling, and the chain flexes whenever the plant shifts toward a brightening ceiling tile. In a patch beside the pump, the soil is threaded with hair-fine copper. New shoots come up holding the threads taut.

It is difficult to find a clean boundary between the equipment and the growth. A pipe enters a trunk and emerges higher up, narrower and without seams. A stem passes through a bearing and comes out rotating so slowly that the movement becomes apparent only after you look away and look back.

Yet the chamber feels settled. The machinery has room to move. The roots have room to thicken. Old collapses have been filled by growth and grit; old leaks have made their own channels. There is little loose rock, no exposed scar that looks recent. Even the hottest stones have acquired a thin, dark coating that does not blister.

If you stay long enough, you begin to notice the smaller changes. Condensation gathers on the underside of a metal ledge. A leaf relaxes, uncovering a patch of yellow light on the floor. The pool rises, wets its grooves, and subsides. Beneath the continuous roar, the piston completes another stroke.

Near the entrance, where a breath of outside air occasionally reaches down, the leaves are smaller and curled tightly inward. Beyond them lies the dry ascending passage. You can stand there with one shoulder cooling and the other still hot, looking back into the hollow.

From that distance the moving flywheel is nearly lost among the stems. A ceiling tile brightens behind it. For a moment its spokes cast a broad, slow shadow across the leaves, then the light fades before the shadow has passed.
