The water lies above most of the clouds.

From the lower country, there seems to be nothing up here but a long, pale summit disappearing into weather. The road climbs through forest, passes the last upright trees, and continues along a slope of wet stone. Then the ground levels out. Beyond the crest is a flooded plateau so broad that its farther shores look like banks of cloud themselves: low, dark, indistinct, continually erased by rain.

There are no dramatic peaks within it. The mountains have been worn down to their foundations. Their surviving backs are smooth and enormous, with the shallow curves of stones that have spent a long time in a river. Some stand above the water as islands; others show only as lighter patches beneath it. Between them, valleys have become channels, channels have become lakes, and the lakes open into one another through passages where the current runs hard enough to hold the reeds flat.

The plateau is nearly four thousand metres above the sea. It feels higher when the weather clears. At its western edge, beyond miles of flooded ground, the land simply ends, and water pours over the rim into a depth that cannot be seen from here. Clouds rise out of that depth. Sometimes they spill back across the plateau, so that rain is falling into the lake while mist travels over it in the opposite direction.

Most of the country is underwater, though not all of it is deep.

Along the margins, clear water covers green meadows by the height of a boot or a person’s waist. Long aquatic leaves stream from the old turf. Moss coats the submerged stones in thick, bright pads, and the stems of water plants crowd together so closely that a boat passing through leaves a temporary corridor behind it. In sheltered bays, floating leaves overlap from bank to bank. Farther out, the bottom drops away and the water becomes dark olive, then almost black. Loose stalks turn upright in the swell and vanish again.

There is so much vegetation that the distinction between shore and water is unreliable. What looks like firm ground may be a raft of sedge roots. What looks like a flooded field may be a deep inlet with a skin of plants stretched across it. The safest islands are the bare ones, where bedrock shows through: grey stone, rusty seams, shallow hollows filled with rain. Even these carry cushions of moss and low shrubs in every crack that can hold a handful of soil.

On the larger islands, dwarf willow grows sideways. Its branches weave into dense mats, glossy with water, and the leaves shiver all at once when a gust reaches them. Ferns occupy the sheltered clefts. Rushes stand in brown-green ranks along the channels, their lower stems polished by moving water. Nothing grows very tall, but almost everything grows thickly. The landscape’s luxuriance is close to the ground.

It is cold without being frozen. Water finds the wrists, the collar, the stitching of a glove. A sunny interval can warm the face while the rest of the body remains chilled. The stones sweat. Wool never quite dries.

For much of the day the light is ordinary, grey daylight, enough to see the veins in a leaf and the stones beneath a shallow pool. When the clouds thin, the whole plateau brightens with startling speed. Sunlight reaches through the water and lays the moving shadows of plants across drowned paths. A low island that seemed distant becomes sharply visible, every shrub and boulder distinct. Then a rain curtain moves across it, and it is gone.

There is always something happening on the water.

Wind races down the long openings between islands and heaps the lake against their lee shores. Waves that were moving evenly across open water strike the mouths of narrow channels and turn steep, disorderly, crowded. Some passages reverse with the weather: water pours through them eastward all morning, stalls at noon, and surges westward under an afternoon squall. Floating vegetation gathers into barriers, breaks apart, and gathers somewhere else. An entire reed raft can travel half a mile in a day, carrying a small, bewildered company of insects and birds.

Rain rarely falls straight. It comes low over the surface, blurring the boundary between lake and air. On the worst afternoons, the nearest island is only a darkening in the spray. Whitecaps cross the shallow meadows. Water washes over rocks that were landing places an hour before. The old, rounded ground remains where it has always been, but everything loose upon it is in motion.

The sound is larger than the visible landscape. Reeds hiss in the wind. Waves slap exposed stone and thump beneath overhanging mats of roots. In the narrow cuts there is a heavy, continuous roar, with smaller sounds inside it: pebbles knocking together, the hollow gulp of an eddy, a branch striking the same rock again and again. Rain on open water makes a fine, dense crackle. Rain on the corrugated roof of a shelter can drown out a shouted conversation.

Birds use the intervals between squalls. Ducks rise from the vegetation in sudden groups, their wings beating low over the lake before they gain height. Grebes disappear into the channels and surface well away from where they went down. Geese feed on the short island turf. Along the rockier shores, small dark birds move so quickly between stones that they resemble pieces of shadow displaced by the waves.

Below the surface, fish hold in the quieter water behind submerged ridges. In clear weather they are visible from a boat: dark backs over pale stone, turning together when a shadow passes. Otters work the margins, leaving crushed stems and heaps of fish scales on the few dry ledges. In the sheltered pools, larvae hang among the roots. During a calm spell, flies lift off in clouds, and the birds begin feeding so busily that the whole bay seems to flicker.

Human structures are scattered and small against all this water.

A pump house stands on one island, built of stained concrete with a green metal door. Another holds a weather mast and a shed anchored to the rock by steel straps. There are a few landing stages, usually tucked into the lee of a stone shoulder. Their ladders extend well below the present waterline. Some have older ladders beside them, bent or furred with weed, left from a different arrangement of channels.

The plateau was already wet before the engineers came. Low barriers across several outlets raised the water a little, enough to join separate lakes and drown the shallow intervening ground. Those barriers are difficult to pick out now. From a distance they resemble unusually straight islands. Nearer, the concrete appears, patched in several colours, with grass growing from joints above the spillways.

Inside the surviving service huts are heavy switches, pressure gauges, coils of cable, and diesel engines with warm oil beneath their smell of damp iron. Electricity is made where the water leaves the plateau. Most of the machinery is far below, hidden in the cliffs, but its workings reach up here through intake grilles, marker poles, and a deep vibration occasionally felt through a landing stage.

When a gate is opened, a sheltered inlet changes character. Loose stems begin drifting toward one end. The water tightens around the rocks. A broad revolving patch forms above the intake, and the birds move away from it. By evening, fresh strands of weed are plastered against the grille.

People travel in shallow workboats with blunt bows and enclosed engines. They keep routes by aligning one island with another, since floating markers sometimes drag in storms. The boats carry spare belts, rope, fuel cans, and bundles of cut poles for testing the depth. A drowned road remains useful in places: its raised bed gives a strip of shallow water across a bay. Elsewhere it disappears into a channel, and the boat must turn aside.

On still mornings, fragments of that older ground can be seen remarkably well. There are walls beneath the water, low boundaries assembled from rounded stones. A culvert opens out of a bank of waving plants. Fence posts stand with their tops just below the surface, each supporting a little cap of algae. Fish pass through doorways where no building remains above them.

By afternoon these details are usually hidden again. Wind roughens the surface, clouds descend, and the wide country closes around the boat. Nearby water is full of stems, spray, wings, and crossing waves; beyond it, the islands fade one after another.

Then, from somewhere out of sight, an engine starts. Its uneven knocking carries across the flooded plateau for a few seconds before the wind takes it away.
