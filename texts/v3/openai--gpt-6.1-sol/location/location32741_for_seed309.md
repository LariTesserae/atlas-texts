The town lies one root-depth below the surface, in a chamber about the size of a small railway station. You enter down a stair that has been worn into the underside of a tree. The steps are warm, shallow, and slightly yielding. Along their edges, new wood continually rises into the places feet have hollowed out, so that the stair has kept almost the same shape for centuries without retaining any of its original substance.

At the bottom, the noise arrives before the view.

There is a heavy, uneven knocking, a shrill continuous whine, the scrape of something being drawn through a narrow aperture. Under these sounds runs a dense rustle, like rain moving over leaves, although there is no rain here. From time to time a deep note passes through the floor and settles in your knees. Conversations stop for it. The inhabitants wait until they can hear themselves again, then continue from the interrupted word.

The chamber is mostly open. Six or seven buildings stand well apart around an empty central floor: a pumping house, a mill, some narrow dwellings, a public shed with three broad doorways. Their walls grow from the same substance as the ground. What first looks like brown plaster is bark, thin and close-grained; what seems to be brick is a succession of rectangular swellings in a root, trained to produce regular joints. There is no bare earth. Beneath the dry litter on the floor is living rind, and beneath that, layers of pale, fibrous tissue extending downward beyond any cellar.

Even the emptiness is a plant.

It is easy to mistake the chamber for a hollow excavated among roots. Stay longer, and you notice that the entire interior is one surface, folded into streets, houses, ducts, retaining walls. There are no corners where unrelated materials meet. The town has been coaxed out of a single branching growth, though grafts from other species have been introduced here and there: a hard black fruitwood for thresholds, a springy yellow vine for hinges, a gray creeping plant whose leaves seal the roofs like overlapping scales.

Only a few shoots protrude into the air. The town keeps its growth flush. Nothing is allowed to trail across a walkway or throw a new branch into the square. Vegetation occupies almost everything without making a thicket; there is room to see from one end of the chamber to the other, if your eyes can find enough light.

Light comes through three shafts in the ceiling. None opens directly to the sky. Each bends somewhere above, admitting a weak green reflection from the surface foliage. At midday, these shafts produce three dim columns in which drifting insects briefly become visible. Elsewhere the illumination is industrial: low amber lamps embedded beside doors and along the machine galleries. Their wires pass through insulated channels in the roots. Around every lamp the bark has thickened into a heat collar, glossy from years of handling.

The power station occupies the rear of the chamber. It has no façade, only an open row of machinery beneath a ribbed roof. Broad belts run between pulleys. The belts are lengths of tough creeper stripped of leaves and kept alive through their attachment to the wall; slowly, almost invisibly, they renew themselves at one end while being consumed at the other. The shafts turn on bearings packed with seed oil. Black armatures, pressed from carbonized wood, spin inside copper coils.

The copper is the one thing the town cannot grow. It is inherited, patched, drawn thinner, never discarded. A worn winding is taken apart strand by strand under a magnifying lens, then spliced into another circuit. Small ceramic trays made from plant ash catch every filing.

The machines do real work. Pumps lift sap into the upper grafts. A press extracts oil. A rack of cutters produces uniform lengths of fiber for neighboring settlements. There is a workshop where the slow growth of the buildings is measured and corrected, because an unbalanced house can divert the circulation of an entire street.

This work is loud. The mill’s cutters chatter against alternating bands of hard and soft tissue. Pressure valves cough. A flywheel clicks once each turn where its rim has split and been bound with cord. Yet most of the noise comes from animals.

Every ledge carries them.

Moths are packed beneath the eaves, their wings laid over one another like shingles. Beetles stand in the warm seams of the walls. Long-legged insects skate over the floor in such numbers that the moving layer seems to lift and settle without any individual creature deciding to go anywhere. In the rafters, small furred bodies hang in bunches, shifting, grooming, squeaking at neighboring bunches. Pale lizards wait beside the lamps with their throats opening and closing. Behind the bark, something larger crawls through a duct; you can follow its progress by the soft bulge moving along the wall.

The open square is empty of buildings and almost empty of people, but never empty of life. Tiny bodies cross it in streams. They choose routes that do not coincide with the paths worn by feet. To walk here is to step over traffic you can barely see.

There are rules for this. Do not tread on the white insects moving toward the pumping house; they clean the valves. Do not touch the brown swarms clustered beneath a dwelling’s windows; they eat the eggs of the wall-borers. Sweep the black, broad-backed beetles toward the mill rather than outside. They carry a fungus the mill requires, and they will find their own way back when their shells are clean.

There are predators for all of these creatures, and predators for the predators. Little blunt-headed snakes sleep in the cable channels. Transparent mites gather on the snakes. A bird no larger than a thumb lives in a hollow above the oil press and comes out to pick the mites away. Its chicks make a noise astonishingly large for their bodies, a metallic squealing almost indistinguishable from a slipping belt.

People work among this abundance with practiced indifference. A machinist pauses to let a procession cross the back of his hand. A woman carrying food brushes a moth from her lip without interrupting her conversation. Someone turns a coat inside out before entering a house, shaking loose a brief, furious cloud.

The warmth is steady, but the moisture is not. Most of the chamber feels dry against the skin. The floor gives off a smell of heated husks and old tea. Dust gathers in places no broom can reach.

Then a pump changes rhythm, or rain falls somewhere far above, and one wall begins to sweat.

Droplets form along its ribs. They enlarge slowly, trembling with the machinery’s vibration. The animals know before anyone else. Creatures leave cracks that appeared unoccupied; moths unfold; the lizards climb away from the wettest channels. When the first drops fall, there is a sudden rush toward them. Each bead becomes a crowded drinking place, ringed by minute feet.

Water is led through shallow grooves to a cistern below the public shed. The cistern is only knee-deep, with a soft green bottom and a rim polished by generations of buckets. It holds fish so small and numerous that the water looks finely textured. Larger mouths occasionally rise through this texture, take something, and vanish.

After an hour, or a day, the wall dries. The town’s dampness withdraws into protected pockets: the cistern, the pump seals, the dark undersides of doors. A sour, cool smell remains in the grooves.

Nothing here has a new edge. Doorways have broadened around shoulders. The central floor dips under the weight of old gatherings. Machinery stands in depressions grown exactly to its feet. Even the animals’ routes have worn shallow channels, some no wider than a thread.

The place is maintained rather than perfected. A bulge is pared away. A failing graft is replaced. A bearing is listened to through a stick held against the teeth. At dusk the lamps brighten slightly, the upper vents close, and the moths rise from the buildings in a single thick movement.

For a moment the air seems solid.

Then they disperse into the rafters, and the square opens again. Across its warm, dark floor, between houses that continue growing in their sleep, the smallest traffic goes on.
