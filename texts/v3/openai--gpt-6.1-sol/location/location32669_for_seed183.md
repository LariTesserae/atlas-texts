The basin lies above the clouds, but it is not dry. Water beads on every exposed surface. It gathers beneath the lips of stones, runs down the undersides of ledges, and seeps from cracks in rock that has no higher ground behind it. By noon, in brilliant sunlight, the whole valley is shining.

There are no trees to give the distances scale. No grass softens the shelves. The ground is pale, laminated stone, blue-white where newly broken and the color of old teeth where the weather has worked on it. Ridges rise around the basin in close ranks. Their lower slopes are rounded, their crests worn into blunt fins, yet they stand at such an elevation that cloud banks pass beneath their saddles. Through gaps between them, you can look down onto thunderstorms.

The air is cool enough to make wet fingers ache. In shadow, thin ice persists until afternoon. In the light, droplets tremble on the stone without evaporating, each holding a fierce white point.

Most of the water is shallow. It lies in plates and strips, follows hairline channels, fills hollows no deeper than a cupped hand. The larger pools are startlingly clear. You can see each grain on their bottoms.

Then, as you move around one, its bottom recedes.

The grains remain visible, but now they are far below you. The little basin in the rock opens into a shaft whose walls descend past shelves, narrow bridges, and pale outcrops. Sunlight reaches all the way down. A pebble dropped into it strikes the bottom almost at once, although you watch it fall for a long time.

This is common here. The old paths avoid puddles.

They cross the valley in short, angular stretches, following dry ribs between the channels. On steeper sections, shallow toe-holds have been pecked into the stone. Their edges are polished by use, though there is no one on the paths now. Small cairns mark turns. Some consist of only three stones, carefully separated so that rain cannot make a continuous wet surface between them.

The basin is about a day’s walk across if the crossings are dry. After rain, parts of it become much nearer to one another, and other parts cease to be reachable at all.

From the western slope, you can see the scattered buildings: low stone shelters on isolated humps, a few roofless enclosures, something like a storehouse beneath an overhang. None has a window facing a pool. The walls are made of hand-fitted slabs, with small dark wedges driven between them. Their roofs are steep and extend well beyond the walls. Long grooves carry runoff away from the foundations.

At every threshold is a broad sill of porous, crumbly stone. Behind it lies a second sill, then a shallow tray of dry white powder.

The arrangement looks excessive until you see water reach a doorway.

A thin thread creeps across the outer sill. As it joins the dampness inside, the room beyond shifts. The back wall is suddenly distant. Its familiar blocks are still distinguishable, but they stand across an expanse of bare floor too broad to fit within the building. The thread breaks; the wall returns. Nothing moves violently. There is only a slight click from the roof joints.

Inside one shelter, a wooden rake rests beside a tray. Its teeth have been replaced many times. A stone mallet, a bundle of coarse cord, and several shallow bowls occupy a shelf. The bowls contain chips sorted by color and texture. The tools are plain, and their purpose seems to have been maintenance: keeping channels open, replacing the powder, separating things that must not become wet together.

Outside, water makes most of the sound.

There is the close ticking of droplets falling into hollows. There is a broad hiss from the wet slopes, rising when sunlight warms them. There are abrupt, hollow reports from pools that appear motionless. Sometimes a stream vanishes beneath a slab with a noise like an enormous sheet being shaken.

The loudest sound comes from the eastern wall.

Across its face, seven waterfalls hang in bright, broken columns. They issue from the same thick seam of dark rock and fall onto terraces at different heights. Spray drifts through the spaces between them. Their roar fills the basin, loud enough that a person could work a chisel on the opposite slope without hearing its blows.

Only four of the falls reach their visible terraces. The others enter a thin band of mist and emerge elsewhere: one from a fissure near the valley floor, one beneath a natural arch, and one from the top of the western ridge, where it flows gently uphill for a few paces before spreading into gravel.

The displaced water is colder. It leaves a white deposit that grows in fine overlapping plates.

Nothing green occupies those deposits. There are no insects on the pools, no tracks in the damp grit, no birds riding the immense currents beyond the ridges. Yet the surfaces are densely worked. Needle crystals crowd beneath dripping ledges. Dark mineral skins wrinkle where streams widen. Fragile shelves of salt extend over water and collapse under their own weight. At the base of the falls, new stone forms around old broken stone, sealing yesterday’s fragments into tomorrow’s terrace.

A gray sediment creeps across the shallowest pools as they warm. It arranges itself in branching lines, draws close to the wet margin, and retreats when a passing cloud cools the water. Perhaps it is alive. Nothing here has been built to feed it or exclude it.

The strange distances are oldest in the center of the basin. There, erosion has rounded the places where one stretch of ground meets another. Smooth channels enter the foot of a ridge and continue halfway up its opposite face. Broad shelves bear the abrasion of water that now passes hundreds of feet away. Some stones have been polished on all sides while remaining firmly embedded in the ground.

The valley has had time to wear these arrangements comfortable.

A narrow bridge near the center crosses a channel less than a stride wide. It is made from three slabs tied together with weathered cord. Beneath it, water runs over clean gravel. Seen from the upstream end, the opposite bank is close enough to touch.

From halfway across, it is a long way off.

The slabs remain beneath your feet. The cord remains slack along their edges. But the bank stands at the far side of a broad, sunlit reach, and the shelter beyond it looks very small. The safest course is to watch the next foothold. Those who laid the bridge cut marks into its surface: one shallow notch after another, spaced for an ordinary step.

By late afternoon the glare softens, though the basin remains bright. The clouds below turn gold around their upper edges. Shadows climb the waterfall wall, and the mineral shelves begin to crack as they cool. Small pieces fall with sharp, glassy sounds.

Water withdraws into the rock.

The valley becomes easier to read. Paths that appeared to end at distant ledges join their next stretches. The center of a pool rises until it is merely a stone hollow again. Across the basin, the shelters settle into their actual proportions: modest rooms, thick walls, enough space for a sleeping platform and tools.

For a short while, the walk between them is straightforward.

Then the first shadow reaches the western ridge. Moisture gathers on its cold face. A dark line lengthens down the stone, passes beneath an arch, and joins a pool.

The roar of the eastern falls comes suddenly closer.
