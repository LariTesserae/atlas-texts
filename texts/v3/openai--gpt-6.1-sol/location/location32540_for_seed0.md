The ground is still sharp enough to cut a foot.

Under the leaf litter, under the fallen fruit and the busy, shifting bodies of beetles, the black stone has edges that weather has not yet softened. Some slabs are smooth on top and hollow underneath. Others stand tilted like broken teeth, their fractures packed with pale roots. Heat comes through them after dark. A hand placed against the ground would find one patch merely warm, the next almost painful, and a third unexpectedly cool where a seam leads down into water.

There is no visible river. Water announces itself in smaller ways: a wet gleam inside a split rock, the cold underside of a leaf, a hollow containing three inches of brown liquid and so many drinking animals that its rim seems to move. Rain comes hard and infrequently. Most of it disappears before it can run any distance, swallowed by cracks in the young stone. The country looks lavishly watered until something tries to cross it without knowing where to drink.

Above the ground, everything is green, though very little of that green can be seen.

It is night, and the sky is closed by low cloud. Only where a branch breaks or the canopy opens over a fresh collapse does the darkness thin enough to distinguish leaf from air. The vegetation has filled the basin in layers: broad-leaved trees with swollen trunks, climbing plants thick as thighs, feathery shrubs lodged in the forks of larger shrubs, and mats of small, hard leaves spread over any surface that will hold dust. Some trees have grown sideways across fissures before turning upward. Their roots descend into the dark like bundles of cables, dividing and dividing until each tip finds a dampness too slight to leave a stain.

These roots are what allow the abundance. They reach past the dry crust into buried beds of gravel where old rain remains. Several kinds of tree seal themselves during the hottest hours. Their leaves fold inward, exposing pale backs, and unfold again at dusk with a noise like many small sheets being shaken out. By midnight the canopy is thick with open blades, each gathering what little moisture the air will surrender.

The basin extends for miles. Its low ridges are difficult to recognize from inside it; they are disclosed by changes in sound, by a band of thinner trunks, by warm air flowing downhill where there should be no downhill. Beyond the nearest stand of trees lie further stands, sunken flats, fields of shattered stone, and long humps of land raised only recently. The whole place rests over a shallow, restless heat. Twenty wet seasons ago, parts of it were bare. Seeds arrived in dung and fur, in the crops of birds, in the folds of animals fleeing the hotter ground. Now there are places where a falling branch cannot reach the earth without hanging up in three different kinds of foliage.

The noise makes the distance hard to judge.

Close by, insects rasp with such force that individual calls merge into a hard, metallic band. Above that are the hollow knocks of fruit feeders striking seed shells against stone. Higher still, in the crowns, something screams in repeated bursts, and other animals answer from so many directions that the screaming seems to spread across a surface rather than pass between separate mouths. Wings slap leaves. Claws scrape bark. Large bodies force their way through thickets with a rolling crackle, abruptly stop, then begin again.

Under all of it is a continuous softer noise: chewing, digging, licking, the patter of falling scraps.

There is no quiet interval between one animal and the next. When one chorus stops, its absence reveals another that was already sounding beneath it.

The largest grazers move in herds through the low ground. They are heavy, low-shouldered animals with glossy hides and pale, flexible muzzles. They pull whole clusters of leaves into their mouths, stripping a shrub in minutes. Along their backs cling small birds whose feet lock into the folds of skin. Other birds run underneath them, taking the insects flushed by each step. When a herd changes direction, the vegetation shakes well ahead of it; a wave of displaced animals travels outward through the undergrowth.

The grazers seldom move slowly for long. Biting flies gather wherever they pause. Predators follow their smell. Heat rises from patches that were comfortable an hour earlier. A fissure that held drinking water yesterday may now breathe hot, sour air. Rest is possible, but never secure.

Even the small animals seem pressed for time. A cluster of dark, long-fingered creatures gathers around a fallen seed pod. One holds it while another brings down a loose stone. The shell gives with a sharp report. Immediately there is a fight over the exposed white pulp, and the stone is dropped among all the other stones. A second pod is carried away unopened. Higher in the same tree, a bird works a thorn into a knot of bark, draws out a grub, and loses it to a smaller bird that has been watching from beneath the branch.

Nothing here has been cleared or built. Useful objects remain loose, borrowed briefly from the ground or a plant and then lost again in the litter.

The fresh stone offers little firm shelter. Cavities open as their roofs cool and contract. The shallow caves are crowded with bodies, and the deeper ones smell of mineral vapor. Most animals shelter in the vegetation instead, where there is always something capable of carrying them, concealing them, or stinging whatever follows.

Some of the vines respond to weight. A touch from a beetle does nothing, but a larger body crossing them sets off a slow tightening. Leaves turn edge-on; tendrils shorten; a slack loop rises until it presses the intruder against a branch. Small climbing animals know how to move through these vines, distributing their weight across several stems. The unwary struggle until the branch breaks, carrying vine and animal together into the foliage below.

The plants release their grip after a while. The fallen body, if there is one, remains close to their roots.

Toward the middle of the basin, the canopy lowers over a broad field of vents. Here the air is warmer and carries a faint mineral sting. The trees grow apart at ground level but lean inward above, their crowns interlocking over the bare openings. White roots fringe the vents, retreating from the hottest stone and advancing where the heat has weakened.

This is where the moisture gathers.

At certain hours, vapor rises from below and condenses on the cooler leaves. Drops enlarge at their tips. For a few minutes there is a localized rain without any change in the sky.

The animals hear its beginning.

A drip strikes an empty husk. Another falls onto a flat leaf. Before the pattering becomes steady, creatures are already arriving: winged bodies dropping through gaps, rodents slipping between roots, broad-backed grazers pushing smaller animals aside. They lick stone, leaves, one another’s wet hides. Some drink so fast that they cough and begin again. Predators come too, but even they lower their heads to the shining ground before attending to the crowd.

Then the vapor thins. The drops stop. Moisture retreats into the litter, and the gathering breaks into pursuit, quarrel, flight.

Farther away, the trees begin to sway.

The movement reaches the basin before the wind does. It passes across the crowns as a long, uneven depression, flattening some and leaving others upright. Birds rise out of it in a dark sheet. The insect chorus falters, then resumes at a higher pitch.

When the wind arrives at ground level it is hot, carrying dry grit from the bare ridges. Leaves lash together. Loose pods hit trunks like thrown stones. A dead tree breaks somewhere out of sight, and the crash brings a brief eruption of cries so loud that the insects vanish beneath it.

The wind reverses through the hollows. It draws cooler air out of the fissures, lifts litter from the ground, and exposes a narrow opening that has widened during the day. Pebbles tick down into it. A root snaps. From below comes the sound of water, briefly audible through all the noise.

Several animals turn toward it at once.

By morning, if the cloud lifts, the basin will show itself in pieces: a low black ridge; a wash of pale new leaves; the backs of a herd moving between trunks; steam rising from a clearing no wider than a fallen tree. The newly opened fissure may be concealed already by leaves and displaced soil. Animals that found it in the night will return by scent, feeling along the stone with their mouths.

For now there is only the dark, warm pressure of the place, crowded from the deep cracks to the highest branches. The ground shifts beneath its cover. Water appears and is consumed. Fruit falls before it can ripen, or ripens unnoticed inside a knot of vines. Everywhere, bodies push through spaces barely large enough to contain them, and each passage opens a route that something else immediately follows.
