By noon the streets have grown narrower.

They were broad at dawn: pale corridors between walls of folded leaf, wide enough for the sun to reach their floors. Now the walls are swelling inward. Fresh ribs have pushed through the paving, and the paving itself—flat, overlapping leaves with the toughness of bark—is lifting into shallow ridges. At some intersections the two sides have met. They press together, shudder, and begin to fuse, leaving the former street as a dark seam inside a new mass of green.

Elsewhere, entire avenues are opening.

The sound is enormous.

It comes from growth: the crack of a sheath splitting, the prolonged wooden report of a trunk unfolding, the wet slap of broad leaves thrown open against one another. Fibers snap under tension with a noise like hail on a hollow roof. Underground, roots drag against roots. The vibrations arrive through the soles before they reach the ear, so that standing still feels like occupying the inside of a drum.

There are no birds above it, no insects within it. Nothing calls, hunts, grazes, or answers. Yet the place has the crowded violence of a city at its busiest hour.

It occupies a low, wide basin, though the basin is also alive. There is no exposed earth. Beneath the streets lies a mat of interwoven root; beneath that, thicker root, descending through successive consistencies until the distinction between root and ground ceases to be useful. Where a fissure opens, it reveals ivory fibers, wine-colored membranes, chambers lined with short stiff hairs. The bottom never appears.

The basin’s margins rise only slightly. From the uppermost terraces, when the foliage moves apart, you can see districts extending toward the horizon: towers of rolled fronds, squat domes swollen with sap, ranks of narrow green spires with their tips still hooked from the effort of emergence. Their arrangement is too regular to dismiss as a thicket. Corridors meet at plazas. Arches span lanes. Broad, buttressed growths stand at the edges of open spaces, with smaller structures crowded behind them.

But there is no masonry, no timber cut from its source, no hand’s work anywhere.

An arch begins as two shoots bending toward one another. Their tips touch, graft, and thicken. A stair is a root repeatedly folding back on itself. A balcony grows when the side of a trunk needs more light. Buildings do not contain plants. They are the temporary shapes plants assume while pressing against other plants.

The tallest structures are not the oldest. Some of them rose this morning.

Their surfaces are tender, almost unmarked. Pale green skin stretches over sharp angles. Newly exposed edges carry beads of clear fluid, and beneath those beads you can see the pressure of the tissue inside: a faint bulging, a slow pulse. Nothing has had time to weather into gentleness. Corners are crisp. Broken surfaces are bright. A fallen tower lies across three lanes with its split core still shining, while from its upper side a row of fresh doorways is already swelling into shape.

Warmth settles everywhere.

In the open squares it is ordinary sunlight, strong and yellow, striking the upper leaves until they show every vein. Within the close-built districts it is stored heat, released by breathing walls. The air smells of bruised stems, green wood, resin, and the faint ferment of sap caught in sealed chambers.

For all this vegetation, there is little water to see.

The leaves are thick and waxed. Many hold their edges downward, presenting almost no surface to the sky. Their undersides are furred, their joints tightly shut. Rain comes infrequently and briefly, passing over one quarter of the basin while another remains under clear light. When it falls, the whole district changes its posture. Gutters uncurl along the walls. Cups open at the ends of stems. Paving lifts at its seams and drinks.

Within minutes, the water is gone.

A few hollows retain it: dark pockets beneath a collapsed arcade, shallow basins cupped in enormous leaves, a narrow channel at the edge of a square. These are places of extraordinary stillness. The water lies black under the greenery, reflecting a sky that may not be the one overhead.

Approach a pool and the surrounding growth slows. A leaf halfway through opening remains folded. A root lifted from the floor stays suspended, trembling slightly. The noise does not diminish, but its individual shocks lengthen. A single cracking sound can continue for several breaths.

Step away, and the unfinished movements complete themselves together.

The inhabitants of this place, if that word can be used, are the structures themselves. A tower leans across an avenue and distributes its weight through the roofs opposite. Those roofs flatten, forcing their lower walls outward. The outward walls close a passage, and the trapped passage pushes up through them as a chimney. Whole neighborhoods adjust to one growing surface. What begins as a small bulge in a wall can move through a district for hours.

The changes are not random. Light is diverted toward some chambers and withheld from others. Roots converge beneath plazas. Young growth appears in repeated patterns, sometimes across miles of interrupted terrain.

A square may fill with tightly packed stems overnight. Each has the same five-sided cross section. Each rises to the same height, then branches at the same angle. In the morning they split open down one side and unfold into a covered market of sorts: aisles, ledges, hanging canopies, shallow compartments lined with velvety tissue.

There is nothing for sale. No one arrives.

By evening the compartments have filled with curled leaves, and the canopies lower until they touch the ledges. The whole formation closes, leaving a warm, corrugated wall. A new street has appeared on its far side.

Distance becomes unreliable inside the thicker districts.

You can watch an avenue stretching straight ahead, its arches diminishing neatly toward a bright exit, then step into it and find the exit close enough to touch. Behind you, the entrance has become the distant point. Looking back does not restore the original length. Sometimes the avenue contains both distances at once: nearby leaves sharply visible along a corridor whose floor takes an hour to cross.

The roots do not seem troubled by this. A single exposed fiber can run beside the path, enter a wall, and emerge at the far end before you have reached the first arch.

There are places where a leaf has an underside on both faces. There are trunks that pass through themselves without a wound. In one broad plaza, a low hedge grows around a patch of sunlight. The sun moves; the patch does not. By late afternoon its brightness lies against the direction of every surrounding shadow, illuminating the lower surfaces of the leaves as though the light were rising from the ground.

The hedge makes a continuous brittle roar.

At intervals, it flowers. The flowers are green, heavy, and almost closed. Each contains a tiny depth: not a seed chamber, but a view into some other part of the basin. One shows a street under rain. Another shows the flank of a tower as it ruptures. Another contains the same plaza, seen from just above the hedge, but empty of flowers.

These openings last only seconds. Their petals clamp shut with enough force to tear their own edges.

Farther out, the organized districts loosen into broad, crowded expanses. Low ridges travel through fields of blade-shaped leaves. They are not wind waves. The ground itself is changing height beneath them, lifting and subsiding as thick roots trade positions below. Occasionally a ridge stops and rises into a long wall, leaving the two sides of the field unable to touch. Fresh shoots climb its face before it has finished growing.

Here the open sky is visible, blue and substantial, with a few small clouds moving in the normal direction. Sunlight falls plainly on the upheaval. You can see miles of it: pale new seams, dark mature crowns, towers opening like fists, districts closing over their own streets. The whole basin is densely occupied, yet offers these sudden long views between its moving parts.

Late in the day, heat draws moisture from the deepest chambers. Condensation gathers under arches and on the shaded lips of doorways. The drops swell slowly. Some fall. Others are drawn back into the skin just as their weight begins to pull them free.

The loudest hour comes before sunset.

Across the basin, woody tissues harden. Growth that has remained flexible all day sets into position. A thousand long fractures sound together. Walls buckle as their outer layers contract. Roofs spring upward; roots wrench through paving; dry sheaths break loose and slide in great rattling sheets down the sides of towers.

Then another sound becomes audible beneath the breaking: a low, sustained groan, too deep to locate. The leaf-streets tremble. Water in the sheltered pools climbs their rims.

For a moment, every visible stem inclines toward the same point below ground.

The sun remains warm on the upper terraces. Newly split surfaces gleam. In a narrow lane that did not exist an hour ago, a row of small leaves opens quietly along the wall, each turning its smooth face toward the light.

Behind them, the lane begins to close.
