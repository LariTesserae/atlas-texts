The entrance is a seam beneath a retaining wall, nearly closed by the roots of a tree growing somewhere overhead. From outside it looks like a place where the masonry has settled: three stones tilted together, a shallow recess full of old leaves. Behind the leaves, the ground falls away. There are six steps, worn concave and narrow enough that a person has to turn one shoulder toward the wall.

At the bottom is a garden smaller than a bedroom.

It lies only a little below the surrounding ground. The ceiling is the underside of a thick stone slab, crossed by roots, with a thin fissure along one edge. In daylight that fissure gives a faint indication of direction rather than useful light. A hand held beneath it becomes a darker shape against a less complete darkness. Most of the chamber remains invisible until the eyes have waited.

Then the plants begin to separate from one another.

There are broad leaves resting almost flat on the ground, overlapping like fallen plates. Between them stand delicate sprays of needle foliage, some no taller than a finger, others brushing the ceiling. A climbing plant has gone along the wall instead of upward, following the mortar seams. Its stems are thick and pale; its leaves turn their narrow edges toward the entrance. Moss occupies whatever the larger plants have left: the backs of stones, the stair corners, a rust-colored flake of metal half buried beside the threshold.

Nothing here flowers conspicuously. The few buds are hard, closed knots beneath the leaves.

The cold is immediate and remarkably even. Outside, weather changes. Here, the same cold rests in the stone, the soil, and the thin air between the stems. Touch a leaf and it feels nearly as cold as the wall. Some have a faint stiffness, though they bend without breaking. Frost forms on exposed mineral surfaces but seldom on the plants themselves. Their leaves have a waxy bloom that feels almost dry.

There is no spring and no stream. Most of the soil is merely cool, not wet. Moisture enters by small, widely separated events: rain finding the ceiling fissure, meltwater traveling down a root, condensation gathering where outside air touches the lowest step. A drop may hang for hours before falling. Below each reliable drip is a shallow hollow, lined with leaves or polished grit. These hollows are the wettest places in the garden, scarcely larger than a cupped hand.

The roots reach into them from every direction.

Elsewhere, the surface is a fine dark dust held together by living threads. An undisturbed patch can look desiccated until something presses it and a little moisture darkens the pressure mark. The garden wastes very little. Dead leaves do not accumulate in a loose heap. They soften where they fall, flatten, and are slowly incorporated into the layered ground. Beneath a fresh leaf may be its predecessor, still recognizable; beneath that, a brown membrane; beneath that, soil.

There are no insects working among these layers. No worms disturb them. Nothing scurries when a stone is lifted, and there are no webs across the entrance. The only inhabitants are rooted or spread thinly over a surface. Their decomposition is quieter than decay in an ordinary garden, without the busy intermediate stages one might expect.

The stonework is old enough to have ceased looking damaged. Water has rounded the lip of the bottom stair. The wall bulges slightly inward, but the bulge is stable, cushioned by roots and sediment. Pale mineral tracks descend from the ceiling and stop halfway down, where some earlier pattern of dripping ended. One corner holds a fan of gravel that has not moved in years. Its individual grains have grown dark on top and stayed pale underneath.

A few manufactured objects remain among the plants.

Near the middle of the chamber is a ceramic ring, flush with the ground, its opening filled with moss. It is no wider than a saucer. Three small rectangular tabs are set into the wall at different heights. Their surfaces are matte gray, without lettering, fasteners, or visible seams. One has been almost entirely enclosed by a stem. Another sits behind a curtain of roots.

The garden depends on these things, though nothing about them announces that dependence.

They were installed to carry a slow exchange across the chamber’s boundary: heat outward, usable energy inward. There are no lamps and no warm beds beneath the soil. The leaves absorb through their undersides, where fine structures in the veins couple to a field confined to the planted ground. A leaf lifted away from its bed remains alive, but stops growing. Put it back, and after several days its curled edge relaxes.

The system runs at a level too low to register as activity to someone standing here. Its parts do not hum. They have no indicators. A thin deposit on the uppermost tab changes color over the course of a winter, from ash gray to a gray with a trace of green. That is the nearest thing to a display.

Occasionally, a brief dry click comes from behind the wall. Then the silence resumes. Water falling into one of the hollows sounds disproportionately distinct: a single soft impact, followed by a faint settling in the leaves. Air moving through the entrance can make the needle foliage touch and release, producing a fine brushing sound. There is never enough wind to sway the whole garden.

Its growth is slow, but crowded. A new leaf must find space already occupied by another. Stems bend around neighbors, hold their positions for months, then lengthen by a few millimeters. Along the ceiling, roots have spread into a shallow net. Below them, the foliage fits into the irregular chamber so closely that the remaining air seems arranged in narrow passages of its own.

Near the last step, one patch of moss is thinner than the rest. Boots have brushed it, perhaps during inspections spaced many years apart. A small smooth stone marks the edge of that patch, placed where a foot might otherwise crush a creeping stem.

From there, the whole place can be reached by leaning and extending an arm. Yet much remains hidden: soil beneath the overlapping leaves, the backs of the wall stones, the ceramic ring’s buried depth. Standing still, one can gradually distinguish the damp mineral smell from the faint scent of green tissue, and hear a drop gathering long before it falls.

Above the slab, the weather passes. Below it, a pale shoot has reached the side of the smooth stone. Given time, it will bend around it.
