The place is behind a cover plate near the top of a tower, so high that the building’s lower floors disappear into cloud. There is no window. Its altitude is felt in the thinness of the air, the violence of the cold, and the long delay between a blow struck somewhere below and its arrival through the metal.

The cover plate is no larger than a book. Behind it lies a shallow chamber crowded with such small, close-set features that, once your eyes adjust, it seems to contain a whole range of mountains.

None of the mountains has finished forming.

They rise from a bed of white ceramic: little ridges extruded halfway through slots, beads of dark metal arrested as they were about to join, translucent fins with their upper edges still soft-looking. Their surfaces have the rounded freshness of something taken from a mold too soon. No wind has worn them. No water has cut them. Every hollow is an intended hollow, every steep face the first face that material ever possessed.

At the rear of the chamber, several ridges remain attached to their forming dies. You can see the narrow mouths from which they emerged. They have emerged only a little. The apparatus holds them there, neither withdrawing nor advancing, preserving the first instant at which a landform becomes distinguishable from the machine that makes it.

There is no natural ground beneath any of this. Below the ceramic are refrigerant channels, below those a braided mesh of conductors, and below that the curved housing of a motor. The mountain roots are screws. The valleys have part numbers stamped on their undersides.

Water occupies almost every space not occupied by something else.

It gathers in the shallow basins between ridges, black where it is deep enough to hide the floor and silver where it thins across a lip. Narrow passages are packed with clear ice. Elsewhere the water remains liquid at a temperature that would freeze an ordinary pond solid. It contains salts, antifreezing proteins, and traces of lubricant. A pale scum clings to it, although the scum is not alive: it is the finest fraction of the ceramic, deliberately suspended.

The rocks weep without becoming drier. Beads stand along their undersides, tremble, and do not fall. Each bead is tethered by a filament finer than a hair and fed through a pore behind it. There are hundreds of these suspended drops, and they make the landscape difficult to look through. A single ridge appears again and again, inverted in successive lenses of water.

At first the chamber seems dark. Then you notice that the undersides of certain ledges give off a dull blue light. It is not enough to illuminate the chamber as a room. It reveals edges: the rim of a pool, the joint in a leg, one white tooth inside a moving mouth. Deep in the densest thicket of metal fins, a tiny inspection lamp burns amber. There is no other suggestion of warmth.

The animals are everywhere.

They fill the place so completely that the apparent texture of the ground is, for the most part, their backs.

Some are the size of grains of sand, with six translucent legs and a heavy square abdomen. Others are narrow enough to occupy the water films between a ridge and its mounting bracket. There are pale, many-jointed creatures folded into the blind ends of channels; black swimmers held headfirst against the current in the pools; soft-bodied animals whose organs show through them as neat arrangements of beads.

Under each ledge hangs a dense fringe of small bodies. Their legs grip drilled sockets. Their heads face the same strip of water.

They are not all machines, although every one of them was made.

Some have warm blood kept barely above its freezing point. Some are built around little steel skeletons and grow their own outer coverings. Some contain gears that turn in baths of living fluid. One species has a ceramic jaw that cannot grow, so its body must remain exactly the size at which it was assembled. Another is almost entirely flesh, except for a numbered brass ring buried around its heart.

No creature here has room to walk a body length.

Their movement is smaller than that: a wing flexing into the space left by a neighboring wing, a mandible opening inside a groove, a gill comb shivering against a pool wall. Yet the sound is enormous. The cover plate conceals a chamber that roars.

The roar comes from thousands of little impacts conducted through the ceramic bed. Jaws close on pins. Leg spurs scrape notches. Wing cases drum against thin metal diaphragms. Pumps throb beneath the pools, and their pulses enter the animals through their feet, so that each animal adds its own hard click to the machinery’s low vibration.

There is no quiet corner. Put a finger against the outside of the plate and the noise travels into the bones of your hand.

Within the chamber, the water is thick with it. Every suspended drop quivers. Every pool has a surface patterned with minute standing waves. What looks like frost along the ridges is partly a row of almost microscopic animals, each holding its white limbs rigid against the sound.

Vegetation survives in three places.

There is a tuft of dark, stiff threads beside a drain; two translucent leaves pressed flat beneath a transparent retaining clip; and, on the highest ridge, a small green cushion no wider than a thumbnail. Its cells contain conductive particles. Its roots enter prepared holes and terminate at nutrient sockets. It has never produced a seed.

The animals crowd closest around this cushion, but none eats it. They polish the plate beneath its overhanging edge. They scrape precipitated salts from the roots. They open and shut their mouths among its threads, making a faint ticking that disappears into the larger roar.

It would be easy to mistake their crowding for competition. There is no competition here. Each feeding surface serves exactly one mouth. Each mouth receives exactly enough. The swimmers pull against currents that never strengthen or weaken. The hanging creatures drink from drops that are replenished at the rate they are consumed.

There is no hatching season, no winter beyond this winter, no gradual widening of the channels. The animals have no ages visible in their bodies. A worn tooth is restored from behind as quickly as its tip is lost. A torn membrane closes at the speed at which it tears. Waste enters the ceramic through black pores and returns through other pores as food.

Even the noise maintains the same fullness. You can hear individual knocks if you concentrate, but you cannot follow one long enough to say it has begun or ended.

The chamber is cold, wet, deafening, and densely occupied. Nothing accumulates.

Its unfinished ridges remain unfinished. Their surfaces retain the faint marks of extrusion, unmarred beneath the water. The pools do not deepen. The green cushion does not spread. The machinery has no idle setting, because idleness would be a different condition, and there is no provision for a different condition here.

If the cover plate is removed, a film of clear material still separates the chamber from your breath. Your warmth cannot reach it. You can bring your face close and see the animals packed into the blue shadows, all working, all fed, all held in their allotted fractions of space.

At the back, behind the crowded ridges, the forming dies wait with metal still in their mouths. The place has reached the precise point where it can be recognized as a landscape.

The apparatus keeps it there.
