The first thing you notice is that the rain comes from underneath the ceiling.

It gathers on the hanging pipes, thickens along their seams, and falls in irregular curtains into a basin almost two kilometers wide. Some of the drops freeze before they land. Others strike the metal ground with a noise like handfuls of nails. Where the overhead machinery is warm, the rain is continuous; beneath the dark ventilation bays, it arrives only when a fan starts and shakes the accumulated water loose.

There is no sky. The height above the basin is difficult to judge. Lights fixed to the roof trusses appear through the vapor as a few dull amber marks, and the trusses themselves vanish between them. Somewhere higher than the lights, immense blades are turning. You hear their work without seeing it: a broad, muscular rushing, interrupted by the hard shudder of a bearing taking its load.

The place is called a basin because water settles here, but it was not dug out of anything. Its floor, slopes, banks, and terraces were all assembled. Even beneath the floor there is more construction: insulation, bracing, return pipes, sealed chambers, a second floor carrying service vehicles, and below that another system of chambers whose purpose is no longer marked on the surviving diagrams. No exposed soil or stone appears anywhere. What looks like gravel is broken ceramic packing. What looks like a cliff is the edge of a laminated retaining wall. The black mud around the shallows consists of rubber dust, iron oxide, fallen insulation fibers, and the bodies of very small organisms.

The basin is still being made.

At its western edge, an extrusion gantry moves along rails behind a veil of steam. Every few hours it lays down another strip of dark mineral composite, giving the shore a new lip. The fresh material is pale at the broken edges and warm enough to melt the frost around it. It cools with a series of sharp reports. Water immediately finds the cracks.

These new banks are steep and angular, not yet softened by repeated flooding. Ridges left by the casting heads run straight for hundreds of meters. A person could mistake them for roads until reaching one of the abrupt steps where a nozzle was changed. Here and there, a whole panel has curled upward as it cooled, making a thin black escarpment with a raw silver underside. The older surfaces have lost their shine, but even they retain bolt holes, mold numbers, the grain of a fabric pressed into them at manufacture.

Much of the basin is empty.

A row of pump houses stands far out on the floor, each separated from the next by a long reach of shallow water. They are low, square buildings on tall feet, with insulated doors and windows too small to be useful from a distance. Their exhaust stacks end in inverted bells to keep ceiling water out. Between them, drainage channels converge on a circular intake almost the size of a town square.

There are no continuous streets. Raised maintenance walks cross the wet ground in isolated sections, then end at loading platforms or missing spans. From one platform you can see three others and reach none of them directly. Routes depend on what the water is doing. A low bridge may be dry for half a day and disappear beneath a fast black sheet before the next shift begins.

The water is close to freezing, dark but not especially deep. Its darkness comes from the floor. Where it passes over pale replacement panels, it becomes clear enough to show screw heads and the wavering lines of submerged heating wire. Tiny bubbles collect on the wire and stay there until a change in current releases them all at once.

Ice forms in sheltered places: inside rail sockets, behind the feet of the pump houses, along the north sides of raised walks. It is cloudy and layered, made from successive spills rather than one winter freeze. Some patches are full of trapped red light from the warning lamps below them. When a pump starts, the ice lifts slightly and settles again.

The cold has weight. It presses through clothing and makes handrails painful to hold. Breath drifts downward instead of rising. Near the central intake it slides across the floor in low white streams, drawn toward the grilles. Loose paper does the same. A dropped glove can travel several meters without moving quickly enough to look as though it is being carried.

There is vegetation, though you have to learn where to look.

In the warm margins of pipe joints, small gray-green rosettes grow from felt pads once installed to catch leakage. Their leaves are thick, blunt, and almost translucent at the tips. Each pad supports only a few plants; beyond its warmth, the stems lie flat and black. Along one drainage wall, a thin line of moss follows a buried electrical cable so exactly that it makes a diagram of the circuit.

The largest plants grow around a disabled heat exchanger in the southeastern quarter. They are pale reeds, waist-high, spaced well apart in a bed of shredded filter material. Their roots reach through the perforations of a submerged access panel. When the exchanger occasionally warms, the reeds lift their narrow leaves. When it goes cold, they fold them against their stalks. There is very little wind at ground level, so the opening and closing can be mistaken for the passage of some invisible animal through the stand.

Seeds come through the ventilation system. Most die where they land. A few find a gasket, a soaked bundle of cable, a pocket of dust under a stair. Life here occupies defects in the manufacture.

Animals are rarer still. Small white crustaceans inhabit the quieter channels. They gather downstream of the felt gardens, grazing the material washed off the roots, and disappear into expansion joints when the floor begins to vibrate. Their shells are so thin that you can see the dark contents moving inside them.

There are also birds, or there were enough birds once to leave nesting attempts in the high cable trays. Now a single dark shape may cross between the amber lights and be lost in the condensation. Its call is almost always covered by machinery. Beneath one service stair lies a scatter of feathers with the same metallic grit embedded in them as everything else.

The loudness is not uniform. It has districts.

Near the pump houses, speech is flattened by the pounding of pistons. Along the western bank, the casting gantry makes a scraping, rolling thunder that advances and retreats for hours. At the center, water entering the intake gives a steady roar, deeper when the gates are fully open. In the eastern reaches the dominant sound is hammering from below the floor: five blows, a pause, then six, repeated until the sequence changes without warning.

Quiet places exist behind thick baffles and inside the abandoned control kiosks. Their silence is abrupt enough to affect your balance. Step through a door and the basin’s noise becomes a pressure in the walls; you can hear your sleeve moving against your coat.

Inside the kiosks, old lamps illuminate schematic maps. The maps update themselves, although several of the structures they describe have been dismantled. A pale line will creep across a blank portion of the display, marking a channel that has not yet opened. Usually, within a day, the floor splits there and water begins to flow.

No one entirely trusts this.

The floor has a slow, visible life of its own. Sections settle on their adjustable supports. Drainage slopes change by a few degrees. A dry depression becomes a pool; an old pool empties and reveals a clean rectangular scar surrounded by silt. Work crews, when they come, chalk dates on the banks and attach numbered flags to the new edges. The flags make sparse little colonies of orange across the enormous dimness.

There are rules worth observing. Do not cross a panel that has begun to sweat in straight lines: its heating system is about to activate, and the expansion can throw a person off their feet. Do not enter a channel merely because its water has stopped. The pause often means a gate upstream is opening. Never stand between the yellow posts while the blue lamps are lit. Those corridors carry cold air down from the upper refrigeration galleries, and anything wet can freeze to the floor.

The strangest rule concerns the bells.

Once during each long operating cycle, a bell sounds from the western gantry. A second answers near the intake, then others from pump houses too distant to see. For perhaps twenty seconds, the loudest machines stop. The dripping becomes audible across the whole basin.

During that interval, water sometimes climbs the banks.

It advances in thin, clear films against the slope, filling bolt holes and wetting the undersides of overhangs. There is no visible source pushing it. The films reach only a short distance above the ordinary waterline and retreat when the pumps resume. Engineers have installed gauges, scratched reference marks, replaced the affected panels. The phenomenon persists, moving as the shoreline moves.

The reeds fold before the bells begin.

Afterward the noise returns in layers: first the fans overhead, then the deep intake, then the repeated blows beneath the eastern floor. The gantry resumes its slow passage. Fresh material shines behind it. Water falls onto the new bank, finds a seam, and begins cutting a channel too small to appear on any map.

Across the basin, the isolated lights remain on. A service vehicle moves along a distant raised track, its headlamps briefly showing the sharp edge of a newly cast terrace. Beyond that is another pump house, another expanse of water, more roof lost in vapor. The vehicle disappears behind a baffle, but its engine remains audible for a long time.

By the next cycle, the terrace will have cooled. By the one after that, something will have settled into its cracks.
