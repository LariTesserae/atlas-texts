The first impression is of snow, though almost none of it is snow.

A white plain stretches beneath a white ceiling, with so much distance between them that the ceiling acquires weather. Long pale bands drift across it. Farther away, whole regions are veiled in a fine powder that looks like mist until it settles on your sleeve: dry pollen, angular as salt.

Everything solid is alive.

The ground is a continuous thickness of plant tissue, hard enough to ring faintly beneath a wooden staff. Its upper skin is waxy, bone-colored, and drawn into low ridges. Where a ridge has recently opened, the edges are sharp and translucent. Underneath shows a green so dark it is almost black. The fissures have not yet learned to crumble. There are no rounded stones, no comfortable banks of old soil. A broken edge is a wound, and a wound is already deciding what shape to grow next.

This country lies beneath the surface of a much larger living body.

No one who travels here has seen its whole extent. The inhabited hollows are connected by openings that can take weeks to cross, and beyond them are further chambers, some broad enough to contain their own pressure systems. People speak of the roof as though it were the sky, and of the deeper tissue underfoot as though it were earth. Both usages are practical. The actual outer surface is a matter for stories, and for the occasional narrow shaft that rises out of sight.

The light comes down those shafts, but not only down them.

It travels through the body.

Bundled fibers collect daylight somewhere far above and conduct it into the cavities. They spread through the roof in branching fans, then terminate in broad, thin membranes whose undersides shine. At midday the brightness is almost painful. Every distant ridge is visible. There is no accommodating twilight under an overhang; even the fractured tissue inside a crack carries a little light.

At night, the membranes continue releasing what they have stored. Their color changes from white to the faint yellow of a cut pear. Travelers sleep under woven black covers. Without one, you can close your eyes and still see the veins in your eyelids.

The brightness offers no warmth.

Cold comes from the enormous floor, from the roof, from every direction. A hand laid on the living ground begins to ache within a minute. The air smells faintly of split stems and, near fresh growth, of green apples stored in an unheated room. Breath shows only occasionally. Most of the time there is too little moisture in the air.

It is a country of abundant flesh and very little available water.

The plant keeps its water behind several skins, in narrow vessels that seal quickly when cut. A walking knife cannot reach most of it. Cutting carelessly gives you a mouthful of bitter fluid and leaves a patch of tissue that may harden into a blade overnight. Useful water gathers at certain seams, where warm sap passes close to the cold inner surface. There, condensation beads on the ceiling and falls with great deliberation.

A drinking place might consist of six hanging points above a shallow cup in the floor.

Around such places the white surface turns gray-green. Small soft growths gather, pressed flat against the cold. Their leaves are no larger than fingernails, but they overlap so completely that there is no exposed ground between them. Some are independent plants, carried here by wind or travelers. Others are little organs of the great body, developed to reclaim what its larger vessels have lost.

For many miles elsewhere, there is nothing to interrupt the plain.

This is the strangeness of its abundance: the vegetation makes the space rather than filling it. No forest stands between you and the horizon. The living mass is floor, roof, distant wall. In the immense volume they enclose, a person is a dark fleck. A settlement may be invisible until you are nearly upon it, though its smoke has been traveling across the chamber for days.

The small buildings are usually anchored beside old seams. Their walls are woven from peeled fiber and stiffened with sap that sets like horn. Roofs are low and steep, not for rain but for falling flakes of ceiling-skin. Wooden pegs are driven only into layers known to be dead. A peg driven into healthy tissue will be loosened, engulfed, or carried away as the surface renews itself.

Nothing is built to last unchanged.

Doorways can be detached. Hearths sit in shallow trays of fired clay, precious because clay must be brought from the few places where an ancient mineral layer protrudes into the hollow. Beds are sledges. Even the communal water trough rests on runners.

A village can move before breakfast.

This is necessary because the landscape is seldom still, though it is mostly quiet.

Growth occurs in fronts. A smooth stretch of floor may rise over several hours into a ridge taller than a house. Two neighboring sheets of tissue may pull apart, opening a bright green crevasse where there had been a footpath. A depression can deepen while you stand at its edge. Far across a chamber, a whole pale shelf may tilt, slowly at first, then fold down with the grave ease of a closing leaf.

There is little crashing. The material is fibrous and moist within; it bends, splits, and slides. The sounds are small for the distances involved: a long leather-like creak, a ticking as surface wax fractures, a soft rush from displaced air. Sometimes the only warning is the movement of loose pollen along the ground.

The great changes often happen beyond hearing.

You look up from fastening a pack and find that the horizon has acquired another level. A landmark that stood apart from the roof now touches it. Yesterday’s pass has become a translucent wall.

The inhabitants watch shadows rather than listen for danger. They set thin sticks upright along the boundaries of camp. If the sticks lean differently from one another, the ground is beginning to shear. They hang threads from the rafters. A thread that swings steadily toward one wall means air is being drawn into a new opening.

Maps are made on strips of peel. They show water seams, reliable thick tissue, and places where people have survived a night. Each bears a date. Directions are often accompanied by a description of the season’s growth: *Take the seam that smells sweet; cross the ridge only if its west face is dull.*

There are seasons here, but they do not arrive everywhere together.

One region thickens while another hollows. Somewhere beyond the visible distances, new chambers are being opened inside solid tissue. Elsewhere old ones are pinched shut, their air pushed into the neighboring spaces. This produces the winds: sudden, cold, laden with pollen, sometimes strong enough to make a traveler lie flat and hook a staff through a surface loop.

A storm may last ten minutes or three days.

During it, the shining ceiling becomes indistinct. Dry scales race along the floor. Loose fibers catch against the ridges and stream out horizontally, each one rattling faintly. The air itself can remain almost soundless, with no trees or stone corners to give it a voice. In a shelter, you hear your sleeves moving, the roof pegs taking strain, and someone counting between inspections of the guy ropes.

Afterward, everything is exquisitely clear. The storm has polished the waxy plains. Newly exposed seams shine. The sparse footprints left before the wind are gone, but fresh tears and folds run across the country.

Animals are uncommon and easy to mistake for debris.

There is a pale, six-legged browser that grazes the tender lips of fissures. It moves a few steps, waits, and moves again, keeping its belly close to the warmer green tissue. Its droppings mark places where a seam has remained stable long enough to become useful. People value the animal less for meat than for this information.

Near the condensation pools live narrow black worms, each with a silver line down its back. They retreat into the living floor when touched. On rare occasions a winged creature crosses the brightness overhead, so high that its shadow arrives more distinctly than its body. It may be following a warm current toward an opening no one below can see.

There are also long periods when nothing moves in the visible world except the world itself.

Walkers learn to stop without expecting an encounter. They sit on insulating mats, eat hard cakes of dried sap and ground seed, and look across the enormous hollow. Under the light, distant structures have the clarity of objects on a table, though some are a week away. A low shelter, abandoned beside a drained seam, can occupy the eye for an entire afternoon.

Beside it, perhaps, is a dark line scratched into the pale floor: a record of heights, made year after year while that patch remained steady.

The line ends where new tissue has lifted through it.

A few of the old marks survive on either side, separated now by a clean white ridge. The ridge is cold, sharp, and still lengthening. In its translucent edge, light has already begun to travel.
