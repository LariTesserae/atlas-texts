The city occupies the shallowest part of a basin. There are no hills close enough to see, only a long, nearly level descent toward the streets, where hot water stands ankle-deep over black stone. Beyond the last buildings, the water spreads into flats. Beyond the flats, the darkness continues without a visible horizon.

It is difficult to tell when you have entered the city. At first there are only isolated walls, widely spaced, their lower edges softened by centuries of immersion. Then the walls acquire corners. A strip of paving passes beneath the water. Farther on, you find yourself in a broad avenue, with buildings set back on either side and an open channel running down its middle. The avenue is too wide for the number of buildings it serves. All the streets are like this: generous arrangements of emptiness, with occasional structures to establish their direction.

The heat is constant, though it has local variations. Near the channels it rises through the soles of your feet. Inside certain doorways it presses against the face like breath from an oven. The water itself is hot enough that you would not want to sit in it. Where it flows over a sill or descends a few worn steps, steam gathers and remains there, sometimes keeping the shape of the steps it has covered.

Nothing shines. There are no lamps, no stars, no pale sky reflected in the flooded squares. Yet after a while the buildings become perceptible. You see their weight and arrangement rather than their surfaces. A wall is a deeper resistance in the dark; an arch is a place where that resistance lifts. At a distance, this gives the city the appearance of something remembered with considerable accuracy by someone who has forgotten what light was.

Up close, the stone is porous and faintly warm. Its colors, when you somehow know them, are brown-black, dark green, and the dull gray of cooled ash. Water beads on it continuously. Some walls sweat more than others. Along their bases, the runoff has deposited thick shelves of mineral, rounded and polished, spreading into the streets like roots.

These deposits are also the way the city grows.

There are no tools, no machines, no heaps of cut stone. There are no unfinished buildings in the usual sense. In certain courtyards, however, water drips upward from the paving into the underside of a lintel. Each drop leaves a small part of itself behind. Over years, the lintel thickens. Supports gather beneath its ends. A sheltered space forms, and once it is deep enough to hold a distinct darkness, it becomes a room.

Elsewhere, rooms are dissolving. Their walls leak inward until the stone loses its substance and the roof settles into the water without a splash. It can take a very long time. A building may spend generations as a shallow depression, retaining its doorways and interior divisions only as changes in temperature underfoot.

The city has reached an old accommodation with these processes. Its avenues persist. Its squares remain open. When a building disappears, another begins somewhere near it, although never on exactly the same ground. The movement is slow enough that the place feels settled, and extensive enough that nothing is quite still.

You can hear it if you stop.

A drop falls inside a distant chamber. Water draws softly across a stone lip. Somewhere below the street, a large enclosed volume fills, and the pavement gives a single low creak. Then there is a silence so complete that the warmth seems to have a pressure of its own.

No animal interrupts it. There are no insects above the pools, no small movements in cracks, no birds roosting beneath the eaves. Nothing swims in the open channels. At times a ripple travels against the current, but it reaches the bank and climbs several inches up the masonry before flattening there. It has the same patient motion as the rest of the water.

Vegetation is rare. A few reedlike stems rise from joints in the paving, separated from one another by whole streets. They are thick, soft, and almost leafless, with a waxy surface that the heat never dries. Each supports a dark cup at its tip. Condensation gathers in the cup until its weight bends the stem; the contents spill, and the plant straightens over the course of several hours.

Some have grown through thresholds. Their stems are pressed flat where doors ought to be, though no doors exist. In the outer flats, low mats of vegetation cling to slightly raised banks. Their edges advance after periods of flooding and withdraw when the water becomes too mineral-rich. Left behind are delicate ridges, barely higher than a fingernail, marking the extent of old growth.

Rain comes without a change in the sky.

You first notice that the open spaces have become warmer than the interiors. Then water begins striking the flooded pavement: widely separated drops, each producing a shallow hollow that remains open longer than it should. The rain increases, but rarely becomes loud. Sometimes it falls so slowly that individual drops hang between roof height and street level. Walking through them leaves warm spots on your face and shoulders.

During these rains, the city’s drainage runs in several directions. Water flows down the central channels toward the basin’s lowest squares. At the same time, narrow streams ascend stairways and enter upper rooms. The stairs that carry water upward are smoother than those that carry it down. Their edges have worn into long curves, and their hand-high sidewalls lean gently toward the flow.

The upper rooms are mostly empty. A few contain stone basins. Others have a raised edge around the floor and a small opening at the level of the ceiling. Once filled, they hold their water even where the exterior wall has worn away. You can stand in the avenue and look up at a dark vertical surface of water suspended three stories above the street. It trembles in the heat. Occasionally a little of it escapes, runs along the outside of the building, and joins the flood below.

There are rules here, though they reveal themselves through repetition rather than explanation.

Water will not cross a threshold while a room is being formed. It accumulates outside, sometimes to a considerable depth, until the room has acquired all its corners. A channel passing beneath an arch takes longer to emerge than its length can account for. A courtyard will always have one dry patch, but that patch may move from the paving to a wall.

Distance is more reliable outdoors. From one square you can see, or sense, the next: a broad opening past a colonnade, a low tower set apart from the buildings, a succession of roofs diminishing into heat and darkness. Indoors, a small structure can contain several long passages. The passages have no decorations and no particular destination. Most eventually return you to a doorway overlooking another part of the same square. You recognize it by the mineral shelves, the isolated plants, the shape of the water against the paving. Sometimes the building you entered is visible across the way.

The largest square lies near the center of the basin. It is almost entirely flooded, with a few raised causeways converging on an empty platform. Around it stand widely spaced structures of different ages: squat halls with deeply eroded bases, slender houses whose walls still show the rippled texture of their formation, and one immense facade with nothing behind it.

Water flows through the facade’s windows. On one side it falls from them into the square; on the other it rises out of the square to meet them. The two movements occupy the same openings without disturbing each other.

Here the heat is greatest. Steam passes low over the platform and collects beneath the causeways. The stone has been worn smooth by water approaching from every direction. There are no marks of feet, no inscriptions, no objects abandoned in the corners.

Still, the place has the proportions of a civic center. The avenues arrive with ceremony. The surrounding halls face inward. Even the empty facade seems to have been retained for the importance of its position.

After a long interval, the water in the square lowers. Steps emerge along its perimeter, then another tier below them. Channels empty with faint sucking sounds. Across the basin, sealed rooms begin releasing their accumulated heat, and thin pillars of condensation rise over the roofs.

For a little while, more of the city is exposed: lower arcades, deep stone gutters, the tops of structures that had seemed to be paving. The avenues continue downward through them, broad and carefully spaced.

Then the water returns. It comes first through the walls, gathering in beads that lengthen and join. It rises from the uncovered gutters. The lowest steps disappear. Far out on the flats, a single plant bends under the weight of its filled cup.

The city remains warm and dark, undergoing its innumerable small alterations. In one courtyard a lintel gains another layer. In another, a corner softens enough to cease being a corner. The wide streets hold their course through the water, with ample room on either side.
