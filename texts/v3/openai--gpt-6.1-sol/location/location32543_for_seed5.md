The entrance is a loose stone at ankle height, deep in the passage between two houses. It looks like every other stone in the wall: pale, rounded at the corners, stained green underneath. Lift it, and a cold brightness falls across your fingers. Beyond it, six narrow steps descend into a courtyard scarcely larger than a room.

The city has been built around this space, then over it, without quite filling it in. Foundations meet along its edges. The underside of a stair forms part of its ceiling. A blocked doorway, a chimney base, and the back of somebody’s bread oven occupy the upper walls, though no warmth comes through the oven stones. Higher still is a narrow opening to the sky.

Everything below that opening is green.

Not the tall, expansive green of a forest, but a close, crowded growth fitted to the dimensions of masonry. Ferns occupy the joints between bricks. Moss has made cushions over the thresholds of doors that no longer open. Small evergreen leaves overlap like roof tiles, glossy on top and silver beneath. Their branches are short and thick, bent around corners they have been touching for a very long time.

There is no bare earth. Where something can take root, something has. Where it cannot, a pot has been set.

The pots are handmade, with uneven rims and thumbmarks still visible beneath the glaze. Some are as small as drinking bowls. Others are broad troughs assembled from slate, held together by wooden pegs swollen tight in their holes. They sit on shelves, on steps, in the mouths of old drains. One hangs from a forked branch by three plaited cords. Its bottom has broken away, but the roots hold the soil in place, and a fringe of fine white rootlets hangs beneath it like a beard.

The courtyard is cold enough that breath remains visible for a moment. Touching the iron ring on the entrance stone hurts. Yet almost nothing is frozen solid. The damp stays in small places: behind a leaf, inside a rolled frond, along the lower lip of a shelf. Most of the paving is dry.

A person could kneel here and find a dozen distinct kinds of wetness without finding enough water to fill a cup.

There is a dark bead where a ceramic pipe emerges from the wall. There is a translucent seam of ice beneath the lowest stair. There is water held in the hollow of a stone worn smooth by generations of bowls being set upon it. Between two flagstones, a ribbon of black soil remains damp from end to end, supporting a dense row of leaves no wider than fingernails.

Once, the courtyard must have received more water. Its gutters are elaborate. They run around doorframes and beneath shelves, turn through clay elbows, divide into little branching channels cut directly into the paving. Now only the deepest parts are used. Moisture gathers there quietly, distributed in such small quantities that the plants seem less watered than individually supplied.

The light is unexpectedly strong.

It comes through the slit overhead, strikes a whitewashed wall, and spreads throughout the court. Pale stones pass it onward. The underside of the stair has been lined with shallow, curved shells, each polished on the inside. Broken pieces of glazed pottery are mortared into the corners. Nothing shines sharply; the whole room is bright, including places that ought to be in shadow.

You can see the underside of every leaf. You can see the fine blue veins in the white lichen growing on the pots.

There is no visible sun. The opening above holds a clear, almost colorless sky. If a cloud passes across it, the courtyard does not noticeably dim. The shells retain their pale glow, and the white wall remains luminous. Someone has arranged this, patiently and with simple materials, though it is difficult to tell where careful workmanship ends and a property of the place begins.

Under that brightness, the old construction becomes intricate.

A brick is rarely just a brick. One has a narrow shelf chipped into its face. Another bears a row of holes for pegs, most now occupied by roots. The edges of the steps are scalloped from use. The grooves in the paving have been deepened, filled, recut, and deepened again. Repairs are visible everywhere, but no repair looks recent. Even the replacement stones have acquired the same rounded surfaces as the things they replaced.

Small wooden bridges cross the drainage channels. They are only a handspan long, made of twigs flattened on one side and lashed with plant fiber. Their purpose becomes clear when you look beneath the greenery: the courtyard has paths within its paths.

Snails use them.

Their shells are pale gray, with a dark band so fine it looks drawn in ink. Several sit along a bridge rail, bodies withdrawn, openings sealed with a thin cloudy skin. More occupy sheltered ledges. One is fixed beneath the lip of a glazed basin, directly above the waterline.

Other animals are harder to distinguish from the materials around them. A moth lies flat against a patch of white lichen, its wings shaped like two overlapping flakes. Under a fern are three beetles with the dull sheen of wet seeds. A tiny bird sits inside the cavity of the blocked chimney, almost entirely concealed by a curtain of roots. Only its breast and one bright eye are visible.

Nothing startles when you enter.

The bird watches. The beetles remain beneath their leaf. Somewhere near the entrance, a whiskered face appears between two stones and then withdraws, without a scrape or rustle.

The silence is unusually complete. The city is close enough that its buildings form every wall, but no cart wheels or voices reach this depth. Moss fills the cracks where sound might enter. Thick leaves press against hollow pottery. Roots have packed the abandoned pipes. Even your movements seem to lose their smaller noises here: a sleeve brushing stone, the sole of a boot shifting on grit.

There are seats, though there is scarcely space to sit.

One is a slab supported on two short pillars, its rear edge disappearing into a stand of ferns. Another is a wooden stool recessed into a wall niche. Its legs have been shortened unevenly to fit the paving. On the stool rests a pair of pruning shears, forged from simple iron, with handles wrapped in faded cord.

A clay cup stands beside them.

Inside the cup is a single dry leaf, curled neatly against the bottom. No dust has settled on it.

At first the courtyard seems abandoned. Then it seems maintained. Neither impression quite holds. There are no weeds crowding out weaker plants, but there are no freshly cut stems either. A broken trellis supports precisely the growth its remaining pieces can carry. Roots have lifted a flagstone just high enough to provide a dry chamber beneath it. The chamber contains a nest of finely shredded bark.

Every failure has found an occupant.

Stay long enough, and the packed greenery resolves into separate lives. Small red berries hang low in one corner. A pale flower faces the white wall rather than the sky. Along the underside of a shelf, young fern fronds stand folded among old ones, all of them the same cold, clear green. Nothing seems to be advancing toward anything else. The flower is fully open. The berry skins are unwrinkled. The old wood is soft at its edges but firm where it bears weight.

The bead at the mouth of the pipe trembles.

It does not fall while you watch.

Above, beyond the narrow strip of sky, people may be replacing roofs, lighting ovens, carrying water through the streets. Down here, the shears lie ready beside the stool. The little bridges remain sound. The bird keeps its eye on the entrance, and the bright cold rests evenly on leaves, shells, stone, and the few inches of space left between them.
