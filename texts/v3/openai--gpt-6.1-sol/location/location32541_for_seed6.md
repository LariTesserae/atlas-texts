The water begins at the threshold and goes all the way to the roof.

There is no shore, only a narrow vestibule where a person can stand waist-deep while the inner door equalizes. Beyond it, the floor falls away in three sharp terraces. From the doorway you can see almost the whole place: a submerged garden about the size of a small cathedral, enclosed by pale walls, with a vaulted ceiling obscured by roots. The water is warm enough that entering it feels less like immersion than the gradual disappearance of the boundary around your skin.

This is a garden on the summit of a tower.

Far below, outside its walls, clouds pass across the tower’s supporting trusses. The actual ground is several kilometers down. Nothing in the garden has ever touched it. Its ridges are molded, its soil extruded, its water delivered through a hollow spine that runs up the center of the building. Even the roughness of the stones was specified before they were cast.

Near the entrance, the newest terrace is still white.

It has a steep, folded surface, like a mountain range reduced to the length of a dining table. The edges are painfully definite. Thin sheets of mineral have begun to grow over the manufacturing seams, but have not yet softened them. Where a casting was joined badly, a line of tiny green filaments occupies the crack. Elsewhere the plants obey the channels cut for them, climbing straight for a handspan before branching into the water.

There is very little open space.

Broad submerged leaves overlap above the terraces. Transparent ribbons descend from the ceiling and attach themselves to bronze rails. Between them grow mats of small, fleshy cups, each holding a bubble of pale gas. Roots fill the gaps beneath the stairs. A thick velvet of algae covers the doorframe on its inner side, stopping precisely at the rubber seal.

Some plants have been trained over frames so long that the distinction between frame and stem has become difficult to see. Others grow from replaceable cartridges. Their roots pass through mesh into pipes carrying a dark nutrient solution, and the pipes disappear into the walls. Once each season, a cartridge is withdrawn and another advances to occupy its place. The movement takes most of a day. Leaves bend against neighboring leaves, find a little room, and settle.

The garden is crowded, but nothing struggles visibly.

There are no fish slipping between the stems, no insects at the surface, no grazing mouths clearing patches in the green. An unbroken fringe of delicate growth surrounds every nozzle. Dead leaves remain where they fall until their fibers loosen; then the water draws them, almost imperceptibly, toward the filtration slots. Without animals to interrupt them, the plants have acquired extravagant margins: long translucent combs, lace finer than cloth, trailing structures that would not survive a single bite.

In places, these margins have grown together.

You can float beside a screen of leaves and see daylight through hundreds of small green chambers. Each chamber contains its own pocket of water. Their membranes flex with the pressure cycle, so slowly that the motion becomes apparent only when a bright point behind them disappears.

The daylight comes through the outer shell.

From inside, the walls resemble milky stone, but they are laminated glass packed with vertical channels. Water circulates through the channels before entering the garden, shedding its excess heat. Beyond them lies a white sky and, occasionally, the gray underside of a cloud. Sunlight reaches the plants from the sides as well as from above. At midday the room is plainly visible from end to end: green on green, white mineral shelves, copper fittings dulled to brown.

The depth gives that ordinary light an unfamiliar weight. Shadows do not lie on the floor. They hang between leaves.

At the center stands the tower’s hollow spine, a broad column enclosed in a sleeve of ribbed ceramic. Its pumping machinery is far beneath the garden, and flexible joints interrupt the vibration before it reaches this height. There is no continuous engine noise. If you rest your forehead against the ceramic, you can hear a faint, steady passage within it, like water moving behind the wall of a neighboring room.

Otherwise, the loudest sound is your own breathing.

A valve occasionally releases a few beads of gas. They rise only a short distance before entering the cups of the ceiling plants. There is no exposed surface under the vault. The uppermost water meets a dense canopy of roots, buoyant leaves, and soft white bladders pressed against the roof. These hold the garden’s breathable reserve. An old maintenance diagram calls it the attic.

The bladders are tethered to a lattice of steel wire. Their shadows make the ceiling look quilted.

Along the far wall, several shallow steps lead up to a service niche. They seem pointless underwater, until you approach and feel the change in resistance. The water here is thicker, enriched by something exuded from the roots. You can brace a foot against a step and remain upright without gripping anything. The niche contains a wheel, a pressure gauge, and a rack of long-handled pruning tools. Their handles are coated in soft green growth except where hands have recently held them.

Beside the gauge is a square patch with no vegetation at all.

Its surface is dark and glossy, warm against the palm. This is where the garden makes its next piece of terrain.

The material emerges a fraction of a millimeter at a time, supplied from the tower below. Over months, the patch will become a ledge. Grooves will form along it; nutrient ports will open; roots waiting in the wall will extend into them. For now, it is scarcely raised above the surrounding panel. A maintenance mark provides the only evidence of change: a white line painted level with the surface, now slightly submerged in the new material.

The oldest parts of the room are only seven years old. They are already almost invisible beneath the plants.

What first looks like a luxuriant interior gradually reveals itself as something still being assembled. The ceiling has unused attachment points. Beneath the lowest terrace, folded frames wait in a recess. A cable runs into a closed panel that will eventually become another window. The garden has no spare volume, so each addition requires a slow accommodation: a leaf redirected, a mat lifted, a root persuaded to follow a warmer pipe.

Nothing is hurried. The tower can bear more weight than the garden will acquire in a century.

Near evening, the clouds below rise around the walls. The outer world vanishes, and the remaining light comes through evenly, without a visible direction. The sharp white terrace becomes the brightest thing in the room. Against it, you can watch a single tendril extend from its channel, its tip pale and tightly folded.

It reaches into water already occupied by leaves, roots, rails, and suspended threads of algae. There is no obvious space for it.

By morning, it will have found some.
