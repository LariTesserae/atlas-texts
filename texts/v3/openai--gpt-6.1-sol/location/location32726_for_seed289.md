The place fits beneath a stone you could lift with both hands, though lifting it would not uncover this place. You would find pale soil, a few root hairs, perhaps the white curve of a grub. The hollow lies a little farther underneath than distance allows.

Inside, it is bright enough to see the grain of every surface.

The light has no point of entry. It is held in the mineral matter: in the blunt tips of crystals, in the chalk-white backs of small animals, in the thin lime crust on the ceiling. Nothing glows conspicuously. Instead, everything is visible with the clarity of an object taken outdoors on a cold morning. Even the undersides of things are lit. The few dark marks are substances—a seam of graphite, the black mouthparts of a beetle—not shadows.

At first the hollow seems to have no floor. There is too much in it.

Pale, soft-bodied creatures lie shoulder to shoulder across the lowest part, fitted between grains of unfinished stone. Their bodies are no longer than a thumbnail, but each has a distinct shape: a broad segmented back, a narrow waist, six translucent legs tucked beneath a shell. There are flat snails with almost transparent spirals, eyeless springtails, ivory mites crowded into the seams. A centipede occupies the bend between two walls, every pair of legs resting on a separate ledge. Under its belly, smaller animals fill the space it cannot touch.

Looking more closely, you can make out the terrain through this occupancy. The floor consists of low mineral ridges, soft-looking but hard, with deep notches between them. There is no rubble. Each ridge joins directly to the material below, as if the substance has only begun to discover the shapes it can hold.

On one side, stone is becoming stone.

Here the wall has a waxy texture. Blunt facets press out of it, their corners not yet sharp, their faces still joined by little webs of undifferentiated matter. Farther along, the same material has acquired definite edges. A crystal the size of a tooth sits with half its outline complete and half still merged into the wall.

None of it develops further. The unfinished parts are as permanent as the finished ones.

The hollow is cool. Its cold is close and intimate, the cold of putting a fingertip against the inside of an earthen jar. Most surfaces are dry, with a faint powdery bloom. Moisture occurs in exact, isolated places: a bead under one mineral lip, a shallow lens in the depression behind a snail, a wet line following three turns of a root.

There is no connection between these waters. They neither gather nor disappear. A drop hangs from the ceiling with the lower end drawn into a point, perfectly clear, bright enough to enlarge the pale animals beneath it. It has all the appearance of being about to fall.

You can remain there as long as you like.

Two roots enter through the ceiling. One is dead, reddish and tightly twisted. The other is alive, cream-colored, and carries six small leaves inside the chamber. They are thick leaves, folded close to the stem, with translucent margins. Each occupies a narrow clearance between the ceiling and the backs of the animals below. There would be no room for a seventh.

A little farther down is a patch of moss, no larger than a coin. It is unusually coarse at this scale, each leaf standing like a green blade among white trunks. Small creatures pack its base. The moss has enough moisture to remain green, enough light to keep every leaf defined, and no spare ground into which it might spread.

The sound is startling.

A coarse, continuous rasp fills the hollow, overlaid by a thinner ringing tone. Close to the walls there is a dense crackle, like dry seeds poured onto a sheet of metal, although nothing is being poured and nothing strikes anything else. The animals are motionless. No jaws work. No wings tremble. The centipede’s legs remain planted on their separate ledges.

Yet the noise is plainly theirs. Near the mites it becomes finer; near the snails it deepens into a low, granular hum. The hollow is full of the sounds these bodies would make, sustained without the movements that usually produce them. It is loud enough that the moss looks sheltered behind the root, though there is no quieter space there.

Among all this are a few built things.

A lintel of three shell fragments crosses a notch in the floor. Beneath it, a beetle stands with its head just inside the opening. Elsewhere, a little wall has been made from translucent grains placed edge to edge. It encloses almost nothing: a pinch of clean white earth, one bent hair, a round animal with its legs drawn tightly under it.

The workmanship is evident, but no tools or loose materials remain. These small arrangements belong among the bodies. A shell fragment has been set at precisely the angle that leaves room for a passing antenna, though the antenna does not pass. The bent hair rests in a groove shallow enough to seem deliberately made. You cannot tell whether the structures were built for shelter, boundaries, or simply to make the crowded ground habitable.

At the chamber’s farthest edge, a narrow shelf is bare.

Its exposed surface makes the rest of the place seem even denser. Fine pores stipple the shelf. A wet stain stops exactly at its rim. Above it, an unfinished crystal projects from the ceiling; below it, animals press against one another in three visible layers. The empty shelf is only wide enough for the pad of a finger.

There is no disturbance, no arrival, no hidden movement waiting to reveal itself. Still, the place does not have the emptied feeling of something abandoned. Every body is whole. The leaves are firm. The little waters are clear.

The stone is in the first stages of formation, the hollow fully inhabited. The drop hangs pointed downward. The beetle’s head remains beneath the shell lintel. Light rests in every pore, and the crowded, unchanging sound fills the small space beneath the earth.
