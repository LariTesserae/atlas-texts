The district lies one level beneath the city, in a hollow not much larger than a public square. Its roof is the underside of a reservoir. Through it comes a dull green light, enough to distinguish the edges of things but not enough to see their true colors. Occasionally a pale bar travels overhead: some machine passing through the water above, briefly disturbing the sediment.

There are streets here, though only three, and none runs farther than a thrown stone. They meet around a sunken garden. Buildings rise along their outer edges, narrow and closely joined, with drainage balconies, service doors, and tall windows turned inward toward the hollow. Their upper stories disappear into the reservoir’s supporting ring. It is recognizably a piece of a city, complete with address plates and curb stones, yet the space between the buildings feels almost vacant. There are no stalls, benches, parked vehicles, or discarded objects. Nothing has accumulated. The district seems to have been opened recently and then left waiting.

Water occupies the places where people might otherwise linger.

It slides down the walls in sheets too thin to reflect anything clearly. It beads along the handrails. At each intersection, a gutter carries a shallow current into a slot beneath the pavement. The paving blocks have slightly raised centers, so that the streets consist of dry islands surrounded by moving seams of water. Crossing them requires an attentive, measured stride. Some blocks are still warm from manufacture.

The walls are made of a dark mineral composite, neither quite stone nor ceramic. Their corners are sharp. Where a service passage has been cut through one building, the exposed material shows pale growth bands, as though the structure were deposited rather than assembled. The youngest band is soft enough to retain the marks of raindrops. It is hardening now, whitening at the edges.

Despite the wetness, there is little smell of decay. The air smells of clean roots, warm dust wetted for the first time, and something faintly metallic from the wall cavities. It is comfortable enough to stand still without feeling cold. Moisture settles on the back of the neck. In the warmer pockets near the service doors, mist forms at waist height, then thins as the ventilation changes.

The garden is lower than the streets by six broad steps. It occupies most of the district’s open ground.

Here the vegetation is full and vigorous: glossy leaves overlapping in low banks, translucent stems, mats of small plants holding droplets in the cups between their lobes. There are no trees. Nothing grows high enough to obscure the surrounding doors. The largest plants have leaves the length of a forearm, loosely folded so that water can gather along the central crease. When the weight becomes sufficient, a leaf bends and pours its contents onto the plant below. The motion travels down a bank in stages.

For a minute or two, the whole garden seems to be bowing.

Then the leaves lift again.

No insects visit the flowers. There are no webs beneath the rails, no nests in the warm vents, no tracks along the mud. Even the open water lacks the quick disturbances that would suggest something swimming. The plants flower anyway. Their blossoms are small and heavy, held close to the stems, and pollen moves through the hollow as a fine suspension in the condensation. Much of it ends up on the windows. A slow clearing pulse passes through each pane at dawn, carrying the yellow film toward its lower edge.

The garden soil is held behind low retaining walls, but in a few places roots have already found the joints. They enter narrow channels built for them and spread into the foundations. The buildings use the roots to move heat. Warmth collected from occupied rooms far above arrives here through the walls; the plants take part of it, and the water takes the rest. A brass-colored line on each retaining wall marks the intended root level. The actual roots have passed it.

There is considerable noise.

It is not a steady underground hum. Water drops through long pipes with a hollow, forceful rush. Somewhere behind the east façade, a pressure chamber empties every few minutes, producing three deep knocks followed by a sound like gravel being drawn across a floor. The shallow streams click against the raised edges of the paving. Condensation falls from the reservoir roof in isolated, heavy drops, startlingly loud when it strikes a handrail.

Between these events, the hollow briefly quiets. Then the small sounds emerge: a leaf unfolding, grit shifting in a gutter, a door seal releasing moisture.

Most of the technology is concealed. There are no visible lamps. Light is gathered from the reservoir and redistributed through the window glass, leaving the streets dim but the thresholds easy to find. The drainage channels do not consistently slope downhill. Water approaching certain junctions flattens, hesitates, and rises into a vertical seam. It climbs without splashing, a dark strip ascending the wall to an outlet several feet above the street.

Near that outlet, loose droplets sometimes remain suspended for a few seconds. They tremble, lengthen, and abruptly join the ascending flow.

The arrangement is ordinary enough to have a maintenance plate beside it.

At intervals the entire district adjusts its weight. This can be felt through the soles before it can be heard. One curb settles a fraction lower; another rises. The garden’s retaining walls ease apart along almost invisible joints. These changes keep the reservoir load distributed as water shifts overhead. A person standing in the square would notice the movement chiefly by watching a puddle migrate across a paving block.

The place is still learning its drainage. Fresh runnels have appeared on the steps, their edges crisp, their beds not yet stained. A small fan of pale sand spreads below one leaking wall. Tomorrow it may be wider. The water has found a route the builders did not provide, and the district has not yet decided whether to seal it.

Toward the back of the garden, beneath a window with no room visible beyond it, there is a patch of uncovered ground. The soil is dark and smooth, with a few green points emerging. Warm water reaches it from a crack in the retaining wall, spreads, and disappears.

Everything else is already elaborate: the pressure systems, the load-bearing façades, the engineered glass, the careful passage of water through solid structures. But this patch remains simple. In the dimness, it is possible to watch a new leaf slowly separate from itself while the empty streets knock and drain around it.
