At street level, the continent is only a handspan above water.

You see this wherever a paving plate has shifted: beneath the ceramic, beneath the load-bearing mesh and the old, rounded foundations, brown water presses against the city’s underside. It trembles with passing traffic. Sometimes it rises through a seam in a clear bead, gathers grit, and becomes a little muddy fountain. People step around these without looking down. Maintenance animals come afterward, flatten their jointed bodies over the leak, and remain there until a repair crew arrives.

The ground itself is low and old. Before the city, there were tidal flats, shallow lagoons, long banks of clay. There are still tidal flats, shallow lagoons, long banks of clay, but they lie under inhabited platforms or inside buildings, and their tides are controlled by gates large enough to have their own neighborhoods. Nothing here has ever been very far above the sea. Even the highest districts are broad rather than tall: immense stacked decks whose upper surfaces disappear into weather, supported on millions of short columns.

From a distance, if distance can be found, the city resembles a shelf of pale stone breaking apart in surf. Up close, every apparent fracture is occupied.

There are rooms in the bridge piers. There are workshops fitted between the walls of drainage canals. Passenger lifts run through the cores of freight lifts. Houses cling beneath viaducts with their doors facing narrow suspended walks; beneath those walks hang pipes, and beneath the pipes hang cages of shellfish in the warm outflow. On the roof of a pumping station, a public bath steams under white awnings. Its overflow feeds a hatchery three levels below.

There is no clean division between infrastructure and habitation. The pumps need tending, so people live beside them. The people need cooling, so water passes through their walls. The warm water attracts animals, so screens, traps, feeding shelves, and nesting ledges have become part of the walls too.

The light is ordinary daylight, made complicated by obstructions. A blue-white sky appears in slots between decks. Sun falls hard on wet parapets and dazzles from canal surfaces. Farther in, it arrives by reflection: greenish from a sluice, copper from a freight hull, pale yellow from the undersides of sunlit clouds. Rooms near the middle of the older districts depend on lamps even at noon. Rooms along the open waterways must shutter their windows against the glare.

There is almost nothing green.

No trees divide the avenues. No roots bind the banks. At a cracked stair, what looks like moss is an accumulation of tiny copper filaments, each drawing a little charge from the wet concrete. The soft black coat on the pilings is alive, but it is not a plant: a low, leathery colony that feeds on dissolved waste and withdraws into its pores when touched. Gardens, where people keep them, are trays of porous stone, shallow pools, arrangements of shells. Some contain translucent animals that open and close like flowers. They are fed minced scraps twice a day.

Life is abundant, but it lives by eating, filtering, scavenging, or waiting for something else to die.

Small six-legged shore creatures pour over the riprap when a gate opens, picking food from the newly exposed stones. Flat fish work their way through flooded corridors. Narrow-winged birds nest in the hot vents of the transit stations; they launch themselves into the exhaust and are carried upward with almost no effort. Their cries vanish beneath the machinery, though you can see their throats working.

The larger animals are harder to notice. A gray back occasionally rises in a freight canal, broad enough to be mistaken for an overturned barge. It moves under the traffic with patient, shallow strokes. Canal pilots track these grazers by the displaced water rather than by sight. Vessels slow near them. Not out of affection—the animals are heavy enough to break a hull, and old enough to know which gates will open next.

The sound never stops.

Water hits steps, baffles, hulls, grilles. Turbines hammer below the streets. Brakes shriek on the elevated freight tracks. Somewhere nearby, always, a metal surface is being cut, struck, dragged, or dropped. The gates announce their movement with horns so low they are felt in the ribs. At a busy crossing, the noise seems to occupy all the available air. Two people speaking stand close and watch each other’s mouths. Shopkeepers use colored lamps to announce prices. Children learn warning patterns before they learn street names.

Yet residents can hear differences inside the roar. They know the uneven knock of a pump swallowing air. They know when a canal is rising too quickly because the slap beneath a landing changes pitch. A sudden thinning of the noise can make an entire market pause.

The city is technologically capable in ways that are most apparent where it has been damaged.

Along a buckled seawall, structural material gathers slowly from the water. It looks like silt until it begins to climb, forming pale ribs along the fracture. A bridge can redistribute its weight when a support fails, though the deck shudders while it does so and every vehicle aboard must stop. Cargo slides through sealed pressure passages beneath the estuary without touching their walls. The largest engines are hidden under the oldest districts. Nobody calls them engines in conversation; they have local names, and people speak of their moods with the practical familiarity used for weather.

They can shift enormous quantities of heat, water, and weight. They cannot make the city quiet. Nor can they keep it dry.

By afternoon, heat has accumulated beneath the decks. The air tastes of salt and warm metal. Condensation forms on any surface cooled from below, so a shaded corridor can rain steadily while the street above remains in sunshine. Water runs down the backs of signs. It beads on handrails and falls from ceiling seams onto the shoulders of pedestrians.

Then the city makes a storm.

Warm exhaust rises from a thousand districts and collects over the broadest platforms. The cloud grows darker at its base. Wind begins in the canals, driving loose wrappers and spray ahead of it. High awnings retract. Open markets fold themselves into covered galleries with astonishing speed: stalls sliding on rails, counters dropping into floor recesses, cages lifted out of reach of floodwater.

Rain arrives in a hard white sheet. Gutters fill within seconds. Canal surfaces turn rough and opaque. People cross the exposed bridges bent forward, one hand on the guide cable. Lightning strikes the tall collector masts, and for an instant their branching wires are visible all the way down to the roofs.

The storm does not cover the continent evenly. Far away, other districts are still dry. Beyond those, another storm is already draining. Weather moves through the city in enormous overlapping cells, stirred by the sea, the roofs, and the heat beneath them.

Under this agitation, the landscape has settled forms.

The oldest canals follow the same shallow curves as the waterways they replaced. Their corners have been rounded by centuries of suspended grit. Steps descend into the water, worn into troughs by feet and tides. Some buildings have tilted so gradually that new floors were laid level inside them, leaving wedges of unused space beneath. Others were abandoned only at their lowest stories; life continued above while the flooded rooms became nurseries for fish.

The city is always being rebuilt, but rarely cleared. A failed district becomes a breakwater. A redundant transit tube becomes a water main. Demolished ceramic is crushed into new paving, so the streets contain fragments of older streets: blue bathroom tile, red warning enamel, the glazed white curve of a public drinking bowl.

There are stretches where three or four generations of quay lie side by side underwater. At low tide you can see them through the murk, each a little lower and more battered than the next.

Toward evening, the rain moves on. Sunlight returns through the steam, low enough to reach beneath the decks. For a few minutes the undersides of bridges glow gold. Workers emerge to clear the screens. Birds drop from the vent towers. The shore creatures follow the retreating water across the steps, each carrying something too small to identify.

Traffic thickens again. Gates open in sequence, releasing the afternoon’s collected rain toward the sea. The canals surge; mooring lines tighten; floating kitchens and repair shops rise against their guides. Somewhere a damaged span closes to passengers, and the crowd finds another way around it.

From the uppermost public platform, the view extends farther than the haze allows you to see. Roofs, sluices, decks, and waterways overlap to the horizon. There is no last neighborhood in sight, no bank where the buildings give way to open ground. Only the occasional broad channel suggests the sea beneath it all.

Down below, at the edge of a market stair, warm water laps against a worn stone riser. A small pale animal waits there, gripping the submerged face. Each passing boat lifts it an inch or two. Each retreating wave leaves it where it was.
