The place is a hollow near the top of a machine.

From outside, it would scarcely count as a landscape: a square opening in the flank of an immense industrial tower, high above the clouds that hide its foundations. The opening is no larger than a chapel door. Beyond it, however, the floor falls away into a chamber tall enough to hold a grove of mature trees, and almost all of that chamber is empty.

There are three ledges, a hanging garden, and a narrow bridge that does not quite reach the opposite wall.

Everything else is air.

The tower stands at an elevation where exposed flesh should go numb and green leaves should blacken overnight. Here, heat rises through the walls. You can put your palm against the metal and feel it coming from somewhere deep below: not a vibration so much as a steady pressure of warmth. The floor is warm, too. Bare feet would be comfortable on it if not for the grit, the exposed screw heads, and the occasional seam breathing a thin ribbon of scalding air.

There is no earth beneath the plants. There has never been earth here. The chamber’s ledges were cast, its slopes were riveted into position, and the hollows that now resemble erosion channels began as drainage grooves. Time has rounded the distinction away. Water has eaten into the welds. Roots have lifted the edges of inspection plates. Ceramic insulation, once white, has become the color of old teeth, and where it has cracked, moss occupies the fracture so completely that the damage looks deliberate.

The garden grows on one wall.

It hangs from a maintenance rack perhaps thirty feet above the lowest ledge, spilling down through a lattice of pipes and cable trays. Broad leaves turn their darker faces toward the opening. Ferns crowd the warmer recesses. Fine, reddish roots run exposed over the metal before disappearing into mats of accumulated fibers: old filter material, dead leaves, shed insect skins, the dust of belts and bearings. In this place, soil is something that happens slowly to machinery.

Some of the plants have swollen stems that store moisture. Others have leaves lined with pale fur, and dew beads along that fur on the few mornings when the outside air becomes cold enough. A vine with yellow flowers has reached the abandoned bridge. It advances so slowly that its progress is measured by which bolt it has covered this year.

The growth is lavish, but confined. It does not fill the chamber. Across from the garden is a vast, bare wall of dark enamel, and between the two hangs a volume of warm air with nothing in it but an occasional moth. A single falling leaf takes a long time to cross that space. Its descent is interrupted by currents rising from the floor, so it may circle for minutes before touching down.

The noise is immense.

At first it seems to come from everywhere: a continuous iron roar, with a higher whine folded inside it and a slow, deep pounding that reaches the ribs before the ears can separate it. Speaking is pointless. Even a shout becomes the visible shape of a mouth, nothing more.

Yet there is very little movement to account for the noise. No wheel turns in the chamber. No piston crosses a shaft. The sound arrives through the structure from engines far below, conducted upward by the columns and released here where the tower is hollow. A loose panel trembles. Water standing in a bolt socket wears a permanent fringe of ripples. Otherwise, the place rests.

After enough time, distinctions emerge. One tone belongs to the left wall. Another strengthens when you stand at the unfinished end of the bridge. Near the garden there is a narrow zone where the roar cancels itself unevenly, leaving a battered, fluttering quiet. Birds build there.

They are small, soot-colored birds with pale beaks and feet surprisingly long for their bodies. Their nests sit behind the pipe elbows, tucked into bundles of dry roots. There are more of them than you first notice. They keep close to the green wall, rarely venturing across the empty chamber unless something has fallen and must be followed.

Their calls cannot be heard. Courtship is conducted with lifted wings, throat feathers spread into little fans, and rapid taps of the beak against a branch or pipe. Through the perch, those taps travel clearly. Young birds learn the vibration of a parent’s landing before they open their eyes.

Moths occupy the warmer heights. Beetles live under the filter mats. Translucent mites gather around the condensation lines, where the water tastes faintly of copper. At dusk, thin-legged hunters come out onto the bare enamel and stand perfectly still, each positioned several feet from the next. Their shadows are larger than they are.

Rain enters only when the wind turns.

Most days the opening admits ordinary daylight: a pale rectangular wash that slides slowly across the chamber and stops short of the garden’s lower roots. Outside, the sky is immense and apparently empty. Clouds sometimes pass beneath the threshold, their white backs level with the tips of the tower’s smaller exhaust stacks. The horizon is too far away to offer useful depth. Looking out gives the peculiar sensation that the chamber is more solid than the world.

When rain does arrive, it strikes the sill sideways and runs inward along grooves worn smooth by generations of brief storms. There is no stream. For an hour or two, certain surfaces darken. A shallow basin beside the bridge fills to the depth of a fingernail. The plants unfold small structures normally held tight against their stems. Beetles emerge. The birds descend to drink.

Then the heat takes the water back.

Far inside one wall, a cooling valve opens at intervals so long that no living creature here depends on a single opening. Its first sign is a change in the pitch of the roar. Later, moisture gathers along a seam high above the garden. The drops swell, tremble, and fall.

They always land in nearly the same place.

Below them, a ceramic plate has been worn into a smooth depression, dark at the center and green around its rim. A thick-rooted plant occupies the lip, its leaves arranged so that falling water is guided inward. The plant is older than some of the replacement panels around it. Maintenance workers once cut a hole in a new panel rather than disturb its roots.

There are marks of people, but no current habitation: a glove hardened into the shape of a hand; a numbered brass tag almost swallowed by a stem; painted instructions visible through moss when the leaves shift. Along the bridge, the railing ends in two blunt sockets. The far span was removed so long ago that the cut edges have rounded, and a small nest now occupies one socket.

Nothing here changes quickly enough to resemble an event. A leaf opens over several days. A rivet loosens over decades. The enamel wall gradually acquires a green margin as the garden makes another fraction of an inch of ground.

The engines continue below. Their purposes are inaccessible from this height.

In the chamber, there is warmth, a little water, enough light, and one inhabited wall. The rest remains open: a great hollow held inside manufactured cliffs, loud enough to erase every small sound, spacious enough that the passage of a single bird briefly gives it a measure.
