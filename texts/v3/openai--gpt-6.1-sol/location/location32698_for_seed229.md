The garden occupies the broken crown of a mountain, in a hollow no wider than a village square. Three sides rise into blunt walls of purple stone. The fourth is open to the sky, and beyond that opening there is nothing nearby: only a dim succession of peaks, their bases lost in airborne dust.

The hollow is warm. Heat comes up through the floor in patches, so that one slab is comfortable to sit on and the next will scorch a bare foot. There is no water here, no dampness underneath a stone, no dark line of seepage along a crack. Even the shaded crevices hold dry powder. A dropped cup would remain empty for generations.

Yet the plants are extravagant.

They grow in seven widely separated islands, leaving most of the floor bare. Each island is lush enough to conceal an animal the size of a person. Low cushions of copper-colored foliage swell around the roots of branching plants, and long, glossy blades spill over them in layers. Some leaves are almost black, with translucent edges; others have the pale yellow of old teeth. Thick stems twist together, separating near their tops into fans of narrow fronds. In the poor light, those fans seem to be holding up small scraps of a brighter sky.

Between the islands lie stretches of pale mineral pavement. The stone has been polished by centuries of grit passing across it. Raised seams have worn down to rounded ribs. Shallow hollows collect fine red dust, which the wind repeatedly empties and refills. There are old channels in the floor, but they were made by roots grinding from one position to another.

Nothing is quite where it was yesterday.

The plants have feet.

At the base of each thick stem, a clutch of hard root-hooks grips the stone. A plant can release one hook, reach it forward, and haul its weight a few finger-widths across the floor. Most move too slowly to watch. Occasionally, though, a seam opens beneath them and exhales a hot mineral vapor. Then an entire island begins to travel toward it.

The cushions go first, bunching and spreading like heavy cloth pushed across a table. Tall plants lean into one another. Root-hooks scrape, catch, and snap free. Smaller growths ride on the larger ones, their tendrils wrapped around stems. A passage that was clear in the morning may be choked by evening, while elsewhere a patch of stone appears for the first time in years, striped with root scars and covered in pale, startled animals.

The garden feeds on the mountain’s breath. Its leaves sift the vapor, taking in sulfur, carbon, and fine particles of warm metal. Its sap is an oily amber substance that sets into flexible wax when exposed to air. Broken stems do not bleed for long. They seal themselves with a shining bead, and within an hour something will come to gnaw it off.

There are animals everywhere, though from the threshold the place can look almost vacant.

Much of the life is tucked into foliage, under loose scales of rock, or inside the old channels. Six-legged grazers cling to the undersides of leaves, rasping off thin layers of wax. Their bodies resemble folded bark. When disturbed, they unfold into broad, absurdly delicate shapes and sail a short distance on the warm updrafts.

Smaller creatures run beneath them in bright, urgent lines. Some carry grains of mineral powder larger than their heads. Others drag shed root-hooks toward communal burrows. A slow, plated animal rests beside one of the hot seams, receiving a steady traffic of little cleaners through the gaps in its armor. Its shell is furred with seedlings. Every few minutes it lifts its head and opens a mouth full of blunt white plates, testing the air.

Above the garden, narrow-winged hunters circle in the confined space between the stone walls. They cannot glide here. The air is too broken. They flap, drop, turn sharply, and strike the cliff with their claws to throw themselves back into flight. Their wings make a snapping sound like sheets being shaken.

The whole hollow is loud.

Wind pours across the open edge and breaks against the back wall with a sustained roar. The plants whistle through holes in their stems. Root-hooks grind on stone. Grazers drum to one another on hollow leaves, and the sound carries surprisingly far: a dry rattling conversation, passed from island to island.

Somewhere beneath the pavement, burrowing animals knock against the roof of their tunnels. The knocks come in clusters. A long silence follows, then an answering cluster from a different part of the hollow. When a hunter drops low, half the garden rattles at once.

The loudest sound comes when a stem splits.

Pressure builds inside the taller plants as they feed. In still weather, they release it through small valves beneath their fronds. During a strong gust, those valves pinch shut. A plant that has been feeding too greedily can burst with a report that hurts the teeth. Fragments of waxy pith fly across the open stone. For a few moments everything retreats. Then the scavengers arrive.

This happens often enough that the floor is scattered with old splinters, worn smooth and hollowed by chewing.

There are three human structures here, all low, all built against the walls.

One is little more than a windbreak of stacked slabs. One has a roof of overlapping mineral scales held down by cords made from stripped fronds. The third is fitted into a natural recess, its entrance so narrow that a person must turn sideways to enter. None has a hearth. Cooking is done on a flat stone above a reliable heat seam, with food wrapped in broad leaves and weighted down against the gusts.

The inhabitants use stone wedges, bone scrapers, and a few short blades of soft, dark metal. Beside the roofed shelter stands a rack of root-hooks, sorted by size. The larger hooks serve as digging tools. The smaller ones become needles or clasps.

Their paths are marked with pale stakes driven into cracks. The stakes do not tell you where to walk. They tell you where the ground has recently opened.

No one sleeps beside a warm seam, however comfortable it feels. No one ties a shelter to a living plant. Before crossing bare ground, people stop and listen for the knocks below it. A shallow tunnel can hold for years and then collapse under a child.

The garden has other hazards. At intervals the wind reverses, drawing air out of the hollow instead of forcing it in. Leaves flatten. Loose dust lifts into thin pillars. The flying animals drop immediately and seize whatever they can reach. Anyone standing near the open edge crouches behind the nearest rib of stone.

Then the mountain exhales.

A rush of hot vapor comes through dozens of cracks at once. The plants open so abruptly that their fronds strike one another. Animals spill from their hiding places. Dust sweeps sideways across the floor, and the seven islands seem for a moment to become a single tossing canopy.

It lasts perhaps a minute.

Afterward there are torn leaves, overturned stones, displaced nests. A small animal may be stranded on the bare pavement, turning in tight circles until it finds the shadow of a stem. People emerge from the shelters carrying baskets and long forked poles. They gather the fallen pith before it hardens, lift trapped grazers out of cracks, and move the warning stakes.

The stone walls show that this has happened for a very long time. Their lower surfaces are scalloped by the abrasive gusts. Higher up, generations of climbing plants have left overlapping scratches. In a sheltered corner lies the remains of an old roof, almost buried in dry leaf husks. New roots have passed over it without settling.

By the time the dust thins, the garden has already begun to feed again.

The light never becomes much stronger. A veil hangs over the high sky, turning the sun into a broad, dull patch that moves behind the peaks. Within the hollow, glossy leaves catch what little brightness there is. Small bodies flash between them. A shelter door opens; someone steps out with a repaired basket.

Across a wide stretch of empty stone, one root-hook releases its hold.

It reaches forward, tests a crack, and pulls.
