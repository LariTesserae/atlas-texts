The place lies in the gap between a loading platform and the ground beneath it. From the yard, it looks like a missing brick: a pale opening at ankle height, half screened by a bent strip of wire mesh. Sunlight enters there and through three holes in the platform above. At midday these openings illuminate almost everything. You can see the far wall, the beads of water on it, even the feet of a fly walking upside down along a pipe.

Inside, the ground has only recently formed.

Six weeks ago, a hot-water line split beneath the platform. The leak carried lime, iron, and fine white grit into a shallow depression. When the line was repaired, the wet sediment remained. It is still setting. Its surface has risen into hard little ridges around the things it buried: a washer, a screw, the broken neck of a bottle, an insect casing. Some ridges are no taller than a fingernail, but they have clean crests and steep, brittle sides. A touch would take off their summits. Nothing has yet rounded them.

The whole basin is perhaps two paces across. There is no broad level floor. Every available patch is occupied by something—mineral towers, folded leaves, husks, cables, animals, fragments of machinery—so that the eye has to travel through it by short, interrupted routes. Beneath one ledge is another ledge. Between two grains of slag is a doorway. A piece of packaging foil, crumpled and cemented upright, divides the western half into a succession of bright, narrow valleys.

Warmth comes through the ceiling. The loading platform serves a laundry, and its metal plates retain the heat of carts brought out from the dryers. Even at dawn the air beneath it feels used and comfortable, with the faint smell of wet cloth and hot dust. By afternoon, the highest ridges become too warm for the pale animals. They descend into the cuts, leaving the crests to the dark ones.

There is little standing water. A shallow pool fills the bottle neck, and a black channel threads along the wall where the old leak ran. Most moisture arrives as condensation. The pipe above cools unevenly, sweating first along its joints. Drops hang there until a cart crosses overhead. The vibration releases them all at once.

This is the local rain.

It falls in a straight line, no wider than a hand. The creatures underneath know the tremor that precedes it. Ants stop hauling. A long-legged mite folds itself into the shelter of a washer. Three soft, translucent things withdraw into a crack with such speed that their tails remain visible for an instant after the rest of them has gone.

Then the drops land. Fresh craters open in the powder. Water runs down slopes too young to have channels, choosing a new route each time. On the southern side, a white spur has been undercut by four successive rains. It will probably fall tonight. Beneath it, several small beetles have already begun moving their eggs.

Plants are few. A single grass shoot rises beside the entrance, its blade pressed flat against the underside of the platform. Farther in, a tuft of moss occupies the cloth lining of an abandoned work glove. The glove has hardened into the ground with its fingers pointing downhill. Each fingertip holds a different degree of dampness. The moss grows only on the third.

Most of the green in the place belongs to a thin film inside the bottle neck. When sunlight reaches it, tiny bubbles collect beneath the film and lift it into domes. Small snails arrive to puncture these. They graze with their bodies hanging over the rim, shells almost transparent in the light.

There are animals everywhere, though none makes the place seem crowded by movement alone. Much of the activity is patient and nearly silent. A centipede takes several minutes to negotiate the legs of a dead crane fly. Ants dismantle a crumb of bread behind the foil, carrying away pieces so small that their procession appears empty. Near the warm wall, a spider has spun seven separate webs between seven adjacent ridges. She moves from one to another along a cable of her own making, testing each with a foot.

The laundry supplies the larger rhythms. A motor starts, and the grains on certain slopes creep downward. A valve closes, and a buried air pocket gives a soft click. During the spin cycle, the pool in the bottle neck develops a circular current. Its snails grip the glass and wait.

A few structures survive from before the ground existed. There is a square drain cover, mostly submerged in lime, and a short length of copper tube fitted with a tap too small for a human hand. Someone has placed a flat shard of tile beneath it, making a catchment. A wire ladder leads from the tile to the platform’s underside. Its lower rungs have disappeared into the new deposits.

No one is seen using the ladder. Nevertheless, the tile is swept clean every morning.

The oddest part of the basin is how readily things acquire undersides.

A pebble left on the ground overnight will be raised by a thin white stalk by morning. Fallen seeds stand on delicate stems of mineral. The washer nearest the entrance has risen high enough to cast a complete ring of shadow. The process is slow while watched, but overnight it makes appreciable progress. Warmth draws something upward through the new sediment, and whatever rests lightly upon it is carried along.

Living creatures rarely remain still long enough to rise. Eggs do. So do the sleeping snails.

Some wake suspended above the ground, on pedestals that have grown around the edges of their shells. They spend the first hour of daylight stretching down to find a foothold. A few have learned to feed where they are, turning slowly while passing insects climb their stalks.

The tallest object is a brass nut, lifted nearly four inches. It stands in the central light with three grains of grit balanced on its upper rim. Below it, the mineral column is ridged with yesterday’s growth. A tiny red animal lives in the threaded hole and comes out only when the laundry stops.

For a few minutes each evening, there is almost no sound. The machinery rests. The warm roof ticks once as it contracts. Light from the yard reaches deep under the platform, picking out every web and fresh fracture.

Then the red animal emerges. It walks around the nut, touches each of the three grains, and pushes one over the edge.

The grain falls into the dense country below. Several bodies shift to make room. By morning, it too has begun to rise.
