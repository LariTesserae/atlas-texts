The entrance is a crack behind a warm slab of black stone. From the outer face of the mountain it looks like nothing: a flaw narrow enough to catch a sleeve, sheltered beneath a lip of newly fractured rock. Beyond that lip, the summit drops away into air. The clouds are far below, their tops flattened by distance, and lightning moves through them without any sound arriving here.

Inside the crack, the air is hot and smells faintly of wet metal.

You turn sideways twice. Your shoulder scrapes a wall that has not yet acquired the softening of weather. Its edges are sharp enough to shave threads from cloth. Then the passage widens, and there is room to stand.

The entire place would fit inside a small house.

At first it appears to be an empty cavity: a floor of dark glass, a low ceiling lost in shadow, two or three pale lines suspended across the space. There is little enough light that your eyes keep inventing objects, then withdrawing them. What seemed a pillar becomes a fold in the wall. What seemed a distant doorway is the reflection of your own entrance.

Gradually, the city becomes visible.

Its streets are etched into the glass underfoot, narrower than the seams between floorboards. They divide the cavity into long, unequal districts. At a few intersections, structures rise to the height of a fingernail: translucent blocks with interiors so dark they appear deeper than their outer dimensions allow. A thin bridge crosses several handspans of empty floor. It carries no road you can see, but along its underside a pinpoint of amber light travels steadily from one end to the other.

There are perhaps forty buildings in all. Most of the space between them is bare.

Each stands well apart from its neighbors, occupying the center of an expanse of polished stone. The layout gives the impression of a settlement waiting for further construction, though there are no piles of material, no unfinished foundations, no marks of work. Some of the smallest buildings throw long shadows. Others throw none.

The cavity lies just beneath the mountain’s highest point. A flake of stone separates its ceiling from the sky. Yet the glass floor gives off enough heat to make the soles of your shoes pliable. The mountain is young. Outside, its ribs still show where enormous sheets of rock tore apart during the uplift. Inside, the city has built directly over a seam that has not finished cooling.

You can feel the heat arranging itself. One step carries you into a still, baking patch; another brings a faint current against your ankles. There are no vents. The streets seem to draw warmth along their lengths, dividing it among the buildings with a precision that leaves the surrounding floor almost untouched.

Water appears only on the ceiling.

A bead gathers above the tallest block, swells, and hangs there trembling. Several more emerge beside it. They make a small constellation in the dark, each catching a dull copper glimmer from below. For a minute nothing happens.

Then all the drops move sideways.

They travel along the ceiling without leaving wet tracks, gathering above an empty square. The combined droplet becomes heavy enough to descend. It falls slowly, flattens halfway down, and opens into a disk scarcely wider than a coin.

For several seconds, rain falls beneath it.

The rain does not reach your knees. It occupies a slender column of air and strikes the square in almost inaudible taps. The stone there pales. Something below its surface shifts, like a page being turned under smoked glass. Then the little shower stops. Its source contracts to a bead and rises back toward the ceiling.

Elsewhere, a second cloud begins to assemble.

This is the weather of the place. It moves from district to district, feeding reservoirs too small to distinguish from scratches. Long intervals separate the showers. Much of the city remains dry, and the unused squares have acquired a fine white edging where old moisture evaporated. There is no moss in the damp places, no root seeking a seam. Nothing stirs toward the water.

Still, the city is busy.

At the far wall, a narrow tower slowly gains height. Its uppermost edge is soft and luminous. Material arrives there as a faint haze, settles, and hardens into another fraction of a story. Across the cavity, a similar tower shortens at the same rate. The exchange is too gradual to watch comfortably, but after a while the difference is unmistakable.

A road detaches from one intersection and bends toward another. It lifts no higher than a hair’s breadth above the floor. Beneath it remains a clean band of glass.

One of the dark buildings opens.

There is no moving door. Its entire face becomes transparent, revealing a room with a pale rectangular surface at its center. For an instant, that room seems large enough for you to enter. You see the far wall, a shallow recess, a second opening beyond it. Then the face darkens again, and the building is once more smaller than your thumb.

You begin to understand why so much of the cavity has been left empty. The buildings need room around them when they open.

The silence is not complete. Once every few minutes, a light tick passes through the floor. It is difficult to locate, though you feel it in your teeth. Water occasionally clicks against stone. The traveling light beneath the bridge reaches the end, pauses, and sets off in the other direction. There is no machinery hum, no distant traffic, no movement behind the windows.

Near the entrance, a patch of unpolished rock holds the only loose debris: a few fresh black chips, a curl of pale mineral dust. One chip is warm enough that you cannot keep it in your palm. Underneath it, the glass bears the beginning of a street.

The settlement is still finding space in the mountain.

Above you, the thin roof contracts with a small, dry sound. Dust drops onto a deserted square. Immediately, three nearby lines brighten. The dust rises again, gathers into a thread, and is drawn toward the growing tower.

You crouch to look closer. In the square where it fell, a new bead of water stands on the glass, trembling in the heat. Through it, magnified and distorted, you can see a flight of steps descending far below the floor.

The steps are lit.

You shift your head to follow them, but the bead evaporates before you can see where they lead.
