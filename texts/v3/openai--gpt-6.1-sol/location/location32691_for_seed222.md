By late morning, the plateau is too bright to look at directly. The ground is the color of bread flour, except where old runoff has exposed bands of rose and gray. Far below its southern edge, clouds gather over a country you cannot see. Up here the air is warm, thin, and almost still. Sunlight rests on every ledge. Even the undersides of stones seem faintly illuminated.

The plateau is not flat. It is a broad, shallow basin divided by low ridges, with enough room between them for several small settlements, though there are only a few buildings. Its slopes have been worn into long backs and blunt shoulders. Gullies open gradually rather than cutting sharply; boulders sit in sockets that fit them exactly. The landscape has been weathering for so long that its damage has become its arrangement.

Walking is easy until you reach one of the smooth gray seams. These cross the basin like streams but contain no visible water. Your soles lose their purchase on them. A dropped pebble may slide several meters uphill, slow down, then settle against a little accumulation of other pebbles. Dust does not collect there.

Elsewhere, dust lies thick enough to preserve the prints of very small feet.

There are more animals than the first view suggests. Most spend the bright hours below the surface. Under a slab, dozens of pale, six-legged creatures crowd together, each holding its body clear of the rock on delicate jointed legs. They turn toward an opened crack without making a sound. In the loose soil, something larger travels beneath the crust, raising a soft ridge that advances for a while, pauses, and collapses behind it.

Above ground, the basin belongs to low, long-bodied grazers with narrow faces and translucent ears. They move between patches of vegetation in widely spaced groups. From a distance they resemble bits of weathered timber changing position. Their feet have broad pads, and they place them carefully. Even a running herd produces little more than a brushing noise.

There is not enough greenery here to account for their numbers.

What grows visibly is scattered: gray bushes with curled leaves, stiff tussocks sheltered behind stones, and dark mats pressed flat against the ground. Around the wetter hollows, finger-thick stems rise without branches, carrying a few waxy cups. Each cup holds a drop of clear liquid. Small animals climb the stems to drink, leaving the plant untouched.

Much of the grazing happens on apparently bare earth. The animals scrape away the topmost powder and lower their faces to a pale film beneath it. In places, this film extends under whole slopes. After a herd passes, the exposed patches slowly acquire a granular skin again. Seen close up, the grains are turning, settling themselves edge to edge. By evening the feeding scars are difficult to find.

Water arrives in brief, local events. A cloud can cross the basin without dropping rain, then leave a single ridge wet. Sometimes moisture gathers on the shaded face of a stone while the surrounding air remains dry. It beads, runs down, and disappears into a ring of darker soil. There are little pools under overhangs, warm at the surface and surprisingly cold below. Their bottoms are paved with smooth black nodules. If you stir one, the disturbance reaches only halfway across the pool before stopping along an invisible line.

The pools support crowds of life. Threadlike swimmers hang vertically beneath the surface; flat amber animals work over the stones; blue-winged insects stand on the water in loose circles. Their wings scarcely move. Occasionally one circle breaks apart all at once, and a slender mouth rises through the place where its center had been.

Nothing splashes very much.

The quiet is partly the thin air and partly the ground. Footsteps have a short, padded quality, even on bare rock. A stone struck with another stone gives one clear note and no lingering ring. Wind passing over a ridge can be seen in the bending stems long before it is heard. The most persistent sound is a faint ticking from the black pool-bottom nodules, audible only when you kneel beside the water.

At intervals across the plateau stand small structures made from the same pale material as the exposed hills. One is a roofless square with a doorway too narrow for a person carrying a pack. Another is a low dome, half buried, with a clean circular opening facing north. Farther away, on a saddle between two ridges, three upright panels stand several meters apart.

None has a road leading to it.

The panels are thin enough that their edges vanish when you approach from the wrong direction. Looking through one shows the slope beyond it, with a slight discrepancy: a bush is a little taller, a stone has shifted, an animal stands where the ground on either side is empty. Move around the panel and the discrepancy disappears. Wait in front of it, and sometimes the animal arrives.

The buildings contain almost nothing. Their floors are cool. Dust enters through the doors but does not cross certain unmarked boundaries. In the dome, a palm-sized depression in the wall fills with water when someone stands nearby. It empties as soon as they leave, without a drain or wet trace. A few of the grazers have learned this and rest their faces against the opening during the hottest hours.

Beneath the technology there must be enormous machinery, or something for which machinery is the wrong expectation. There is no hum, no vibration, no visible source of power. Its effects are distributed and specific. It keeps some pockets damp. It draws minerals into the gray seams. It redirects a little heat from one surface to another. On the western slopes, shadows cool the soil so efficiently that condensation begins a few minutes after a cloud passes over, then stops when the cloud moves on.

The system has been here long enough to enter the habits of the animals. Small burrowers nest along the edges of the slippery seams, where predators cannot accelerate. The blue-winged insects lay eggs only in pools that stop a ripple. Birds roost on the upright panels, though from certain angles their feet seem to clasp empty air.

The birds are numerous but widely spread: small white bodies, long tails, dark bills curved sideways at the tip. They fly low over the basin, taking insects from the air without calling. Once or twice an hour, a bird climbs until it is barely visible, hangs there, and folds its wings. Its fall is slow at first. Then it drops rapidly, opens just above the ground, and sweeps along a gully. Other birds follow that route afterward. They seem to be testing the air for something.

By afternoon, activity moves into the narrow shade. Grazers lie with their ears extended. The animals beneath the soil rise closer to the surface, and their traveling ridges can be followed for tens of meters. A patient, angular predator waits beside a damp hollow. Its skin is mottled with the exact colors of the local stone, but its outline is easy to see; camouflage matters less than remaining motionless. When it finally strikes, there is a brief flurry of feet and one dry contact sound. A few minutes later the insects return to the water.

At the basin’s center is a shallow depression about the size of a village square. It looks empty at noon. Toward evening, parallel lines begin to show across it, faint and dark beneath the soil. They do not form letters or a diagram. They resemble the marks left by a rake, except that some bend around buried stones and others pass straight through them.

Animals gather along these lines. They keep generous distances from one another. Birds settle; burrowers come fully out of the ground; the grazers stand with their heads lowered. For a while the depression contains more living bodies than anywhere else on the plateau, all of them quiet.

A little moisture rises between the lines. It is too slight to be called mist. It catches the low sun at ankle height, and the ground darkens in narrow strips. The animals drink, feed, or simply remain there. What each species receives is hard to distinguish.

Then the lines fade. The gathering disperses without a common signal.

The warmth lasts after sunset. From the plateau’s edge, the clouds below are visible as an enormous dim surface, still holding light while the basin turns gray. Here and there, a pool reflects a patch of sky brighter than the sky above it. The dome’s doorway remains black. The three panels become visible edge-on for a few minutes, each a fine luminous stroke, and then vanish again.

At night there are small sounds: soil loosening, pads crossing gravel, the careful tearing of a leaf. Moisture collects under the stones. In the pale feeding films, grains begin their slow rearrangement. Far beneath your feet, something changes the course of tomorrow’s water.

By morning, the gullies will look the same. A damp patch will have appeared on a different ridge, the grazers will find it, and the birds will begin testing the bright air again.
