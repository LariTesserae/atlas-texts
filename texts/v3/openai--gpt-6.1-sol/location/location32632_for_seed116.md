The water is behind a small square door near the top of the world.

The door stands in the side of a service shaft, seventeen thousand meters above the cloud floor. Its hinges have worn down to smooth cylinders. Its handle is polished by hands that no longer exist. Around its frame, the metal has lost every sharp edge: even the stamped warning symbols have become shallow bowls holding beads of condensation.

Open it, and the water does not come out.

There is a threshold of dry air, no wider than a finger, and then a vertical surface of warm water. It dimples if touched. Beyond it lies a chamber scarcely larger than a bedroom, entirely submerged.

This is the landscape.

At first it seems empty. A pale green distance, a floor, a far wall softened by light. The room’s small dimensions are difficult to judge because there is nothing floating in its center. All the growth keeps to the margins. All the animals have places to conceal themselves. The open water has the spaciousness of a clearing.

Then the machinery takes a breath.

A broad vibration passes through the doorframe, through the bones of anyone standing there, and through the chamber. The green distance bends. Leaves flatten against the wall. A little procession of silver animals lifts from a floor seam and hangs in the current, each one bright on one side and dark on the other.

The chamber is an old balancing cistern in the crown of a manufactured mountain. Far below, pumps still lift water through columns of pipe. The mountain has no stone inside it: only girders, cast ballast, ceramic insulation, and machinery layered around older machinery. What looks from a distance like a snowline is powdered enamel. What looks like a glacier is a spill of pale glass slowly slipping down the northern face.

Here, near the summit, the last pumps warm the water before sending it onward. No one remembers where onward is.

The cistern remains.

Its terrain is made of wear. A raised inspection walkway runs along the left wall, its grating eroded until the individual bars resemble flattened roots. Beneath it is a shallow trench where grit travels during each pump stroke. On the opposite side, three pipe mouths emerge from the wall at different heights. The lowest has almost disappeared under successive layers of ceramic repair. Its rim is a soft white crescent. The middle mouth exhales warm water. The highest is stopped by a valve so old that its handwheel has become a disk.

Everything has been rounded by movement repeated beyond counting. Water has polished the bolts without loosening them. Fine suspended particles have hollowed the floor around its supports. A loose washer lies in a depression exactly fitted to its shape; it must have rocked there for ages before the rocking finally ceased.

There are no fresh breaks anywhere. Even the failures have settled into durable arrangements.

The vegetation grows in four dense patches.

One hangs beneath the inspection walkway: broad translucent ribbons, olive at their bases, yellow-green where light passes through them. They are rooted in the old grate, not in soil. Their holdfasts penetrate the metal along lines of corrosion, drawing what they need from the slow decay of the room. When the current slackens, the ribbons lift and spread into a canopy low enough to brush the back of a passing animal.

A second growth surrounds the warm pipe. It is thick and dark, made of tiny branching fronds with copper-colored tips. Small bubbles accumulate among them. Every so often a branch releases its bubbles together, and they rise like a handful of beads poured upward.

The third patch occupies the ceiling near the door. It looks like wet velvet, but close inspection reveals an intricate forest of transparent stalks, each bearing a red cup. The cups turn toward vibration rather than light. During a strong pump stroke, thousands of them lean in the same direction.

The fourth is a single great leaf growing out of a split weld.

It is nearly as long as the chamber. Its stem is hidden in the wall. The leaf lies diagonally across one corner, glossy and deeply pleated, green so dark it seems blue. Several generations of small animals live beneath it. Its outer edge has been grazed into a delicate scallop. Occasionally the whole leaf folds, very slowly, squeezing the water from its pleats and sending a mild current across the floor.

Between these four presences there is mostly open water, pale ceramic, and room for movement.

Light comes through a narrow inspection pane above the far wall. Outside, the air is thin enough that daylight arrives hard and white. Inside, two layers of cloudy glass and the water itself soften it into an ordinary afternoon brightness. The chamber has a clear bright side and a dim side. Animals crossing the center cast little shadows on the floor.

At this height, clouds usually pass below. Once in a while a storm rises far enough to cover the window. The room darkens. Rain scratches the outer glass, a faint sound almost lost beneath the pumps.

The machinery is loud.

There is a continuous deep hum, with a rasp inside it where a bearing runs dry. Every few moments comes the heavy knock of a remote valve. Smaller sounds fill the intervals: grit sliding through a drain, bubbles striking the ceiling, the rapid clicking of animals feeding on deposits around the pipe mouths. The room is too small for the noise to fade. It enters through the walls and floor from every direction.

Its inhabitants have learned the rhythm. They move freely during the hum, settle before the knock, and scatter when a pump hesitates. A missed stroke sends more of them into hiding than any ordinary disturbance.

The silver swimmers number perhaps fifty. They are flat and narrow, with blunt heads and tails that divide into fine combs. In the open water they spread far apart. Under the great leaf they crowd together, flashing in the reflected light. They feed on the drifting seeds shed by the ceiling forest, but they are themselves eaten by two amber predators that spend most of the day motionless inside the lower pipe.

A colony of translucent snails crosses the inspection walkway. Their shells are almost invisible; their bodies look like moving drops of milk. Behind them, the metal is clean. Ahead of them, a faint green film continually regrows.

In the floor trench live jointed scavengers with red legs. They hurry from hollow to hollow as each pulse sweeps food toward the drain. One has learned to brace itself against the loose washer and catch particles in its front limbs. Others shelter behind its body.

There are stranger creatures too, though they are easy to overlook. On the back wall, pale disks rest against the ceramic, indistinguishable from mineral stains until they begin to move. They travel toward the warm pipe at night and return to their separate positions by morning. Each leaves a little ring of condensation on the other side of the wall, out in the dry shaft.

The water sometimes remembers the weather outside.

When a strong wind bends the summit structure, a current develops across the room from door to window. It gathers loose fibers from the plants and carries them in a slow revolving cloud. As the wind subsides, the fibers settle. A visitor can watch a storm pass without seeing the sky: first the plants bow, then the red cups lean, then the silver animals turn together and hold their positions.

Nothing here has an entire quiet day. The warm pipe exhales. The leaf folds. The snails uncover the old metal. The scavengers sort what the current brings them. Heat rises into the ceiling growth and returns along the cooler door, making a circulation gentler than the pumps, persistent even when the machinery pauses.

Beyond the walls are immense distances: the hollow body of the tower, its deserted lifting stations, the weather far below. Inside, those distances are reduced to pressure, a vibration, an occasional change in light.

Near the threshold, a small shoot has appeared in a screw hole.

It has two clear leaves and a stem the color of rust. During the pump’s breath it bends flat. In the interval it stands again. Around its roots, the worn screw head is slowly turning green.
