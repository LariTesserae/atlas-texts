At first the plain appears empty because everything on it is the color of light.

Then the eye adjusts. The white separates into cream, pale brass, the gray of a polished bone, a pink so faint it seems to belong to the air rather than the ground. There are seams everywhere. Some are thin as scratches; others are wide enough to walk inside. Between them lie broad, slightly convex plates, their edges worn round by an age of expansion and contraction. Nothing stands much higher than a person. Yet the plain is crowded: lips, sockets, shallow bowls, closed apertures, rows of blunt projections, all nearly touching, all extending to a horizon that looks less like a boundary than a failure of sight.

There is no exposed rock beneath these things. Digging would uncover more construction.

The upper plates rest on lattices; the lattices rest on older plates whose patterns no longer correspond to anything above them. Below those are folded ducts, packed mineral fibers, chambers filled with dry gas. The whole landscape is built to a depth no one has measured. Even the fine powder in its seams consists of manufactured grains, each with a tiny internal arrangement that becomes visible under magnification. The dust has worn off something. It has not weathered out of stone.

The plain is warm underfoot. In the shallow channels it is a little cooler, and a traveler learns to follow them despite their inconvenient turns. The air tastes faintly metallic, without enough moisture to soften the taste. Cloth stays crisp here. A breath leaves no mark on a polished surface.

Far away, broad patches brighten and fade.

These are weather fronts.

They move across the continent at the pace of a person walking, though their edges are hundreds of miles long. Ahead of a front, the plates become almost painfully white. Within it, colors deepen, warmth rises from below, and every seam opens by a fraction. Behind it comes a cooler interval in which the dust settles and the low growths unfold.

The sky is bright at every hour. A gauzy lattice hangs so high that it can usually be mistaken for a featureless atmosphere. Sometimes, when the ground enters one of its warmer phases, the lattice becomes visible as enormous pale diamonds, curving away overhead. Light gathers along their edges and spills down in broad, even fields. There are no hard shadows except beneath objects held close to the ground.

Whatever once supplied this light is hidden by the distribution system. There is no sun to look toward.

With so little change of elevation, small depressions become important places. A basin half a meter deep can have its own temperature, dust, and living community. Some hold beds of translucent scales laid over one another like discarded fingernails. Others are filled with soft black granules that creep slowly toward the rim, pause there, then return to the center.

Those black basins are best crossed on the old bridges: short strips of pale material, barely raised above the surface. A foot placed in the granules is accepted without resistance. When lifted, it finds itself heavier. The grains are redistributing mass from somewhere else in the plain, and they do not distinguish between a machine part and a boot.

Usually they let go once the load has balanced.

Usually takes time.

The vegetation grows along boundaries where materials disagree. A seam between two plates may support a narrow hedge of wiry stems, no higher than the ankle, its leaves folded into silver wedges. The roots enter the join and branch through the fittings below. During the warm fronts, the wedges close. In the cooler wake they open, exposing dull red interiors that absorb light and electrical leakage.

These plants use almost no water. Their tissues are slow ionic gels enclosed in tough membranes; their nourishment comes from the gradual chemical exhaustion of the ground. They grow where one alloy touches another, where a seal is failing, where a buried conductor still holds a slight charge.

A healthy stand marks a defect.

There are long stretches where the plain is too perfectly repaired to support anything living.

Elsewhere the growth becomes unexpectedly dense. Around a broad, low mound, silver stems crowd into bands, interrupted by bare lanes a finger wide. The mound has been lifting by a few millimeters each season for centuries. Its surface is a patchwork of fresh plates and softened old ones, and as it rises, new faults open around its base. Plants occupy them before the maintenance systems can close them.

This is one of the few places where the silence has texture. The leaves tick as they unfold. Dust touches their undersides with a sound like sand passing over paper. Under the mound, something changes position at long intervals, producing a faint vibration felt through the soles.

Most work here happens without noise.

Across the plain, plates are constantly being exchanged. A worn section sinks until its rim is flush with the surrounding seams. For a while there is an open rectangle, revealing densely packed components beneath: pale rods, flexible meshes, dark surfaces carrying minute points of light. Then a new plate rises from below.

The operation may take three days. It may take a minute. There is no visible crew, and very little displaced air.

In the distance these replacements appear as isolated flashes, as though someone were turning small mirrors toward the sky. Close by, they have an unsettling intimacy. A square of ground on which one has rested a hand goes down into darkness and comes back different.

Despite this activity, much of the surface is profoundly old. The plain has settled into broad, stable forms that repair no longer changes. Former towers survive as flattened ovals, their substance absorbed into successive layers. Vast circular foundations appear only as differences in grain direction. An ancient wall has become a low ridge extending beyond the visible distance, high enough to shelter a ribbon of dust and support a few plants on its cooler side.

Some seams curve around spaces where there is nothing at all. Their fittings face inward. Every surrounding plate has been replaced many times, but the empty spaces retain their exact dimensions.

A traveler can stand in one and feel the heat withdraw from their body, gently and steadily, as if the absent object is still being cooled.

Animals are rare enough that their movement looks, at first, like another maintenance process.

The most commonly seen is a flat, six-limbed creature the color of tarnished foil. It shelters beneath the silver growths, where it feeds on shed membranes and on the tiny organisms that graze the leaves. Its back carries a loose arrangement of scales gathered from the ground. When a warming front approaches, it climbs to the highest available edge and spreads itself thin.

Several may lie on a single rim, almost touching.

They remain there until the heat has passed, then slip down and vanish into gaps too narrow to seem habitable.

Larger tracks are occasionally found in the powder: paired indentations, widely spaced, accompanied by a continuous brush mark. They cross open ground directly, ignoring seams and bridges. Whatever makes them does not need the cooler channels. The tracks tend to end at a plate that has recently been replaced.

No one has seen enough of the animal to know whether it enters the opening or comes out of it.

Toward the edge of one weather front, the ground grows crowded with low fins. There are thousands in sight, arranged so closely that passage requires turning sideways. Their tops are rounded, their flanks scored with fine parallel lines. Warm air moves between them without much wind above. A traveler descending into this field loses the horizon and encounters a different scale: pale surfaces close to the face, narrow views, the faint smell of heated ceramic.

The fins transfer heat into another part of the landscape.

Occasionally a narrow band crosses them, and every fin it touches darkens. The band continues through the field, over a basin, beneath a ridge. The warmth disappears with it. A few minutes later, far out on the open plain, a patch of air begins to shimmer.

There are many such exchanges. Weight passes through basins. Heat travels along invisible connections. Worn material descends and returns with its defects removed. Dust lifts in orderly sheets, migrating toward distant collecting seams. The continent is active everywhere, but its movements are distributed over so much space that one can spend an hour watching a single join widen and feel that nothing has happened.

Rest is easiest beside the old ridges. Here the upper systems have conceded a little unevenness. The powder is deep enough to take a footprint. A few silver leaves open overhead at the height of a seated person’s knee. From this low position, the brightness of the plain is hidden, and the underside of the vegetation shows red through the dust.

A foil-backed animal may come out and examine a crumb of clothing fiber.

Beyond the ridge, another bright front approaches. Its leading edge picks out the plate rims one after another, giving the ground a brief appearance of waves. The leaves fold. The small animal withdraws. Under the traveler’s hand, the ancient ridge begins to warm, and far away a fresh panel rises into place so smoothly that its arrival can only be seen by the change in color.
