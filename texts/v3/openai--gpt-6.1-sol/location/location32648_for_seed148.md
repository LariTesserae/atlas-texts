The town occupies a hollow in a single leaf.

The leaf has not finished growing. Its edges stand a little higher than its center, forming a shallow basin perhaps two hundred paces across, though distances change when the veins contract. Beyond the rim there is darkness, cool enough to sting the inside of the nose. No trunk can be seen. No neighboring leaves. Occasionally something immense passes beyond the edge and brings a draft smelling of cut stems, but the inhabitants do not agree on whether it is weather.

Underfoot, the surface is pale, firm, and faintly yielding. It resembles stone until pressure brings out its grain: long bundles of translucent fiber, densely packed, with green fluid moving slowly inside them. There is no soil. Everything that would ordinarily be soil—support, nutrition, darkness for roots—is supplied by other living tissue.

Even the dust is alive. Disturbed by a footstep, it crawls back into the seams.

The streets are broad and nearly empty. They divide the basin into a dozen municipal blocks, most of which contain nothing but air. There are street corners without buildings, foundations without walls, doorways standing upright at the ends of long avenues. This sparseness is not abandonment. The town is being grown according to a plan that gives priority to the spaces between things. First the avenues, then the public squares, then the rooms; their contents will come later, if the necessary emptiness can be preserved.

For now, the place has the proportions of a city and the population of a garden shed.

Its buildings are made from leaves folded while young and persuaded to remain that way. They have flat façades, vertical windows, regular floor heights. The living material has been trained to imitate masonry so precisely that each block carries the outline of individual bricks. At close range, these outlines pulse. The mortar is a narrow strip of younger growth.

Some buildings are no more than shells. Through their windows one can see the windows on the other side and, beyond those, a second street. Inside others, partitions bud overnight. A room may become a corridor by morning; a corridor may close around a forgotten chair. Growth is carefully regulated here. Left unattended, a building would become one solid green body, perfectly healthy and impossible to enter.

The town’s most important workers are its pruners.

They move in the early hours, though the early hours are distinguished from the late ones only by a slight cooling of the air. Their shears have ceramic blades and handles grown to fit their hands. They trim stairwells, separate houses sharing a vascular system, reopen alleys sealed by new tissue. On municipal buildings, they cut away the small mouths that sometimes appear near the lintels.

There is a rule against allowing architecture to acquire an appetite.

It is not always enforced in the poorer streets.

Water arrives irregularly. There is never enough to make the basin a pond. Instead, moisture condenses along the undersides of cornices and gathers in the joints of the paving. A gutter may hold a tablespoon of clear liquid for an entire week. At certain hours, the streets sweat. Then every surface glistens briefly, and the town smells like a snapped cabbage stem.

Rain, when it comes, falls upward.

It begins as beads rising from the pavement. These elongate, let go, and ascend through the air, carrying faint reflections of streets that have not yet been built. People put out shallow bowls beneath awnings to catch the last ordinary drops before they depart. Water already in flight cannot be recovered. A hand passed through it returns dry and several seconds older.

Most of the town’s water is stored in the dark flesh beneath the streets. The central pumping station draws it up through three thick veins and distributes it by pressure. This station is the largest building in the basin: a low, rectangular hall whose roof bows gently with each stroke of the pumps.

The machinery is vegetable too, but it is unmistakably machinery.

A line of revolving seed drums drives belts of tough, flexible bark. Pistons slide within polished chambers lined with wax. The flywheel is a dense spiral of woody tissue, heavy enough to continue turning long after the workers shut the valves. Metal is scarce. It appears mostly as a tooth, a contact, a bright pin where living material cannot be trusted to hold a precise edge.

Electricity comes from stacks of thin, sour membranes housed in ribbed cabinets. Their current is weak but steady. It operates the street signals, the nursery clocks, and the lamps.

There are only seven lamps.

They give off a dark green light that does not travel far. Beneath one, you can see the pavement, your hands, the lowest part of a wall. Above it, the rest of the building remains invisible. A person crossing between lamps disappears for so long that you begin to wonder whether they have taken another street.

The darkness has texture. Near the ground it is granular, crowded with tiny moving points. Higher up it is smooth. At roof level it occasionally folds.

There are animals everywhere, although almost none remain visible for long.

Moths the size of fingernails roost inside the lamp cabinets, feeding on the warm electrical membranes. Transparent millipedes travel beneath the paving in such numbers that their movement produces a faint, collective shimmer. Small, long-bodied grazers browse the soft growth on neglected walls; they have the color of unripe pears and delicate feet that leave no marks. Their jaws open sideways.

Predators wait inside doorframes. They are thin enough to resemble the cracks between panels, and for most of the day that is what they are. When a grazer approaches, the crack detaches from the wall.

There are also birds, or animals that fill the town’s idea of birds. They have hollow bones and leaflike wings, but they do not fly through the open air. They enter one patch of shadow and emerge from another. At times, a whole flock can be heard crossing a square that appears completely empty: a soft succession of wingbeats, moving overhead at the height of a roof that has not grown yet.

The abundant life does not make the town crowded. Most of it inhabits the thickness of things. A wall contains burrows, tunnels, galleries, nesting chambers. The underside of a stair supports a grazing country no larger than a hand. There may be a thousand animals within reach, and only one on the street.

Their activity is mostly quiet. The smallest creatures work with patient, continuous motions. They scrape wax from stems, carry scraps of dead tissue into the foundations, pollinate the municipal crop, lay eggs in warm bearings. At night—which is also most of the day—their combined movement can be felt through the soles of the feet.

The pumping station gives an occasional soft knock. Belts whisper. Somewhere a valve opens with a sound like a person swallowing.

Otherwise, the loudest thing is usually a drop of water.

The ground is very young. It has no accumulated history of frost, flood, or wear. A cut in the paving does not erode; its edges remain sharp until they heal. Channels are opened by splitting tissue along a prescribed line, and the streets beyond them are still deciding which side is downhill.

This uncertainty causes practical difficulties. A drainage ditch may begin carrying water in the wrong direction. An entire block can tilt by a degree while the surveyors are at lunch. Once, a public square developed a slight crest and had to be pressed flat beneath weighted sheets.

The inhabitants keep records of such events in a small office beside the pumping station. Its filing cabinets bear fruit: hard, square pods with dates embossed on their skins. Inside each is a folded membrane recording the measurements taken that day.

Older records are less reliable. As the pods ripen, some of the distances lengthen. Addresses migrate between streets. On particularly cool mornings, a map may show the town before it existed, with every avenue intact and no substance beneath it.

Visitors are told to consult only fresh maps.

They are also told not to walk along an unlit street if they can see its far end.

A properly finished street should diminish into darkness. One that remains visible all the way to its boundary has not yet acquired depth. It may be only a flat arrangement on the surface of the leaf. Crossing it can leave a person spread thinly over several blocks, still conscious, unable to find the thickness needed to stand up.

The pruners have techniques for such accidents. These involve warm water, shade, and the careful lifting of an edge.

At the center of town lies an open square. No house faces directly onto it. No lamp illuminates it. The paving there is smooth and tender, a color almost visible in the dark. Beneath its surface, something is assembling itself.

You can feel it if you sit quietly beside the square: the slow placement of mass, the shifting of small internal chambers, a minute warmth moving from one place to another. Animals gather around it without entering. The pumping station sends it a measured ration of water every hour.

The town council calls it the next phase of construction.

The pruners keep their shears sharpened.

For now, the square remains empty. Cool air slides across it. A moth passes from one lamp to another; a belt turns in the factory; somewhere inside a wall, a predator folds itself back into a seam. At the rim of the basin, the unfinished leaf makes a small new portion of itself, pushing outward into the dark.

By morning there will be room for another street.
