Behind the last occupied floor of the mountain city, between the lift shaft and the outer wall, there is a court no wider than a small room. You reach it through a maintenance door, down three metal steps. It does not appear on the public maps. From the street, it is hidden by the building’s white ceramic ribs; from above, by the overhang of a reservoir.

The court has no roof of its own. A narrow strip of sky lies overhead, bright enough at noon to cast a clean shadow from every screw head. Beyond it, unseen, the mountain rises another thousand metres.

The floor is mostly bare rock.

When the building was anchored, its foundations cut through a seam of pale granite. Rather than excavate the whole seam, the builders left this piece exposed: a sloping slab, a thumb-shaped ridge, and a hollow about the size of a washbasin. The cut edges are still sharp. Along the wall, you can see where a drill passed through a vein of black crystal, leaving half-round grooves as regular as the marks of a comb. Nothing has yet weathered enough to soften them.

Water is beginning that work.

It comes from three places. A pipe beneath the reservoir releases a measured trickle. Meltwater creeps down behind the outer wall and emerges through its joints. And the air itself gives up moisture wherever the metal stays colder than the stone. By evening, the handrail is covered in droplets, each holding a small, inverted piece of the court.

The water never occupies the whole floor. It follows narrow routes, darkening the granite in branching lines. Most lead toward the hollow. One disappears beneath a metal plate and can be heard, if you kneel beside it, dropping into a space below.

The hollow freezes first around its rim. Its centre often remains open, a patch of dark water no larger than a palm. There is heat somewhere underneath: not enough to warm the court, only enough to keep this small opening from closing. On very cold mornings, the ice grows inward in clear shelves. The fresh trickle runs across them, adding a skin so thin that it bends before it breaks.

There are no leaves caught in the ice, no moss along the waterline. The nearest cultivated plants are several floors below, inside sealed rooms. Here, even the dust is mostly mineral: powdered granite, fragments of ceramic coating, the fine grey grit carried up from the streets. After rain, it settles in little fans behind the raised bolts.

The surrounding city is present without being visible.

Its water mains pass through one wall. Its freight lifts move behind another. Under the granite lies a layer that adjusts the building’s weight across its foundations, transferring loads before the rock can fracture. You cannot hear this happen. Occasionally, however, a puddle becomes slightly oval, then round again. A loose crystal grain moves a few millimetres uphill. The instruments inside the wall have detected a strain somewhere far below and answered it.

Most of the machinery has no exposed moving parts. The drain is a smooth ceramic circle, almost flush with the stone. It draws water without a whirlpool. Twice each day it reverses, sending a clear bead upward until the bead swells, slips free, and spreads across the floor. This washes grit out of the intake. For a minute afterward, every wet surface shines.

The court remains cold through all of this. Touching the steps with an ungloved hand leaves a dull ache in the fingertips. In winter, frost thickens beneath the reservoir overhang, where daylight reaches but direct sun never does. The frost is loose and white on the wall, transparent and hard on the pipes. A thaw does not clear it all at once. Small sections release at different hours, and their falls leave fresh dark rectangles on the metal.

Despite the cold, something is always happening.

A drop gathers beneath a bracket. Water advances along a hairline crack. A piece of ice lifts from the rock as the seam beneath it fills. Wind crosses the opening overhead and draws a brief current through the court, enough to stir the suspended drops but not enough to make them fall. Then the air settles again.

The sounds are small and widely separated. A click from a valve. One hard drip. The dry tick of cooling metal. The lifts behind the wall are more felt than heard, a passing pressure through the soles of your shoes. Sometimes a gust finds the gap under the door and gives a low note, cut short as soon as the pressure equalizes.

There is little animal life. Once in a while, a small grey bird enters from the roof, lands on the reservoir lip, and drinks from the unfrozen trickle. It stays only a few seconds. Its claws leave four wet marks on the pale surface. On warmer afternoons, a winged insect may arrive through the open maintenance door and settle near the drain, where the ceramic retains a trace of heat.

Neither has a permanent shelter here. The court offers water, a little warmth, and almost nothing else.

Late in the day, sunlight reaches the upper wall and moves downward. It picks out the fresh crystal faces in the granite, then the beads on the rail, then a narrow strip of wet floor. For perhaps ten minutes, the basin glows from within: light enters through the open centre, strikes the pale stone below, and returns through the ice.

By then the reservoir has begun its evening release. The trickle strengthens. New water runs beneath the frozen shelves, loosening them from the rim. One turns slowly, catches against another, and stops.

When the light leaves, the shelves freeze together in their new positions. The court becomes quiet again, though the water continues moving underneath.
