The high country begins well below the horizon.

What looks at first like a bank of cloud is the edge of the plateau: pale rock rising through several layers of atmosphere, its lower slopes lost in suspended dust. Above it, another rim catches the light, and another beyond that. There is no single summit. The land has been lifted so broadly that whole regions stand at the height where mountains elsewhere would end.

Up here the sky is dark blue, almost violet overhead. The sunlight is white and extraordinarily clear. A pebble casts a sharp shadow several times its own length; the underside of a leaf is a small, cool night.

Nothing glitters with wetness.

The ground is made of an old, porous mineral, ivory in some places, yellow-gray in others, with thin seams of green running through it. Its exposed surfaces have worn into generous shapes: low-backed ridges, shallow bowls, wide steps descending into basins whose far sides disappear through the curvature of the land. There are no fresh, spectacular wounds. Even the great escarpments have rounded shoulders. Wind has been working here for so long that sharpness survives mostly in things that are growing.

Across the bare expanses, the rock is polished to a dull sheen. Loose grains collect in long, narrow lanes. From a distance these resemble roads, but they branch wherever the air divides, stop at the lee of a stone, and sometimes move a little during the afternoon.

Between these expanses are the gardens.

They occupy sheltered depressions, the northern feet of ridges, and broad fissures where the bedrock gives off a faint, bitter vapor. Some are no larger than the shade beneath an overhang. Others cover entire basins. Seen from above, they are deep green stains scattered across a continent of bone-colored stone.

Inside one, the growth is almost extravagant.

Tall fronds rise from cushions of densely folded leaves. Their stems are translucent amber, their edges lined with fine white fibers. Beneath them spread thick mats of small, blue-green tongues, overlapping so closely that the ground cannot be seen. There are branching plants with the soft silhouette of cedar, but each branch is assembled from flattened blades that can turn independently. Low shrubs carry heavy clusters of violet sacs. In the coolest pockets, pale fans hang from the rock faces, layered one behind another until a crevice becomes a soft wall.

There is no damp soil beneath this abundance. Roots enter the stone itself, following hairline cracks and the old boundaries between mineral grains. They draw up compounds released slowly from the plateau’s interior. The plants keep those compounds in dense, oily sap, protected from the thin air by wax and silver down. Water is a trace substance here, locked away rather than gathered into pools. Nothing has to spend its life waiting for rain.

The tallest growth reaches only a few times the height of a person. Beyond each lush hollow, open country resumes abruptly. One step takes you from crowded foliage into bright mineral emptiness. Ten steps farther on, the garden’s scent has almost vanished.

It smells faintly of crushed needles and cold resin, with something sharper underneath.

The air never becomes warm. At midday the sun heats exposed surfaces enough to loosen tiny flakes from the rock, but shade remains cold, and the wind carries the chill of enormous altitude. Along the garden edges, plants hold their leaves nearly vertical. Deeper inside, where neighboring stems break the wind, they open wide.

Their movements are slow enough to miss and numerous enough to change the whole place. A basin that looked dark green in the morning can be silver by noon, every leaf presenting its reflective underside. In late light it darkens again. The change passes across the vegetation in bands as shadows reach it.

Weather has room to develop on the plateau.

Far away, pale columns of dust stand over sunlit basins. They travel for hours without reaching the nearest ridge. Some unravel into veils; others join a high, diffuse front that stretches across the sky. Beneath these fronts, cooler air slides close to the ground, carrying mineral powder and the fine reproductive threads of plants.

There are no storm clouds swollen with rain. The largest weather systems are dry: sheets of suspended dust, pressure changes, slow invasions of cold. Occasionally the sky whitens from horizon to horizon. The sunlight becomes broad and directionless, and distances cease to make sense. A ridge a day’s journey away seems close enough to touch, while a nearby garden fades into a dim green patch.

Then the particles settle.

For a few hours the foliage is furred in white. Small leaves bend beneath the load. Larger fronds tilt and shed it into the roots, where the powder is gradually consumed. Along the lee side of every hollow, new growth is thickest.

Much is happening, but very little of it is loud.

Wind passes over the open stone with a low, continuous hush. In the gardens it makes a dry brushing sound. A stalk may click as it turns. A loosening slab may give a single deep knock, followed much later by the faint patter of fragments arriving below. There is no running water to fill the spaces between these sounds.

Animals are uncommon, and their absence leaves no sense of vacancy. Most of the visible life is rooted.

Once in a long while, a low-bodied grazer moves through a garden. It has six narrow legs and a back covered in overlapping, chalk-colored plates. At rest among fallen stones, it is nearly impossible to distinguish. It feeds on the violet sacs, opening them with two small hooked mouthparts and leaving the outer skin behind.

A grazer may spend an entire day crossing one hollow. Between hollows it travels mostly in darkness, when the bare stone releases its stored warmth. Its routes follow shallow folds in the land, where the wind is weaker. There are journeys here that take a season and end at a patch of vegetation visible from the starting point.

Under some of the broad leaf cushions live much smaller creatures. Their presence is betrayed by a stem dipping, a thread drawn inward, a brief disturbance in the white dust. On certain cold mornings, slender winged animals rise a little way above the fronds. They hold themselves in place without an audible hum, then drift down again. Their shadows move over the rock like detached seeds.

The gardens do not stay where they are forever.

Over generations, roots widen fractures. The underside of a ledge becomes lacework; eventually it settles, gently or all at once. A vapor-bearing seam is exposed, and vegetation spreads along it. Elsewhere a seam is exhausted. The tallest fronds diminish, the ground cushions thin, and sunlight returns to stone that has been shaded for centuries.

Old garden basins can be recognized by their darkened rock and the intricate grooves left by roots. Some contain only a few tough plants holding on at the edges. Others have been empty for so long that wind has nearly erased the grooves.

From a high ridge, all these stages are visible together: a young green ribbon following a fresh crack; a dense basin slowly turning silver in the afternoon; a pale hollow with only scattered tufts remaining. Between them lie immense stretches of exposed country, apparently still, crossed by moving dust.

Late in the day, the escarpments cast shadows large enough to contain weather. Cold air gathers there and begins its long outward passage. The gardens close by degrees. Violet sacs draw tight against their stems. Fronds fold along their central ribs. Far below the rim, the lower atmosphere turns peach-colored, then brown.

The plateau remains bright after the lowlands have disappeared.

For a while, every west-facing leaf shines. Every shallow groove in the rock is outlined. The sparse animals have already become stones again.

Then the light withdraws from one ridge to the next, revealing how much country is still beyond them.
