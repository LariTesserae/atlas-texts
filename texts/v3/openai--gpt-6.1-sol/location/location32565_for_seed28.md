Beneath one paving stone in the sun court is a city.

The stone is large enough for two people to stand on, though nobody stands there now. Its upper face has been worn almost white. A chipped corner admits sunlight into the hollow beneath it, and other, finer cracks divide that light into blades. At noon the city is bright enough that its inhabitants used to wear smoked mica over their eyes.

There are no inhabitants left in the streets. There is no particular sign that they fled.

The hollow is shallow: a low basin scooped into the pale mineral beneath the court, with a few rises scarcely higher than its houses. From the broken corner, the ground descends through a succession of terraces to a central square. Everything has the scale of something made to be held in a palm. Doorways are the width of seeds. A staircase winds around a thumb-sized column in forty painstaking steps.

Yet there is nothing crude about the arrangement. Streets meet at angles chosen to interrupt the wind. Roofs overlap without quite touching. Covered passages connect the workshops to the square, and the square to a ring of broad, unbuilt ground. The city understands its site. It has been fitted into this small hollow with an exactness that suggests generations of adjustment: a wall shifted a little farther from a fissure, an alley widened after some long-ago obstruction, a roof rebuilt lower where the paving stone above it began to sag.

Beyond the last houses, most of the basin is empty.

Not rubble, not a wilderness of abandoned possessions. Simply bare ground, fine-grained and faintly yellow, extending in long, clear intervals between a few isolated structures. A watch platform. A kiln. A row of posts with no fence between them. Their shadows reach much farther than their builders could have thrown anything.

The vegetation grows on the roofs.

It is lush there, extravagantly so. Green ribbons spill over parapets and gather beneath the eaves. Thick, jointed stems crawl across lintels. In the richest patches, several layers of foliage obscure the house below, leaving only a doorway open under a hanging fringe.

The leaves are not soft. They are thin, resilient mineral plates, green where light passes through them and nearly black where they overlap. Each has a dark seam along its underside. That seam opens during the hottest part of the day, exposing a row of delicate red combs.

There is no water in the basin. No dampness under the foliage, no cool soil beneath the houses. Lift a fallen leaf and it leaves a clean print in the dust.

The plants feed on a gas that seeps from hairline faults in the floor. It has a bitter, metallic smell, strongest near the kilns. Their roots are bundles of hard white fibers that enter the faults and slowly force them wider. What they draw upward becomes leaf, stem, and a waxy amber deposit that appears at the joints. The deposit can be scraped off with a blade. In several workshops, shallow clay trays still hold it in cakes.

The old roof gardens were laid out to bring roots to the seams in the house walls. Channels cut into the plaster lead downward, toward the breathing ground. Some have become swollen with roots; others remain empty, waiting.

Away from a fissure, nothing grows. A seed may lie for a century on the bare floor without changing. The city is green in concentrated, overflowing patches, and between those patches there is space enough to see each grain of dust move.

You hear the hollow before you see any of this.

At the broken corner, the sound comes out so forcefully that it seems the paving stone ought to tremble. It is a continuous, deafening roar, thick with higher notes: a shriek that climbs, breaks, begins again; a clatter like thousands of dry teeth; beneath them both, a deep tone too steady to resemble any animal.

Inside, the source becomes less obvious rather than more.

Warm air enters through cracks along one side of the stone and escapes through the other. The city was built across that passage. Every roof has slots. Every chimney narrows into a shaped mouth. Clay pipes pass through walls and emerge beneath small stone hoods. The wind plays them all.

In the morning, the western streets roar. Toward noon, the central square begins to sing. Late in the day, a descending whistle travels through the workshops, house by house, as their walls release stored heat. Loose leaves chatter against the tiles. The mineral vines tighten in the warmth, shifting little wooden shutters that have outlasted the hands that hung them.

Some shutters bear rows of notches. Others are weighted with beads. These are not decorations. Even now, they regulate the air: opening a passage, choking another, diverting a hot draft away from a roof garden. The city’s machinery is clay, cord, wood, and leverage. Most of it can be understood by looking closely. Understanding it does not make its noise easier to endure.

In a narrow street beside the square, there is a surviving workbench. A stone knife rests in a groove worn specifically for it. Beside the knife lie three bored shells, a coil of root fiber, and a small clay mouthpiece whose lip has been repaired twice. The repairs are careful. Whoever worked here knew how to restore a particular sound.

Perhaps the inhabitants spoke between tones. Perhaps they put their hands on walls to hear one another through the material. At the entrances to several houses, paired hollows have been worn into the plaster at about shoulder height. Nothing establishes what made them.

Time is easier to read elsewhere. The stairs have rounded fronts. Fine dust has polished the narrow alleys, carried back and forth by the same daily drafts. A fallen cornice has weathered into a low ridge, and the paving around it has been repaired rather than cleared. The oldest vines follow that ridge, treating it as part of the ground.

Very little lives here apart from the foliage. Occasionally a pale six-legged creature crosses a wall, stopping whenever a note changes. Its feet grip the mineral leaf edges. It eats the amber deposits, leaving small, squared bites. There may be only a handful of these creatures in the whole city. Their paths do not often meet.

Under a porch near the central square is a deeper recess. The light barely reaches it. There, among roots and worn steps, the roar is reduced to a heavy humming that can be felt through the soles of the feet. Dust settles instead of traveling. A few young leaves have grown broad and flat in the shade, green as cut glass.

From this recess, the city appears to be operating.

A shutter lifts. Sunlight enters a room through a roof slot. A vine, heated along one side, draws itself slowly across a lintel. Somewhere beyond the square, a pipe clears an obstruction with a violent cough, and a small cloud of yellow powder rises into the light.

Then the afternoon wind takes hold. The loose shutters settle into new positions. The streets fill with a different chord.

Above it all, the paving stone lies flat in the court, warm and apparently solid. A little dust stirs at its broken corner. Anyone passing might hear a whistle there and mistake it for an insect, without stopping to look.
