The hollow is behind the waterfall, but not behind the falling water itself. It lies several paces to one side, entered through a slit where a slab of limestone has eased away from the bank. From the stream, the opening looks like an ordinary dark seam beneath some ferns. You could sit beside it for an afternoon and never notice that there is room inside.

To enter, you turn one shoulder forward and step down onto a stone worn smooth in the middle. The next step is lower than expected. After that, the floor levels, and the rock opens into a chamber about the size of a small house.

There is almost nothing in it.

The ceiling is high enough to disappear in the darkness. The walls retreat beyond the light admitted by the slit, leaving a broad, unoccupied middle. No fallen heap fills it, no thicket reaches across it. Even the few loose stones lie against the edges, as though they have been gently sorted there over a very long time. A person standing inside has the curious impression of having entered somewhere that was cleared, although most of the clearing was done by water, frost, and gravity before anyone found it.

The waterfall cannot be seen from here. It can be heard with the whole body.

Its noise comes through the limestone as well as through the entrance: a deep, continuous impact beneath a higher, tearing hiss. The chamber strengthens some parts of it and swallows others. Near the back wall there is a low note that presses against the ribs. By the doorway, the sound is sharper, almost metallic. Two people standing an arm’s length apart would have to shout, and even then the ends of their sentences would vanish. A dropped pebble makes no audible sound. Neither do footsteps.

Yet very little moves.

The waterfall runs in a neighboring groove of the rock, close enough to shake this enclosed air but not close enough to flood the floor. Its spray reaches the entrance only when the wind turns upstream. Most days the interior is merely damp around its margins, with a dry patch in the center where pale dust has collected between the stones. The water is elsewhere; the noise is here.

It is cold in a way that outlasts the weather. Even on a mild afternoon, the floor takes warmth quickly from a bare hand. In winter, thin ice forms in the entrance seam, feathering inward from the wet rock. The chamber itself seldom freezes hard. There is too little moving air, and the stone keeps the same deep chill for weeks after the world outside has changed.

The vegetation gathers where the light gives out.

At the mouth, moss covers the lower stones in thick, close cushions. Ferns lean from pockets of black soil above the slit, their old fronds flattened beneath newer growth. Small evergreen leaves occupy the sheltered ledge outside, glossy with spray when the rest of the bank is dry. Roots pass through cracks no wider than a knife cut. One fern grows almost sideways, its crown wedged under the detached slab, its fronds reaching toward a dim strip of sky.

A few paces inside, this abundance diminishes to isolated green patches. Farther back, there are only dark stains and the occasional pale thread of a root. The boundary is gradual enough to miss at first. Looking out from the chamber, however, the vegetation appears as a dense, intricate lining around the entrance. Beyond it is a narrow glimpse of gray streambank. Everything else is stone and vacant air.

The stone has been worked by long, repeated weather rather than by any recent collapse. Its projecting corners are rounded. Shallow channels run down the walls, ending above the floor where the last drops usually evaporate. In places the limestone has folded-looking ridges that are hard to distinguish from mineral deposits. A rusty band crosses one wall at knee height; beneath it is a small polished basin that holds no more than a cupful of water.

No stream feeds the basin. Moisture gathers above it, slowly enough that you might wait a long time for a single drop. When one finally falls, you see the surface move, but hear nothing.

People have left a few things here.

Three shallow cuts in the entrance rock make uncertain footholds. They are irregular, struck by hand, and their outer edges have worn soft. Along one wall lies a low arrangement of flat stones, too deliberate to be a natural fall and too simple to say much about its purpose. It might have supported a plank. It might have kept bedding above the damp floor.

In a recess is a broken earthen bowl, its thick rim darkened on one side. There is also a short wooden peg driven into a crack. The exposed end is polished, though whether by hands or by years of damp air is difficult to tell. These small signs do not make the place feel occupied. They make the emptiness more particular: someone knew which patch stayed dry, where a light could be sheltered, which step needed improving.

There is little soot on the ceiling. Perhaps fires were kept small. Perhaps anyone who stayed here soon learned that smoke had only the narrow entrance to escape through.

Animals use the margins without making much of a home of them. A small bird sometimes searches the wet moss outside, hopping along the ledge and disappearing behind the fern fronds. Slugs move beneath the fallen leaves. In the warmer part of the year, a few insects settle on the wall just inside the slit. Their presence is easy to overlook. Nothing crosses the open floor regularly enough to leave a path.

The place changes by increments. A root thickens in its crack. A grain loosens from the ceiling. The lip of the little basin acquires another nearly invisible layer of mineral. After heavy rain, the waterfall’s deeper roar rises until the floor seems to hold a faint vibration; during dry weather, the same chamber fills with a thinner, harder rush.

But the hollow remains much as it is: cold, dark, sparsely furnished, with green growth crowding its narrow threshold. Outside, the stream keeps going. Inside, there is room to stand still, and enough noise that even your breathing becomes private.
