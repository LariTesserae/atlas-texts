The plateau is high enough that the horizon falls away on every side, but there is no coolness in the height. Heat comes from below as steadily as light comes from above. It passes through the soles of your feet, through any cloth you lay on the ground, through the underside of your shadow. At midday the air is clear enough to see fifty miles. The distances do not shimmer. Instead, near objects occasionally acquire the pale blue cast of things seen from far away.

Most of the country is empty.

A long slope of dun-colored mineral rises to a rounded crest. Beyond it lies another slope, then a shallow basin floored with white powder, then a ridge so worn that its highest point is scarcely distinguishable from its shoulders. There are no sharp summits. Whatever broke this land happened long ago; wind, heat, and the slow movement of its inhabitants have rubbed the fractures down. Even the exposed faults have smooth lips.

The ground looks soft until you touch it. It is a hard, fine-grained ceramic, faintly translucent at the edges of broken pieces. Here and there a darker seam crosses it, polished black by something that has passed along the same route for centuries. Dust collects in the shallow hollows. It smells of hot metal and, when disturbed, produces a brief yellow spark.

Nothing grows upright. Nothing makes shade.

There are no channels that suggest water. The depressions are broad and shallow, their rims softened equally on all sides. No rain has sorted the debris. A chip of red stone remains beside a chip of white stone until an animal moves them, or until the land slowly grinds them both into powder. The air is so dry that an open mouth begins to feel like another exposed mineral surface.

Yet the place is full of animals.

At first you hear them without seeing any.

A bass note fills the plateau, too continuous to resemble a call. It enters through your jaw and ribs. Above it are several narrower sounds: a high metallic whine; the measured knock of something large reciprocating; a hiss that swells for an hour, subsides, and swells again. Different basins have different pitches. Crossing a ridge is like stepping between the open doors of enormous workshops, although there are no doors and no workshops.

The noises travel through the ground better than through the air. A stone held between your teeth makes the distant mechanisms painfully distinct.

The first animal you recognize is a line on a hillside.

It runs horizontally for perhaps half a mile, a thin charcoal stroke beneath a pale shelf. While you watch, one end detaches itself from the rock. Then the other. The whole line lifts a few inches and settles higher on the slope.

It is a grazer.

Its body is flatter than a sheet of roofing metal, flexible in some places and rigid in others, with hundreds of short gripping limbs along its lower edge. Its mouth occupies nearly its entire length. It feeds on the difference in temperature between the sunlit skin of the hill and the hotter stone beneath. Once every several days it changes position, exposing a band of subtly bleached ground.

There are thousands of them. You begin to distinguish their feeding marks on slopes you thought were bare. Some are no wider than a finger; others extend around the far side of a ridge. They leave almost no silhouette.

Farther down, a circular patch of white powder turns with astonishing slowness.

It takes most of the afternoon to establish that it is turning at all. The patch is the upper surface of a buried animal. Around its circumference, tiny teeth draw dust beneath the crust. There the dust is sorted, crushed, heated, and separated into its constituent metals. A narrow opening on the downhill side periodically releases a perfect dark pellet.

Other creatures come for the pellets. They arrive singly, hours apart: six-legged things the size of a hand, with heavy copper-colored backs and transparent joints. Each lifts a pellet, fits it into an opening beneath its body, and waits while something inside begins to spin. Then it leaves, walking along one of the black seams.

These are roads, but no one laid them.

They are the accumulated residue of traffic: lubricants baked into the stone, conductive scales shed from bellies, powdered metal pressed smooth by generations of feet. At a junction the seam may be broad enough to lie down on. It remains hot after sunset.

The animals use electricity openly. Some trail delicate branching conductors over the ground. Others hold their limbs apart and draw an arc between them, a blue-white thread that clicks in the dry air. On the exposed ridges, immense jointed creatures stand almost motionless, charging themselves from the electrical difference between earth and sky. Their bodies contain rotating assemblies, pressure chambers, ceramic bearings. You can hear a bearing fail from miles away.

Nothing here was manufactured by a separate maker. The machinery is flesh, and the flesh is capable of tolerances that would seem impossible in a living body.

Reproduction is slow and exacting. In a sheltered hollow, a parent extrudes a row of nearly identical casings, then spends a season passing minerals into them through a movable feeding head. For years the young remain fastened to its underside. There are scars where older offspring have detached: neat empty sockets, still warm.

Predators are harder to locate. Their sound is easier to recognize.

When one enters a basin, the high whine disappears.

The bass continues. The buried grinders continue. But all the little sounds stop, and in the resulting absence you hear a soft, irregular tapping. It seems to come from directly behind you, however often you turn.

A predator occupies very little space. It may be a black angular creature standing on three legs beneath a ledge. It may be a thin ring moving over the dust. The difficulty is that its body does not occupy the same location as its appetite. A grazer half a mile away can fold suddenly into a narrow crease and be gone. Much later, the predator’s abdomen expands.

Distances are dependable for walking and less dependable for feeding.

This has shaped the land. The shallow basins are places where large animals were consumed. Their weight remained after their bodies disappeared, and the ground settled under it for several centuries. Some of those hollows still deepen. Dust slides down their sides in little dry avalanches, though nothing stands at the bottom.

There are other irregularities.

An animal crossing a ridge sometimes arrives before its legs have finished leaving the previous basin. For a while you can see both ends of it, separated by miles of empty country. Shadows occasionally persist after the creatures that cast them have moved on. The older shadows are pale and ragged, worn by traffic. Small animals shelter inside them during the hottest hours.

At certain places the inhabitants accumulate without increasing the amount of matter present.

You can look at one low rise and see a single broad-backed animal resting there. Shift your head slightly and there are six. Move again and there are so many overlapping limbs that the hill resembles a slowly operating gearbox. All of them are real enough to displace dust. None seems troubled by the others. The ground bears the same weight in every view.

This is why the plateau can be so empty and so crowded. Across the visible miles, almost nothing breaks the low sweep of stone. But there is scarcely a position from which you can look without looking through an animal, or through the place where an animal is about to be.

Late in the day the light becomes amber. The upper surfaces cool by a few degrees. The grazers tighten against their feeding slopes, and current begins to flow along the black seams. Fine red points appear at the joints of creatures waiting beside them.

The sound changes very gradually. One basin lowers its note. Another answers. A vibration passes through the plateau, not a tremor but a long adjustment, as if some immense shaft beneath the country has taken up a fraction of its load.

Nothing hurries in response. A copper-backed scavenger steps over a crack. A buried mill releases its next pellet. On a distant ridge, an animal taller than a house lifts one narrow foot and holds it in the air.

By nightfall it has not put the foot down.

The plateau remains bright for a long time after the sun has gone. Heat shines weakly through the thinnest edges of the stone. The empty slopes reveal their seams, their old feeding bands, their almost invisible resting bodies. Far below, beyond the worn rim of the high country, darkness gathers in a depth that has no audible inhabitants.

Up here, the work continues.
