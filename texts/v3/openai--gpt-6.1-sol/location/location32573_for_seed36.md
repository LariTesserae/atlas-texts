The country lies just beneath the surface, shallow enough that the heat of noon reaches it, but too deep for any view of the sky.

Above it is a continuous roof of living tissue. From below, this looks like pale leaves laid edge to edge, their seams reinforced by roots as thick as tree trunks. Sunlight passes through the leaves almost undiminished. It loses its blue on the way down. Everything beneath is illuminated in clear yellow-green: the floor, the distant ridges, the undersides of branches, even the narrow spaces between roots. There are shadows, but none are dark enough to hide in.

The roof rises and falls over a basin several miles wide. In places it is low enough to touch; elsewhere it arches high above broad hollows. You can look across these hollows and see what resemble wooded hills, except that there is no hill under the wood. Root rests on root. Stems pack the spaces between them. The ground is a close intergrowth of pale rhizomes, bark plates, stiff leaves, and a fine felt of smaller growth that gives slightly underfoot.

Nothing here has become soil.

There are no loose stones, no grit at the bottom of a slope, no brown layer of old leaves. Even the deepest visible fissures contain living material. A broken surface would show green beneath the green, then white, then the pinkish translucence of something not yet ready to meet the light. The landscape has the clean, crowded character of the inside of a bud.

Yet it is not small. There are long rises with broad backs, level reaches where a person could walk for an hour, and deep corridors between banks of bundled roots. One corridor opens into a basin; another divides around a rounded mass of leaf sheaths and rejoins beyond it. From a high place, these features make a complicated but readable country. Close up, every apparent empty space proves occupied by tendrils, folded blades, or stems thin enough to be mistaken for threads.

The heat is steady. It comes down through the luminous roof and up from the thick floor. There is no cool face to a root, no relief in a hollow. Most surfaces are dry to the touch, almost papery. The air smells faintly of warm sap and the white interior of a freshly split stalk.

Moisture gathers only in certain places.

At the joins in the ceiling, clear beads remain suspended along the veins. Below them are little wells lined with waxy leaves. Some are no larger than a cupped hand; others occupy whole depressions, their water so still and shallow that the green bottom seems exposed until a reflection betrays it. The surrounding growth leans close without entering. Fine roots lie along the water’s edge in parallel rows.

Between these pockets, the country is surprisingly dry. Curled leaf margins hold their shape. Broad, leathery surfaces meet the heat without sweating. A long ribbon of dampness follows the lowest corridor, but it is not quite a stream: there is no current, only a succession of connected shining places beneath an overhang of roots. Water has found its allotted space and gone no farther.

The slopes have no worn paths. Their ribs are blunt and fresh, with no scouring at their bases. Nothing has fallen away to expose an older layer. Looking at them, you might think they had only just risen into position, and that another few days would bring a ridge to completion or open a passage through a wall of stems.

But the same unfinished shapes extend everywhere. A leaf remains half unrolled over a distant hollow. A pale swelling stops short of becoming a trunk. At the edge of a basin, dozens of root tips rest just above the floor, each with the small glossy cap of new growth.

This place stays at that stage. It is not visibly growing, nor has it aged into a forest. Its features are fully present but still soft in their outlines, as if formation itself were a condition they could inhabit indefinitely.

Here and there are shelters.

They are widely separated, usually beside water. Each consists of a low chamber under a dome of interwoven stems, with a narrow entrance and a smooth, firm floor. Their regularity distinguishes them from the surrounding hollows. Someone, or something, seems to have persuaded the growth into a useful shape.

There are no cut ends, bindings, or tool marks. The stems join by ordinary grafts. Inside, a broad leaf forms a sleeping shelf, and smaller recesses open in the wall at the height of a seated person’s hand. The chambers are empty. Their floors carry no footprints. A few have two entrances facing different corridors, though neither corridor shows signs of traffic.

Animals are rare enough that a single one changes the scale of what you are seeing. Beside a pool, you may notice a small pale creature pressed flat against a root. It has six delicate legs and a narrow head tucked beneath its body. Its outline is difficult to follow against the bark. Nothing nearby bears a bite mark. Farther on, an empty translucent casing clings to the underside of a leaf, the only evidence that anything here has passed from one form into another.

For most of the distance, there is no visible animal life at all.

The quiet is substantial but not absolute. Close to a thick stem, one can hear a low internal murmur, too soft to separate into pulses. At a water pocket there is sometimes the minute sound of a bead touching the surface. The packed growth absorbs footsteps. No branch creaks, and there is no loose foliage to rustle overhead.

The brightness makes this silence peculiar. Nothing suggests night or concealment. The deepest chamber is lit through its walls; the farthest slope remains plainly visible. Even inside a shelter, with the entrance at your back, you can see the fine veins of the leaf that forms the sleeping shelf.

From there, the country looks ready for occupation: warm, sheltered, supplied with small amounts of water, crossed by passages that need no clearing. But there is no rubbish, no smoke, no abandoned implement. The shelters stand among the living ridges as fresh as the root tips around them. Beyond their entrances, the bright corridors continue through miles of closely fitted growth, offering room without ever becoming empty.
