Beneath a stone that has not yet acquired an underside, there is a small, bright country.

It is roughly the size of the hollow made by two cupped hands. Its boundaries are difficult to locate. A finger laid across it would cover most of the sky, but an animal moving away from that finger might travel all afternoon before reaching the shade. From outside, the place appears to be a pale seam in compacted earth. You would have to kneel to notice it. You would have to leave the stone where it is.

The ground is cool and almost white. Here and there it darkens to the gray of wet flour. It has the texture of chalk without chalk’s dryness: a fine, yielding material that holds a footprint for a moment, then slowly fills it from below. Nothing here has been worn smooth. The smoothness is original. Ridges rise with round shoulders because they have not yet learned how to break.

Near the center, a little escarpment is coming into existence. All morning a lip of white earth lifts out of the plain, grain by grain. By noon it is high enough to shelter a beetle. Later, a second edge appears behind it, and the narrow space between them becomes a valley. No stream has cut this valley. The ground is making room.

There is plenty of light.

It comes through the stone overhead, although the stone is opaque. On its upper surface, in the world of roots and weather, it remains an ordinary stone, dark on one side, flecked with lichen on the other. Underneath, its thickness shines. The light has a clean, faintly yellow quality, like sunlight passing through the skin between fingers. No single spot is brighter than another. Even the undersides of things are illuminated.

Shadows do occur, but they arrive late. An animal may cross the plain and vanish into a fold of ground before its shadow enters from the opposite direction. These shadows are small and soft, and mostly harmless. Sometimes they graze on the damp patches.

The air smells of newly split stone, cold milk, and the green inside a stalk.

Water gathers only in certain places. A bead forms where two unfinished slopes meet. It grows clear and heavy, trembles for an hour, then lets go. Its fall is less than the thickness of a seed. Below it, the receiving basin deepens at the instant of impact, as though the water has persuaded the ground to become a bowl.

The sound is a tiny click.

There are long stretches of silence between these clicks. You can watch a procession of creatures cross a ridge without hearing anything. Feet meet earth; wings open; mouths work on tender mineral crusts. The silence is not imposed on them. Their movements simply seem to use very little of the air.

Vegetation is scattered. Along one damp ledge grows a line of four translucent blades, each with a dark green vein. They bend toward passing bodies rather than toward the light. A low plant near the eastern edge has two leaves and a root visible through the ground. Its root keeps withdrawing from soil that has become too definite, seeking the softer material beneath.

Elsewhere there are small round cushions of moss. A cushion may be no wider than a mite, but its interior holds a dense, dark forest. Animals enter it pale and come out green with spores.

Much of the country is bare: a wide apron of powdery stone; three little hollows; a raised bank with nothing on it; the bright interval between one plant and the next. You can see nearly the whole place at once, yet there is almost always an animal you have missed.

The most numerous are the seam-creatures. They resemble narrow silverfish, except that their bodies are jointed sideways. They slip into boundaries: between wet and dry earth, between a slope and its future shadow, between the moment a ridge begins and the moment it is visible. Often only their feelers remain outside, testing the air. They breed in these seams, and a new slope may emerge with a dozen young already inhabiting it.

There are soft, six-legged grazers with transparent backs. Through them you can see the white terrain, slightly magnified. They eat the faint blue bloom that develops on new rock. After feeding, each leaves a single dark grain behind. Those grains are the oldest objects in the country.

A few winged creatures hover over the water bowls. Their wings are broad and furred, their bodies no heavier than dust. When one descends to drink, its reflection rises first. The two meet at the surface, exchange places, and separate. The creature that flies away may be wetter, or older, or carrying an egg it did not have when it arrived.

Predators wait beside these bowls. They are long-legged, almost colorless animals, patient enough to be mistaken for cracks. A grazer comes close; one leg bends; the crack closes around it. There is no struggle you can hear. For a while the predator becomes more visible, taking on the milky opacity of its meal. Then it thins again.

Despite all this life, the place never looks crowded. Animals are continually arriving from small absences in the terrain and leaving through others. A bare patch can remain bare while six creatures cross it, each occupying a slightly different version of the patch. Occasionally two versions touch. A grazer stops with its head against something invisible. A winged animal makes a sudden correction in the air. Then the separation returns, and their routes continue.

At the foot of the new escarpment stand three structures.

One is a dome made of overlapping flakes, with a doorway facing the nearest damp hollow. Another is a ring of white posts, each scarcely taller than a leg joint. The third is unfinished: two walls joined at a corner, a roof lying beside them.

Nothing here uses tools. The structures are grown, but they are unmistakably dwellings. You can tell by the swept thresholds, by the sleeping hollows within, by a small bead of water kept just inside a doorway. Their occupants are rarely present when the houses are visible. Sometimes the dome lifts and walks a short distance on delicate feet, settles again, and resumes being a house.

The ring of posts encloses an especially quiet patch of air. Young animals pause there. They stand with their feelers lowered while the ground beneath them firms. When they leave, their legs no longer pass through the surface.

The weather comes from below.

First the soil grows cold enough to shine. Moisture beads on the undersides of ridges and on the bellies of resting animals. Then a low mist rises, usually no higher than the smallest leaf. For several minutes the plain is hidden while its raised edges remain brilliant. Creatures climb onto those edges. Predators abandon their cracks. The houses close their doors.

During the mist, the landscape changes quickly. A basin fills. A ridge forks. Somewhere underneath, a hollow opens that had been pressing upward for days. The new cavity releases a breath of air so cold that the nearest plant curls both its leaves around the same stem.

Then the mist thins. Water remains in a few pockets. Most of the ground is dry again, though darker, firmer, a little more committed to its shape.

You might expect the country to grow old by degrees. There are signs that it does. The dark grains accumulate. A deserted house loses one roof flake. Along the central ridge, a hairline fracture persists through several weather cycles.

But other parts keep beginning.

Near the boundary, a smooth patch lifts into a mound. A seam opens along its crest. Feelers appear. The light enters the opening before there is space enough for it, and for a moment you can see a second little country inside the first: bare white ground, a suspended bead of water, one green blade bending.

Then the mound settles, leaving a shallow doorway.

A pale animal goes in. Another comes out carrying something dark and round. Above them, the stone remains warm on its upper face and cool beneath, and the next drop gathers without a sound.
