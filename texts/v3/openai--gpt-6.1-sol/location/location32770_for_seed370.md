The place is a court at the top of a vast construction, although “court” suggests more open space than there is. It is scarcely thirty paces across. Low walls divide it into slots, stalls, blind passages, and shallow chambers, all roofless, all so tightly fitted together that from above the whole would resemble a tray packed with broken pottery. There is no central square. Even the place where several passages meet is occupied by a squat pillar, broad enough that you must turn sideways to get around it.

Everything is made.

The floor is laid in thick clay slabs. The walls are built of clay blocks. Beneath the slabs, visible through one worn corner, are smaller blocks packed on edge; beneath those, another pavement. Nothing here reaches bedrock. The edge of the court gives onto a sheer face of masonry descending into dimness, its courses too numerous and too closely spaced to count. Far below, other walls and platforms disappear into a brown haze. There is no visible valley, only lower work.

The clay is the color of old flour, with darker red patches where its surface has worn away. It was kneaded coarsely. Some blocks still show the ends of chopped fibers, now only narrow empty channels. Others contain pale grit. Thumbprints survive in protected places, behind projecting lips or inside the mouths of tubes. Out on the exposed faces, even the marks of hands have gone.

The workmanship is simple: rolled coils, pressed molds, stones used to polish edges, wooden stamps that left their grain in the wet material. There are no bolts, no glass, no fine metal fittings. A few joints are secured with dull copper staples as thick as fingers. Their ends were hammered flat rather than threaded. Most things hold together through weight, friction, and the patience of a close fit.

Yet there is hardly a handspan of plain surface.

Every wall contains holes. Some are round, some narrow and vertical, some shaped like the mouth of a small jug. Clay pipes pass through the blocks and join larger pipes hidden inside them. The dividing walls are hollow. So is the pillar at the crossing. Beneath your feet, cavities open into other cavities through carefully restricted necks. Each slab has a little vent near one corner.

The court is an instrument, and it is sounding.

The noise is so great that at first the place loses its visible depth. You see a wall a few paces away, but the sound seems to press it against your face. It is a low, crowded roar with higher tones buried in it: a hard nasal note, a thin whistle, something like the noise heard through a shell, enormously enlarged. None of these comes forward as a separate event. They occupy their places continuously.

There is no beat to anticipate. No knock, no call and answer, no interval in which another sound can enter. Your footfall vanishes. If you speak, you feel your throat working and hear almost nothing of what it makes.

At first the roar seems to come from below. Then from the walls. Beside the central pillar it appears to be inside your teeth. Each narrow passage holds a slightly different part of it, but the arrangement is fixed. A deep tone fills the western slot. A sharp one lies beneath a shelf of small sealed jars. At the perimeter, beside a waist-high parapet, the sound becomes broad and heavy enough that leaning over feels like leaning into a solid object.

Air passes through the construction somewhere out of sight. You cannot find an inlet large enough to account for it. There is little breeze on your skin. The loose dust does not lift.

It is warm here, steadily warm, as though the entire mass had finished taking in heat long ago and now gave back exactly what it received. A clay slab under your palm is neither cooler nor hotter than its neighbor. The sheltered recesses hold the same warmth as the parapet. There is no pleasant shaded side.

Above the court is a pale brown sky without a discernible sun. It provides enough light to distinguish the courses of blocks and the openings in them, but not enough to give the place a crisp outline. The narrowest corridors are dark at their floors. Deep inside a pipe mouth, the darkness looks almost like a material with which the pipe has been filled.

Nothing grows in the joints. No crust of lichen softens the masonry. No insects inhabit the fine cracks. The jars beneath the shelf contain dry pellets of pale mineral, their lids cemented shut with the same clay from which they were made. There are no cisterns, gutters, or stains left by running water. A powder collects in small recesses, but it is clean mineral dust, without the rich smell of soil.

The age of the court is visible chiefly in its loss of sharpness.

Steps have become broad inclines. Wall tops sag in long shallow curves, though no block appears newly displaced. The mouths of the pipes have rounded lips. Here and there a mason once carved a line or symbol; what remains is a depression you notice only because dust lies differently in it. The edges have worn past the appearance of damage. They look settled, almost cast that way.

Several repairs are as old as the work they repaired. A cracked slab was bridged with another slab, which later cracked in turn and was braced by a low ridge of hand-packed mortar. All three now share the same dulled surface. At one corner, broken blocks were carefully gathered into a retaining pocket. Their fracture faces have become as smooth as their outer faces. Nothing seems recent enough to be debris.

There are seats, or objects made at the height of seats, but every one occupies the full width of its recess. Sitting would leave your knees in the passage. There are shelves too close together to reach between, doorways that open directly against other walls, and small steps leading onto ledges already filled with clay vessels. The court has been occupied by its own construction until almost no room remains for a body.

Still, a route can be followed. You sidle past the pillar, turn into a slot, step over a low pipe, and emerge beside the parapet. From there you can look down at the immense supporting face. Blocks recede beneath blocks. Narrow ledges mark older tops that were built over. The same pale clay continues until distance takes it away.

The height has no grandeur of mountains. It is the height of countless loads carried up, placed, and followed by others.

You can remain here as long as you can endure the sound. No shadow advances across the pavement. No tone weakens. There is no movement in the lower haze to help measure the passing time. Only your own discomfort changes: the ache in your jaw, the heat under your clothes, the desire to find a wider passage.

When you turn back toward the interior, the court presents the same packed walls and rounded openings. The narrow route is still there. The pillar still leaves exactly enough space to pass. From every hollow in the ancient clay, the same enormous note continues.
