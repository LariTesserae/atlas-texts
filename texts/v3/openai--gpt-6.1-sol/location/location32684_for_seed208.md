The land is so old that water has almost forgotten how to run downhill.

It spreads instead. Rain lies in broad, shallow sheets over the stone, joining one hollow to another until the low country becomes a chain of lakes with no certain boundaries. A rise that looked like an island in the morning may be a submerged ridge by afternoon. Paths survive as differences in texture: a strip of rough rock beneath clear water, a line of pale pebbles, the worn crowns of stepping stones. From any of the scattered shelters, you can look a long way without seeing anything tall enough to interrupt the horizon.

There are hills, though it takes time to recognize them. They are long-backed swellings in the bedrock, polished and rounded, rising only a little above the flooded flats. Their slopes have been worn past the point of holding loose soil. Dark bands of harder mineral lie exposed across them, all that remains of formations that once stood much higher. In places, a band ends abruptly at the edge of a pool and resumes hundreds of paces away. Everything between has gone.

The rock is predominantly gray, but never quite the same gray. Underwater it is blue-black. Where recently exposed, it shines the color of a knife blade. Above the waterline it dries to a powdery silver, streaked with the brown of dissolved iron. Small holes hold darker water than the pools around them. Some have been drilled by generations of burrowing animals; others are the sockets of stones that turned in the same place for ages before finally wearing themselves away.

Nothing grows upright.

There are no reeds to give the wind a shape, no grass along the banks, no trees gathered around the buildings. The few green patches are copper stains. Life lies close to the mineral surface, in thin skins of cream, rust, and violet that can be mistaken for deposits until something feeds on them. These films gather most thickly where cold water seeps from cracks. The water carries a faint smell of struck stone and rotten eggs, and it is warmer by a finger’s breadth than the rain.

Below, far below the flattened country, buried seams are still reacting with water. The seep-fed films live on what comes up from that darkness. Where the seams approach the surface, the rock sweats continuously. In dry weather, a person can find the wet places by watching the animals converge.

Dry weather rarely lasts long.

The sky hangs low, usually a smooth, dim cover without a visible sun. Light arrives through it evenly, making distance difficult to judge. A shelter half a mile away may look close enough to touch; a feeding animal beside you may be almost invisible against the wet stone. Sometimes a brighter region travels behind the cloud, and every pool takes on a dull pearl sheen. Then the landscape seems full of water even where there is none.

It is cold enough that wet sleeves soon become a burden. Not cold enough for the great pools to freeze, except briefly at their margins. The air is full of minute droplets. Water gathers on eyelashes, on the undersides of stone lintels, on the cords people wear around their wrists to keep their tools from being lost.

And everywhere there is noise.

The loudest animals are the hollow-backed shell grazers. They are broad, low creatures, some no larger than a hand, others nearly the width of a doorway. Their soft feeding surfaces pass over the rock beneath them, scraping up the living films. Across each back rises a domed shell with a narrow opening near the rear. At intervals the animal draws water into a muscular chamber and expels it through that opening with a sound like a horn being blown underwater.

A single grazer can be heard from the next pool. Several hundred calling across the flats make the air tremble.

Their calls are not continuous, though the silence between them is seldom complete. One begins at a seep, another answers beyond a low ridge, and the sound moves outward through the colony. Sometimes the answer returns from the far shore after the first animal has already called again. The overlapping notes beat against one another. Water quivers in shallow cups. A loose stone in a shelter wall may begin to rattle.

People here do not try to speak over the colonies. They use hand signs outdoors. In the shelters, conversation is conducted close to the ear, or written with a wet finger on pale slate.

Between the grazers move other animals: flat, translucent swimmers that fold themselves through narrow channels; jointed scavengers with dark limbs and white bellies; long-bodied hunters that wait in drainage grooves, almost indistinguishable from the shadows there. Small animals are abundant enough that every exposed surface seems to shift when watched carefully. A puddle that appears empty will suddenly clear as dozens of tiny bodies retreat beneath its rim.

The hunters take advantage of the calls. At the height of a chorus they move, their splashing and scraping lost in the din. You can see a whole pool become alert a moment too late: swimmers pressing flat against stone, scavengers raising their forelimbs, a large grazer closing its shell opening with a wet, decisive slap.

Then something is dragged into a crack, and the calling continues around it.

The buildings stand far apart, usually on the widest dry crowns. They are small and squat, made of slabs wedged together rather than cut into regular blocks. Some have roofs of overlapping shell. Others are covered by a single stone supported on short walls, with the entrance low enough to require crawling.

There is little ornament. The useful objects are beautiful from handling: smooth bone scoops, perforated stone weights, scrapers with handles wrapped in cured hide. Thin metal is rare. It is saved for the edges of tools that cannot be made sharp enough in stone.

Beside each inhabited shelter is a yard of small pools arranged by hand. The people move flat stones to regulate the seepage, scrape away unwanted films, and carry the young of certain animals from basin to basin. They cultivate feeding grounds rather than crops. A good pool must be left uncovered, kept at the proper depth, and protected from the hunters that enter during heavy rain.

Rain is therefore both provision and damage. It renews the water, dilutes the bitter seepage, and connects the nursery pools to fresh feeding surfaces. It also erases their edges. After a long storm, people come out with hooked poles and search the flooded yards by touch, resetting stones they can no longer see.

Some structures are abandoned. Their lintels have sunk until the entrances look like dark mouths in the rock. Others remain intact, standing beside water that no longer supports the animals they were built to tend. A seep can dwindle over generations. When it does, the colored films thin, the grazers stop gathering, and a household eventually carries its tools elsewhere.

The old shelters are not pulled down. There is space enough to leave them.

Toward evening the wind often drops. The air becomes wetter and the calls carry farther. Across the regional flats, widely separated colonies seem to find one another, drawing into a slow common rhythm. Deep notes come from the larger pools; sharper ones answer from the shallows. Between them are the slap of bodies entering water, the clatter of scavengers on exposed rock, the steady dripping inside every shelter.

For a little while the whole country is audibly busy, although almost nothing rises above the height of a person’s knee.

Then the tide begins to enter through the distant channels. It is difficult to see it arrive. A dry mark darkens. Water touches the next stepping stone. A grazer lifts itself and turns toward a newly opened passage. Far out on the flats, a pale ridge disappears without a wave.

By night, several pools that were separate have become one, and animals are crossing ground that no one could cross that morning. In the shelters, people lift their sleeping mats onto the upper ledges and secure the doors with stone bars. Outside, the callers change their notes as the water deepens. The sound passes through the walls and into the slabs beneath the sleepers, who can tell, without getting up, how much of the country has gone under.
