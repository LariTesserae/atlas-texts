The shelf is small enough to walk around before the rain returns.

It projects from the mountain just below the summit: a shallow basin of pale stone, perhaps sixty paces across, with a broken rim on the valley side. Beyond that rim there is nothing nearby to measure distance against. Clouds move far below. When they open, you can see a river making broad turns through a country so distant that its forests look like stains.

Up here, the air is hot.

Not merely warm in the sun. Heat comes through the soles of your shoes. The undersides of stones are hotter than their exposed faces, and water standing in shade remains uncomfortable to touch. Yet there is no smell of sulfur, no smoke, no vent breathing at the center of the basin. The rock gives off heat everywhere, steadily, as if this little shelf has been left on a stove.

Rain falls on it several times a day.

You can watch each shower assemble. Moisture gathers above the basin in a thin gray patch, no wider than the shelf itself. Its lower surface darkens. A few heavy drops strike the leaves, then the whole patch releases water for three or four minutes. Beyond the rim, the air remains clear. Sunlight shines on the rain from the side.

Afterward, steam rises from the exposed rock, and the gray patch begins to form again.

Most of the basin is open stone and shallow water. The vegetation occupies three dense islands: one around a seep at the mountain wall, one on a low mound near the middle, and one beneath the only surviving roof. These patches are lush enough that their edges seem abrupt. Bare mineral crust gives way in a hand’s breadth to crowded stems, fleshy leaves, and roots thick as knuckles.

The leaves are long and loosely folded, dark green above and copper-colored beneath. After rain, they slowly turn their undersides toward the light. Water rolls along the folds and collects at their tips, where it hangs in large drops. Occasionally a leaf bends under the weight and empties itself onto the ground. This happens all through the afternoon, separately, without wind.

Between the plants, the shelf is remarkably new.

Its stone has the soft white color of unglazed porcelain, but none of porcelain’s smoothness. Thin ridges intersect at sharp angles. Small cavities are lined with transparent needles. Along the edge of every pool, wet mineral paste hardens into a fresh lip, enclosing water that was not enclosed yesterday.

The basin is growing.

Not quickly enough to see as a whole. But sit beside a trickle for an hour and the little channel will acquire a raised border. A fallen leaf becomes fixed by its edges. An insect wing develops a white rind. Here and there, older crust has cracked away, exposing a warmer, faintly translucent layer below. Light enters that layer farther than it should. A shadow cast across it has depth.

Near the mountain wall stands a small shelter with no door.

Four narrow uprights support a roof shaped like an overturned dish. They look metal from a distance and woven from close up. There are no joints. Each upright separates near the ground into hundreds of fine threads that enter the rock individually.

The roof is dry even during rain.

Water approaching it flattens into shining beads and slides sideways through the air. From beneath, you can see the rain bending over the roof’s outline, leaving a clear space above the surface. There is no hum and no visible mechanism. The shelter contains a stone bench, a shallow cup fixed into one end of it, and three finger-sized hollows worn into the wall.

Whatever was operated here required a hand, but not quite a human hand.

The cup is always full. Its water is cool.

That is the first thing anyone notices after sitting down: the impossible cold of it against the tongue, while the bench warms the backs of the legs and the air lies damp against the face. Drawing water from the cup does not lower its surface. Leaves dropped into it sink, disappear, and do not return.

There are two other signs of construction. A dark ring is embedded in the basin’s center, mostly concealed by roots. At the outer rim, a row of short posts follows the drop. Only seven remain. They lean at different angles, and none casts a shadow in the same direction as the others.

The posts have a practical effect.

Loose things do not readily fall off the shelf. A seed blown over the edge descends a short way, pauses, and drifts back inward. A stone tossed out follows an ordinary arc until it passes below the posts; then it slows and comes to rest in the air. There are several such stones beneath the rim, suspended at different depths, wet on top and dry underneath.

Farther down, birds fly normally. Rain falling beyond the posts continues into the valley. Whatever holds the stones is local, selective, and old enough that roots have grown through its fittings.

Animal life is scarce. A few pale snails live in the grooves of the mountain wall, emerging after each shower to graze the new mineral film. Their shells are so thin that the dark contraction of their bodies shows through. In the hottest pool, something jointed occasionally moves under the silt. It leaves pairs of pinholes along the waterline and retreats before its whole shape becomes visible.

Once in a while a small black bird arrives from below. It lands on the same leaning post, opens its beak in the heat, and waits. It does not drink. After several minutes, it takes off toward the mountain rather than the valley and vanishes behind a fold of rock.

There is little sound to accompany any of this.

The rain makes a brief, close pattering. The leaves empty themselves with isolated taps. A bubble breaking in a pool is audible from several paces away. Beneath the shelter, you sometimes hear a soft tick from within an upright, followed much later by another from a different one.

Wind passes above the basin. You can see it pulling apart the high clouds, but it rarely reaches the plants.

By late afternoon, the exposed pools have drawn back from their fresh white borders. The seep at the mountain wall strengthens. Water travels outward along narrow channels, filling the low places in a particular order. First the pool beside the shelter, then the pair near the rim, then the central hollow around the buried ring.

Only when that last hollow fills does the heat lessen.

The change is slight but unmistakable. Steam stops rising from the bare stone. The leaves unfold. Beneath the rim, one suspended pebble drops an inch, catches, and hangs again.

For a few minutes, the shelf seems about to become an ordinary mountain garden.

Then droplets begin gathering in the clear air overhead.
