There is almost no height left in the country.

What look like hills from a distance prove, after days of approach, to be differences in the thickness of the mud. The stone beneath them has been worn past fracture, past grain, into broad, blunt surfaces as smooth as the backs of teeth. No ridge is young enough to cast a clean shadow. Even the hollows have rounded bottoms. Water rests in them without quite becoming lakes.

The warmth rises from below. It comes through the soles of the feet, through a sleeping body, through the belly of anything that lies against the ground. Rain does not cool the place. It falls warm and remains warm, filling shallow depressions, soaking the few patches of exposed earth, gathering in the creases of the stone. Under the low cloud, the water has the color of tarnished lead. Sometimes a pale opening appears overhead, and a hundred miles of flooded plain glimmer at once, too briefly for the eye to make sense of what has been revealed.

There is water everywhere, but no expanse is empty.

The shallows are full of folded sediment, submerged skins, knotted mineral growths, banks of soft rubble that lift and subside. Thin films lie over thicker films. Bubbles push up beneath mats that are not quite plants and not quite deposits. Channels carry such dense suspensions of silt that their currents stand higher than the water around them, low moving ridges that travel for a while before spreading apart. The rain strikes these ridges and stays there, trembling in a skin, until some pressure underneath breaks the surface.

A person could stand for hours looking at one square yard and never see all that happens in it.

Yet across the whole country there are remarkably few living things.

Here and there, black reeds grow from a seam in the bedrock. Each is thick as a wrist and no taller than a person. They have no leaves. Their blunt ends open at night, exposing a yellow tissue that gathers moisture from the air. The reeds stand singly, separated by distances that seem excessive until one notices the dark fans of rootwork visible beneath the shallow water. A solitary stem may feed from an area the size of a village.

On the warmer stones grows a low, copper-colored crust. It can remain unchanged for generations, then peel away in a single wet afternoon. The exposed rock is blue underneath. Before night, the blue is gone.

Nothing here stays dry long enough to become dusty. Nothing gets carried away far enough to leave a vacancy. The country has been eroding for so long that nearly everything which could be broken off has already been broken off, transported, ground smaller, and returned. The last movements happen among particles too fine to settle. Water presses between them. They pack together, loosen, pack again.

The noise begins beyond hearing.

Before there is a recognizable sound, the jaw aches. The ribs feel occupied. A pressure passes through the ground and up the legs, followed several seconds later by a rolling concussion that seems to come from the entire horizon. The shallow water stipples. Reeds bend in different directions, some toward the sound, some away.

Then come the nearer noises: the snapping of water skins; the long tearing sound of silt banks separating; a wet, immense breathing from places where the ground has lifted. Rain hammers the plain. Channels strike each other head-on and throw up walls of slurry. Far away, something collapses with the force of a cliff fall, though there are no cliffs.

There are intervals in which all these sounds gather into one continuous roar. During them, a person cannot hear their own shout. Speech has to be felt against a hand held to the speaker’s throat.

The animals, when they appear, seem adapted less to hunger than to this pressure.

One walks on six narrow legs, placing each foot with exquisite care on patches of water that will bear its weight. It has a low, plated body and a smooth head without visible eyes. Along its flanks are small pale hollows that open when the noise becomes intolerable. It stands absolutely still through the loudest upheavals. Once the vibration passes, it resumes its crossing.

Another is seen only as a line moving beneath the mud. Its passage leaves a chain of dimples. Occasionally it rises enough to expose a wet, gray back, carrying the impressions of things that have rested above it: reed stems, rain circles, a human heel. Those marks move with the animal for a few yards before sinking out of sight.

There may be many more below the surface. There may be almost none. The country is too loud to listen for them, and too full of movement to recognize the movement of a living body.

From a little distance, the upheavals resemble weather. Dark fronts advance across the flats, raising spray ahead of them. Behind each front the water becomes smoother and deeper, and the air briefly smells of opened shellfish. But a front can pass without moving the reeds or disturbing the rain. It can leave a new channel where solid stone was lying a moment before.

Some fronts alter distance.

A flooded strip that took half a day to cross can narrow while a traveler watches, its opposite bank drawing near with no visible motion in between. The water continues to run at the same speed. A floating stem may pass out of reach and arrive at the far bank almost immediately. When the strip widens again, the stem is still there, small and distant.

These changes are most common late in the long dim afternoons. They pass through the country in broad, irregular belts. A sound may arrive before the event that produces it: the crash of a mud bank falling, then the shudder, then, after a wait, the actual fall. Elsewhere the surface gives way soundlessly, and its noise arrives the following morning.

Nothing made a long journey here in the usual sense. There are stretches from which the northern horizon can be reached by walking north. Beside them are stretches where north gradually turns downward, so gently that a traveler notices only when the water has begun climbing their clothes.

The few standing structures occupy places where these changes happen less often.

They are small, usually a single chamber of thick, pale walls, with an opening wide enough to crawl through. Some have a rounded roof. Others are open to the rain. They sit on beds of tightly nested stone, each foundation extending farther than the building itself, sometimes visible beneath the water for a hundred paces.

Inside, the noise is reduced enough to hear breathing.

There is nothing to indicate who built them. No tool marks, no hearths, no objects left behind. The walls have the faint layered texture of shell, though the layers include grains of rock and impressions of roots. Around some structures, younger walls have begun to grow out of the flooded ground. They are only a few inches high, already divided by doorways.

Travelers shelter in the old chambers when they can. They sleep sitting upright, because the floors seep steadily, and because a room occasionally becomes smaller during the night. The change is rarely violent. A shoulder pressed against one wall finds the opposite wall nearer by morning. A doorway becomes too low for the person who entered it. There are chambers containing bones in positions that suggest their occupants continued sleeping through the adjustment.

Outside, there are signs of passage, but few lasting paths. A trail is a series of warmer footprints in the water, discernible by touch for a little while. Sometimes it is a row of reed stems bent at the same angle. Such a trail may predate the walker who follows it. People have come upon their own footprints ahead of them and turned aside; others have followed them to shelter.

The vastness is difficult to perceive from ground level. Cloud closes the distance. Spray fills the spaces beneath it. Most days, the visible world is a dark circle a few miles across, with rain falling through the edges.

But sometimes the atmosphere clears in layers.

The nearest mist thins first, showing the low folds of the plain. Beyond them, farther than the eye expects to see, another weather system is rising: a wall of vapor lit from within by broad, noiseless flashes. Behind that stand more weather systems, their upper surfaces dimly illuminated beneath a ceiling of cloud that follows the curvature of the world.

Through those openings, the country can be seen continuing. No mountain interrupts it. No dry inland appears. The same shallow water, packed sediment, scattered dark reeds, and faint pale shelters extend until distance turns them into a single bruised color.

Then the opening closes. A warm wind brings the smell of newly exposed stone. Water begins pouring from a crack beside the feet, though the crack lies higher than the surrounding pools. The low ground swells. Somewhere close by, under the roar, a reed opens.

For a moment its yellow interior is visible, soft and neatly folded. Rain beads on its rim. Then a pressure moves through the plain, the stem bends, and the tissue withdraws before the next enormous sound arrives.
