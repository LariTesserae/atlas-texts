The city occupies a plain so low and broad that its farthest districts disappear behind the curvature of the ground. There are no mountains to give it a boundary. In one direction the land rises into a succession of shallow green shelves; in the other it falls toward a basin where a little water shines, sometimes, beneath the roots. Above it all is an enormous amount of empty air.

The buildings stand kilometers apart.

This is not abandonment. Paths are maintained, addresses are legible, and the public transport arrives exactly when promised. A tower near the southern horizon is a hospital. A pale horizontal mark to the east is a market. Between them lies a breadth of vegetation that would take half a day to cross on foot. The city has chosen—or has been obliged—to leave most of its volume unoccupied.

It is difficult to see this clearly at first, because the light is poor. A thick upper atmosphere disperses the sun into a warm gray patch, without edges or rays. Under that dimness the greens are deep and almost black. Polished surfaces gleam more distinctly than white ones. Windows look like cuts through which a brighter place can be glimpsed.

The heat is steady. It rises from the ground through the soles of your shoes, gathers beneath your clothes, and remains after the sun’s patch has slipped away.

And the sound is tremendous.

It reaches you before anything that seems capable of producing it: a low, continuous concussion, overlaid with brittle rattling, shrieks, and a rolling noise like immense quantities of stone being poured down a chute. Conversations happen close to the ear. Doors have to be pulled shut against their vibrating seals. On a sheltered balcony, a glass of water develops a little standing wave, a pointed trembling hill that remains at its center until the building’s dampers come on.

Much of this is the landscape itself.

The plain is young. It emerged within living memory, lifted from beneath a former seabed by an engineering process whose final stage is still underway. Fresh black rock shows between the plants, sharp enough to open a shoe. Some slabs retain parallel grooves from the apparatus that raised them. Others have split into polygons with clean, blue-gray interiors. There has not been time for rivers to round the edges, or for soil to fill every seam.

The city’s earliest streets were laid across these slabs. Several are now a hand’s breadth higher than their adjoining ground. Elsewhere, a road dips through a new crease, and municipal warning lights move slowly along it, marking a fracture as it lengthens.

Below the plain, the lifting structures continue their work. Their movements are too small to feel as earthquakes. You hear them instead. A deep report passes beneath the district, followed several minutes later by a second, farther away. Loose fragments settle. Seeds jump in their cases. For a moment, every hanging leaf turns the same way.

The vegetation has taken extraordinarily well.

There is little rain, and almost no running water. What moisture there is arrives in brief warm showers or condenses in places where materials with different thermal histories meet. At dawn, the undersides of certain public walkways bead with water. People put shallow dishes beneath them, though taking more than a household’s share is forbidden.

Plants use smaller opportunities. Their roots enter stone fractures and grow along the cool faces below. Their leaves are thick, waxed, sometimes translucent at the edges. Fernlike trees unfold enormous crowns from trunks no wider than a wrist, each crown supported by a lattice of rigid veins. Succulents spread in terraces, their fleshy blades overlapping like roof tiles. Creepers fasten themselves to bare rock with pale adhesive discs and carry their water in swollen joints.

From a distance, all this looks extravagantly lush. Up close, you can see how carefully it is arranged: leaves shading root mouths, fallen husks tucked into fissures, stalks bending to direct a few drops toward a neighbor’s base. Almost nothing drains away.

The densest growth occupies hollows only a few meters deep. These hold a still, leafy darkness at midday. Warm condensation gathers on the lower fronds, and the air smells of resin, mineral dust, and fruit ripening inside closed skins. Step out of one of these hollows and the plain feels abruptly exposed, despite the vegetation underfoot. The next tall object may be an hour’s walk away.

Animals are everywhere.

Small six-legged grazers work through the ground cover, clipping only the translucent edges of leaves. When disturbed, they flatten themselves and become almost indistinguishable from pieces of shed bark. Long-bodied hunters slip through the same vegetation, so thin that you see a movement without at first identifying what moved.

There are burrows beneath most sound slabs. Some have entrances neatly rimmed with discarded seed cases. Others announce themselves by an intermittent breathing noise, surprisingly loud, as their occupants ventilate chambers through narrow rock throats.

Above the crowns, flying animals cross the empty air in widely separated lines. Their wings are nearly invisible until they bank. Then a dull silver surface flashes, and vanishes. They travel for kilometers without changing height, guided by pressure seams too faint for people to sense.

At dusk, their calls become part of the city’s great volume of noise. One gives a falling whistle; another answers beyond the hospital tower; soon the exchange spans the plain. They seem to be calling across distances for which their bodies are much too small. Their throat sacs swell to the size of ripe fruit before each cry.

The insects are louder still. Whole patches of undergrowth rattle in coordinated bursts. The sequence moves from hollow to hollow, falls silent, then returns from an unexpected direction. Some species time their choruses to the underground machinery. Others sing in the gaps. Predators have learned to hunt during the loudest passages, when a snapped stalk cannot be heard.

The buildings offer relief, though rarely complete silence.

They are narrow structures, usually raised on a single foot. Their upper rooms broaden and branch, hanging over gardens without touching them. A few are so slender that it seems impossible for them to contain stairs. Most do not.

At the base of each stands a small chamber with a seat, a handrail, and a glazed door. You enter, select your floor, and feel a momentary pressure behind the eyes. When the door opens, the view has changed. The chamber has not moved in any ordinary sense. On some mornings a faint smell of the destination arrives before the transition: cooking oil, disinfectant, the dry sweetness of an upper-floor nursery.

The same system connects districts. Its entrances are modest, often no more than a shaded recess beside a footpath. People still walk, especially after rain, when the plants open and the young rock releases its stored warmth. But nobody needs to fill the distances between buildings with roads, traffic, or shops. A dense civic life occupies remarkably little ground.

There are limits to the system. Transfers stop during certain movements below the plain. For those hours, each building becomes an island, and distant windows are only distant windows again.

Everyone recognizes the warning. The lowest mechanical note drops out of the surrounding roar. A yellow line appears around each transit door. Birds settle on the vegetation, and the grazers withdraw beneath the slabs.

Then something under the city shifts.

You cannot see the shift itself. You see its consequences: a new sheen on a rock face, a row of stems shaking in otherwise still air, a narrow cleft releasing warm vapor. On an undeveloped edge of the plain, the ground may lift enough to reveal a fresh ledge. The survey lights find it within minutes. By the following evening, insects are exploring its cracks.

The city keeps its young edges rather than smoothing them. There are fences around the worst ones, and soft markers that change color when a cut becomes dangerous. Children learn early which leaves can hold their weight, which hollows belong to breeding animals, and which low humming stones should never be touched.

After one of the brief rains, the whole place becomes more visible. Water darkens the rock and outlines every sharp surface. The plants loosen their folded portions. Pale feeding organs emerge from beneath their leaves. Animals climb out to drink from droplets before those droplets can disappear.

For perhaps twenty minutes, there is moisture everywhere.

Then the exposed surfaces dry. The roots retain what they have caught. The great distances turn hazy again.

From an upper room, you can watch the dim city resume its scattered life: a light entering the market building, a flight of silver wings crossing a kilometer of vacant air, a green shelf trembling as a chorus begins. Far below, a path threads through thick growth toward a transit door scarcely larger than a cupboard.

The door opens. Someone steps out carrying groceries, waits for a particularly violent burst of sound to pass, and takes the long way home.
