The water lies high above the rest of the world. Below its basin, beyond the outer rim, clouds move across lower country, but almost nothing of that can be seen from within. Here there is cold water, dim blue light, and a floor that rises in broad terraces toward a submerged pass.

The basin is not rock.

Its sides are built from shells, though most of them have lost the shapes that would make them recognizable as shells. They have been crushed, cemented, worn away, and covered by further generations until they resemble a pale, fine-grained stone. In places where the current has opened a fresh fracture, the material shows its history: a curve of pearly lining, a chamber packed with white sediment, the dark seam where two immense valves once met. Elsewhere it is smooth enough to reflect the faint light descending from the surface.

Everything solid here was made by something that lived in the water. The terraces, the rim, the scattered pillars, even the rounded grit shifting along the floor are shell or the remains of shell. There is no exposed underlying earth. If it exists, it is too far down to matter.

Across the basin, old structures stand hundreds of body-lengths apart. Some are low domes with openings on their sheltered sides. Some are tall, narrow stacks, hollow throughout, with holes worn through several walls at once. Their original builders are gone. The stacks have settled and tipped until their upper portions touch, making long, accidental bridges. Beneath them, the water appears almost black.

There are no green things. No fringe of weed softens an edge; no meadow occupies the shallows. Small animals gather instead wherever the currents deliver food. They cluster under overhangs, inside broken chambers, along the downstream sides of pillars. From a distance these gatherings look like stains. Close by, they resolve into pale legs, transparent bodies, fine fans opening and folding.

Many are nearly invisible until they move.

A flattened animal rests against a terrace with its skin matching the chalk beneath it. When a shadow crosses, it lifts at the edges and slips into a crack. Farther out, long-bodied swimmers hang almost upright, keeping their heads toward the weak light. Their fins make such small corrections that they seem suspended on threads. A loose procession of armored creatures travels along a groove in the floor, each touching the one ahead with a pair of slender feelers.

The largest animals are sparse but easy to mistake for part of the landscape. A dome may breathe. A slope may draw itself inward. What looks like a spill of broken plates can slowly gather its pieces and retreat into deeper water.

They build as they live, adding rims around their bodies, extending chambers behind them, sealing off old compartments when those compartments become too small or fill with silt. Construction is slow in any one animal. Across the whole basin it is continuous. A little fresh white appears on the edge of an ancient wall; a thin partition closes a passage that was open last season. The newest work is sharp, almost delicate. It does not stay that way.

Water enters over the upper saddle.

Sometimes it comes as a broad, steady descent, clear enough to see through for several terraces. Sometimes an entire dark mass crosses the saddle and folds downward, bringing colder water from another basin. Its arrival turns the suspended animals sideways. The fans beneath the ledges snap shut. Loose shell grit rises from the floor in low, traveling clouds.

There is little warning. A current that has run eastward for hours may stop, leaving every creature briefly drifting under its own momentum. Then it returns from below.

The terrain divides these movements into countless smaller disturbances. Water pours through the hollow pillars, curls around domes, and rolls backward beneath shelves. Two neighboring channels can flow in opposite directions. In open water, a ribbon of pale debris twists upon itself, tightens into a knot, then stretches out beyond sight. Whole shoals are carried across the basin, scattered among the structures, and gathered again in a place they did not choose.

Yet the place is mostly quiet.

Inside the deep chambers there is a muted rubbing of particles against shell. Occasionally a loose plate knocks once against a wall. The animals make small dry clicks that are difficult to locate in the water. Even during the strongest upheavals, movement reaches the body before sound does: a pressure against the chest, a pull at one shoulder, the sudden cold of a different current passing across the face.

Near the surface the light brightens, but never becomes warm. The water overhead is thick with fine white particles. Sunlight enters it as a dull patch that widens and narrows with the weather. When a storm passes over the basin, the underside of the surface darkens. Pale bubbles descend, trapped in turning water, and lodge briefly beneath the roofs of old chambers.

From those upper terraces, the rim can sometimes be seen. It is broad and rounded, worn down by an age of overflow. Water slides across it in a shallow sheet before dropping out of sight. Beyond is a strip of cloud, and occasionally the remote flank of another pale basin standing at a similar height.

There is no climbable shore. The shallows end against curved walls polished by generations of storms. Animals that venture close to the lip spread hooks, pads, or gripping limbs against the shell. Those that cannot grip stay below.

The safest places change. A sheltered alcove can become the mouth of a powerful channel when a partition breaks elsewhere. A hollow pillar may stand for centuries with water moving gently through it, then empty its accumulated silt in a single afternoon. The survivors move into neighboring chambers. By evening, delicate feeding fans are already opening along the newly exposed edges.

Much of the basin looks finished: low, rounded, settled under its own weight. But beneath that worn surface, living bodies continue to make room for themselves. They push thin new walls into the water, fill old cracks, build across gaps. The currents take these additions apart or wear them into the larger forms.

In the dim distance, a white arch gives way without any visible impact. Its pieces turn slowly as they fall. A cloud rises where they strike the terrace below, hiding the animals that scatter from beneath it.

For a while there is only pale water.

Then the cloud thins. The fallen arch has left a new opening, and water is already passing through.
