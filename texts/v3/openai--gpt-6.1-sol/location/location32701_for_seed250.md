The ocean begins high above the clouds.

From below, it looks like a second sky: pale blue, unevenly bright, with weather moving through it at depths that cannot be judged. Its underside sags into broad, transparent valleys. Sometimes a valley lengthens until a column of water descends through the air, thick as a mountain, touches the lower cloud deck, and draws itself back up. Rain falls between these columns. Much of it slows before reaching the ground, hangs for a moment, then rises.

There is no shore at this altitude. There are places where the water becomes thin enough to see through, and places where it thickens into darkness, but its edges are thousands of kilometers away. To enter it is to pass through a cool, trembling surface and find ordinary daylight still reaching you.

Inside, the distances are almost unbearable.

There is very little to interrupt them. No fish, no drifting shells, no busy veil of plankton. A small mineral flake might descend beside you for an hour, turning slowly, its shadow keeping pace on nothing visible. Far off, a white formation catches the sun. You cannot tell whether it is a nearby spur of stone or a range on the other side of the sea.

The water is clear enough that darkness arrives by accumulation rather than murk. Blue deepens to cobalt over many kilometers. Looking upward, you see the sun spread into a broad, steady brightness; looking sideways, you see light gradually losing its power to distinguish anything.

Most of the terrain lies near the ocean’s upper surface.

Here, new stone gathers into enormous submerged shelves. They have not been worn into their shapes. Their faces are smooth because nothing has yet happened to them, and their edges can be sharp enough to divide a current without stirring it. Some are translucent, the color of wet porcelain. Others are dark, almost purple, with a faint metallic sheen beneath their skin.

A shelf may end abruptly in a lip a few handspans thick. Underneath it is open water, then more open water, and finally the dim undersurface of the ocean, with clouds moving far below. Standing on such a shelf would feel like standing at the edge of a continent that had not finished acquiring its weight.

The rock is still being decided.

At a distance, a mountain begins as a pale disturbance in the water. Its outline strengthens over several days. Grains settle toward an invisible shape, adhering where there seems to be no support, until slopes and ridges become apparent. Sometimes the process continues and a whole massif hardens. Sometimes a current passes through before the last bonds have formed, and the mountain comes apart into a shining suspension.

There is almost no rubble. Failed terrain returns to its ingredients.

The older formations have seams, but few cracks. Their surfaces show long bands where growth paused: a colder season, a change in current, an interruption in the field that holds the ocean up. The bands resemble the layers in a shell, though there are no shells here and never have been. In places, the stone is hollow. Light passes through a ridge and reveals several nested surfaces inside it, like successive attempts at the same mountain.

Nothing has been built on these heights. There are no stations, pylons, roads, or instruments to explain how the water remains suspended. The mechanism is distributed through the water itself.

Under strong light, its smallest mineral particles align into faint, straight threads. The threads join at angles too exact to be accidental, hold for a fraction of a second, then rearrange. Across an immense volume, these momentary connections alter the way matter bears upon matter. Weight is carried sideways, handed onward, dispersed through regions that look empty.

The whole sea is involved in keeping the sea aloft.

Whether this was once an invention is difficult to determine. Whatever designed it left no durable object apart from the behavior of water. The process now propagates through new mineral growth, as naturally here as freezing. A fresh shelf acquires its load-bearing pattern while it forms. Break off a fragment, and for a little while it falls in the wrong direction.

The place is cool. Currents bring water down from the sunlit surface, but the warmth spreads thinly through the enormous volume. On the sheltered side of a ridge, the water has the still, penetrating chill of a spring. Out in the channels, sudden streams can be several degrees colder. They arrive without cloudiness or color, announcing themselves first through the contraction of the stone.

That contraction is loud.

The ocean carries sound remarkably well, and the growing terrain is seldom quiet. Long reports travel from one range to another. A shelf adjusting its position produces a deep note that lasts for minutes, too low to hear cleanly but strong enough to tremble through the teeth. Finer noises fill the spaces between: sharp mineral clicks, a continuous rushing from distant vertical currents, a high hiss where sheets of water slide past each other.

Often the source is too far away to see. A crack sounds close enough to touch, but its echo arrives from several horizons. During a storm, the separate noises merge into a sustained, immense roar.

Storms do not need air here.

They begin when a region of the supporting field falls out of agreement with its neighbors. Water accelerates along a plane hundreds of kilometers wide. Crosscurrents roll into columns. Light bends through the moving layers, making distant mountains jump, multiply, disappear. Near the surface, the ocean raises low, broad domes; underneath, its hanging valleys deepen.

A storm can uproot a newly formed range. For hours, pale ridges drift through the water intact, rotating with terrible slowness. Then their internal pattern fails and they soften at the edges. The last part to dissolve is usually the highest crest.

There is vegetation, though one can travel a great distance without finding it.

It grows where mineral warmth leaks from fresh stone: sparse, dark ribbons lying flat against the surface, and small branching fans no larger than a hand. These plants have little need for sturdy stems. They unfold into favorable currents, absorb dissolved material, and close when the water becomes too cold. Their color is a muted green visible only at close range; from afar, a colony looks like a slight deepening of shadow.

On a sheltered ledge, a few square meters of growth can become the most intricate thing in sight. Tiny bubbles collect beneath the leaves. Their edges shine. Occasionally a ribbon detaches and drifts away, taking weeks to find another surface.

Nothing grazes it. Nothing comes to rest among its branches. When a plant dies, its substance disperses almost completely, leaving a faint stain that the next increment of stone may seal inside.

After a storm, the great distances become visible again.

A clear interval opens between currents. Sunlight falls through the ocean in broad, ordinary shafts, illuminating unfinished summits and the undersides of shelves. Far below, white clouds slide across the view. Somewhere beyond sight, a mountain is forming; you can hear its low, intermittent settling.

Nearby, a new edge of stone extends into the water. Its tip is soft and faintly luminous. Grain by grain, it acquires a surface. A current bends around it for the first time, and the shape holds.
