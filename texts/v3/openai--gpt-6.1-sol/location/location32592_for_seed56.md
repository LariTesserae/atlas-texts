The hollow lies just beneath the frost line. From above, it would look like a shallow dip in a snowfield, a place where the snow has settled a little farther than elsewhere. Below, it is large enough to contain a grove, though there are no individual trees. Everything growing here joins everything else.

The ceiling hangs low at the edges and rises in the middle, where several pale trunks thicken into a single mass. Roots descend from it. Stems ascend into it. There is no reliable way to distinguish the two. Their surfaces are smooth and swollen, the color of celery kept too long in darkness, with green visible deep inside rather than on the skin. Thin leaves lie pressed against them in overlapping scales. At intervals a leaf lifts, admits a bead of moisture, and closes again.

The cold is immediate and thorough. It enters the teeth before the lungs. Breath falls instead of rising, gathering around the knees in a dim white layer that gradually draws apart into threads. Frost grows on any surface exposed to the air, but the living surfaces shed it from within. A rind of crystals loosens, slips down a stem, and disappears into the soft floor.

Underfoot, the place has the firmness of an unripe fruit. Weight makes a shallow depression that remains for several minutes after a foot is lifted. The floor is a dense felt of flattened shoots, translucent bladders, minute leaves, and roots finer than hair. There is no visible soil. Where the surface parts, another living layer shows beneath it, then another: a pale green depth through which water moves in broad, sluggish sheets.

Only near the margin of the hollow is there anything resembling rock.

Here, beneath curtains of rootlets, small gray nodules are forming inside the plants. Some are no larger than peas. Others have begun to touch and fuse. Their faces are soft enough to take an impression from a fingernail, yet their interiors ring faintly when struck. These are the beginnings of stone, assembled grain by grain in cold sacs. The plants move the grains into alignment before sealing them together. There are no fractures, rounded pebbles, or old weathered surfaces. Even the little ledge along the western edge is still wet from its manufacture.

Water comes from several directions. It beads out of the ceiling. It wells through the floor when pressure changes. It travels along the undersides of leaves without falling, thickening into trembling seams. Near the center is a pool only a few paces across, its dark surface crowded with floating leaf tips. It has no open bank. To reach it, one must push between broad vertical fronds whose stiff margins rasp against one another.

The pool remains liquid in air cold enough to frost eyelashes. Fine white hairs extend from its rim and touch the surface at regular intervals. Beneath each hair, a clear patch opens in the frail ice that is always trying to form. The warmth released there is too slight to feel. It is enough.

Elsewhere, ice is allowed to grow.

It occupies narrow channels within the vegetation, forming long clear splints. As each splint expands, the plants give around it. A rib shifts outward. A bundle of stems separates. The hollow is slowly enlarging through this managed pressure, gaining perhaps the width of a seed in a season. Its ceiling has never been excavated. The surrounding mass is being persuaded to make room.

The plants conduct this work through their smallest structures. Under the translucent skin, tiny bodies pass along branching channels, pause at junctions, and change shape. Some split into finer bodies that slip between the walls of individual cells. Others carry flecks of mineral, or gather around a damaged fiber until its ends knit together. Their movement resembles circulation, but they sometimes stop a wound before it happens. A stem will reinforce itself on the side toward which a falling ice fragment is about to turn.

There are no instruments, seams, housings, or places where machinery has been fitted into the grove. Whatever knowledge governs it is inherited in the growing tissue. A new leaf unfolds with capabilities its parent did not possess. Those capabilities spread slowly through the joined roots, reaching the far side of the hollow years later.

Light emerges from the same deep channels. It is sparse: a blue-green point at the end of a root, a faint wash inside a leaf, the brief illumination of a vein beneath the floor. Most of the hollow remains difficult to see. Shapes become apparent because they interrupt other shapes. Occasionally the central mass brightens from within, exposing several layers of vegetation at once. For a few seconds, the walls appear farther away than they are.

Animals occupy nearly every interval.

Small, six-legged creatures rest belly-up beneath the ceiling leaves, their dark feet hooked into pores. Clear grazing animals move through the floor felt. You see the clipped shoots inside them before you notice their bodies. In the pool, flattened swimmers fold themselves between submerged leaves and open slowly, showing violet gills. Pale burrowers inhabit the mineral sacs, swallowing failed stone and returning it as a fine powder.

Larger animals sleep in pockets among the stems. One has a narrow furred body and a face covered entirely by flexible sensory fingers. Another is broad and nearly circular, with short limbs hidden beneath a fringe of stiff white hair. Their resting places fit them closely. As they grow, the surrounding plants grow back.

The animals are slow enough that the first impression is of statues. Then an eye turns under its membrane. A claw releases one stem and closes on another. A swimmer changes the angle of a fin.

Yet the hollow is loud.

The living floor clicks as fluids pass through stiff valves. Ice sounds continually: sharp ticks nearby, deeper reports transmitted through the ceiling. Thousands of hooked animals scrape their mouthparts against the undersides of leaves, producing a coarse, even hiss. The broad white animals make low calls without waking. These roll through the vegetation and set loose droplets trembling.

Sound carries so well through the joined stems that its source is hard to locate. A feeding animal at the far edge may seem to be grinding directly behind your ear. A movement beneath your foot returns a moment later from the ceiling. Longer calls circulate several times before fading, altered at each passage.

For all this noise, little happens quickly. A dropped bead of water may hang from a leaf for an hour. The mouth of a mineral sac opens over several days. Frost advances along an exposed stem, stops at a vein, and waits there until the next slow pulse of fluid passes beneath it.

At the innermost edge of the hollow, behind the central pool, the floor rises into a cushion of tightly folded leaves. This is the youngest part of the place. Its tissues are almost colorless. Small animals gather on it, warming discrete patches with their bodies; beneath them, new channels acquire their first light.

There is no path across the cushion yet. No depression has become a basin, no ridge has hardened enough to bear weight. If watched for long enough, a folded leaf can be seen loosening from its neighbors. Cold water enters the gap. A clear-bodied grazer noses into it and begins to feed.

By the time it moves on, there is a little more room.
