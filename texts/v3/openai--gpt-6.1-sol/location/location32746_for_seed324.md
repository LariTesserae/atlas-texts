The place is a narrow court inside the uppermost machinery floor of a cooling tower. It is not on any plan as a court. On the plan it is the clearance between a return-water manifold and the outer wall: a strip six paces long, scarcely two paces wide, left open so that someone can reach the bolts.

A maintenance door admits you sideways. Beyond it, there is just enough room to stand.

Everything underfoot has been made. The floor is poured resin over a steel deck, pitched toward a drain. The little escarpment on the left is the folded edge of a drip tray. The overhang is a duct. Far above the ground, where the tower narrows, these components enclose a pocket of weather that nobody intended to build.

It is always wet.

Water beads on the manifold’s cold skin, grows heavy, and falls. Some drops land directly in the tray. Others strike bolt heads and break into fine spray. A seam in the overhead duct leaks at irregular intervals, producing a brief, broad sheet of water that flattens against the floor and spreads between your boots. The sheet withdraws almost immediately, but its edges remain: thin silver outlines around screw heads, shallow pools in the depressions left by the resin roller.

Nothing here has had time to become rounded. The tower has only recently entered service. Cut edges are sharp beneath their paint. The seams of the floor stand up in little ridges. A fresh bead of sealant, pale gray and still faintly elastic, makes a dam across one corner. Behind it, water accumulates in a pool no larger than an open hand.

That is the deepest water in the place. Its bottom is blue, because the floor is blue. A single shaving of stainless steel rests there, curled like a loose clock spring. When the pumps change speed, the shaving turns a few degrees and settles again.

The sound leaves no room for smaller sounds.

On the other side of the wall, fans draw air through the tower. Below the floor, a pressure-reducing valve discharges into a return line. Their noises overlap: a continuous coarse roar, a hard rushing hiss, and a low mechanical pulse that comes through the deck and into the bones of your feet. The pulse is easier to feel than hear. Once you notice it, standing still becomes difficult; your body keeps preparing for a movement that never arrives.

A falling droplet makes no audible impact. Neither does a boot. You can see water hitting metal without hearing it, as though the little court belongs to a silent film being projected inside a factory.

If someone were to stand at the door and speak, their mouth would move and nothing else would happen.

The air is cool, unusually cool for a room so close to working machinery. Cold return water chills the broad pipe along the left wall. Air entering through the inspection louvers has already crossed the tower’s wet interior. It carries suspended droplets that catch on your eyelashes and the hair of your wrists. Your clothes become damp before you can identify a source.

Through the louvers there is no view of the ground. There is a narrow portion of cloud, an occasional pale movement of sky, and the dark ribs of the tower’s outer cladding. Sometimes the cloud comes through. Then the far end of the court fades, although it is only a few steps away, and the safety label on the drain housing becomes a red rectangle without words.

The light comes chiefly from the maintenance doorway: a weak yellow bar reaching across the blue floor. There is also a little daylight through the louvers, cold and diffuse. Between them lies a dim zone where the metal looks almost black until water crosses it. Every moving film briefly supplies its own shine.

There is very little vegetation, but not none.

At the base of a pipe bracket, a patch of green has established itself in the grit caught behind a washer. It is moss, scarcely thicker than felt. The grit is mostly construction dust, paint flakes, and particles brought in by the ventilation. Farther along, two narrow grass blades rise from the crease where the wall coating meets the deck. One blade leans into the water running down the wall. The other has browned at its tip.

No soil was put here. What little they have is accumulating around their roots.

Their presence is easiest to miss during a flush. Every twenty minutes, the overhead line clears itself. The first sign is a shudder in the duct. Then the leaking seam opens under pressure and the floor receives several seconds of heavy water. The moss darkens. The grass lies flat. Loose grit moves toward the drain in small, branching streams.

Afterward, the grass slowly lifts.

There are no insects gathered in the wet corners, no birds at the louvers, no tracks beyond those left by maintenance boots. The surfaces are too new, the access too narrow, the water too cold and frequently renewed. Even the green growth seems provisional. A cleaner could remove all of it with one pass of a rag.

For now, the bracket shelters it.

Most of the court is empty: bare wet flooring, bare wall, the broad pipe, a ladder fixed vertically beside the door. The ladder ends at a locked hatch. Its lower rungs gather droplets along their undersides. You could stand here for a long time and learn the order in which they fall.

The wetness has boundaries. Water clings to metal before it drops. It follows the pitch of the floor, hesitates at new seams, gathers behind sealant, and disappears into the drain. Air rises through the grating around the manifold and pushes spray toward the outer wall. During stronger gusts, droplets travel almost horizontally, striking the paint at shoulder height.

So the driest place is not beside the door. It is beneath the pipe’s lowest elbow, where the machinery makes a small shelter from its own weather.

There you can look down the length of the court and see its whole landscape at once: the folded-metal ledge, the blue shallows, the white sealant bank, the two blades of grass, the dark opening of the drain. Behind and around them, immense equipment works continuously, moving quantities of air and water too large to see from here.

Inside this clearance, the consequences arrive one drop at a time.
