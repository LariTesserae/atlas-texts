The plateau begins above the clouds, but it is not dry.

Water stands in the hollows between roots. It gathers on the undersides of leaves and darkens the rock long after the sky has cleared. Even on the highest ridges, a heel pressed into the moss would bring up a little pool. There are lakes here with no visible shore: their edges pass beneath floating vegetation, and the vegetation passes into thickets so densely rooted that open water and solid ground cannot be distinguished by looking.

Beyond the nearest rise are more rises, blue-green and rounded, carrying the same heavy growth. Beyond those, the country becomes weather. Rain hangs over one district while sunlight reaches another; a line of white vapor marks a valley too far away for its depth to be guessed. The plateau is large enough that storms can spend their whole lives upon it. Some begin above the western bogs, travel for days through the upland forests, and dissolve into the eastern lakes without ever crossing lower ground.

The air is cold and remarkably still. Sunlight has its ordinary brightness, but little warmth. In a clearing it shows every bead on a fern, every orange patch of lichen, every pale hair along a leaf’s edge. Under the canopy, the light is green and broken into small, dependable quantities. Nothing is hidden by darkness so much as by the sheer number of things in front of it.

Trees grow low and broad. Their trunks divide near the ground, sometimes disappearing into moss before emerging again several paces away. An individual tree may occupy an entire slope without ever rising much higher than a person. Its branches carry gardens of smaller plants: leathery leaves, translucent liverworts, strings of swollen seed cases, ferns that root in the wet forks. Below them, shrubs crowd against shrubs. Grass fills the openings. Where grass cannot grow, there are cushions of moss; where moss cannot hold, a thin film of algae lies directly on the stone.

The vegetation does not have the hurried softness of a warm jungle. It is tough, close-jointed, slow to relinquish anything it has gained. Leaves remain through several seasons. Dead branches stay standing until something grows over them. A fallen trunk becomes a ridge of roots and fungal tissue, and eventually there is no clear moment at which it ceases to be a trunk and becomes terrain.

Rock is almost always nearby, though rarely bare. It lies beneath a hand’s depth of saturated soil, or rises as a smooth shoulder through the growth. Much of it is dark, with rusty seams and dull metallic grains. Old fractures have been widened by roots and rounded by water. The cliffs are not fresh breaks. Their faces lean back in steps, supporting gardens on every ledge, and the streams descending them have worn broad grooves rather than narrow cuts.

In places, water runs down the rock in sheets so thin they are visible only when they catch the sun. Each sheet follows a slightly different path through mineral crusts and green films. At the bottom, it enters a basin already full, spills into another, and then another. A whole hillside may be draining this way without producing anything louder than the intermittent fall of a drop.

There are larger rivers, but they are seldom seen from above. They run beneath mats of interlocked roots, through channels roofed by living growth. Their presence is marked by a line of taller vegetation, a cool draft issuing from a hole, a faint tremor felt through the soles of the feet. Sometimes a root mat has fallen in, leaving a long window into black water. Pale fish hold position there, scarcely moving their fins.

The plateau’s depths are less quiet.

Beneath the weathered surface is a great thickness of porous, iron-rich stone. Water percolates through it, warms slightly, dissolves what it can, and returns upward along other seams. Entire watersheds circulate this way. They fill underground chambers, compress trapped air, and release it through narrow vents under the marshes. The rising bubbles are caught by peat and roots. At the surface there may be only a slow lifting of the moss, followed by a settling, as if someone below had turned in sleep.

The same channels carry dense colonies of organisms that live on the minerals in the water. Their black films line the stone. Their filaments bridge fissures. Across them, small electrical differences accumulate and discharge, too weak to throw sparks but strong enough to alter the chemistry of a pool or guide the growth of a root.

This is where the plateau does its heavy work: pressure, circulation, heat exchange, the continual separation and recombination of dissolved metals. It operates throughout the year, at a scale beyond any visible animal or tree. Red sediment is drawn out of the water and laid down in soft terraces. Other pools remain astonishingly clear. Warm seams keep pockets of forest alive through the coldest months, while a short distance away frost stiffens every blade of grass.

There are no pipes, buildings, cut roads, or discarded objects. Nothing has been installed. Yet the country has the organization of a vast works whose moving parts have grown into one another. Its channels are older than the forests above them. When one closes, pressure finds another route; a marsh dries, a spring opens farther down the slope, and the vegetation begins its patient rearrangement.

Animals move through this crowded ground along passages that are nearly invisible until occupied.

Small, thick-furred grazers push beneath the shrubs, leaving tunnels whose ceilings spring back behind them. They eat the pale shoots in the shaded centers of moss cushions. Their tracks are more often detected by warmth than by sight: small places where dew has evaporated, or where frost has softened.

Higher on the ridges, broad-hoofed animals browse the tree crowns from the uphill side. Their coats resemble wet bark. They stand for long intervals with water dripping from their bellies, ears turning independently toward sounds too slight to carry far. When a group moves, the whole slope seems briefly to loosen and flow.

Predators follow the drainage lines. Long-bodied and low to the ground, they pass through fern beds without breaking the stems. Their hunting is mostly waiting. A head rests beside a root-channel opening; a whisker touches the current; an animal that has come down to drink vanishes with a small displacement of leaves. The forest closes around the event almost at once.

Birds are abundant, though the landscape is not full of song. Most call softly at close range. A few exchange single clear notes across the bogs, with long pauses between them. Wings make more sound than voices: a low brushing in the canopy, the dry flutter of a bird shaking rain from itself. Insects remain close to warm bark and sheltered flowers. On still afternoons, their movement can be seen as a fine trembling above patches of sunlit growth.

Sometimes the ground gives a faint, regular knock.

It comes from far below, where a chamber has filled and a column of water is finding its way into the next one. The interval may remain steady for an hour, then lengthen and stop. Nearby animals notice but do not startle. Birds pause on their branches. A grazer lifts its head. Somewhere beyond sight, a pool rises by the width of a finger.

By evening, cloud enters the trees.

It comes horizontally, drawn along the shallow valleys, gathering around branches and collecting in the open centers of bogs. The far ridges disappear first. Then the nearest trees become separate dark masses, each carrying a blurred fringe of wet leaves. The light remains adequate for a while; distance simply stops being available.

Underfoot, water continues toward its hidden channels. Roots tighten as the air cools. Small animals emerge into passages vacated by larger ones. On a rock face, a new thread of seepage finds an old groove.

The plateau goes on beyond the cloud, packed ridge after ridge with the same close life, though no two watersheds are quite alike. In one, the bogs are brown and acidic. In another, pale mineral fans lie beneath clear pools. Farther still, there are forests kept warm from below, and lakes that breathe through their margins, and broad, treeless heights where the moss is deep enough to conceal moving water.

From any one place, only a little of this can be heard. A drop lands on a leaf. Something small passes under the roots. Then there is a long interval in which the country appears perfectly still, while water is being lifted through the stone beneath it.
