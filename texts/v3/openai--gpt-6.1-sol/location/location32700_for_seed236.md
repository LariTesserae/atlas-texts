The place occupies the crown of a single stone pillar, high above a pale desert. You can walk from one edge to the other in a few minutes, provided you can find a passage through the growth. From below, the crown looks like a green fist. From within, it is a close little town of chambers, overhangs, narrow courts, and leafy roofs.

There is no stream, no well, no damp place beneath a shaded stone. The air is warm enough that the pale surfaces are uncomfortable to touch at noon. Even in the deepest passages, where the light turns green, the ground is dry.

The plants grow in the joints of the pillar. Their roots enter the rock as fine white threads and emerge farther down as thick, translucent cords. They draw up an oily mineral substance from somewhere inside it: amber-colored, slow-moving, almost without scent. A broken root seals itself with a bead of wax. Nothing drips.

Aboveground, the growth is luxuriant. Broad leaves overlap in heavy tiers, dark green on top and pale copper underneath. Some rise vertically like folded fans; others hang across the lanes so that anything passing must push beneath them. There are cushions of small fleshy needles, tangles of jointed vine, and low plants whose leaves fit together so closely that a whole bed resembles one corrugated surface. Dust gathers in their seams. At intervals, a leaf flexes and spills a thin line of it onto the ground.

The stone is young. Its exposed edges are sharp, its little fractures clean and pink within. At the western rim, a blade of rock has lifted through a root mat without yet breaking it; the mat stretches over the ridge, taut as skin over a knuckle. Fresh mineral growth appears beneath the older layers each warm season. It raises the crown by a little, opens a seam, tilts a passage. The plants adjust slowly around it.

Most of the town is alive.

Large, nearly motionless animals have attached themselves to the stone and built pale shells around their bodies. Each shell has several entrances and a rounded upper chamber. Where two animals have grown against one another, their walls join, leaving arches between them. Where several meet, there is a court. Their backs form terraces, their thicker limbs make sheltered colonnades, and their buried bodies keep the floors gently warm.

The shell surfaces resemble unglazed clay. Close up, they show fine lines where material has been added, along with patches of greenish dust and the polished marks of repeated feet. An entrance may take years to narrow as its owner grows. A passage that once admitted a broad-backed animal now accommodates only its young.

Other creatures occupy the chambers. Soft-footed grazers rest in the larger rooms, their narrow heads laid across one another’s shoulders. They browse by pressing their mouths to leaf margins and taking small, deliberate bites. Beetles travel the roots in quiet files. Flat, six-legged animals cling beneath the copper leaves, almost indistinguishable from blemishes until one changes its grip. In the warm wall cracks, pale larvae turn very slowly.

There are many bodies here, but few calls.

The grazers recognize one another by touch. The shell animals exchange faint movements through the floor. At sunrise, when their bodies warm at different rates, a shiver passes across a court: dust trembles, a vine lifts a finger’s width, a resting animal opens one eye. Unless your hand is against the wall, you may miss it.

What you can hear is smaller. A dry leaf brushing another. The faint tick of a beetle crossing a shell seam. Occasionally a crisp snap from the stone below, followed by a long silence. Even feeding makes little noise. There is no open water to trouble, no loose gravel beneath the soft feet, and the thick growth catches the high wind before it reaches the lanes.

Near the center is a space no wider than a modest room. Six shell walls enclose it, and the leaves overhead leave an irregular opening to the sky. Ordinary daylight falls through. It lights a square of pink stone, a curled leaf, the backs of three resting grazers. Along one wall, a vine has begun to bridge the opening. Its newest tip is still several seasons from reaching the opposite side.

This is where the animals gather during the hottest hours. They arrive separately and settle wherever there is room. Beetles pass beneath their bellies. Small flyers, with clear wings held tightly against their bodies, crawl into the shade of their necks. The court grows crowded without becoming restless.

At the outer rim, the green thins abruptly. Roots grip the last sharp ledges, and beyond them is the drop: warm air, distant salt, the pillar’s own narrow shadow laid across the desert. The boundary is busy with insects turning back from the glare. A few pale plants reach outward, their stiff leaves held level over empty space.

Standing there, you can see how little ground sustains the whole place. Behind you are layered leaves, occupied rooms, generations of worn thresholds. Ahead is a desert too dry to carry any of them. A root crosses the stone beside your foot and enters a fresh crack. Its tip has only just found purchase.
