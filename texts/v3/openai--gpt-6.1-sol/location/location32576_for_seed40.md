The first impression is of a white valley raised into the sky.

Its slopes are made of closely fitted plates, pale ceramic over ribbed steel, descending in terraces toward a channel too straight to be a river. Beyond it, more terraces rise into the brightness. They carry towers, overhead conveyors, tanks with rounded shoulders, and ranks of narrow buildings whose roofs nearly touch. From a distance the valley looks crowded with teeth. Up close, there are passages everywhere: slots between cooling fins, stairs folded behind pipes, lanes beneath the low bellies of reservoirs. The place extends for miles, and almost every cubic yard has something fitted into it.

There is no exposed earth. Even the dust is manufactured: powdered gasket, worn enamel, a fine gray bloom shed by the bearings of machines that move too slowly to be seen moving.

The air is cool enough that a hand left on a rail begins to ache. Altitude is measurable here, though it is difficult to say what the valley stands above. Through certain maintenance grilles, far below the walking level, another bright district is visible. Its buildings are smaller and differently arranged. Through other grilles there is only a white depth, crossed by tension cables. Occasionally a bird drops into one of these openings and comes out of a window higher up the opposite slope.

Most people do not watch long enough to determine whether it is the same bird.

Light comes from the sky and from the construction itself. Broad luminous panels occupy the undersides of bridges. The water channel shines from within. White towers catch daylight along their edges and return it into the streets through angled shutters. Shadows are sharp, narrow, and rarely black; a person crossing an open square may carry three of them, each of a slightly different length.

At midday the brightness washes the color from distant objects. A red maintenance gantry becomes pink, then white, then briefly appears to be a gap in the world. At close range the colors return: orange cable sheaths, blue inspection marks, the green of oxidized copper where a seam has leaked.

The valley is relatively new. Its corners are still acute. Roof ridges have not rounded under weather, and there are long stretches of paving on which no wheels have yet left marks. Along the upper terraces, protective film remains on whole facades, lifting at its corners in stiff transparent curls. Some structures have been commissioned without having been unwrapped.

Nevertheless, the valley has begun to acquire dampness.

It gathers first where the warm pipework passes through cold walls. Droplets bead beneath flanges, spread across bolt heads, and fall into shallow cups built into the paving. A cup may take several days to fill. When it does, the water tips through a concealed drain and appears somewhere downslope as a dark line across a wall.

Rain is infrequent and local. One street can receive a thin, persistent shower while the next remains dry. The shower has a boundary as straight as a wall. Birds waiting on a railing lean into it to drink, then withdraw to shake themselves.

Near the bottom of the valley, moisture collects in a system of stepped basins. The largest is perhaps the length of a station platform, scarcely ankle-deep, with blue tiles visible beneath water of remarkable clarity. Smaller basins are hidden inside the dense machinery: between generator housings, on the roofs of substations, in the abandoned beds of conveyors. These pockets support most of the vegetation.

There are grasses with stiff silver blades, cushions of dark moss around warm fasteners, and a creeping plant that follows copper joints with almost diagrammatic precision. Its roots enter the smallest openings. In places its leaves have grown around the stamped numbers on a pipe, leaving each digit exposed.

Higher on the slopes, trees grow singly in circular sockets. They are low, crooked things, with dark bark and leaves thick enough to resist the dry air. Each has a feeding line at its base. The lines disappear into the paving, so the trees seem to be plugged into the valley. Some sockets are empty. Around these, birds gather as if something remains there worth sheltering beneath.

The animals are numerous.

Small pale birds occupy the latticework under nearly every bridge. Black ones walk along the parapets with the deliberate gait of inspectors. Long-bodied grazers move between the cooling towers, feeding on the tough grass in expansion joints. Their hooves make almost no sound on the ceramic. At dusk—when the light changes temperature rather than fading—they kneel beside the warm walls, arranged in close rows.

There are moths as broad as a palm, translucent lizards in the drainage recesses, and colonies of tiny furred creatures living behind removable panels. Fish inhabit the shallow basins, including some that seem much too large for the available water. A fish can lie under a centimeter of water without breaking the surface. Its back is visible, its gills work, and beneath it the blue tile continues uninterrupted.

The basins are deeper for fish.

This is one of the valley’s settled peculiarities. Depth is not shared equally. A maintenance worker can step across a pool that holds an animal longer than the worker’s arm. Birds can pass through openings too small for their wings. The narrow service stairs are spacious when climbed carrying a load, but cramped when descended empty-handed. These differences have been incorporated into the operating manuals. There are weight tables, diagrams, warnings about looking back on the third landing.

Much of the industry concerns the management of such discrepancies.

The tallest towers are fitted with slow counterweights. Once every few hours, a weight descends several inches, drawing a band of darkness down the tower’s white face. Banks of enclosed flywheels keep neighboring streets at approximately the same length. Along the central channel, squared chambers receive portions of the valley that have drifted out of alignment. Their doors remain closed for weeks. When a chamber opens, it contains a flight of stairs, a section of roof, or an empty room whose windows overlook a place nobody recognizes.

Workers mark these things with chalk and leave them to settle.

For all its machinery, the landscape is mostly quiet. Motors are isolated in deep housings. Conveyor chains advance one link at a time. Freight moves on broad pneumatic platforms that slide through the passages with the soft sound of a sleeve brushing a wall. Even the animals are subdued. A bird may open its beak and produce only a little disturbance in the air, like heat above a vent.

What can be heard is intimate: water releasing from a full cup, the small crack of cooling enamel, a latch closing three terraces away. Occasionally a single metallic note travels through the structure. It lasts so long that it becomes part of the silence before it ends.

There are residents, though they seldom fill the streets. Their homes occupy the upper stories of industrial blocks, where laundry hangs behind clear wind screens and window boxes contain the same sparse silver grass found below. Footbridges connect kitchens to workshops. Children keep small animals in ventilation cages and learn which reflections can be trusted.

Changes happen slowly enough to become familiar before anyone names them. A distant tower spends a season leaning toward its own shadow. A walkway lengthens by the width of a boot each month. One of the lower basins has been moving uphill for years, still joined to its drains, leaving behind clean rectangular impressions in the paving.

The machinery compensates. Additional pipe is fitted. A new stair is bolted into place. Birds relocate their nests a little at a time.

From the uppermost terrace, the valley opens into a broad view of pale roofs and intricate, near-touching structures. Damp seams glitter between them. The animals are visible as dark strokes on ledges, white specks under bridges, slow groups passing through the colonnades of cooling equipment. Far away, a great shutter is closing across the side of a building. It has been closing all morning.

Beside the viewing rail, a drop of water swells on the underside of a bolt. Within it, the valley appears inverted, clear and densely inhabited.

The drop holds for a long time.
