The land lies below the river, though from the middle of it you cannot see the river at all. You see its embankment: a dark, nearly level line carrying a few lamps, a pipe bridge, and the silhouettes of trucks that appear to move along the sky. Beyond that bank the tide is rising. Here, several meters lower, warm water stands in every depression, and the ground between depressions is scarcely firm enough to deserve a different name.

It is late afternoon under a low roof of cloud. The light has the brownish-gray quality of daylight passing through rain that has not quite stopped. Distance remains open but indistinct. A pumping house two kilometers away looks close until a flock of birds crosses in front of it, tiny against its wall. Farther out, another bank divides the basin, and beyond that are more flooded compartments, more pipes, more pale strips of deposited silt. The whole lowland extends for several kilometers. No single overlook gives a view of all of it.

This is new ground, being made rather than worn away.

Dredged sediment comes here from the shipping channel through steel pipes wide enough for a person to crawl inside. The pipes terminate at different points around the basin. At an active outlet, brown water pours onto a low apron of coarse sand, then spreads into a shallow fan. Heavy grains settle first. Farther away, the flow carries only fine silt; farther still, the clay remains suspended, making the standing water opaque and pale. The differences are slight enough to miss while walking, but from the embankment you can see whole acres sorting themselves into color.

There are no established streambeds. A runnel follows the edge of a sand deposit for a few weeks, then disappears beneath the next layer. Broad patches that look solid are still slurry under a thin skin. Elsewhere, the sand will hold a boot but releases water around it with every step. Survey stakes mark surfaces whose heights are still changing. The painted bands at their bases are gradually being buried.

The noise is enormous.

The nearest dredge pump has a deep mechanical note you feel in your chest before you identify it as sound. Along the bank, drainage engines add a rougher vibration. At the spillway, water drops through a concrete throat and pounds against the receiving pool. None of these sounds stops long enough for the others to become clear. They overlap with the cries of gulls, the nasal calls of ducks, and the rattling of loose sheet metal on a service shed.

A conversation requires two people to stand close and shout toward each other’s ears. From farther away, you see mouths moving and hands pointing, but hear nothing that belongs to them.

Despite the volume, very little seems to happen. The pump runs at the same speed. The discharge keeps spreading across the same fan. Rain stipples the pools. A marker buoy leans against its tether and returns. Only after an hour does it become apparent that the fan has lengthened, or that a strip of dark mud exposed when you arrived is now under water.

The warmth is held low. Rain collects heat from the pipes and engines, and the soft ground retains the day’s temperature long after the sky darkens. In sheltered pockets behind the banks, the air feels close and damp. There is a smell of brackish water, diesel exhaust, wet clay, and the sour decay of organic material brought up from the channel bottom.

Vegetation has barely begun. A few reeds grow beside an older drainage ditch where the sediment has had time to settle. Their lower stems are coated with gray residue from recent flooding. Small salt-tolerant plants occupy a raised margin near the service road, spaced widely enough that each casts its own faint shadow. Algae makes a slick green border around some pools. There are no trees within the basin, and almost no patch where roots have turned the loose material into soil.

The apparent barrenness is misleading. The place is crowded with animals.

Waterbirds occupy the banks, the pipe crossings, the shallow pools, and every piece of timber with a dry upper surface. Small waders advance along the freshest deposits, probing rapidly at the waterline. Gulls gather where the discharge uncovers shellfish and worms from the dredged mud. Ducks feed in the quieter compartments. Herons stand in the drainage channels, sometimes so still that rain beads along their backs before they move.

In a ditch beside the pumping house, small fish hold their positions against the current. Shrimp flick backward through the cloudy shallows. Worm casts cover the older mud. Where water has retreated by only a few centimeters, the exposed surface is already punctured with breathing holes and crossed by fine tracks.

The birds are accustomed to the machinery. They lift when a truck passes, circle briefly, and land behind it. Some feed within a few meters of a running engine. At dusk, their numbers increase until the distant banks seem to have acquired a restless, pale edging.

Human structures are scattered and mostly practical. A concrete pumping house sits high on a gravel pad. There is a corrugated workshop with an open door, a fuel tank behind a bund wall, and a shelter containing electrical switches. Long pipe runs connect these isolated buildings across the mud. Their supports are mismatched: concrete blocks, timber cradles, steel frames driven down to firmer material.

At one crossing, a narrow walkway has been laid over the pipes. Its handrail is wet even on the sheltered side. Underfoot, the grating trembles with the flow. Someone has hung a faded life ring from a post, though the greater danger here is sinking into mud too dense to swim through.

A board near the access gate shows the planned ground levels. The finished landscape, according to the drawing, will contain straight roads, drainage canals, and rectangular plots. Looking from the board to the basin, it is difficult to connect one with the other. The roads currently end at soft margins. The plots are indicated only by stakes. Most of the surface is water carrying the material from which the future land will be made.

Along the perimeter, seepage emerges through the inner face of the embankment. Each leak darkens a small patch, then feeds a clear thread down into the cloudy pools. Behind the bank is the higher river; beneath the basin, groundwater presses upward. The pumps can lower the water, but they cannot make the place dry for long. Rain, seepage, and the incoming slurry keep replacing what is removed.

As evening comes, the nearest objects remain distinct while the far compartments vanish into damp haze. Lamps switch on above the pumping station. Their reflections lie in broken yellow strips across the water, interrupted by raindrops and swimming birds. The engine noise continues at full strength. A worker closes the workshop door, climbs into a truck, and drives slowly up the bank.

Out in the basin, a newly exposed ridge of sand is already occupied by gulls. Behind them, water drains from a thin layer of silt. The layer settles almost imperceptibly, gaining the strength to support another deposit. By morning the discharge will have covered part of the ridge, the birds will have moved to its new edge, and the survey crew will have another level to measure.
