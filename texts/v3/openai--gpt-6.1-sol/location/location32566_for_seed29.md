The place is a split in a stone, just below the summit.

From outside, it would be easy to miss: a black seam in a block of lava, narrower than a fingernail, almost concealed by the way the block leans against its neighbor. Beyond it are altitude, glare, and a slope of loose volcanic rock. But none of that light reaches far inside. Here the landscape is the interior of the seam—the two walls, the packed floor, and the little chambers where flakes have lifted without falling away.

The stone is still warm from its making.

Heat comes through the floor more strongly than through the walls. In the deepest recesses it is too much to touch; nearer the entrance, where the mountain wind draws a little air through, it is merely oppressive. Each surface has its own temperature. A beetle moving the length of its body can leave a tolerable ledge and step onto something that drives it back.

There is no water to see. No bead hanging from a ceiling, no damp patch beneath a stone. A small amount of vapor passes through from below, carrying a sharp mineral smell, but the hot air takes it away before it can gather. The finest dust stays loose. Where an animal crosses it, the track remains until another animal crosses in turn.

Nothing is green.

The walls are basalt: dull black in some places, faintly lustrous in others, with fresh edges so thin they transmit a brownish light. Their shapes have not yet been softened. There are hooks, blades, curled skins of stone, little shelves with undersides still glassy. A bubble that burst as the lava cooled has left a chamber like the inside of a broken tooth. Another bubble remains intact except for a pinhole opening. Warm air breathes through it.

The floor looks solid, but it is crowded with fragments. Grains of ash fit between splinters; splinters are wedged among larger chips; pale crystals occupy the remaining hollows. Very little is empty. What seems at first to be a flat patch is the upper surface of several layers, each with narrow routes underneath.

The animals use all of them.

Mites move among the grains in numbers too large to count. In the darkness they are almost indistinguishable from the material around them, until a speck turns against the drift of the dust. Some are pale and soft-bodied. Others carry glossy plates that catch the weak light from the entrance. Springtails shelter farther in, where the air holds a little more moisture, though there is no wet surface anywhere. Along a cooler wall, two small beetles work their way through a wad of windblown debris.

Most of the food has come down from the sky.

A moth wing is jammed beneath a flake. Its scales have rubbed off into a silver-gray smear. There are insect legs, fragments of seed husk carried up from much lower slopes, a feather barb, a dry knot of tissue too far consumed to identify. The high wind delivers almost nothing regularly, but the seam retains what it receives. Once a fragment passes the entrance, it catches on one of the new, sharp edges and stays.

The smallest animals feed on thin microbial films and on this imported debris. Predators follow them. A minute spider occupies a hollow behind a projecting chip, its web stretched across a passage scarcely wider than itself. The web is dusty enough to resemble another mineral crust. Only the spider’s occasional adjustment gives it away.

There is movement everywhere, yet the place changes slowly.

A mite circles a crystal and disappears. The beetles remain at their meal. The spider waits. Heat rises steadily through the same stone, and a grain loosened yesterday may remain balanced for another month. Even the mineral deposits grow too slowly to watch. Around one opening, a pale rim is accumulating from the gases that pass through; it has the delicacy of frost, but the chamber is hot.

The noise is surprisingly large.

Wind passing over the mouth makes a low, uneven roar. Within the seam, the sound becomes confined and resonant. Loose flakes tremble against one another. Sometimes a high vibration hangs in the stone, and the dust on an exposed ledge shifts by fractions of a grain.

Under the wind is a deeper, steadier sound: machinery.

Far below the summit, beyond this little landscape, an industrial drilling site works into the mountain. No pipe, cable, platform, or manufactured surface reaches the seam. Inside, there is only rock and the material the wind has brought. But vibration travels through the mountain’s body. Motors arrive here as a continuous low shudder; a change of load becomes a pulsing pressure in the floor. The air may fall quiet while the walls continue to hum.

For the animals, that sound is part of the ground. Their feet meet it wherever they stand.

Occasionally a machine stops. The absence travels upward, and for a while smaller sounds become distinct: the faint click of a cooling flake, the dry scrape of a beetle forcing itself through a tight passage. Then the deep vibration returns. It is loud without requiring much to move.

Light occupies only the first few millimeters. At certain hours, the mouth becomes a bright vertical cut, and grains nearest it cast long shadows into the interior. Farther back, sight has little use. The animals follow surfaces, air currents, heat, the traces left by other bodies. A passage may be open but uninhabitable because its floor is too hot. A apparently blocked hollow may be full of life because the spaces between its grains are just large enough.

This is young terrain. Its edges still record cooling rather than weathering. The crystals are beginning to occupy it; the loose fragments have barely settled. A thin plate may eventually detach and close a route used by hundreds of animals. Another may lift enough to admit air, cooling a recess and making it habitable for the first time.

At the back of the seam, beneath a ceiling scarcely higher than a beetle’s shell, a fresh crack is opening. It is too narrow now for anything but gas. Its edges are clean, its interior dark and hot. Heat comes from it in a steady thread.

Around that opening, the dust is motionless. Farther out, where the warmth weakens, small bodies gather between the stones.
