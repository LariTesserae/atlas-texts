The town occupies a hollow near the top of a mountain that has not yet learned to look old.

Its stone is dark and sharply broken. Along the rim, slabs stand edge-on like teeth, their pale inner faces still clean of lichen. A few have split within living memory. One split last winter, without falling: it simply opened a narrow black seam from top to bottom, dividing an old lookout stair between two slightly different heights. The stairs still work if you know where to take the longer step.

Beyond the rim there is very little to see. Cloud lies below the town most days, and higher peaks emerge from it far apart, blue-black and apparently uninhabited. The hollow itself is perhaps three hundred paces across. You can walk its circumference before the light changes, although you will probably stop several times to let something pass.

There is no river. No fountain, no well mouth, no water running down the lanes. Rain comes rarely, in brief, cold showers that leave the stone dark for an hour. Most of the town’s water arrives as beads on the undersides of leaves.

The vegetation has made an elaborate economy of this.

Across the northern half of the hollow grow broad, low trees with horizontal limbs and foliage the color of old bottle glass. Their leaves are thick, nearly circular, cupped underneath. During the night they turn those cups toward the sky. By morning, each holds a little condensation. Then the branches twist—not quickly enough to watch comfortably, but quickly enough to notice between one task and the next—and carry the leaves back under cover.

Beneath them are gardens of fleshy ferns, pale flowering cushions, and trailing plants whose stems feel like braided leather. Greenery climbs every retaining wall. It hangs from lintels and follows the narrow channels cut into the paving. Certain streets are roofed entirely by leaves, yet the spaces between the trees remain surprisingly open. The town has the lushness of a carefully tended collection rather than a forest: one enormous plant, a stretch of bare stone, another enormous plant.

Nothing grows carelessly here. Even the moss forms distinct islands, each surrounded by a dry border as crisp as a shoreline.

The houses stand in those intervals.

There are twenty or thirty of them, depending on whether you count the empty ones, built of fitted slabs, wooden pins, and thick mats of woven root. Their roofs are low and heavily weighted. No chimney rises above the foliage. Hearth smoke is drawn sideways through clay-lined passages and released beneath the outer walls, where it spreads thinly along the ground.

The buildings face inward onto small paved courts. A court might contain a grinding stone, a bench, a rack for drying cut leaves, and a single immense stalk growing through a hole in the pavement. Alleys connect them by turns and short flights of steps. From above, the town would resemble a loose knot pulled almost—but not quite—tight.

There is organized life here: a communal storehouse, a place for weighing bundles, a long room where disputes are settled, a stair reserved for carrying the dead to the ridge. Yet there are few people in view. Doors remain open. Tools wait where they were put down. A stone adze rests on a bench beside a half-shaped bowl, and its owner may be only an arm’s length away behind a curtain of leaves.

Much of what moves through the town is not human.

At first you notice the large animals. Six-legged grazers stand under the trees, their narrow backs brushed with gray fur. They feed by inserting their heads into the thick cushions of greenery and withdrawing them slowly, as if pulling thread through cloth. Their extra pair of limbs lies folded along the ribs until they reach a wall. Then the limbs open, grip, and lift the animal neatly onto a higher terrace.

They make almost no sound. Their feet have soft, divided pads. Even their chewing is difficult to hear.

Then you begin to see the smaller bodies.

Flat blue creatures are attached to the undersides of the leaves, indistinguishable from shadows until they release their grip and glide to another branch. Tiny antlered animals occupy cracks in the masonry. Pale worms travel through the translucent outer stems of the hanging plants; you can watch them stop where the stem contains a bead of water. There are wingless insects that leap sideways, beetles carrying scraps of moss on their backs, and soft-bodied things that have learned to resemble the wooden pegs used in the houses.

A peg beside a doorway may unfold and walk away.

The abundance takes time to become apparent. It is easy, at first, to think the town deserted. Then one patch of pavement resolves into hundreds of minute bodies lying flat in the warmth retained by the stone. A fern uncurls, and an entire colony shifts with it. What looked like the worn fringe of a roof lifts its heads.

There are animals inside the walls, between roof mats, underneath thresholds, in the folds of clothing hung to air. Some are tolerated. Some are cultivated. Some are neither, but cannot be excluded without taking the building apart.

The inhabitants put collars of polished stone around their grain jars. They suspend bedding from cords rubbed with bitter sap. They shake out their shoes, then wait a moment before shaking them again.

Predators move among all this profusion. A long, jointed hunter lies along a branch in the southern court, its limbs arranged to match the angles of the wood. It may stay there for two days. When a grazer passes below, the branch suddenly appears to come apart. The hunter drops, folds around its prey, and climbs back up.

Leaves tremble. A few small creatures scatter across the paving.

Afterward there is silence again.

There are no calls here that carry far. No dawn chorus. No insect drone. Even the flying animals have fringed wings that disturb the air only slightly. The town’s living things communicate by touch, scent, and vibrations transmitted through stem or stone. A creature can be surrounded by activity and hear almost nothing.

People have adopted the same restraint. They close doors with a hand on the edge. They lower baskets rather than setting them down. In the public room, an argument may be conducted in voices too soft to reach the street.

A dropped bowl is an event. It causes the moss nearest it to contract. Grazers lift their heads. For several seconds, the tiny animals in the cracks retreat so far that the walls seem newly built.

The quiet does not mean stillness.

By midmorning, the leaf roofs have changed their angles. Light enters one alley and leaves another. The dimness is green beneath the canopy, violet near the mountain rim. On clear days the sun never quite clears the highest slabs; it moves behind them, touching the town in narrow bands.

Cold comes through the paving even when the air is mild. People wear short, padded cloaks and leave their hands bare for work. Iron is scarce. Knives are resharpened until they become slender as thorns. Most cutting is done with flaked stone, most lifting with wooden levers, most fastening with cord. A house can be repaired using what grows beside it, though the repair may need to be watched for several weeks to ensure it does not sprout.

Near the center of the hollow is a sunken square, broad enough for perhaps forty people to stand together. Nobody builds in it. Its floor is a single sheet of pale rock, unweathered, with thin green veins running toward a closed fissure.

At dusk, the veins darken.

Then the animals begin arriving.

They come out of walls and foliage, down roof cords, along the retaining terraces. Grazers descend carefully. Small bodies pour around their feet. Even the branch-hunters leave their perches. They arrange themselves across the square with remarkable precision: the largest near the edge, the smallest packed around the fissure.

The people remain in their doorways.

For a while, nothing happens. The square is full of breathing, shifting life, and still quieter than a room of sleeping people.

Then moisture rises out of the rock.

It does not pool. It beads across the pale surface, each drop growing in place. The animals bend to it. Those without mouths press their bellies against it; those too small to reach crawl onto the bodies of others. For a few minutes the square becomes a dense, slow rearrangement of backs, limbs, and folded wings.

By the time the first stars appear, the stone is dry.

The animals disperse. A few fail to return: an old grazer lies beneath the eastern steps, or a hunter carries away something soft and pale. The people come out with shallow clay dishes and collect the traces left in the green veins. There is seldom enough to drink. They use it to start seedlings.

At night the vegetation settles lower over the town. The leaf cups turn upward. In the houses, bowls are covered, cords checked, embers buried. Beyond the hollow, wind passes hard over the exposed ridge, but down among the courts it reaches only the highest branches.

You can sit on a doorstep and watch a whole slope of leaves gradually lift to receive the dark.

Close by, an animal no longer than your thumb emerges from beneath the threshold. It pauses beside your foot, touches the leather once, and continues along the wall. Behind it comes another, and then another, each following the same narrow route through a town that seems empty until you learn where to look.
