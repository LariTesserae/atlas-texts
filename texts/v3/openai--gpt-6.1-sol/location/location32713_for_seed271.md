The hollow is small enough that you can see its far side from almost anywhere within it, though not all of it at once. Low limestone shoulders overlap around the edges, hiding one shallow recess behind another. There is no dramatic descent into it: the ground simply lowers, levels out, and becomes a floor of pale clay between the rocks. Beyond the rim, the land continues at much the same height.

The air is warm. Even in the shade, the stone has none of the coldness you might expect from its color. A faint mineral dampness hangs close to the ground, strongest beside the northern wall, where moisture darkens the limestone along a seam no thicker than a finger.

There is no stream. Water occupies a few places and leaves the rest alone.

Under an overhang lies a shallow pool, perhaps three paces across. Its bottom is visible through brownish water: flat stones, a little fine sediment, the pale outline of a submerged ledge. Along one edge, the water meets bare rock without a fringe of plants. Along the other, it rests against clay so smoothly that the boundary is difficult to distinguish. A smaller wet patch sits several yards away, caught in a depression that would not hold much more than a bowlful. Between them is dry ground, firm and faintly cracked.

The hollow receives little direct light. Its sides are not high, but their inward slopes, together with the broad overhang, keep much of the floor in shade. Above the open center, the sky is a low, even gray. Everything is visible without being brightly lit: cream-colored stone, gray-brown earth, a dull green tucked into cracks. The pool reflects a soft patch of sky rather than a distinct image.

The rocks have been worn past sharpness. Small shelves end in rounded lips. Channels in the limestone are broad and shallow, their edges softened by long exposure. Nothing looks freshly broken. Here and there a darker bed runs horizontally through the pale rock, making the wall resemble a stack of thick, imperfect slabs, though it is all one continuous formation.

Near the widest part of the hollow stands a remnant of masonry.

It is scarcely a building now: a wall at knee height, turning once at a right angle, with a few larger blocks still seated at its base. The stones were taken from nearby and differ little from those behind them. Only their fitted faces distinguish the work from the hillside. Whatever enclosed space they once made is now an open rectangle of bare earth. There is no roof timber, no door, no object left inside. On the shaded side of one block, a thin patch of moss reaches across both stone and mortar without marking the difference.

A worn step survives at the gap in the wall. Its center is slightly lower than its ends. It leads nowhere beyond the small rectangle, but the shape is still unmistakably a step.

Vegetation is sparse and confined to places where roots can find a little purchase. A low shrub grows near the rim, its dusty leaves gathered tightly around crooked branches. Two clumps of coarse grass stand at the foot of the southern slope. In a sheltered fissure, a fern has only a handful of fronds. Much of the damp ground remains bare; moisture here does not necessarily mean greenery.

There is a large quiet space between each of these presences. The shrub does not touch the grasses. The grasses do not reach the wall. Even the moss forms separate patches, with clean stone between them. From the step, one can look across most of the floor without anything interrupting the view.

No bird calls from the rim. Nothing stirs in the shallow water. The only occasional sound is a drop falling from the underside of the overhang into the pool. It makes a small, blunt note, followed by silence long enough that the next drop seems unrelated. Elsewhere, the wet seam merely shines.

The place feels settled rather than abandoned. The masonry is the one clear trace of an earlier use, but it occupies so little of the hollow that it does not determine its character. The pool has its recess, the shrub its crack, the wall its level patch of ground. None appears to be spreading into the others.

Even the loose stones seem to have found their resting places: a few against the wall, two half embedded in clay, one lying alone near the center. There are no fresh tracks through the sediment and no bright debris on the weathered surfaces.

At the far end, the floor rises gently toward a gap between the limestone shoulders. It is an easy way out, only a few steps up to the surrounding ground. Seen from inside, though, the gap is narrow enough to leave the hollow feeling enclosed. Its whole extent holds together beneath the muted light: the rounded stone, the sparse plants, the low remnant of wall, and the small amount of water resting in the places that retain it.
