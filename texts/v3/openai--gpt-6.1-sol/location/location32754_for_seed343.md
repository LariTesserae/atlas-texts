The height is difficult to understand at first because there is so little left to climb.

You come up through three days of steep country, through broken escarpments and slopes where roots have dragged entire shelves of stone out of place, and expect a summit. Instead, the land opens. Ahead lies a broad, gently rolling surface, warm under a clear sky, extending to a horizon so distant that weather can be watched forming there long before it arrives. The mountains have worn down to their bases. What remains is their high country: a plateau standing above the clouds, its oldest valleys reduced to shallow depressions, its ridges rounded almost beyond recognition.

Only at the edges does the altitude become undeniable. There the ground ends in enormous soft-looking bluffs, and the air below is white. On clear afternoons, gaps in the cloud reveal other hills far beneath, blue and small as pebbles seen through deep water.

Nearly everything underfoot is alive.

The surface resembles faded felt, ochre in some places, silver-green in others, with wide bruised patches of purple. It is a continuous growth of low plants, their stems pressed sideways and their leaves fitted so closely together that there is rarely a glimpse of soil. Walk on it and the ground gives a little. Kneel and part the leaves, and there are more leaves beneath them, then runners, braided roots, old fibers, black crumbs of vegetable matter. Farther down lies pale wood, compressed by centuries of growth.

Stone exists, but much of it is buried under this accumulation. Sometimes a boulder shows through as a white tooth, polished by grit. More often, what looks like a rock is a dense knot of roots that has been exposed, dried, and rubbed smooth by generations of passing feet.

The plateau has very few trees. From a distance it seems almost vacant. A low rise, a hollow, a dark line of crawling growth; then miles of open sky. There are no woods to enter and scarcely anything tall enough to stand behind. Plants have occupied all the ground without filling much of the air.

The tallest are wind-combed shrubs, widely scattered, each one resembling a green flag stretched permanently in the same direction. Beneath them, little hollows shelter glossy leaves, tender flowers, and insects with bodies as translucent as peeled fruit. A shrub may be the only upright object visible for half a day’s walk. Animals gather around it so thickly that its shade appears to move.

The first thing you notice, however, is the noise.

It reaches you while you are still below the plateau: a bright, continuous friction, like an enormous quantity of grain being sifted. Up close it separates into thousands of sounds. Dry clicks travel over the ground. Whistles descend from overhead. Something in the root layer knocks in pairs, pauses, then knocks again. Wingbeats make a low, unsteady thunder that passes across the country without any visible cloud.

Most of the animals are small, but there are too many of them for smallness to matter.

Long-legged grazers move through the vegetation in flowing bands, each no larger than a thumb, their yellow backs flashing as they climb over one another. Moths lift from warm hollows in sheets. Hard-bodied insects cling to every stem exposed by a footstep. Tiny lizards live almost entirely within the foliage; you see their heads for an instant before they vanish under the surface, making ripples that continue well beyond where you expected them to stop.

There are larger animals too. Low, broad-backed creatures with pale horns graze in loose groups, cropping the tougher growth around the plateau’s shallow basins. Their hooves spread into several blunt toes, distributing their weight over the living mat. They rarely run. When frightened, they flatten themselves against the ground and become difficult to distinguish from hummocks.

Above them, birds occupy the apparently empty air.

At noon they are often too high to see properly. Their shadows slide over the land, narrow and fast. Some hunt insects; others follow the grazers and dive into the places disturbed by their mouths. Farther up are broad-winged scavengers, scarcely moving, held aloft by heat rising from the plateau. Occasionally one folds its wings and drops. The insects nearby quiet for a few seconds. Then the sound closes over the interruption.

Water is scarce on the surface, though the country never becomes entirely dry.

Rain arrives in short, hard visits. A cloud drifts across the rim and unloads itself on one stretch of plateau while the next remains in sunlight. The water disappears quickly between the leaves. For an hour afterward the whole soaked area darkens, smells sharply green, and steams.

Most moisture comes at night. The temperature falls just enough for dew to form on the smallest leaf tips. Certain plants turn their leaves upright after sunset, exposing fine combs along their edges. By dawn these are beaded with water. The droplets run down grooves toward the root layer, where they collect in spaces protected from the daytime heat.

You can cross a seemingly dry hollow and hear water beneath your feet.

In a few places the plant cover has opened around permanent pools. Their edges are soft, overhanging lips of interwoven roots. The water is tea-brown and usually still, except where animals drink or something rises beneath it. Narrow channels connect some of these pools underground. During a rain, their surfaces lift together. During the long bright intervals, they fall so slowly that a daily change is hard to measure.

The pools are loud places. Frogs live in pockets around their margins, each pocket amplifying the voice inside it. A single animal produces a hollow note that seems far too large for its body. Hundreds calling at once make the air tremble against your chest.

People have learned not to build beside them.

Their houses stand on dry swells of ground, often several miles apart. At first they look like overturned baskets: low roofs of woven stems, held down with smooth stones, their entrances facing away from the prevailing wind. The walls are thick bundles of dead plant fiber. A few dwellings have foundations of exposed rock, but most rest on broad wooden rafts, which can be lifted and shifted when the growth beneath them becomes uneven.

Inside, the light is greenish and the sound is reduced to a dense hum. Sleeping chambers are lined with loose seed wool. Doorways have two hanging curtains rather than one, and people wear soft plugs in their ears while working outdoors.

A visitor soon learns why conversations happen at close quarters. Someone can shout from twenty paces away and be lost entirely in the insects. People catch one another’s attention with a raised cloth, a hand held high, or a flash from a polished shell.

Their tools are simple and carefully kept: stone blades, wooden needles, hooks made from horn, small copper cutting edges traded up from the lower country. Much of their work consists of separating one plant fiber from another. Roof cord, water wick, sandal mesh, carrying basket, ear stuffing—each comes from a particular layer of growth and must be gathered at a particular season.

Paths do not last unless they are used. A newly cut track is visible for a week or two; then leaves turn into the opening and runners cross it. The older routes are marked by stakes, set far apart, with strips of pale bark tied near their tops. Between markers, you follow subtle signs: vegetation pressed in a certain direction, a succession of firm places beneath your feet, a bend around a hollow where the surface will not bear your weight.

There are ruins, though it is difficult to tell how old they are.

On one rise stands a waist-high circle of masonry, its stones so rounded that each looks gathered from a stream. The vegetation has filled the interior and climbed over the wall, leaving only the upper halves of the blocks exposed. Elsewhere, a lintel lies alone, supported at both ends by living wood that has grown around it. No road reaches either place. Whatever once connected them has been absorbed.

Toward evening, the plateau becomes easier to read. Low sunlight reveals long shallow folds invisible at noon. You can see how water once moved across the land, how ridges have slumped, how the plant cover has bridged cracks and followed the settling ground. The enormous age of the place lies in these small differences of height, in a drainage channel too faint to notice until a shadow enters it.

The warm air keeps rising. Birds wheel along the escarpment. A band of grazers works steadily westward, surrounded by flies that glitter whenever they turn into the light.

Then the dusk callers begin.

Their voices pass from hollow to hollow, deeper than the daytime rasping, with long wavering notes and sharp answering bursts. Winged animals emerge from under the vegetation. They skim close to the surface, then climb into the last warmth above it. For a while the whole broad country seems to be lifting away from its own ground.

From the doorway of a house, with the fiber curtain held aside, you can see almost nothing except land and sky. A stake on the next rise. One bent shrub. A pale animal lowering its head miles away.

All around, the unseen life continues at a volume that makes speech impossible.

By morning the leaves will be wet, the roof will be dry, and the first sunlight will find the grazers still feeding where they fed through the night.
