The country drops away behind a rim of broken turf. From above, it looks like a wet hollow in the land, broad enough to hold a town. Once you descend, the rim becomes a horizon: a dark, uneven line of roots and freshly exposed stone, enclosing a pale sky.

The floor lies well below the surrounding ground. It has only recently settled. Along the margins, slabs of peat have slipped down almost intact, carrying their shrubs with them; some still stand upright, while others lean against the exposed bank with their roots in the air. Pale clay shows beneath the black soil. Stones have sharp corners. Rain has begun drawing little channels through the new slopes, but has not yet softened them.

Water arrives everywhere.

It beads along the cut ends of roots, seeps through the clay, and falls from ledges in threads too fine to hear. Near the bottom, a footprint fills before its maker has moved far away. What appears to be a meadow is often a shallow pool with grass growing through it. What appears to be solid bank may be a raft of roots, firm enough for a small animal and trembling under anything heavier.

Across this low country, beavers have built a settlement.

There is no single pond and no single dam. Water occupies successive levels, held by barriers that cross the hollow from one patch of higher ground to another. Some barriers are new: pale, freshly gnawed sticks lie crosswise over black mud, with willow leaves still attached. Others have become broad embankments under sedge and moss. Young trees grow from them. Between these older banks, the water is dark and almost still.

The lodges rise close together in the deepest reaches, each a mound of branches plastered with mud, each with its entrances below the surface. From a distance they resemble low, shaggy islands. Up close, their differences emerge. One has been enlarged around a living willow. Another rests against the fallen trunk of a birch, using it as a spine. A third is almost buried in reeds, visible chiefly by the cleared water around its underwater doors.

Canals connect them. Narrow passages run through the sedges, widen into feeding pools, then disappear under hanging branches. Logs float in some of these passages, stripped of bark and waiting to be moved. Elsewhere, paths climb from the water to stands of saplings. Their edges are crowded with clipped stems, low regrowth, and the fan-shaped impressions left by broad tails.

The construction extends farther than you can see from any one place. Beyond the nearest pools are more banks, more low islands, more channels slipping between thickets. A bend that seems to end the hollow opens into another reach of water. The settlement has grown around differences of only a few inches: a ridge of clay, a buried trunk, a place where the seepage is stronger. Its many small works have made a continuous landscape.

There is scarcely an unused surface.

Moss coats the wet wood. Ferns open in the sheltered spaces between lodge branches. Willow roots descend through the dams and bind their loosened material. Sedge grows thickly enough to conceal the banks, though the narrow waterways remain clear where bodies pass each evening. At the colder edges, alder scrub stands over carpets of liverwort and pale green moss, with droplets suspended beneath every leaf.

The vegetation is lush but low. Trees grow slowly here. Many have several trunks, replacing the stems that were cut or drowned. Their crowns spread sideways into the light, knitting above channels without quite closing them. Reeds rise in dense, tawny walls, and through their bases grow soft new blades, vivid green against the old stalks.

The light is ordinary daylight, softened by cloud. The hollow is wide and open to the sky, despite its depth. At noon, there is enough brightness to see the pebbles beneath the shallower water and the red veins in a fallen leaf. The high banks shade the edges early, leaving the middle pale long after the lower thickets have gone dark.

Cold remains even when the sky clears. It comes through the ground and off the water, settling against ankles and wrists. In the morning, thin ice forms in unused pools. It whitens the rims of flooded leaves and joins the stems of grass with delicate bridges. By afternoon most of it has vanished, except beneath the northern banks, where fragments turn slowly in seep-fed hollows.

The place is busy without being loud.

A beaver passes with only its nostrils and the upper curve of its head showing. Its wake spreads into the stems and stops. Water voles use the banks above it, slipping beneath roots at the slightest movement. Ducks feed among the submerged grasses. Their bodies tilt forward, then right themselves with a small, wet settling sound. In the muddy shallows, tracks overlap until individual journeys become impossible to follow.

There are insects under bark, larvae in the pools, fish sheltering against drowned root systems. A heron can stand for a long time beside a channel, almost indistinguishable from a dead stem, while small lives continue around its feet. Otters sometimes enter from the outlet, leaving the water briefly restless behind them.

Most sounds are close: a stem bitten through, a foot drawn out of mud, a drop striking a floating leaf. The thick plants absorb them. Even the flow through the dams is subdued, divided among hundreds of small leaks. You hear a fine, irregular pattering only when you stand beside one.

During rain, the whole hollow changes level. The channels fill, the banks darken, and water spreads into grass that was dry the previous day. Fresh mud is pushed into weak places. Branches shift. A pond rises against a barrier, finds a new spillway, and begins cutting through it before the rain has ended.

At the margins, the young ground continues to settle. A little more clay slips from a bank. An exposed root loses its hold. A slab of turf tips into the water and carries a flowering plant out from shore.

By evening, the new edge has already been visited. There are tracks on the mud, a duck resting against the fallen turf, and a pale willow branch drawn across the channel below it.
