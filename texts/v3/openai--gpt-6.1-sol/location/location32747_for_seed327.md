The place is a fold in the mountain, high enough that clouds usually pass beneath it.

It is not quite a valley. There is room for a shallow pool, a strip of green ground, and a person to walk around both without climbing, but only just. One side is enclosed by a curved wall of dark rock. The other opens into air. From that edge, nothing interrupts the view until a distant range, pale and small, rises above the cloud floor. On clear mornings you can look down between the clouds and see lower mountains, with snow on their own summits.

Up here, the fold feels less like land than somewhere the mountain has allowed things to remain.

The rock wall leans slightly outward. Its upper lip is broken into blunt teeth, worn by frost and by the long pressure of an icefield hidden above. Water comes through the wall at many heights: beads forming along a seam, threads trembling over a bulge, a steady clear flow issuing from a hole no wider than a wrist. Some of it is meltwater. Some has traveled so slowly through the mountain that it emerges almost unchanged through every season.

There is no great waterfall within the fold. The water collects quietly, runs among stones, then slips over the outer edge in several narrow falls.

Their sound comes back much larger.

Far below, the streams strike shelves and enter hollows in the cliff. The whole drop works like an instrument too large to see. One fall gives a continuous, low rushing note. Another beats unevenly, with the hard clatter of water hitting loose stone. When the wind turns uphill, these sounds arrive with startling force. You can stand beside a thread of water you could stop with your palm and hear its distant impact shaking the air around your head.

There are moments when speech becomes useless. Then the wind shifts, and you hear a beetle scratching under a leaf.

Most of the fold is empty rock and open space. Life occupies its margins with considerable extravagance.

Along the sheltered foot of the wall, plants grow in a band scarcely wider than a bed. The soil is black, fibrous, full of roots and the broken stems of previous summers. Small ferns overlap so thickly that their fronds look poured into the cracks. There are low shrubs with tough, glossy leaves, cushions of flowering saxifrage, and grasses whose wet blades lie flat until the sun reaches them. A few plants carry leaves much larger than seems sensible at this height. They grow only behind rocks, out of the wind, and their undersides are covered in pale felt.

Nothing stands tall. Even the old shrubs spread horizontally, their woody limbs pressed against the stone. Several have trunks as thick as a forearm and crowns no higher than a knee. They may have been here for centuries. Fallen grit lodges among their branches, moss grows over it, and new roots enter the little islands of soil they have made.

The lushness is close and low. From a few steps away it disappears into a green line beneath the cliff. Kneel down, and it becomes a crowded country.

Flies rise from the wet moss in bursts. Black-and-yellow insects crawl into flowers that remain shut until warmed by direct sun. Tiny snails shelter beneath flakes of rock, their shells almost transparent. In the pool, dark larvae jerk between submerged roots. The water is so clear that they seem to be moving through air until a gust dimples the surface.

The pool occupies a hollow in a slab of stone, perhaps the size of a small room. Its edges are rounded and polished. Sand lies in one corner, sorted into faint curves by the current. Near the outlet, a pale pebble rocks in place all day, tapping against the bottom. It has worn a cup for itself. During dry weather the tapping stops; after rain it starts again.

Birds make the place loud even when the falls are muffled.

They arrive singly out of enormous distances, then gather in a crevice above the pool. Small dark swifts circle the cliff, cutting through the spray to catch insects. Their cries are sharp enough to carry against the water. A larger bird with rust-colored wings lands on the outer stones and breaks snails by striking them against the same battered slab. Shell fragments collect there, among white droppings and splinters of bone.

Most mornings, a pair of mountain goats crosses the upper lip. Their hooves dislodge pebbles that tick down the wall and stop in the vegetation. Sometimes a young one descends to drink, testing each foothold with absurd delicacy. It leaves damp prints across the bare slab, eats three or four flowers, and returns to a route that looks impossible from below.

The animals do not stay in the center. Almost everything flies across it, drinks at it, or briefly settles at its edges. The broad stone between pool and precipice remains bare. There is a great deal of activity here, and very little that obstructs a view.

Someone once built a shelter against the wall.

It consists of a low semicircle of fitted stones, roofed with three long slabs. You must crawl inside. There is space for one person to sit curled up, perhaps two if neither expects to sleep. The floor has been raised with flat stones above the seepage. A smoke stain darkens the back wall, but no hearth remains.

Outside, two holes have been pecked into the rock. A weathered wooden pin still occupies one of them. The other holds rainwater. Near the shelter entrance lies a shallow stone bowl with a chipped rim, too heavy to have been carried here casually. Rain fills it; birds bathe in it. Its hollow is rougher than the pool’s, marked by many small blows.

No path is visible across the fold. Beyond it, however, a few footholds have been cut into a sloping rib of rock. They lead toward the ridge and vanish beneath a crust of old ice. Whoever used them must have known when the ice would soften, where the stone stayed dry, and how long the afternoon weather allowed for a crossing.

Weather arrives quickly. The distant range loses its edges; the light on the pool turns flat. Then cloud rises past the precipice and flows inward, carrying a fine wetness that settles on hair and feathers. For a while the outer edge is gone. The place contracts to the pool, the shelter, and the nearest few feet of green.

Droplets gather on every leaf. The swifts disappear. Insects descend into the plants. Water begins to run across surfaces that looked solid and dry an hour earlier.

When sunlight returns, it enters through the thinning mist in broad, ordinary brightness. The rock steams faintly. Flowers reopen. A bird drops onto the bathing bowl so soon that it seems to have been waiting just out of sight.

By late afternoon, the shadow of the mountain covers everything. The air cools at once. Water continues arriving from inside the wall, but the smallest threads acquire clear rims of ice. The grass stiffens where spray has reached it. Beneath the shrubs, the soil remains unfrozen and alive.

At the open edge, the falls keep pouring into the dark. Their noise rises from below long after the last bird has settled, while above the fold, beyond the teeth of rock, the unseen icefield slowly feeds the streams.
