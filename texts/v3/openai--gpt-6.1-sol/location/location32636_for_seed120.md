Near the summit, in the underside of a stair, there is a pocket too small for a closed fist.

The stair belongs to a mountain made entirely by hand. Its slopes are stacked bowls, fired bricks, woven baskets packed with clay, slabs cut with stone blades and carried upward on backs. Even the material inside the baskets has been kneaded; even the dust has been ground. Far below, the mountain’s builders are still raising the foothills. Here, almost at the top, the air outside is thin enough that a dropped feather pauses before deciding whether to fall.

The pocket is hidden behind a little door of plaited reeds. Push it aside and heat comes out hard against your knuckles, carrying the smell of wet leaves, hot pottery, and something sweet just beginning to spoil.

Inside, there is no room left over.

The floor is a cluster of small terraces, each fashioned from a thumbnail-sized cup of red clay. They fit lip against lip. Their walls are soft and shiny, still bearing the crescents of the tool that shaped them. Between them run gutters made from split stems. Overhead, a net of knotted root-fiber supports little shelves, each loaded with damp green growth. A wooden peg occupies the only place where a finger might rest.

There is water everywhere, though nowhere enough to swim. It beads on the ceiling, runs down braided cords, fills cups, spills, and vanishes beneath crowded roots. One gutter carries a stream no wider than a vein. Another holds a succession of round drops that move uphill, each waiting until the drop ahead has rounded the bend. The water is hot. Where it touches the cooler edge of the reed door, it smokes faintly.

The whole pocket sounds much larger than it is. Water striking a clay cup makes the report of a mallet on a hollow pillar. A leaf rubbing another leaf produces a long, hoarse rasp. Beneath these sounds is an incessant working noise: tapping, scraping, pressing, the soft thump of something being tamped into shape. Sometimes a sharp crack rings out, and a new terrace appears.

It has not broken away from anything. It has just arrived with the sound of breaking.

Everything here is unfinished, but nothing is abandoned. The clay has never had time to weather. No edge is rounded except by a thumb. No channel has been cut by flowing water; the gutters are pinched open immediately ahead of the streams, and the streams follow while the pinch marks are still wet. At the bottom of each terrace, little square tiles push upward through the mud. Their undersides arrive first. Then their sides, then the impression of a maker’s finger, and finally the top surface, already crowded with roots.

The landscape is being made in the small intervals between its movements.

A drop lands, and there is a bowl beneath it.

A root lengthens, and a seam opens to receive it.

A leaf unfolds against the ceiling, and the ceiling gains exactly that much height.

From outside, the pocket remains the same size.

The plants are thick, fleshy, and vividly green in the poor light. Most carry leaves smaller than lentils, overlapping so closely that water cannot fall straight through them. Their stems bend around pegs and pass through the handles of tiny jars. Moss grows in plaited mats, its wet tips lifting and lowering as water passes underneath.

Some of the plants have clearly been assembled. A fern has a spine of twisted thread and leaflets pressed from green paste. A creeping vine is stitched together at every joint. The seams do not prevent either from growing. The fern puts out new leaflets with faint thumbprints on their backs, and the vine extends by drawing a loose length of thread from the wall.

Once a day—or once whenever the ceiling has finished its current row of knots—the vegetation flowers.

The flowers open all at once, crowding the space with blunt yellow mouths. They release a warm, floury pollen that settles on the water and thickens it. For a little while the streams move slowly, like syrup. Then the cups absorb the pollen through their unfired walls. A low hum passes through the terraces, and the plants begin to grow again.

There is almost no light source to account for the green. A little gray daylight enters through the reeds. Otherwise the illumination comes from freshly made surfaces: a new tile gives off a dull red glow for several breaths, and a newly tied knot shines weakly until pulled tight. Beneath the foliage, these brief lights disclose deep spaces—an undercut ledge, a steep shaft lined with miniature rungs, a row of pots hanging over a drop that ought to reach beyond the stair.

Looking down one of those shafts makes your knees loosen. Clouds move far beneath it. They are not the clouds outside the mountain.

There are few animals. One translucent snail lives on the underside of the largest cup. Its shell is a small clay jar, complete with two handles, repaired in several places with dark resin. It eats the soft edges of new construction. Wherever its mouth has passed, the clay firms and becomes waterproof.

There is also an insect you mostly hear. It moves behind the walls with a brittle, busy clicking. Every so often it emerges carrying a splinter longer than its body, braces the splinter against a sagging shelf, and disappears again. Nothing hunts either creature. There is barely space for them to turn around.

The heat does not lessen when the door is opened. Cool air enters and becomes hot before touching a leaf. The tiny room sweats steadily. Its weather is driven by the work: new clay gives off steam, wet cord shrinks, a shelf tilts, water rushes into the newly opened space, and the vegetation bends to follow. There are violent little showers when a ceiling bead grows too heavy. A shower lasts only a second, but every leaf shakes, every cup rings, and the gutters roar.

At such moments the whole mountain can be felt through the stair. A distant weight is set down below. The reed door trembles. One cup tips and is caught by a root that was not there before.

You cannot take much from the pocket. A leaf removed through the doorway becomes a folded scrap of green cloth. Water carried out in a spoon tastes of warm stone, then dries without leaving a stain. A clay fragment survives, but remains soft however long you keep it. If you press it flat, a tiny hollow slowly rises in its center.

Leave the door open too long and the plants begin to make use of you. A tendril feels along a fingernail. Water gathers in the crease of a knuckle. Beneath your hand, something gives three small, deliberate taps, testing where another terrace might fit.

Best to draw back then, and let the reeds fall shut.

Behind them the noise continues: cups filling, shelves lifting, roots pulling stitches tight. On the vast, dry-facing mountain, just below its unfinished summit, that concealed handful of country goes on making enough ground for everything already living there.
