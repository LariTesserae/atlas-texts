The ground gives a little before it holds you.

It is not mud, though water rises around a footprint. Beneath the low, overlapping leaves lies a pale felt of roots, compressed stems, and the softened remains of earlier growth. Farther down, the fibers thicken into cords. Farther still, they become trunks laid lengthwise, dark and waterlogged, bearing the weight of everything above them. There may be stone beneath all this. In a few places, something hard arrests a root and forces it sideways. But nowhere does the old foundation show through.

The country stands high above the lowland weather. Along its southern edge, clouds pass below the turf, obscuring the immense descending walls of vegetation. From within, however, it feels almost level: broad rises, shallow basins, ridges so worn and rounded that their slopes scarcely announce themselves. You can walk toward a hump in the distance for half a day before realizing you have already climbed it.

Once, this was a range of mountains. Their last steepness survives in the buried courses of rivers and in the way certain roots descend, almost vertically, through miles of darkness. The summits have been ground away, dissolved, carried elsewhere. What remains is a high, warm tableland whose valleys have filled with living matter until the distinction between a hollow and a hill is mostly a difference in how deeply your feet sink.

The vegetation is not simply growing on the land. It has taken over the land’s work.

There are plants that hold slopes together and plants that spread beneath them, receiving their weight. There are plants whose hollow stems carry water, plants whose dead leaves form waterproof linings, plants that grow as long tensile cables between heavier masses. A low fern, common enough to go unnoticed, has roots that swell in drought and contract in wet weather; whole districts rise and settle by a few inches with its breathing. The oldest woody growth is scarcely recognizable as wood. It has been buried, rooted through, broken down, and incorporated into new growth so many times that it resembles a dark, pliable mineral.

Only the uppermost skin is green.

Below it are amber tissues, white root hairs, rust-colored membranes, translucent bulbs. A torn bank reveals layers folded over one another like the interior of an organ. Clear water collects in the folds. Sometimes the exposed surfaces close over several days, and sometimes they remain open for years, becoming gardens for small, shade-loving plants that never occur anywhere else.

The warmth comes from underneath as much as from the air. Decomposition proceeds everywhere, slowly and deeply. On still mornings a hand pressed into the ground feels the gentle heat of it. The air smells of wet bark, crushed leaf, and something faintly sweet from the buried layers. There is little rot-smell except where a deep wound has opened. Most of the old material is consumed before it can become foul.

Above, broad crowns meet without quite interlocking. Their branches grow sideways for great distances, supported here and there by descending roots. Between them hang sheets of finer vegetation: trailing fronds, thin vines, veils of tiny leaves. Daylight arrives through several ceilings. It is green near the clearings, brown beneath the oldest crowns, silver where mist catches it.

At midday, pools may reflect a brightness that never reaches the ground around them. You notice the light first in the water, then look upward to find the small, distant opening that made it.

Water is almost always near the surface, but there are few open lakes. Most of it occupies the land rather than lying upon it. It gathers between leaf scales, moves through the packed root felt, stands in cavities inside fallen trunks. A walking stick pushed into firm ground may come out dripping. A ridge may be drier than the basin beside it, yet release a continuous trickle from its flank.

The streams are broad, shallow, and difficult to follow. They pass beneath floating mats, reappear among fingerlike stems, divide into threads too small to see. Their banks travel. Over decades, a watercourse kills the roots along one side and nourishes those along the other, shifting sideways without ever exposing earth.

Rain is soft and prolonged. Much of it begins here. Moisture rises from the vast warm interior, condenses against the cooler crowns, and falls again before leaving the plateau. There are districts where the same water has circulated for centuries. Their plants taste subtly different from those near the edges, and animals that graze there carry pale bands in their shells or coats.

Sometimes droplets climb.

Along certain stems, beads of water advance upward in small, deliberate movements. The stem tightens beneath each bead, lifting it toward a leaf that folds around it. In the dimness this is easy to mistake for an insect. Whole thickets can be feeding in this way while the air around them remains perfectly still.

There are many animals, though the place rarely sounds crowded.

Small grazers move beneath the lowest leaves, parting them with blunt faces and closing them behind their bodies. They have soft feet and damp, close fur. Their paths are visible only as slightly shinier lanes through the ground cover. Snails as wide as a spread hand cling to warm root walls, their shells almost completely overgrown. Pale climbing animals sleep in knots of aerial root, indistinguishable from the knots until one opens an eye.

In the flooded hollows, long-bodied creatures lie with their backs against the undersides of floating plants. They breathe into the dry chambers formed there. When disturbed, they do not splash; they pull themselves farther into the mat, and a patch of vegetation several yards away lifts briefly.

Predators listen through their feet. They wait on taut roots and feel the passages of smaller animals below. Their hunts end in a sudden local shudder, a few leaves drawn downward, then stillness.

Birds are most evident at the margins, where there is room to fly. In the interior, many have become walkers and climbers. They use their wings to steady themselves on yielding surfaces. Their calls are low and brief, absorbed by the leaves. Even the insects tend to click or tap rather than drone.

The quiet is full of near sounds: a shell scraping a stem, water squeezing through fibers, the slow release of a leaf folded too tightly. Farther away, a heavy branch settles. The noise passes through the ground as well as the air, so that you may feel it beneath your soles before hearing it.

Scattered through this country are enclosures that seem, at first, accidental.

A ring of upright growth surrounds a dry depression. An arch opens in a living ridge, its underside worn smooth. In one place, seven chambers lie beneath a single roof of woven roots, each chamber large enough for a person to stand in. They have thresholds. Their floors drain. Small recesses occur at a height convenient for hands.

Nothing has been cut.

The enclosing stems curve into place without scars, and the roofs remain alive. Perhaps some vanished inhabitants trained them. Perhaps they selected natural hollows and occupied them until the plants grew around their habits. The distinction is hard to recover. A doorway may have begun as the resting place of a large animal, then been kept open for generations simply because bodies continued to pass through it.

Some chambers still receive visitors. Animals shelter in them during the long rains. Seeds accumulate in the dry recesses. A few retain a central patch of bare, resilient fiber where nothing takes root. Sit there, and warmth rises through your clothing. After a while, the surrounding leaves turn slightly inward.

Across the plateau, change is slow enough to be mistaken for permanence. A drowned district may take two hundred years to regain a walkable surface. A great crown can lower itself gradually onto the crowns beneath it, remaining alive while its own roots become supporting pillars. When it finally dies, there is no single fall. Its weight is accepted a little at a time.

Yet the place has weather on a scale larger than its visible sky. Far inland, enormous areas flower in sequence. The flowering front moves for years through the dim forests, releasing warm, fragrant air that travels above the canopy. Elsewhere, a season of root growth draws water out of the surface and leaves the pools empty. Animals follow these changes without haste. Routes open; other routes become impassable. A clearing remembered by one generation may be a shallow roofed tunnel for the next.

Near the edge, you can find a place where the growth thins enough to look out.

The lowlands are hidden beneath white cloud. Dark green shelves descend into it, each carrying its own pools and groves. Water threads from their undersides, vanishing before it reaches anything you can see. The distant rim continues beyond sight, gently curved, without a peak or sharp outcrop to measure it by.

Behind you, a path is slowly recovering from your passage. Leaves lift out of the impressions of your feet. Water clears in the deeper hollows. Somewhere below the surface, an animal resumes moving through the warm, crowded dark.

If you stay until evening, very little appears to happen. The green light fades. A few flowers open close to the ground. The roots become easier to hear.
