The climb ends without a summit. For the last hour the ground has been rising so gently that you notice the altitude mostly in your breathing, and then the country opens: a warm, worn tableland extending to a pale rim of sky. There are no young peaks here. Even the mountains have been reduced to their foundations, broad swells of stone with shallow valleys between them, their outlines softened until distance is difficult to judge. A ridge that seems a morning’s walk away may take several days to reach.

The rock is gray when dry and the color of old honey where moisture touches it. Underfoot it has a close, almost fleshlike grain. Windblown grit collects in depressions no deeper than a palm, but there is very little loose rubble. Whatever could break away has already broken away. Whatever could be carried off has gone. The remaining stone lies in rounded plates, each fitted against the next, with seams rubbed smooth by ages of water that now arrives only occasionally.

At midday the light is ordinary and generous. Shadows gather beneath overhangs; small bright surfaces flash when you turn your head. The warmth comes upward as well as down, stored in the rock through the short nights. Despite the height, there is no sharp alpine cold. The air smells faintly of dust, warm shell, and the mineral sweetness of a nearly dry watercourse.

From a distance, the plateau looks bare.

It takes time to see how crowded it is.

The first apparent pebbles have feet tucked under them. Along a shallow crease in the ground, hundreds of thumb-sized animals rest shoulder to shoulder, their backs matching the stone so precisely that only the occasional opening of a dark eye separates one body from another. Farther out, the low ridges are lined with larger creatures, flat-bodied and heavily folded, their overlapping hides resembling layers of eroded shale. They lie with their heads pointed downslope. Some have another animal sleeping on them, and that animal has smaller passengers lodged in the grooves of its back.

The space between these resting bodies is occupied too. Pale segmented things thread slowly through the grit. Long-legged grazers stand among them, placing their feet with such care that a single step takes several breaths. Thin, almost transparent wings cling to the undersides of rock lips. In the sheltered cracks there are soft bodies pressed into every available recess, opening and closing against the air.

Nothing swarms. Nothing hurries. The abundance has the stillness of a crowd waiting through a ceremony whose beginning no one remembers.

Vegetation is sparse: a few wiry tufts in the deeper soil pockets, dark mats beneath damp ledges, small trees set far apart across the shallow valleys. The trees rarely reach above a person’s head. Their trunks grow sideways before turning upward, and their leaves are thick, dull, and widely spaced. A single tree may carry so many motionless animals that its branches seem to have knuckles, pendants, swollen joints. Only after watching do you distinguish the leaves from the little leaf-shaped bodies holding to them.

Much of the feeding happens on surfaces that appear empty. A faint skin of growth occupies the stone, too thin to show except where a grazer has drawn a clean arc through it. Other creatures spend whole afternoons rasping minerals from a seam, gathering the invisible film that follows condensation, or sifting the dust caught beneath a sleeping neighbor. Their mouths are small. Their needs seem smaller still.

Water follows a geography of inches.

There are no great rivers crossing the plateau, but every valley has a lowest line, and along those lines moisture persists. At dawn the stone sweats. Beads gather beneath overhangs and along the shaded sides of bodies; they enlarge, touch, and descend. A drop may travel several feet along a shallow groove before falling into a basin polished by generations of such arrivals.

These basins are crowded with life. Some hold only a dark meniscus beneath a roof of interlocked shells. Others are broad enough to reflect the sky, though the reflection is broken by the backs of animals resting just below the surface. Small mouths appear at the edges. A folded tube extends from one body, takes a measure of water, and withdraws. No one jostles. Even thirst proceeds slowly here.

Rain comes in narrow, wandering curtains. You can watch a shower pass over a distant part of the plateau while the stone around you remains warm and dry. When rain does reach you, it smells intensely of the ground. The animals lift their heads, or loosen one fold of skin, or turn a pale inner surface upward. The whole valley changes texture without becoming much louder.

The scattered structures are easiest to find after rain.

On dry days they look like worn knolls or isolated outcrops. Wet, their seams show: low chambers built from saliva-bound dust, hollow towers of shell, broad roofed mounds with openings along the base. Some stand alone. Others have grown together into small compounds around a damp hollow, their walls rounded and repaired so often that no first shape remains.

There are no tools inside, no hearths, no cut stone. The makers carry material in their mouths and press it into place with their bodies. Several kinds of animal contribute to the same structure. One supplies a hard outer crust; another maintains the moist chambers beneath it; a third keeps the narrow entrances clear. Their movements are too slow to look like work until you return after several days and find a doorway extended by a finger’s breadth.

Older buildings have settled almost level with the ground. Their chambers become cool pockets in the plateau, places where moisture lingers and eggs survive the hottest weeks. New walls sometimes rise on those buried foundations. A roof can outlast the species that made it, then become shelter for animals whose ancestors once fed on its occupants.

The quiet is deep, though never complete. Close to the ground you hear a dry brushing as a body shifts. There is the faint click of a shell opening, a drop landing in an unseen cavity, the prolonged scrape of a grazing mouth. Far away, one of the large animals exhales through several narrow vents, producing a low note that carries along the stone. Minutes later another answers from beyond the next swell.

At sunset, the plateau becomes easier to read. The slanting light catches all the edges that noon concealed: thousands of eyelids, folded limbs, translucent fins, the pale undersides of bodies lifting slightly away from the cooling rock. Long lines of grazers begin moving toward the damp valley bottoms. They advance so gradually that their shadows seem to travel first.

The trees stand dark and separate above them. Beyond, shallow basins repeat into the distance, each with its own crowded margins, its small reservoirs, its weathered shelters. There is no empty valley waiting farther on. The abundance continues past the limits of sight.

During the night, moisture forms again. It gathers on shell and leaf and stone without favor, beads along the rims of the old roofs, and enters cracks polished smooth before the present animals existed. A sleeper uncurls just enough to expose the hollow beneath its breast. Others draw close to the warmth it leaves behind.

By morning, a few feet of ground have changed occupants. One chamber has deepened slightly. A worn patch of mineral film has begun to return.

The light rises over a country already awake, though almost nothing in it appears to have moved.
