The place is hidden inside the crown of a transmission tower, several kilometers above the plain. It occupies a recess behind a maintenance plate: a shallow chamber scarcely wider than a bed, with a low ceiling and a floor that drops away in three abrupt terraces. From outside, there is nothing to distinguish it from the thousands of other compartments in the tower. Even the plate has no handle. Its seams are flush, its fasteners painted over.

Inside, there is a landscape.

The highest terrace is white ceramic, scored into ridges as narrow and sharp as the edges of broken crockery. Below it lies a basin of gray metal granules, each granule welded to its neighbors at the points where they touch. The lowest terrace is black, a dense carbon surface pierced by square holes. All three levels were manufactured separately and fitted together. Where they meet, a pale line of sealant traces the contours with extraordinary care.

Nothing here has an underside of earth. Beneath every apparent outcrop is a bracket; beneath every hollow, a stiffening rib. Even the empty spaces were specified.

The cold is sufficient to make most materials seem temporarily borrowed from some harder substance. The ceramic gives off no powder when touched. The sealant will not yield to a fingernail. A glove set down on the upper terrace stiffens almost at once, retaining the small collapse of its fingers. There is no frost. The chamber contains too little moisture for frost, and the air that enters through its filters has been dried until it carries scarcely anything that could condense.

Light comes from two recessed panels in the ceiling. It is bright, ordinary light, the sort used over a workbench. It shows every groove and screw head without making anything look theatrical. The little ridges cast clean shadows. The black terrace has a faint oily sheen, although no oil is present.

Near the rear wall stand seven plants.

They are widely spaced, each rooted in its own circular socket. The tallest is no higher than a hand. Their stems are ivory, their leaves thin gray blades arranged in opposing pairs. At first they look like measuring instruments, or like the dried remains of something that once grew elsewhere. But there are small differences among them: one blade folded before it fully opened, a thickened joint, a stem that leans slightly away from its lamp.

They were bred for an industrial purpose no longer evident from their shape. Their tissues use a solvent that remains liquid in this cold; their roots enter sealed reservoirs beneath the floor. Nothing spills, evaporates, or darkens the ceramic around them. They take carbon from the filtered air and trace metals from their sockets. They grew very slowly until the chamber reached its operating temperature. Now they persist at that size.

There are no insects among them. Nothing visits the leaves. A narrow space between two stems remains empty with the completeness of a space inside a machine.

The sound is enormous.

Opening the plate exposes the body to a continuous roar that seems impossible to fit inside such a small recess. It comes partly through the walls, partly through the floor, partly from narrow slots along the ceiling. Deep in the tower, compressors hold a constant load. Somewhere above them, toothed couplings engage at a fixed rate. Their combined noise has been led into this chamber through metal ducts and solid conductors.

Ear protection reduces little of it. The lowest frequencies arrive through the knees and teeth. The higher ones press painfully against the ears. A person trying to speak here can feel the effort in their throat but cannot hear the result.

Yet nothing rattles.

The granules in the basin are fixed. The plants have stiff leaves. Every panel is damped except where it is meant to resonate. The chamber was tuned with the precision of an instrument, though no one would call what it produces music. Its roar contains a broad, steady pressure and several narrower tones so closely held that a small movement of the head changes their apparent location. Lean over the gray basin and one tone disappears. Move a few centimeters toward the wall and it returns inside the jaw.

The terraces were shaped to distribute this pressure. Their ridges, holes, and sealed hollows are acoustic components. Looked at closely, the chamber is a test fixture. Seen from the height of its lowest ledge, it becomes a severe country of escarpments and dry basins, with pale growth scattered along a distant shelf.

Its surfaces still have the freshness of their manufacture. The ceramic crests have never rounded. No loose fragment lies beneath them. Tool marks remain on the carbon, regular enough to follow with a fingertip. The landscape seems newly exposed, but there has been no exposure, and there is almost nothing here capable of wearing it down.

The chamber stays at one temperature. The lamps remain at one brightness. The compressors do not alternate between work and rest. Their load is the tower’s continuous function, and this small interior receives the same fraction of it at every moment.

Even dust is excluded. The last particle left by assembly was removed before the plate was fitted.

At the front edge of the lowest terrace is a narrow inspection window. Beyond it, a structural channel runs through the tower. The channel is lit by service lamps descending in a straight line until distance gathers them into a single pale thread. Far below that thread is the plain, though there is no angle from which it can be seen. The window admits only a sense of height: the great length of the manufactured shaft, the remoteness of its bottom, the knowledge that this little floor is suspended near the top.

You can stand there with one hand against the wall and look back across the entire place. Seven plants. Three terraces. Two lamps. A basin with nothing loose in it.

The roar makes that short distance feel vast. There is no audible scale by which to measure it, no small sound nearby against which the machinery can be judged. A footstep vanishes. Breath vanishes. The chamber fills perception so completely that the person inside it becomes the only uncertain feature.

Behind the maintenance plate, the ridges keep their edges. The gray leaves hold their slight imperfections. The light falls on the same surfaces, and the sound continues at a force that elsewhere would announce disaster.

Here it is simply the condition in which everything was made to remain.
