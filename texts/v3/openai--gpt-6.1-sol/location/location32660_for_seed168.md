The plain stands high enough that the horizon falls away rather than rises. From its outer edges, lower country is visible through a pale depth of air: another shelf, then another, each broader and less distinct, until the last is only a warm discoloration beneath the sky. There are no peaks. The elevation belongs to the whole place, a lifted expanse with room for a small mountain range but almost nothing upon it.

Underfoot, the ground is a cream-colored mineral, faintly translucent at its thinnest edges. It has the texture of unglazed porcelain. Some stretches are smooth enough to reflect a dull patch of sky; others are covered in blunt, widely spaced facets. None has been rounded by weather. A ridge can retain an edge as fine as a broken plate for kilometers.

The land looks newly formed, although nothing here is noticeably forming.

Broad swells rise out of the plain and stop before becoming hills. There are shallow depressions with no channels leading into them, terraces without broken rock beneath their faces, fissures so clean that they might have opened this morning. Yet the same small shadow lies inside each fissure all day. Loose grains do not accumulate. Slopes do not creep. The landscape seems to have reached the first instant of its existence and found that instant sufficient.

It is warm. Not fiercely hot, not furnace-warm: the comfortable, almost bodily warmth of a stone that has held sunlight for an hour. The air is dry enough that distance remains clear. There is no haze rising from the ground and no darker line anywhere to suggest a river. The hollows are as dry as the heights.

Across this emptiness, vegetation grows in isolated, luxuriant stands.

One might walk half a day toward a dark green mark and discover, on arrival, that it is a grove large enough to get lost in. Its outer leaves reach nearly to the ground. Farther inside, thick stalks branch into overlapping canopies, and beneath them a lower growth fills the space with folded blades, broad fans, and soft-looking tassels. There are many greens: the deep color of fir needles, a dusty olive, the yellow-green of a leaf held against light. Some surfaces are almost black.

The abundance is startling after the walk. Beyond the last leaf, the bare plain resumes immediately. There is no scattering of smaller plants, no grass thinning toward the distance. A grove has an edge as definite as its shadow.

These plants contain no sap.

A split stem reveals a pale, dry mesh with a metallic sheen. Its channels carry charge rather than liquid. The leaves are mineral laminations grown thin enough to flex: layers of dark conductive tissue, translucent film, and green surfaces that collect light. Their roots enter the ground through holes scarcely wider than themselves and descend toward seams of carbon-rich stone. Nothing needs dissolving. At the points of contact, the roots separate what they need from the lattice and draw it upward, atom by atom.

There is no scent of damp earth in a grove. The air smells faintly of warm graphite and, near certain stems, the sharp clean odor that follows an electrical discharge.

Seen close up, the vegetation has the intricacy of heavy machinery. A trunk is ribbed with parallel conduits. At branching points, collars of dense tissue sit around the stems like bearings. Some leaves overlap in toothed arrangements; others hang in long pleated banks, exposing an immense working surface within a small volume. Beneath their green coating are materials as dark and hard as iron.

Nothing was manufactured. No one has assembled these parts. They grow, or once grew, into shapes that perform their work with industrial precision.

The work is mostly inaudible. Rest a hand against a large stalk and a fine vibration passes into the palm. Lean close to one of the low fans and there may be a subdued hum, too quiet to distinguish from the pressure inside the ear. The groves collect sunlight, conduct it downward, and sustain deep reactions within the plateau. There are no chimneys or exhaust plumes. The waste heat spreads through the stone so evenly that the mineral in shadow is scarcely cooler than the mineral in sun.

Below the surface lies something vast and cellular: cavities bounded by thin mineral walls, seams carrying electrical current, dense nodes where matter is sorted and held. The visible plateau may be only its upper skin. Here and there, at the bottom of a fissure, a green-black surface gleams far below. It is impossible to tell whether this is buried vegetation or a layer of the ground that has acquired the same properties.

The plain is hollow enough to feel light beneath its own size.

A footstep occasionally produces a muted resonance, broad and deep, which ends almost at once. There is no echo from the horizon. Even within the groves, where leaves nearly touch on every side, sound finds little to continue through. No animal has ever moved between these stems. There are no nests, burrows, tracks, chewed margins, or husks left by feeding. Nothing watches from the shade.

The leaves are immaculate without looking young. Their oldest surfaces are thick and matte, their edges darkened, but none has fallen. At the center of some crowns, paler folds remain enclosed around shapes that could be buds. They do not open.

The whole ecology operates at an exquisite balance. Each stand receives enough light to maintain itself. The roots take only what the deeper seams replace. Heat passes outward at the rate it is made. Growth has become maintenance, and maintenance leaves almost no visible trace.

This steadiness makes the distances difficult to judge. A small green crown on the horizon might be a single plant or the canopy of an immense hollow. There is no moving cloud-shadow to measure it against, no flock crossing the intervening air. The sky is pale blue, the light clear and ordinary. The strangeness is all below it.

From one low rise, three groves are visible. One stands close enough to show the bronze undersides of its leaves. Another occupies the lip of a distant terrace. The third is a soft green band on a shelf across an immense gap, apparently suspended until the eye locates the pale ground beneath it.

Between them are kilometers of clean, unfinished stone.

At the edge of the nearest grove, a broad leaf extends over a shallow depression. Its tip hangs a finger’s width above the mineral. Underneath is a narrow shadow, sharply defined. Nothing has gathered there: no dust, no seed, no moisture.

Deeper in the grove, the dark stems continue their nearly silent work. Out on the plain, the light rests on every new edge.
