The first impression is of a high, empty country: long gray slopes, a low horizon, a few dark towers standing far apart. Then the nearest slope contracts, almost imperceptibly, and the loose-looking gravel draws itself into rows.

There is no stone underfoot.

The whole plateau is vegetable matter, from the brittle surface scales to the deep structural roots that hold it above the clouds. What looks like gravel is a field of closed seed cases. What looks like an exposed ridge is the back of a single, immensely old stem. The gray comes from dead outer tissue, polished by centuries of abrasive wind; break a flake and its underside is pale violet, lined with fine conducting threads.

Below that is living wood. Below the wood, more living wood.

Nothing here is green. The plants use the dim light that reaches this altitude, but most of their energy comes from electrical differences between the upper atmosphere and the buried root layers. Their leaves are narrow black vanes, usually folded flush against the ground. Every few minutes a patch of them rises together. A whole hillside briefly acquires a nap, like brushed velvet, and then lies smooth again.

The air is cold enough to sting the teeth. There is no frost. Frost would require something this place has almost entirely lost.

Water exists only as a tightly rationed component inside sealed cells. Nothing drips, pools, steams, or softens in rain. The organisms circulate a clear, heavy fluid through capillaries too small to see, a solvent that stays liquid in the cold and turns cloudy when exposed to air. A cut in a living surface seals almost at once with a white crust. The smell is bitter resin, dry pepper, and the metallic odor of something electrically overworked.

You could walk all day without finding a wet place.

You could walk all day without stepping off a plant.

***

The plateau was once mountainous. That history is still legible in the broad saddles and the direction of the grain, but almost none of the sharpness remains. Wind has planed down the old growth. Successive generations have grown into its hollows, filling rather than raising them, until the country has reached a kind of agreement with its own weather.

The surviving hills are low and very wide. Their crowns are bare of upright growth, though the crowns themselves are alive. Between them lie shallow basins filled with pale, jointed matting. These are not meadows. They resemble floors after the furniture has been removed: great uninterrupted expanses with a few isolated fixtures and a great deal of room around each one.

Here and there a solitary plant rises above the substrate. A black fan on a thick stalk. A ring of stiff white spines enclosing empty air. A column whose branches all point downward.

Each has a clear space around it. Younger growth approaching that boundary folds back into the ground. From above, the plateau must look sparsely inhabited, almost abandoned, although there is no unoccupied surface anywhere.

Beyond the southern rim, the sky falls away. Clouds move far below, their tops dull and blue beneath a thin band of distant light. The plateau’s underside disappears into them: hanging root buttresses, slack loops of conducting fiber, old growth shaped like the ribs of an overturned hull. Sometimes a discharge lights those roots from within. For a second the hidden foundations show through the cloud.

The sound arrives long afterward.

***

The town occupies three shallow depressions and the low ridges between them. From a distance it could be mistaken for a loose stand of burned trees.

Its buildings are grown from the same substrate as the terrain, but here the grain has been persuaded into corners, stairways, vaulted rooms. Outer walls are thick, matte, and almost featureless. Doorways form only when someone approaches; the wall loosens along a seam, folds inward, and closes behind them. Window membranes admit the gray sky while keeping the dry wind out.

There are perhaps a hundred buildings, widely spaced. No district is crowded. A tower may have an entire square to itself, with only a single bench rooted near its entrance. Long avenues cross apparently vacant ground. Their surfaces are smoother than the surrounding tissue and slightly warm.

The roads do not merely carry traffic. They carry instructions.

At certain junctions, pale lines move beneath the surface, branch, pause, and recombine. A person standing there can ask for a destination by pressing a palm to the ground. For a while nothing happens. Then the avenue ahead subtly changes its curvature. Distant buildings shift in relation to one another.

The town can shorten a journey, though it rarely does so for anyone in a hurry. It waits until the traveler’s breathing slows. Nobody runs on these streets.

Freight travels through thick horizontal stems that cross the open squares a little above head height. Cargo enters one end enclosed in a dry husk and arrives elsewhere without passing through the length between. Each transfer causes a brief ache in the jaw and makes the local seed cases click shut. The oldest stems are banded with repairs, some precise and nearly invisible, some broad and lumpy. Several have been out of service for generations. Small animals nest in their intake mouths.

A few towers rise well above the roofs. Their upper chambers contain instruments that reach into neighboring versions of the weather. At dusk, one may bring a ribbon of stronger wind into a street, or exclude a dangerous charge building over the plateau. The towers sway while working. Deep inside them, something knocks with the regularity of a very slow engine.

The town looks weathered because it is weathered. Its walls shed scales. Stair treads wear hollow. Roots lift old thresholds, and maintenance crews come to coax them level again.

***

The animals are easy to overlook until you stop walking.

Then the ground around your feet becomes busy.

Six-legged grazers, the color of ash, move between the closed seed cases. They rasp off the conductive dust shed by the leaf vanes, occasionally stretching upright to expose their bellies to the charged air. Their claws make a sound like fingernails dragged over a comb.

Small jointed creatures live beneath the surface plates. You hear them traveling in groups through the shallow channels: a quick, hard pattering that follows you for a few steps before veering away. Occasionally one lifts a scale and peers out with a cluster of glossy eyes. It closes the opening carefully behind itself.

The larger animals keep more distance. Pale, long-backed browsers wander along the basin margins, feeding where the matting has swollen. They use hooked forelimbs to open a seam, then insert a narrow mouth into the living tissue. The plant tightens around the wound but tolerates them for several minutes. When its surface begins to hum, they move on.

Predators wait in the hollows behind standing fans. Their bodies are flattened and heavily furred, their feet broad enough to distribute pressure. One can cross a whole basin without waking the alarm structures below. Its prey gives itself away by chewing, by an incautious claw, by the faint static crackle of fur against a doorway.

Above the town, winged animals ride the electrical gradients. Their wings are translucent sheets crossed by black veins, hardly moving. From below they resemble scraps caught against the sky. When a tower changes the weather, dozens turn at once and stream toward the new current.

***

The place is loud.

Not continuously loud in the same way, but there is almost never silence. The wind blows across fields of hollow stems buried just beneath the surface, making a low, enormous chord. Different slopes contribute different notes. As the air shifts, the chord changes slowly, with intervals that seem to press against the chest.

Seed cases snap. Animals rasp and chatter. The freight stems give their abrupt, hollow report. Somewhere under the town, a root the thickness of a street adjusts its load, and the sound is like a heavy door being dragged across a room beneath your room.

The inhabitants leave broad gaps between buildings partly because of this. A house crowded against another would hear both through its roots. In the open squares, the sounds disperse enough for conversation.

Toward evening, the horizon loses its faint silver edge. The black vanes rise in larger patches. One slope after another darkens as it exposes its leaves to the final usable light, and the plateau begins its loudest work.

Charges pass through the folded country. White pinpricks appear along the avenues, spaced just far enough apart to guide a walker. The standing fans tremble. Inside the walls, fine fibers pull rooms into their nighttime shapes, reducing unused spaces, thickening the sheltered side.

For a few minutes the town smells sharply of fresh resin.

Then the upper air discharges. A broad flash shows the basin, the towers, the pale backs of grazing animals, every scale on the ancient slopes. The ground holds that light for a moment in violet veins.

The thunder comes up through your feet before it reaches your ears.
