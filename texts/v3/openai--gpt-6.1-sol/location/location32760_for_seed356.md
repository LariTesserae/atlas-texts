There is no dry ground, but there is almost nowhere you could call open water.

The surface is a dark, continuous growth, low enough that its highest ridges scarcely interrupt the horizon. Broad leaves lie edge to edge, overlapping like the scales of something that is not an animal and has never moved. Between them, water stands in narrow seams. It has the color of weak tea where a leaf lets it show, and no visible color at all beneath the thicker growth. A little pressure on the surface would bring it up: first a sheen, then beads, then a shallow pool slowly filling the depression.

Below this first layer are others. Tough, flat stems cross beneath the leaves. Below those, pale roots are packed so closely that they have become a fibrous ground. Older roots do not decay into soil. They soften, join, and lose their individual outlines, becoming a dense living material through which newer roots pass. Farther down, where no light has ever reached, this material is almost white. It is wet, warm, and firm enough to carry the weight of everything above it.

Nothing rests on the landscape. Everything is part of it.

The ridges are growths of this same substance, lifted a few feet above the plain. Their sides have the clean creases of newly opened shoots. Some end in sharp fins; others divide into three or four thin walls around a pocket of water. There are no rounded stones, no worn channels, no banks broken by a previous flood. Every exposed edge looks recent. Yet the ridges have held these forms for longer than any season could account for. Their surfaces renew from within, replacing each softened strand along the precise line where it stood.

The land does not mature. It remains freshly formed.

Darkness occupies nearly all of it. Overhead, the atmosphere is low and opaque, pressed close to the leaves. Sometimes a faint dilution of black makes the nearest ridge distinguishable from the sky, but there is no source to look toward. Water gathered along a leaf edge can briefly hold this faintness. A hundred such edges become visible at once, outlining a patch of ground, and then the eye loses them again.

The plants have no need to reach upward for light. They spread, connect, and deepen. Their leaves are thick, almost fleshy, with dull upper surfaces and glossy undersides. Smaller growth fills the seams: translucent blades, folded membranes, fine branching filaments stretched across wet gaps. Even where the ground appears exposed, a close look would find a skin of minute, tightly fitted cells. There is no bare material.

Across enormous distances, the growth changes its organization. A region of broad leaves gives way to a region of low columns, each column hollow, each joined to the next by roots as wide as riverbeds. Farther on are plains of corrugated tissue, their folds running parallel for hundreds of miles. Elsewhere, circular depressions repeat to the horizon. Their centers contain motionless black water; their rims are thickened with pale, waxy growth. Seen from high above, these regions would resemble the working surfaces of a machine too large to have an exterior.

There are no factories here. There are no ruins or buried foundations. But the place carries out processes on an industrial scale.

Within the low columns, water passes through layers of living membrane. Salts separate. Dissolved substances gather in specialized chambers. Starches become oils; oils pass through bundled conduits into distant tissues. Wide sheets of cells maintain electrical differences across their surfaces, and buried cords conduct the resulting current through the continent. In the deepest growth, pressure is held with extraordinary precision. Fluid enters a chamber, is compressed by its living walls, and leaves through a passage narrower than a hair.

There is no exhaust cloud, no hammering, no vibration felt at the surface. The immense throughput is divided among so many small passages that each carries almost nothing. Their combined work supplies the entire land.

Occasionally, near an open seam, there is a sound like a wet fingertip lifting from glass. It comes from a membrane releasing pressure. The sound does not repeat soon enough to become a rhythm. Elsewhere a bead of water slips from one leaf to another, falling less than an inch. These are the loudest local events.

No wings disturb the air. No mouths crop the leaves. Nothing swims in the pools, and no tracks cross the surface. The spaces between stems are occupied by thinner stems. Cavities hold water, vapor, or suspended threads of tissue. What appears to be a path is only a long crease where two fields of growth meet.

The air is warm enough to carry a close, green smell, but cool against the wet surfaces. Moisture settles continuously out of it. On the upper leaves, droplets enlarge until their weight draws them toward a seam. Within the ground, water returns as vapor through countless fine pores. There is no recognizable rainstorm. Condensation is the weather, and the weather belongs to the growth.

Its provinces are vast. One can have a denser ceiling of cloud than another, or a slightly greater depth of standing water, but each keeps its own condition. There are no traveling fronts. No wind arrives carrying news of a different place. Above a broad region of ridges, the air may rise a little, cool, and settle again along its margins, completing a circulation so slow and regular that no leaf trembles.

The whole continent is doing this: lifting water, sorting matter, holding warmth, making and distributing power. Its innumerable operations do not accumulate into change. A root thickens as the root beside it yields exactly as much space. A membrane is replaced without interrupting the flow through it. A leaf opens into the place of a leaf that has already been absorbed from below.

There is growth without advance, work without a finished product.

At the edge of one shallow basin, the surface folds back on itself. The fold exposes a narrow band of pale inner tissue, sharply pleated and beaded with water. Beneath it is a depth of closely packed conduits, disappearing into darkness. They look newly made. Their walls are clean, their junctions exact, their fluids clear.

Beyond the basin, the leaves continue at the same low height until they can no longer be distinguished. Nothing announces the distance. There is only the knowledge, gradually imposed by the uniform air and the unbroken surface, that this is not a marsh surrounding some firmer country. It has no inland to reach.

The darkness covers a living plain large enough to sustain its own weather and its own electrical currents. Under every quiet patch of water, production continues. At the surface, a droplet hangs from the tip of a leaf, held at the size at which it neither falls nor retreats.
