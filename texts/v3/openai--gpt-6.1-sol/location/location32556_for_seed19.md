The path reaches the plateau without ever quite arriving at its edge. For the last few miles, the ground rises by such shallow degrees that you might take it for level country, except that each time you look back there is more of the world beneath you. The lower ranges become pale wrinkles. Beyond them, entire mountain systems lie almost flush with the distance.

Up here, there are no peaks. Whatever summits once crowned this height have worn away. You walk across their roots.

The stone is gray, cream, and a muted red, exposed in broad adjoining plates. Some plates have been polished until they resemble bone handled for generations; others retain the shallow sockets of minerals that disappeared long ago. The seams contain dry powder so fine that individual grains are difficult to see. It does not lift when you step beside it. There is very little wind, and what air there is feels cool enough to make the joints of your fingers ache.

No water has collected in the hollows. No dark stain suggests that it ever will. There are basins wide enough to hold lakes, but their floors are as pale as their rims. Salt lies in some of them, not in dazzling crusts but in low, dull banks, rounded like everything else. Across one basin, an ancient channel branches and branches again. Its smallest tributaries are still visible. They end in the footprints of animals.

The animals are everywhere.

At first you notice the nearest ones: a low-bodied creature with six heavy legs standing beside the path; a flock of something narrow and woolless on a distant shelf; little jointed bodies tucked into the seams of the stone. Then your eye adjusts, and the apparent texture of the plateau separates into presences.

Every ridge has backs along it. Every depression is occupied. Under the ledges, creatures overlap in close ranks, some no bigger than your thumb, others large enough that a ledge is only the shadow beneath their ribs. The open ground extends for miles, but almost none of it is empty.

They do not make a great collective noise. There is no calling, no clatter of hooves, no vibration of wings. One small animal near your boot makes a dry clicking sound. After a long interval, it makes the same sound again. Farther away, something exhales through a corrugated throat, very gently, as if it is trying not to disturb a sleeper.

Many have eyes. Some have too many; some carry a single eye low on the flank, where it can look beneath their own bodies. Their colors belong to the rock: chalk, ash, pale iron, the yellow of an old tooth. Fur, where it exists, is short and close. Horns have blunt ends. Shells fit so neatly against shells that a group of animals may look like a single outcrop until one of them regards you.

None appears hungry. None appears satisfied, either. You see mouths touching stems, claws resting beside burrows, heads bent toward eggs. These acts are not interrupted by your approach, but neither do they progress. A mouth remains just open around a leaf. An egg has a crack in it, and through the crack a small hooked beak is visible. It has been visible for as long as anyone has known the place.

There are plants, though it takes care to find them.

They grow low, usually where several kinds of stone meet. Their leaves are thick, gray-green, and divided into hard scales. A few have woody trunks no taller than a finger, with elaborate miniature crowns. Others spread in flat discs under the bellies of animals. The roots disappear into hairline fractures. No soil surrounds them.

On a broad shelf to the east stands a grove of seven trees. Each tree is about shoulder height, with a twisted trunk and a canopy the width of an outstretched hand. Their branches do not cast seven separate shadows. On the stone beneath them is the shadow of a much larger grove, densely wooded, extending beyond the shelf and down into the empty air.

The light itself is ordinary. That is one of the difficulties of the place.

You can examine a pebble, judge the distance to a wall, distinguish a red thread from a brown one. The sky is a clear, shallow blue. There is a sun, white enough to look away from. Its warmth is slight. Yet the animals cast shadows in several directions, and some cast none at all. A great plated beast standing on the western rim has a shadow that reaches eastward across three valleys. Along that shadow, smaller animals arrange themselves with extraordinary precision, each fitting into a notch or darkened joint.

It is possible to walk across it. Your own shadow joins it for a moment and does not immediately leave.

The valleys are not deep cuts in the plateau. They are immense, gentle furrows, their sides abraded almost to slopes. Their depth becomes apparent only when you see how far below their floors lie. On those floors, more animals stand in bands and clusters, so numerous that they form a soft-looking surface over the mineral ground. Here and there, a white back catches the light. Here and there, horns rise through the mass like dead branches.

The terrain has been worn by weather that no longer occurs. Tall pillars survive in a few places, but their tops are round and their bases are broad; nothing looks on the verge of falling. Even the loose stones have settled into positions that seem less accidental than comfortable.

Scattered among these forms are small buildings.

One is a shelter made from flat slabs leaned against a central post. The post is wood, dark and deeply checked, polished where hands have touched it. Beside the doorway lie a stone hammer, a coil of braided fiber, and a bowl turned upside down. There is no hearth. Inside, the floor has been swept clear of powder, though no broom is present.

Another structure consists of a low wall enclosing three animals. The wall would not keep them in. They could step over it without effort. Two are facing inward and one outward, and their bodies leave just enough room for a person to sit between them.

Higher on a rise, there are little arrangements of stacked stones, each with a strip of hide pinned beneath the topmost rock. The strips hang straight down. Marks have been cut into them with a sharp flake: rows of short strokes, circles, a few careful outlines of feet. The hides show no sign of decay. The cuts show no sign of closing.

The people who made these things are difficult to distinguish from the rest of the landscape.

A figure sits beside one wall with a length of bone across their knees. Another stands among the seven trees. Their clothing is assembled from small pieces of hide, stitched with thick fiber. They wear no metal. At a distance you might mistake them for upright animals, except for the deliberate way their hands are held.

If you pass close to one, their eyes may be on you. You cannot catch the moment when they turn.

There is room to travel. Despite the crowding, a way remains between the bodies, a narrow continuity of exposed stone. It does not always lead where the arrangement ahead suggests. A passage between two shell-backed creatures may open onto a basin you saw several miles away. Going around the other side may bring you back to the same shelter, now approached from behind. The shelter is not duplicated. The overturned bowl bears the same chip.

These discrepancies are small enough at first to blame on your sense of direction.

Eventually you notice that distance here belongs partly to the animals. Where a long-bodied creature lies across a slope, the slope is longer. Where several tiny ones cluster nose to tail, a stretch of country contracts around them. The immense beasts at the rim seem to hold the horizon in place. You can walk toward them for hours without their growing much larger, while the ordinary stones underfoot remain ordinary stones.

Nothing struggles against this arrangement. There are no predators pursuing prey, though you see teeth and talons, and animals shaped perfectly for pursuit. A small white creature rests inside the open jaws of a larger one. Its fur is undamaged. Both are looking toward the same distant shelf.

The longer you remain, the more carefully you hear the few sounds. The faint click. The breath. A minute scraping from beneath a shell. Each has its own place, and ample silence around it. There is no rising chorus, no sudden hush. The little sound you thought had stopped is still there when you return.

Near the outer rim, the rock bears thousands of shallow depressions. They are old sleeping places, fitted to bodies of different sizes. Many are occupied. Others are empty, with the same cool light lying in them.

Beyond that rim is height: so much height that the lower world has lost nearly all its detail. You can make out dry plains, darker uplands, a few remote ridges. Nothing from below reaches you.

Behind you, on the plateau, a tiny animal stands with one foot raised above a grain of salt. Its foot is translucent. Through it you can see the grain, the stone beneath the grain, and a very small part of the far horizon.

There is no reason to expect it to put the foot down.
