The plateau begins above the clouds, but the clouds have learned to climb.

They come up the escarpments in warm, slow surges, following the gullies and the old river cuts. At the rim they spill across the ground rather than rising into the air. Their passage can be felt against the ankles: a wet pressure, a slight cooling, then the touch of droplets large enough to run together. Somewhere below, rain is falling through miles of open space. Up here, it arrives sideways.

There is no light.

The darkness does not thin toward a horizon. There is no pale patch behind the weather, no moon concealed by it. Looking upward and looking into a hollow between roots give the same result. Distance has to be learned through other means: the temperature of air, the delay of an echo, the mineral taste of spray. A nearby tree announces itself by shedding water. A cliff may be discovered first as a change in the sound of your own breathing.

The ground is warm.

Not hot enough to smoke, except in certain fissures, but warm enough that a palm laid against bare stone feels heat coming up to meet it. The plateau is immensely high and immensely broad, a raised continent whose interior has never quite cooled. Water descends into it through cracks, travels for years through buried chambers, and returns under pressure. Every basin has its springs. Every slope has a damp seam. Some of the springs pulse, filling and emptying in a rhythm too slow to notice during a visit, while others knock beneath the rock like machinery on the far side of a wall.

The rock itself has had time to become complicated.

It is not a landscape of sharp peaks. The high ground consists of worn shoulders, immense saddles, long depressions with rounded edges. Towers remain here and there, thick at the base and hollowed underneath, their load-bearing curves polished by moving water. The largest stand so far apart that the space between them contains its own storms. Smaller ridges have softened into banks of clay. There are boulders with deep channels worn through them and boulders that have settled into their own silt, leaving only the crowns exposed.

Nothing looks newly broken, when it can be examined by touch. Fractures have lips. The lips have moss. Even the places where the ground is moving seem to have been moving in this particular way for a very long time.

Vegetation occupies nearly every surface that can hold moisture.

At first it seems to be a forest: trunks close together, branches meeting overhead, wet leaves brushing the face. But the trunks are not all rooted downward. Some grow sideways from the walls of ravines. Some descend from enormous mats spanning the gaps between stone shoulders. Others are cables of braided roots, carrying water from a marsh on one level to a grove two hundred feet below.

Their foliage is broad, heavy, and folded. There is no reason here for a leaf to turn toward the sky. The leaves cup mineral-rich condensation, cultivate films of microbes, and drain into channels in the bark. Beneath them hang soft combs that strain the air. A mature tree drinks from the ground and from the weather simultaneously; around its roots, the mud is threaded with pale feeding tissues that must be identified by their texture, not their color.

Where heat rises closest to the surface, the growth becomes almost impassable. Pillows of moss swell around the vents. Reeds with jointed, fist-thick stems fill the shallow pools. Creepers loop from trunk to trunk, and smaller plants live upon those creepers, holding pockets of soil in which still smaller plants take root. A person moving through such a place has vegetation against both shoulders, above the head, and underfoot. Each layer is inhabited.

The animals are mostly heard.

Small ones work inside the bark with a sound like someone sorting dry nails. Larger ones push through the reeds, pause, and push again. There are colonies that drum against hollow roots, transmitting warnings through the entire grove. Some creatures answer with a scrape; others with three blunt knocks. A wet rustle can spread across acres without anything crossing the ground: thousands of folded leaves closing as the signal reaches them.

Over the marshes, broad-winged animals fly by listening to the air they displace. Their wingbeats are slow and irregular. They pass close enough for the moving air to touch your forehead, then bank away from the sound of your breath. Smaller fliers travel beneath them in dense streams, clicking continuously. Their passage makes a fine crackling noise, like rain striking a stretched hide.

Predators listen, too. For this reason, many of the smaller animals are loud only in company. Alone, they stop scratching, stop calling, and wait.

There are paths through the forest, though few were made by people. Heavy grazing animals keep them open. Their feet press the clay into deep, overlapping cups, and water stands in every print. Along the sides of these routes, bark is rubbed smooth at shoulder height. The animals move in herds whose advance can be heard long before the first body arrives: the sucking release of feet from mud, the knock of horns against stems, a low continuous exchange of throat sounds.

When a herd crosses a stream, the water changes course around it. Sometimes the animals remain there for hours, feeding on growth loosened from the banks. By the time they leave, a channel has been widened or blocked. The next rain begins a different marsh.

The plateau is full of such small revisions.

Yet its great arrangements persist. Water keeps returning to the same broad basins. The forest advances over one bank while drowning along another. Slopes sag by inches. A ridge that has been hollow underneath for centuries lowers itself into the mud with a long, grinding sound, and the surrounding roots tighten to take its weight.

That sound may continue for several days.

There are machines here as well.

You encounter their workings before you encounter a building. Beneath the calls and runoff, beneath the close sounds of feeding animals, there is occasionally a regular concussion. It travels through the soles of the feet. At longer intervals comes the deep thud of a valve closing.

Pipes follow the contours of the ground, usually under vegetation. Their outer casings are rough with mineral deposits. Roots wrap them; small animals nest in the warmth of their joints. Where a pipe crosses a ravine, it does so on a single heavy arch, or hangs from cables anchored deep into either bank. Moisture falls from it in a straight, narrow rain.

The installations are scattered across enormous distances: a pumping house on a stone island; an intake tower standing in a flooded depression; a machine shed built into the sheltered side of a ridge. Between them lie days of marsh and forest. There are no streets, and little cleared land. Maintenance routes are marked by iron studs set into the ground and by handrails where falling would be fatal.

The buildings have thick walls and doors that must be shut with both hands. Inside, turbines run on the pressure of water rising out of the plateau. Some drive pumps; some generate electricity carried away in buried cables. Ventilation fans pull damp air through chambers where it condenses against cold metal. The collected water leaves through separate conduits.

Nothing is illuminated. Instruments click at different rates. Pressure gauges raise pins beneath the fingertips. A failing bearing announces itself in the pitch of its housing, and workers carry listening rods as carefully as tools. Their boots have distinctive heel plates so that each person can be recognized approaching along a metal floor.

Much of the labor is clearing living material from machinery. Roots enter seams that ought to be watertight. Filter-feeding animals establish themselves inside intake grilles. The warmth of a generator can support a dense patch of growth even through a concrete wall.

Older stations have accommodated this. Their foundations pass around major roots. Their drains empty into planted beds. In one pumping house, a trunk thicker than the main discharge pipe occupies the center of the engine room; the floor has been widened around it three times. When the tree contracts during a change in groundwater pressure, the workers hear it creak over the turbines.

Beyond the stations, the sound becomes overwhelming again.

Rain strikes foliage at several heights. Streams fall unseen through shafts in the rock. Herds call across the basins, and their calls return altered by distances too large to judge. Wind enters the hollow stone towers and makes low notes that move from one part of the plateau to another as weather crosses it.

Sometimes all the nearer animals fall silent. Then a sound comes from far below: water moving through a chamber large enough that its surge takes several minutes to pass.

The ground trembles. Pools rise against their banks. In the forest, leaves begin to drain at once, pouring accumulated water down the trunks. Springs that had dwindled to a trickle push strongly again. The turbines accelerate, their governors closing with heavy, repeated blows.

Afterward the animals resume, one colony at a time.

Far inland, beyond the maintained pipes, the plateau continues through warm wetlands and worn ridges. There are rivers broad enough to be mistaken for open country until their current catches the legs. There are forests suspended over flooded hollows, held together by roots so old and interwoven that their individual origins are no longer traceable. There are basins where the rising mist brings the smell of salt, though the sea is far below.

Travel there is slow. You stop often to listen. You test each unfamiliar surface before trusting your weight to it. A warm draft may lead to a sheltered passage, or to a vent with no bottom within reach of sound.

And when you rest, the place keeps moving close around you: water gathering on the leaf above your face, an animal pulling itself through the mud beside your boot, the steady expansion of a root against stone. Far away, a pump starts under load. Its vibration enters the ground, travels through the saturated clay, and arrives where you lie with your hand against the earth.
