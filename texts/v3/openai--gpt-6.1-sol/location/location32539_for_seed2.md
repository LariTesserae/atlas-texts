At first there seems to be no landscape, only cold green water and light.

The light falls in broad, trembling sheets. Clouds passing overhead dim whole districts at once; when the sun returns, brightness travels across the bottom faster than anything swimming there. Twenty metres above, the surface breaks into silver facets. Twenty metres below, a pale floor extends to the limit of visibility. There is nothing between them but water, an occasional strand of weed, and fish so widely scattered that their abundance becomes apparent only with time.

Then the floor resolves into panels.

Each is larger than a town square, cast in a slightly different shade of concrete. Their seams run straight until they disappear into the green distance. At the intersections are recessed steel covers, lifting sockets, and small ceramic markers whose numbers remain legible beneath a thin film of algae. The basin has been flooded for only eleven years. Corners are still corners. The lips of drainage channels have not rounded off. Where a panel stands a few centimetres higher than its neighbour, its edge casts a narrow, exact shadow.

There is no bedrock beneath this sea. There are more panels, waterproof membranes, inspection galleries, structural cells. Even the scattered stones are broken construction aggregate. What looks, from a distance, like a reef is the toothed upper edge of a poured breakwater.

The water itself came through gates.

***

This is one compartment of a made sea that stretches for thousands of kilometres. Its boundaries cannot be seen from within it. The nearest perimeter wall lies several days away by service vessel, and most of that journey would pass over the same open floor, interrupted by pumping works, pressure ridges built into the slab, and channels deep enough to carry the heavier water of winter.

The builders divided it into basins, but the divisions are submerged. A fish can cross a continental boundary by swimming over a concrete sill.

Here the bottom rises almost imperceptibly toward one of those sills. For hours there is no obvious change. Then the current strengthens. Loose fibres slide along the floor. Fish turn their heads into the flow, and the seams begin to collect little drifts of shells. Finally a wall appears out of the haze, only six metres high but extending sideways beyond sight.

Water pours over it continuously.

On the downstream side, the fall becomes a broad underwater tumble. Clear water folds into cloudy water. Bubbles dragged down from the surface turn slowly in the eddies. The wall’s base is freshly scoured, pale enough to look illuminated from within, and the seabed beyond it is combed into long bands of sediment.

This sediment is the first soft ground the place has ever had: powdered concrete, airborne dust, shed scales, fragments of plankton, material carried through the intake screens. It lies a finger deep in sheltered places and nowhere at all in the fast channels. Small worms already work through it. Flatfish settle into patches too thin to conceal them fully, leaving the outlines of their bodies visible against the manufactured floor.

***

The sound is almost continuous.

At a pumping station, it becomes a physical pressure in the jaw and ribs. The station stands on the floor like a windowless factory, its upper decks breaking the surface far overhead. Grilles occupy most of its submerged walls. Behind them, slow impellers draw water into culverts wide enough for a railway train. Their rotation produces a deep, regular beating, overlaid by the harsher noise of water passing through screens.

Elsewhere there is the distant hammering of maintenance, the descending whine of a vessel under power, the scrape of a gate being lifted. These sounds travel far. It is often impossible to tell whether a machine is nearby or working beyond the horizon.

Storms add another layer. Rain strikes the surface in a vast granular roar. Waves slap the exposed service towers and send hollow impacts down their legs. During heavy weather, the whole water column pulses. Sand moves on the floor. Schools break apart, regroup, and shelter behind structures that looked insignificant in calm conditions.

There are quiet places, but they are small: the lee of a buttress, an abandoned valve housing, the narrow interval after one pump stops and before its replacement begins.

In those intervals, the animals become audible. Shrimp crackle inside the grilles. A fish crushes a shell. Loose mussels knock against a metal plate.

***

Life gathers around anything that interrupts the water.

Most of the basin remains open and nearly bare. A traveller can spend a long time looking into an immense, apparently vacant volume before a shoal arrives. The fish come as a change in the texture of the distance, then as separate bodies, then as a glittering wall. Thousands pass in a few minutes. Afterward the water looks empty again.

Their feeding routes follow currents rather than scenery. Plankton collects above warm outfalls, along submerged sills, and where two pumping districts exchange water. Small fish follow it. Larger fish patrol the margins of the schools, making brief, violent entries that leave scales turning in the light.

On the structures, mussels grow in dark ropes. Barnacles roughen bolt heads. Anemones occupy the sheltered mouths of unused pipes. Crabs move between inspection covers, lifting their claws when a shadow passes over them. Seals come down from the surface to hunt around the pump houses, slipping through spaces too narrow for a vessel.

Vegetation has established itself more slowly. There are tufts of green weed at panel joints and a few stands of kelp where someone placed rough anchoring blocks. Their fronds lean almost flat in the current, then rise when the pumps change speed. Beyond those small stands, the floor remains exposed.

The cold helps preserve the impression of newness. Growth is steady but modest. Paint survives. Galvanized rails still shine where fish rub against them. There are places where the maker’s lettering can be read on a pipe while an eel rests beneath it.

***

The basin has seasons, though they arrive partly by schedule.

In winter, colder water is admitted through the northern gates and sinks into the deep transport channels. It moves there beneath a lighter surface layer, carrying oxygen into the long excavated trenches. In spring, rising sunlight reaches the floor for more hours each day. Algae blooms soften the view. Fish spawn around the grilles, and maintenance crews postpone some of the cleaning.

The pumps never quite repeat the same pattern. Demand changes. A gate jams. A storm raises the outer water level. One basin receives too much freshwater, another needs flushing. These adjustments send moving boundaries through the sea: colder tongues, cloudy fronts, narrow bands where the taste and density of the water change. Animals track them. Sediment lifts and settles somewhere new.

At one long seam, a panel has begun to tilt.

The difference is slight, less than the height of a hand, but water has found it. A steady jet issues from beneath the slab, carrying a pale plume into the basin. The jet has hollowed a pocket in the young sediment. Shrimp shelter there; a small fish waits facing the flow. Far below, in an inspection gallery, instruments will have registered the loss of pressure.

Soon a repair vessel will arrive. Divers will set a temporary barrier, inject grout, and bolt a steel strip across the joint. The plume will stop. The shrimp will scatter. Within weeks, the new strip will have its own fringe of growth.

For now, it is the only irregularity visible in an enormous plain. Sunlight moves over it. The pumps beat in the distance. Above the floor, a school of fish turns as one, exposing thousands of bright sides, and passes out into the open water.
