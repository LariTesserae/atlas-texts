The ground is still being laid.

At first it looks like a plain of dark clay, wet enough to hold a dim reflection of the sky. There are shallow pools, low ridges, distances obscured by steam. Then you notice the seams. Every few paces, a straight line runs through the mud. Another crosses it at an angle. The plain consists of slabs pressed by hand into wooden molds, fitted together while soft, and left to harden under the weight of whatever comes next.

Some slabs are the size of a palm. Others extend beyond sight. Both carry fingerprints.

There is no earth beneath them. At the edge of an unfinished pool, where a corner has lifted, you can look down into a warm, lightless interval. Moisture gathers on its upper surfaces and falls upward, striking the underside of the plain. The slabs are damp from both directions.

The heat is steady and bodily. It comes from the ground, the water, the air inside your sleeves. Nothing glows. Nothing needs to burn. Even the thin drizzle that sometimes passes over the plain is hot enough to make you turn your face away.

Overhead hangs a low ceiling made of overlapping sheets of something like beaten lead, though it has the texture of fired clay. Its joints disappear into haze. Here and there, a sheet has been propped up with a forked pole, creating a brighter patch beneath it. The poles are fashioned from bundles of smaller poles, which are themselves bundles of smaller ones. Follow the structure closely enough and you reach no wood, no original material: only lashings around lashings, rods assembled from the idea of rods.

The light enters through imperfect work.

It leaks from thumb holes, badly fitted corners, a hairline fracture extending for hundreds of miles. Under those openings, the pools acquire a pale yellow depth. Elsewhere the water looks black, though it is clear when lifted in a cup.

There are cups. They stand alone on the plain, often far from anything that might have used them. Thick vessels, badly balanced, with pinched lips and handles made for hands of several incompatible shapes. Some are filled with warm water. Some contain a smaller landscape: a wet floor, a low ceiling, a cup standing somewhere in the distance.

You learn not to pour these out.

Much of the terrain is shallow. Long basins slope so gently that their water seems to have accumulated through indecision. Between them lie broad causeways, kiln-colored humps, ranks of square depressions awaiting their final lining. Nothing has been worn down. Edges retain the blunt precision of fresh work. Ridges are raised because someone or something placed material there, not because softer surroundings washed away.

Even the channels have been cut.

They begin as narrow grooves drawn with a pointed tool. Farther on they widen into canals with vertical banks, then open into inland seas bounded by enormous ridges of hand-packed clay. Along the margins, the maker’s repeated gesture remains visible: press, turn, smooth; press, turn, smooth. Across a whole horizon, that same motion has left its marks.

Water moves through these channels in fits. A trickle may travel steadily for an hour, then all its ripples gather into one ridge and slide sideways out of the channel. A pool rises until it stands knee-high above its own banks, held in place by a trembling skin. It stays that way long enough to cast a reflection, then folds inward and is gone. The damp hollow left behind may already contain a newly laid floor.

There is little sound to any of this. A vast basin can empty while you hear only a few soft clicks from the cooling rim.

The louder sounds are small: water dripping into a pot, a cord drawn through a wet hole, the suck of a foot coming loose from clay. Once, very far away, something drops a mallet. The knock reaches you through the ground before it comes through the air.

Tools lie where the work appears to have stopped. Wooden wedges, stone scrapers, cord, shallow baskets, mallets with rounded heads. Their handles are polished by use. Beside one scraper sits a heap of fresh shavings, still curling. The blade is sharp enough to leave a clean line in the wet ground, but its edge is soft. It would need frequent dressing. Whoever made this place has done so with tools that require patience.

There are no furnaces, no towers of machinery, no cables humming behind the walls. At intervals, you find a workbench beneath a small lean-to. A few slabs rest on it under damp cloths. The lean-to shelters an area scarcely larger than a bed, although the next one may stand beyond a water basin wide enough to have its own storms.

Those storms develop close to the surface. Heat draws vapor from the pools until the haze thickens into low, moving banks. It presses against a ridge and curls back on itself. Condensation runs down the banks in hot sheets. Occasionally the vapor darkens enough to obscure your ankles while the air at face height remains clear, so that everything distant appears to stand on a blank white floor.

There is no green anywhere. Wet surfaces darken, salts gather at drying edges, but nothing roots. No insect troubles the pools. No call carries from one shelter to the next.

On some walls, however, fine white marks spread while you watch. They look at first like mineral deposits. Then a branch divides at the exact location of a crack that has not yet formed. By the time the clay splits, the white pattern has outlined every piece. You can scrape it away, but a fresh mark appears beside your hand, describing the path of the scraper.

The place is full of such preparatory growth. Thin films, arrangements of grains, beads of water forming where a surface will later be. They thrive most thickly around unfinished structures. Once a wall is complete, they lose their definition and become ordinary dampness.

There are whole regions where the ground is crowded with these pale drafts. Under them, new terrain rises quickly. A low fold becomes a shelf; the shelf thickens into a platform; the platform receives grooves, banks, pools. You may cross an empty stretch in the morning and find it blocked by a broad embankment on your return. Its crest is soft, its corners carefully rounded. A mallet rests beside it, still warm from the hand that held it.

No worker is ever visible.

Sometimes there are footprints, but they run from completed ground onto the underside of a lifted slab. Sometimes a drying wall bears the impression of a forearm long enough to span a valley. Most often there is simply work in progress, with the impression that you have arrived during a brief absence.

Distances become uncertain near these sites. A shelter on the horizon may be reached in twenty steps. A cup lying close enough to touch may remain beyond reach for hours, while the sunless light changes and hot rain passes over you. The unfinished parts have not yet acquired reliable dimensions. You can see this most clearly in a slab being set: at one moment it is a little square balanced on a wedge; at the next its far edge is lost behind weather.

Walk around it, and your own tracks may have grown broad enough to serve as channels.

The changes come in waves. For a while everything holds still. Then a seam opens, and a whole district begins to move. Causeways turn on hidden joints. Pools separate into smaller pools. A wall rises, carrying its puddles upright. Sheets in the ceiling slide past one another, admitting brief lanes of dull light.

During these periods the air remains almost quiet. You hear drips accelerate. You feel the ground shift beneath your soles. Far across the plain, a ridge lifts and lowers like a cloth being shaken flat.

Afterward, the new arrangement seems settled. Water finds its grooves. A few vessels tip over. The white patterns begin again along fresh edges.

There is a place where the plain ends, though it takes a long time to recognize it. The seams grow farther apart. Tools become more frequent. Pools stand beside stacks of slabs wrapped in wet cloth, and the ceiling is held up by rows of temporary props.

Beyond the last row lies a dark space with no visible floor. It gives off the same humid heat as the finished landscape. The edge is lined with shallow baskets of clay, each kneaded and covered to keep it workable.

From that darkness comes an occasional small sound: a scrape, a pat, the creak of a handle under pressure.

Then a fresh square of ground appears at the edge. Water beads on it. Its surface bears the broad sweep of a palm.

For a little while, you can stand beside it and watch it harden.
