At first the country seems to consist entirely of edges.

Ridges stand behind ridges, each one sharply cut against the next. Pale cliffs break into long staircases of fallen stone. The scree beneath them has not yet acquired the settled look of old slopes: individual fragments still show clean fracture faces, and the tracks across them are narrow, tentative, easily lost. Far below, in clefts too deep for the midday sun, there are seams of snow that have survived several summers.

Then the eye begins to distinguish the walls people have made from the walls the mountain has made.

A straight line continues too far along a contour. A dark opening repeats at regular intervals. What seemed to be a band of broken rock resolves into houses, thousands of them, built from the same gray limestone on which they stand. Above those houses are more houses. Below them, terraces descend toward another inhabited ledge. Between one ridge and the next, roofs fill the sheltered folds.

There is no single city to arrive at. There is a country of adjoining cities, spread across a high plateau so broad that weather can occupy one end of it while the other remains clear.

From the highest paths, whole districts look like rough scales on the flanks of the hills. Settlement thins where the land is exposed, then thickens again behind a ridge, around a spring, beneath a cliff that turns the wind. Towns that were once separate have joined by degrees: a row of storage chambers, a winter enclosure, a shrine at a pass, another cluster of households. Their older names survive. A person can walk all day through inhabited streets and still be told, at an unmarked stair, that this is where one town ends and another begins.

The streets are mostly steps.

They rise between thick walls, pass through low tunnels, emerge onto roofs, and descend into courtyards too small to admit a cart. Some are scarcely wider than a loaded person. Others are broad enough for pack animals to pass, with shallow grooves hammered into the stone for their feet. At difficult corners, the outer wall is polished by generations of baskets and woolen sleeves.

Everything has been made by carrying.

Roof beams came uphill on shoulders or behind patient animals. Stone was levered out of nearby seams, split with wedges, dressed with small iron chisels where iron could be afforded. Many walls are dry-laid, their weight doing the work of mortar. The finest houses have a little lime plaster on their sheltered faces. Most are plain stone, darkened around the doors by hands, smoke, and animal grease.

The construction is dense because warmth is easier to keep between neighbors. Houses share walls. Their lower rooms are partly buried. A family may sleep above its animals, with stored fodder packed against the cold outer wall and grain sealed in clay jars beneath the floor. Windows are small, but not absent. On clear days wooden shutters open and squares of ordinary sunlight reach deep into the rooms.

The light is often beautiful in an unceremonious way. There is no perpetual dusk here, no strange color to the sky. Morning illuminates the east-facing settlements first, finding every chipped sill and strand of wool drying on a parapet. At noon the plateau is plainly visible: white stone, brown earth, black openings, the dull yellow of last year’s grass. Distant buildings look close because the air contains so little haze. Walking to them takes most of a day.

It is cold even in the light.

In sheltered places the sun can warm a seated person’s back while frost remains on the ground beside their boots. In winter, moisture from breath freezes on scarves. Door latches are wrapped in hide so that bare skin will not touch the metal. The cold settles deepest in the storage passages and the shaded lanes, where ice can persist after the roofs have cleared.

Yet the country is not entirely dry.

Water appears in small, particular places. A spring seeps from beneath a bed of darker rock. Meltwater runs for an hour along a gutter and stops when a cloud covers the snowfield above it. In some houses, damp gathers on the cold underside of a roof slab and falls into a stone cup placed there for the purpose. The amount is small, but nobody leaves the cup empty.

The cities have channels instead of rivers. Narrow cuts in the paving lead toward covered cisterns. Roof edges are arranged to shed meltwater into a neighbor’s gutter, then another, until it reaches a communal tank. In the oldest quarters, these routes are older than the streets themselves. A wall may be rebuilt slightly crooked rather than interrupt one.

Water rights are marked on lintels and boundary stones. A household has its hour at the outlet, its share of a thaw, its responsibility for clearing grit from a particular stretch of channel. Children learn these divisions while accompanying adults with jars. Disputes are settled at the cistern steps, where everybody can see how much water remains.

Beyond the inhabited slopes, the plateau opens into large basins of stony ground. Their distances are difficult to judge. A dark patch that seems to be a nearby field may be a herd several miles away. Some basins hold shallow lakes for a few weeks after the thaw; afterward there is only a pale crust, a rim of brittle sedge, and damp earth under the surface. Others contain permanent pools no larger than courtyards, their margins crowded with tracks.

Vegetation keeps low.

There are thorny cushions rooted between stones, gray herbs with small tough leaves, and grasses that grow in bunches rather than covering the ground. On south-facing slopes, people preserve strips of soil behind retaining walls. The fields are modest beside the immense spread of houses: narrow beds of barley, bitter greens, roots, and hardy pulses, manured so heavily that their earth is almost black.

Every usable cutting is gathered. Dry stalks become bedding, fuel, fodder, or packing. A bush growing beyond the city may belong to a household as specifically as a room does. In some districts, sheltered trees are kept within high-walled courts, their branches trained low to avoid the wind. They are small and crooked. Their spring buds draw a crowd of birds.

The animals are much more numerous than the plants first suggest.

There are domestic herds on the open benches: goats, thick-fleeced sheep, and broad-backed pack animals with long hair hanging beneath their bellies. Their movement is slow and economical. At a distance they appear to stand still, but an hour later the whole herd has shifted across the slope.

Wild grazers occupy the inaccessible ridges. They come down at dusk to lick minerals from exposed seams and to feed where snow has pulled away from the grass. Small burrowing animals live beneath the basin floors, using a depth of earth that the surface gives little indication of possessing. Their paths connect feeding patches to holes tucked beneath rocks.

Foxes follow those paths. Larger predators travel the empty ground between settlements, usually seen only through what they leave: a narrow track across old snow, a disturbed flock, bones beneath an overhang.

Within the cities, life occupies every available recess. Small birds nest in the dry joints of walls. Mice work through grain stores. Goats stand on roofs built to bear their weight. Dogs sleep in doorways with their noses tucked under their tails. Pigeons crowd the sheltered faces of towers and flutter down to the threshing terraces when grain is spread.

There are so many animals that one expects more noise.

But the stone absorbs little and carries much, so people have learned not to waste sound. A shouted name can travel across three streets and return confused from a facing wall. Work is done close together, with brief instructions. Market stalls stand in sheltered courts rather than open squares. Their owners sit beside folded cloth, baskets of roots, salt blocks, repaired harness, and bundles of dried herbs, waiting for customers who already know where to find them.

The ordinary sounds are small: a hoof testing a step, a wooden shovel scraping snow, a loom knocking behind a wall. The metalworker’s hammer is distinct enough to tell which courtyard it comes from. Occasionally a bird calls from a roof and another answers several terraces below.

At night, shutters close and the houses become almost featureless. Smoke leaks from squat chimneys, lies briefly along the roofs, then lifts into the cold. Somewhere an animal shifts against a partition. Somewhere water drops into a cistern. People sit near hearths that burn dung, brushwood, and carefully rationed charcoal, mending the things they will use again tomorrow.

The land beneath this patient life is comparatively young.

The high country has risen more recently than its scale seems to imply. Beds of stone stand tilted and exposed. Faults cross the plateau in long abrupt steps, and streams have only begun to cut their way through them. There are broad surfaces that have not yet been divided into valleys, cliffs without deep aprons of soil, and ridges too sharp to carry much more than lichen.

The builders watch these surfaces closely. A new crack in a terrace wall is filled with a small wedge of wood; if the wedge falls, the crack has widened. Channels are inspected after hard frost. Rockfalls are uncommon enough to be remembered individually, but common enough that nobody builds directly beneath a newly exposed face.

Change arrives in increments. A path is moved a little farther from an unstable edge. A stair acquires another step as the lower paving settles. A household adds a room above its courtyard. A spring diminishes, and a district farther along the ridge begins to grow.

Over generations, the settlements move through the landscape without appearing to move at all.

At the outer limits, old houses stand roofless, their beams carried away for use elsewhere. Frost loosens the upper courses. Birds occupy the rooms. Beyond them are recent walls, fitted tightly around a dependable seep, and half-built terraces waiting for the next season’s labor.

From a distant ridge, both look much the same: gray construction on gray rock, crowded into the sheltered places of an enormous cold country. Only the thin smoke, the darkened paths, and the animals moving between the walls show which parts are still inhabited.
