The basin lies just below the surrounding country, deep enough that you cannot see the road until a truck passes along it. Then its roof moves above the rim, briefly visible against the low gray sky, and disappears behind a bank of frozen peat.

From down here, the horizon is mostly vegetation: the blunt ends of roots, turf folded over the edge of an excavation, a few pale grasses standing higher than the snow. The basin extends for several kilometers. It is not a deep pit, but a broad, shallow removal from the land, with enough depth to hide its own distances. Channels and low banks divide it into long compartments. Farther away, these merge into a level, dull-colored expanse.

Almost everything underfoot is made of plants.

There is a living skin of moss, brown-green where exposed, nearly white where frost has settled into it. Beneath that is compressed moss, then older moss, sedge stems, roots, and leaves laid down so long ago that their separate shapes are difficult to recover. The ground is fibrous rather than stony. Where a drainage trench has been freshly cut, its wall shows the material in dark, clean layers. A root hangs out of it with the sharp little bend it had when it grew around something now gone.

The cuts are recent. Their corners have not slumped. Machine tracks remain plainly impressed beside them, every rib of the tires preserved in frozen turf. Even the loose fragments thrown up by the excavator hold their shape: angular pieces of a substance that would soften and spread in warmer weather. Here they lie like broken bread, their torn edges bristling.

The moss does not form a lush, rising forest. It makes a floor. That is what gives the place its strange spaciousness without making it barren. There is vegetation everywhere, but little stands above the ankle. An isolated dwarf birch occupies a whole reach of the eye. A patch of rushes becomes a landmark. Beyond it, another hundred meters of low, close-growing life lies almost perfectly still.

The cold has taken most of the available water out of circulation.

The ditches contain narrow strips of dark ice. In hollows, thin ice rests directly on the moss, with the shapes of the tiny leaves visible through it. There has been little snow, only enough to gather in the wheel ruts and on the sheltered sides of banks. Wind has brushed the open surface clean. The exposed moss feels dry and brittle, although a boot pressed hard into a thawed patch near the machinery comes away wet.

Moisture persists in small, specific places: under a pipe, inside a tuft, along a seam in the peat where groundwater still moves. It beads on metal and freezes there. On the downstream side of an outlet, a little water runs beneath a shell of ice, making a dark passage no wider than a hand.

That small movement is difficult to notice because of the noise.

At the southern end of the basin stands a pumping shed clad in corrugated steel. It is low, rectangular, and stained around its vents. Two large engines run inside. Their exhaust stacks extend above the roof, and their discharge pipes cross the bank on concrete supports before entering a raised drainage channel.

The engines are old enough to be loud and sound enough to continue working.

Their noise fills the basin: a dense, hammering roar with a higher mechanical whine caught inside it. Close to the shed, the sound presses against the body. A loose panel rattles so quickly that its separate impacts become a metallic rasp. Farther away, the engines lose their detail but not their weight. They turn into a continuous roughness spread over the land.

There is nowhere down here that is quiet.

The peat banks soften some frequencies; the frozen channels and metal pipes carry others. In one compartment, the low vibration is strongest. In another, a thin, piercing note seems to come from the empty ground ahead. Moving a few steps changes it. Conversation requires people to stand close and raise their voices. At a distance, a person can be seen calling long before any words become distinguishable.

Yet very little happens quickly.

The pumps lift seepage out of the drained workings. Their impellers turn at speed, but the effect on the landscape is patient: a water level held a few centimeters below the moss, a channel kept open, one margin prevented from flooding. Fuel arrives by the road above. Once a day, perhaps, someone comes down the gravel ramp to inspect the gauges. The shed door opens, showing a brief rectangle of warmer light, then closes.

Elsewhere there are only scattered remnants of use. A rusting cable spool has sunk partway into the turf. Three short posts mark a crossing that is no longer safe. An abandoned conveyor frame reaches over one trench, its belt removed, its rollers capped with frost. Beside it, moss has grown across a timber platform, leaving the bolt heads exposed. The plants advance so closely along the wood grain that the platform is still recognizable from a distance and difficult to distinguish from the ground when standing on it.

No substantial trees grow in the basin. Those on the upper margin are stunted, their trunks bent away from the prevailing wind. Down below, the cold settles and stays. The dim daylight arrives late over the eastern bank and withdraws early behind the western one. Most surfaces never acquire a clear shadow. Their colors separate by texture instead: the soft ocher of dead sedges, the dark velvet of exposed peat, the pale crust on a steel handrail.

Animals are scarce enough that their traces matter. A hare has crossed one frosted strip, leaving a double line of prints that ends at a cluster of shrubs. A small bird sometimes works along the trench walls, taking whatever the cut soil has exposed. You see it move, pause, and move again. You cannot hear it.

Toward evening, the far compartments disappear into the same gray as the sky. The moss nearest you remains distinct for a little longer. Each tuft holds frost differently. Some have fine white tips; some contain tiny hollows too sheltered to freeze completely; some have been pressed flat by a boot and will remain that way until the ground softens.

The pump shed lights come on. One lamp illuminates the service door, another the pipe joints. Their glow does not travel far enough to make the basin smaller.

Beyond those two small pools of light, the low vegetation continues across the floor, over the abandoned boards, up the banks, and into the surrounding land. The machinery keeps roaring. Beneath the frozen surface, water moves slowly toward it.
