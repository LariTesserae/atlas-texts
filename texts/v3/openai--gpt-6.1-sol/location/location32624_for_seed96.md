The first impression is of wet white stone, but none of it is stone.

Every ledge, ridge, and steep-sided hollow has been made from fired clay. Even the mountains at the far end of the basin are assembled: pale courses stacked in steps, their seams visible where the brightness allows a little shadow. The country rises above the cloud layer on a foundation no one can see. At its edges, water slips through slots in the masonry and disappears into white air.

There is no shore of bare earth, no outcrop from which the builders began. A broken paving tile exposes another tile underneath, set at a different angle. Below that is a layer of hollow blocks. The landscape is construction all the way down.

It is cool here. Water left in a shallow dish stays cold through the brightest part of the day. The light comes almost vertically from a sky the color of watered milk, with an occasional blue opening so clear and intense that it seems farther away than sky should be. Everything catches this light: the porcelain-white faces of the walls, the wet gray seams, the thousands of little pools fitted into the terraces. Looking across the basin is like looking across a settlement after rain, except that the settlement includes its own hills, its own ravines, and the high enclosing shoulders that determine where rain can go.

The basin is several miles wide. You can see across it, but there is nowhere empty within it.

There are stairways alongside stairways, channels laid over channels, rows of vessels embedded mouth-up in the floors. Low walls divide the terraces into narrow courts. Each court contains a basin or a stack of basins, and each basin has a notch that empties into the next. Broad slopes that look smooth from a distance resolve into close-packed clay funnels. On the upper ridges, slender hollow pillars stand shoulder to shoulder, each capped with a shallow bowl. Between their feet are smaller cups, then thumb-sized cups filling the intervals between those.

Even the walking surfaces gather water. The tops of the paving tiles are very slightly concave. A heel-width strip along their edges provides a continuous dry route, though “dry” here means merely free of standing water. Everything wears a cold film.

The water comes frequently, but seldom as ordinary rain. Cloud enters the basin through gaps left in the high walls. It moves low over the terraces, catches against the pillars, and thins. Droplets form on the clay. At first they make the surfaces look darker; then the drops join and begin to travel.

This takes time. A bead may hang under a lip for several minutes, growing until its clear lower half trembles. When it falls, it usually lands on a slanted face and spreads rather than splashes. Water passes through the whole place in sheets too thin to have much voice.

You hear an isolated tick from a covered cistern. Much later, another answers from farther downhill. A damp tile gives a small click as the air warms. In the deep collection courts there is sometimes a low, sustained note: air escaping through a wet opening as a chamber fills.

The sound is easy to mistake for someone working at a distance.

There are signs of hands everywhere. Some blocks carry the shallow heel of a palm. Others have the crescent left by a thumbnail smoothing a corner. Long channels were shaped with strips of wood drawn through soft clay; you can see where the tool snagged and where someone repaired the furrow with two fingers. A few enormous retaining walls lean slightly because their courses were judged by hanging cords rather than by anything more exact.

The means were simple. Clay, water, fire, rope, wooden frames. Yet the repetition of small tasks has produced a terrain large enough to have weather.

In one district the hills are almost entirely made of jars.

They are thick-bodied jars, unglazed on the outside, fitted into one another so tightly that only their rounded shoulders remain visible. Some are filled with crushed pottery. Some hold water. Some are empty and serve as air chambers beneath the slopes. Where a wall has been left unfinished, the cross-section shows their dark mouths in orderly layers, as densely arranged as cells in a cut fruit.

There is no fruit here, and nothing that would eat it. No trees interrupt the terraces. No grass grows in the mortar. The water is clear even in the lowest pools, where one might expect a skin of green. Under certain lips there is a faint yellow mineral stain, but it has no softness and does not spread. The place smells of clean wet clay, with a trace of ash when the wind crosses an old firing court.

Those courts are scattered through the basin. They are long, sunken enclosures with blackened walls and narrow mouths at either end. Beside them lie stacks of unfired blocks beneath overhanging roofs. Some have hardened enough to lift; others would take the print of a finger. Wooden paddles, looped cords, and flat smoothing stones rest in shallow recesses, protected from the wet.

Nothing seems abandoned in a dramatic way. There are no fallen doors, no belongings gathered for flight. Work appears to have paused at many different points. A course of coping tiles ends halfway around a reservoir. A stair reaches a landing where the next step has been marked but not set. Across a broad eastern slope, hundreds of little clay teeth wait in rows to be fitted into the channels.

Much of the landscape is new. Edges remain keen enough to scrape skin. The pale walls have not rounded under the water, and the channels keep the exact angles of the tools that cut them. Older portions are distinguishable chiefly by the chalky deposits beneath their outlets. Here, age accumulates as a narrow white beard.

At the basin’s center stands a low rise, broad and densely built, which from a distance resembles a temple hill. Approaching it reveals no entrance or central hall. Its sides are covered with tiny steps, each step holding a cup, each cup linked to the one above by a short, dark strip of cord.

These cords draw water upward.

Only a little, and very slowly. The lower cups feed the higher ones through their soaked fibers. Each ascent is no greater than the height of a finger. There are thousands of ascents. Around the hill’s base, the broad collecting pools have been emptied by this patient lifting; at the summit, a shallow bowl remains full.

The arrangement needs no moving parts. It can work for days without making a sound.

From the summit you can make out similar rises elsewhere, some complete, others still missing their upper courses. Their full bowls reflect the sky. Water overflows from them into clean channels that lead outward along the ridges, feeding districts too high to receive the basin’s ordinary drainage. The whole country appears to be engaged in lifting its water a little farther, then finding another place to hold it.

Near the summit bowl there is a narrow bench built from three slabs. Its seat is sheltered by a clay hood and polished in two shallow places. Sitting there, you can see the far retaining walls disappear behind a bank of cloud. Closer at hand, a droplet creeps along the underside of the hood, pauses at a flaw in the firing, and swells.

Below, the crowded terraces shine. Every hollow has its measure of water; every wall supports another wall or guides a thin current. The brightness makes the distance hazy, but nearby details stay extraordinarily clear: a blue thread in one of the lifting cords, a thumbprint beside a spillway, a single tile set crooked among hundreds of careful neighbors.

After a while the droplet falls onto the bench beside you.

Its dark mark remains long after the sound is gone.
