At first the ground seems to be moss, because everything underfoot is green and gives slightly beneath your weight. Then the surface lifts. Not much—a few centimeters, over several minutes—but far enough that water drains away from your boots in a new direction. What looked like a hummock is the top of something still growing. What looked like a valley is a crease between two growth fronts.

There is very little old ground here.

The landscape stands high above a colder, darker lowland that is seldom visible. Its edges hang in cloud. Across its interior, broad swells rise to the height of mountains without ever becoming peaks: they remain rounded, crowded with leaves, wet at their crowns. Some are pale with new tissue. Others have darkened into immense buttresses of woody material, their surfaces upholstered in smaller plants. The horizon consists of one living elevation behind another, fading into green-gray weather.

Nothing has had time to become a ruin. Even the deepest channels have smooth walls, still enlarging from within.

The surface is not a forest growing on a continent. It is a continent whose exposed layers happen to leaf.

Here and there, a split reveals the thickness beneath. Fine roots make a felt just below the surface; below those are braided roots as thick as rivers, and below those, load-bearing trunks lying horizontally in the dark. Their grain changes direction around pockets of water. Some are translucent at the newest ends, showing bundles of vessels running through them like wet threads through wax. Deeper still is a hard, pale material precipitated by the roots: mineral laid down in sheets, already strong enough to support the next several hundred meters of growth.

It breaks occasionally. A low booming travels through the terrain, a seam opens, and glossy green tissue immediately begins to fill it.

The coolness is pervasive. It lives in the water caught between leaf and stem, in the fog pressed against your face, in the heavy spray that shakes loose whenever the ground moves. Your hands go numb even though there is no frost. The air smells of bruised fern, clean sap, and stone before it has dried.

There are enormous quantities of water, but surprisingly little open water. Almost every pool is occupied. Rafts of small, fleshy leaves fit together so closely that their edges rise where they press against one another. Transparent roots hang beneath them, drinking from several depths at once. Taller stems push through the rafts and unfold narrow leaves into whatever space remains above. In the hollows, where a lake might otherwise form, layers of floating growth pile up until the water is only visible through dark finger-wide slots.

Rain reaches this surface in stages. It falls first on the high foliage, then gathers at the tips of leaves, falls again onto a lower canopy, and again onto another. A storm can end overhead while its water continues descending for days. Down in the deep folds there is always rain, even under a clear patch of sky.

Most of the sky is never clear. The upland makes its own weather. Vapor rises from millions of square kilometers of wet growth, collects against colder air, and returns in low, dragging cloud. Light enters diffusely, without a reliable direction. New shoots are the brightest things in view: muted yellow, silver-green, sometimes a faint pink along a tightly rolled edge. Beneath them, mature foliage absorbs almost everything. Looking into a dense stand is like looking into deep water.

There are no birds. No insects circle the flowers. Nothing grazes the tender margins or disturbs the floating leaves from below.

That absence does not make the place quiet.

The noise is so continuous that, for a while, you fail to understand it as separate sounds. Water hammers down vertical root faces. Vast leaves rub against each other with the harshness of dragged canvas. Seed cases split in abrupt volleys. Somewhere underneath, pressurized sap passes through a narrowing channel and produces a note too low to hear cleanly; it makes your ribs vibrate instead.

Wind entering the upper growth is divided among countless hollow stems. Each has a slightly different length. In a strong gust, whole slopes become wind instruments, but without the orderliness of music: whistles, groans, choking blasts, a shrill vibration that climbs until something bends and releases it. Conversation would require shouting directly into another person’s ear. Even then, the words might vanish beneath the next hydraulic concussion.

The loudest events come during the raising of new terrain.

You can recognize the beginning by the water. It retreats from a broad area, pulled down into the rootwork. Leaves flatten. The surface stiffens. Then an entire ridge begins to swell, slowly enough that you can watch without seeing a single definite movement, quickly enough that the horizon has changed when you look back.

Inside, new supporting tissue is being assembled. Minerals are drawn out of the water, separated, fitted into a structure more exact than crystal and more forgiving than wood. The process gives off little heat. There are no furnaces, no machines exposed to view, no buildings or abandoned instruments. Yet the precision is difficult to mistake.

Broken vessels seal before their contents have finished spilling. Root junctions redirect pressure around a wound without waiting for any visible signal to arrive. Widely separated slopes sometimes close their pores together, although the weather reaching each is different. They seem to be responding to conditions somewhere else—perhaps beneath them, perhaps far beyond the visible horizon.

Under certain leaves, where the dim light strikes at an angle, there is a finer structure than veins: repeated bands, smaller than the eye can resolve, making a faint moving sheen. These tissues admit some kinds of light and reject others. Around them the air cools more sharply. Moisture gathers on a leaf that is not cold enough, by ordinary expectation, to collect it.

Whatever carries out this work is part of the organism. It is grown, renewed, folded into the same wet substance as the rest. There is no useful boundary between the vegetation and its equipment.

Space is the scarce resource. Every exposed surface is claimed immediately. A new ridge scarcely emerges before films spread over it, followed by tiny cups, followed by fleshy blades that push the cups aside. Plants grow on plants, inside plants, across wounds in plants. In the darkest depths, colorless growth threads through the load-bearing wood and feeds on the substances delivered there. Nothing needs to find bare soil. Bare soil would be an interruption.

And yet the place is not finished filling itself.

At the outer margins, beyond the established heights, great green lobes extend into the cloud. Their tips are soft and almost white. Water beads on them in perfectly round drops. They advance a little, brace themselves, thicken. Behind them, the first shallow folds appear. A drainage line forms where no drainage line existed that morning. Farther inward, it will become a cleft deep enough to hold permanent mist.

The new land arrives already wet, already occupied, already loud. Its streams have not worn their beds; their beds have grown around them. Its cliffs have not been exposed by loss; they are the steep faces of additions.

By evening—if the gradual dimming above the cloud can be called evening—the growth fronts become harder to distinguish. The pale margins remain visible longest. Water continues falling through the layers. The ground tightens underfoot, and somewhere beyond sight another ridge opens its first leaves.

There is no path to follow. Behind you, the slight depression left by your weight has filled with clear water. Small green blades are already leaning into it.
