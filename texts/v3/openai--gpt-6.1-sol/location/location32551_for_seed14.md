The bottom is not visible at first. There is only dark water carrying more dark water past you, and within it a crowded drift of fine material: silt, shell dust, fragments of dead plankton, grains too small to fall while the current is running. A light would not open a view so much as reveal the density of what lies between you and the view. The nearest particles would shine; everything beyond them would remain concealed.

This is a drowned lowland beneath a cold sea. It extends for miles between broad, nearly level shoulders of rock, with a deeper channel winding through its middle. The water above is heavy enough to make the surface irrelevant. Storms happen there, but here their consequences arrive without their appearance: a change in pressure, a long displacement of water, a current that strengthens over several hours and begins to move things that had seemed settled.

The rock is old and worn almost past distinction. There are no sharp peaks, no fresh faces of stone. Ridges have become rises; broken ledges have become rounded shelves. In places the bedrock emerges from the sediment as a low, dark pavement, its seams filled flush with mud. Elsewhere it lies buried beneath pale deposits that have been lifted, sorted, and laid down too many times to retain a clean history.

A stone exposed here may have spent centuries under the bottom. By the next tide it may be covered again.

Across the lowland, the current runs in broad lanes. Some pass over the stone shoulders; others squeeze through shallow gaps and accelerate. The deepest lane follows the buried channel, though the channel itself is difficult to see. Its presence is disclosed by movement: grit traveling close to the floor, a loose valve of shell turning over and over, sediment pulled into a low veil that stretches away into darkness.

At slack water, the veil subsides. For a little while the bottom acquires detail. Small pits appear, and feeding traces, and pale curves of shell embedded in gray mud. There are narrow ridges of sand, no higher than a hand, with one steep side and one gradual side. Their edges are precise but temporary. When the flow returns, sand runs up the gradual face and spills down the other. The whole ridge advances grain by grain.

The place is ancient; its exposed surface is seldom more than a few hours old.

Cold enters everything. There is no patch of sun-warmed water, no sheltered shallows where warmth can collect. Water that has sunk elsewhere arrives along the bottom, carrying the chill of ice and winter into a seasonless depth. The few living things move slowly unless movement is required of them. Energy is scarce, and any exposed surface must endure both the cold and the persistent abrasion of suspended grit.

Nothing like a meadow grows here. Along the highest rock shoulders, where a little dim light sometimes reaches during the clearest season, there are thin stains of algae. They lie close against the stone, brown or almost black, easy to mistake for the stone’s own discoloration. Farther down, vegetation survives only as material carried from somewhere else: a softened strand caught under a pebble, a scrap of weed circling in an eddy until it comes apart.

Life is sparse but not absent. A small brittle star occupies the lee of a rounded slab, extending two arms into the passing water. On a patch of firm mud, a sea pen stands with its delicate branches open. When disturbed, it contracts toward the bottom, leaving little to distinguish its place from the surrounding sediment. A pale fish rests in a shallow hollow. It makes small corrections with its fins, keeping its head into the flow. Eventually it lifts, moves a short distance, and becomes impossible to follow.

Much of what lives here is concealed. Worms occupy fine tubes in the mud. Small crustaceans remain beneath stones or within the sediment, emerging during intervals when the water is less forceful. Their openings are scattered rather than numerous. A patch of bottom may show no visible animal at all, only the evidence that something has fed there and withdrawn.

Yet there is no feeling of emptiness. The water is packed with matter in transit. Between the shoulders of rock, sediment streams through every available passage. Depressions collect it, lose it, collect it again. Even in the apparent stillness beside a stone, tiny particles circulate in closed, wavering paths.

The turbulence is often local. Two currents meet above a low sill and produce a slow, rolling boundary. Water descending from the shoulder folds into water rising from the channel. A cloud of mud expands, narrows, tears into smaller clouds. Farther on, the flow catches the lip of a deposit and lifts a sheet of sediment from the floor. For several minutes an entire stretch of bottom disappears. When it emerges, a hollow has deepened, a shell has shifted, and a previously buried stone is bare.

There are no ruins beneath these deposits. No wall interrupts the current; no cut edge or worked surface appears when the mud is stripped away. The shapes here come from fracture, wear, deposition, and the repeated pressure of moving water. A long groove might resemble a path until it branches into smaller grooves around stones and fades into rippled sand.

Sound is subdued. The strongest current brings a low, continuous vibration, difficult to separate from the water itself. Close to the bottom, sand makes a faint granular hiss. A pebble occasionally knocks against exposed rock. These small sounds vanish quickly into the larger volume. Much of the upheaval happens quietly: mud lifting, a bank softening, a dense cloud traveling down the channel with no visible front.

There is almost no light by which to witness it. The darkness changes chiefly in texture. Clear water gives it depth; suspended mud brings it close. Sometimes a pale object passes near enough to be seen—a shell fragment, the underside of a fish—and offers a brief measure of distance before it is gone.

Toward slack tide, the water begins to clear. Particles descend, the sand ridges stop advancing, and the low shoulders become faintly distinguishable from the channel between them. The bottom seems to settle into a final arrangement.

Then, slowly at first, the flow turns. Fine sediment lifts from the crests of the ridges. The brittle star withdraws an arm. A shell shifts in its hollow, tips onto its edge, and starts to travel.
