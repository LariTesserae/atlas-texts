The plain is warm and almost level. Its slight rises are enough to hide the ground ahead, but never enough to hide the horizon: a long, pale boundary visible between leaves, interrupted here and there by the blunt backs of newly emerged stone. Nothing has been rounded by weather. Even the smallest mineral fragments have clean corners. A shard no larger than a fingernail casts a precise, triangular shadow.

There is no water in the soil, no dampness beneath the roots. Break the ground open and it separates into dry plates, cream-colored on the outside, dark violet within. The exposed surfaces fit together too exactly to look geological. They are not tiles, however, and nothing laid them. This is how the earth forms here: each plate arrives with its edges already decided.

Vegetation fills the plain.

At ankle height, thick mats of narrow leaves overlap like green scales. Above them stand clusters of fleshy, translucent stems, crowded close enough that their shadows merge. Broad leaves spread at waist level, some smooth and dark, others furred with copper filaments. Farther out, groves of low, many-trunked trees make islands of shade. Their crowns interlock without tangling. Between the groves lie open reaches of pale mineral ground, dotted with red rosettes and sprawling, silver-veined creepers.

The abundance suggests rainfall until you touch it.

A leaf is warm, dry, and unexpectedly light. Its underside has the faint grain of a crystal face. The veins are not channels carrying sap; they are boundaries between differently aligned portions of the leaf. Turn one toward the sun and a thin gold line passes through it, moving from tip to stem. Nothing follows that line. It is simply the moment at which the leaf’s internal geometry agrees with the light.

These plants do not drink. They maintain themselves through arrangements of matter that would elsewhere require vast machinery. Their roots reach into the violet layers beneath the surface, where distances become negotiable. A root can end six inches below its stem and also within a mineral deposit several miles away. Nutrients cross without traversing the intervening ground.

There are no buried pipes, no stations, no instruments. The sophistication belongs to the living tissue. Under magnification, a root tip would reveal structures smaller than atoms yet composed of ordinary matter, folded so that their interiors have more room than their exteriors permit. The plants have no need to understand what they do.

For all this hidden intricacy, the view is clear and familiar. Sunlight falls across the plain with the ordinary generosity of a warm afternoon. Greens remain green. The white ground is bright enough to make you narrow your eyes. In the groves, shade is deep and cool-looking, though the air there is scarcely cooler. The sky is a clean, faint blue.

Only after walking for a while do the distances begin to misbehave.

A stand of copper-fringed trees appears perhaps half a mile away. You cross an open patch, step between two low bushes, and find yourself beside their trunks. Looking back, the patch you crossed remains the same apparent width. The footprints are there, spaced normally, but they account for only a few yards.

Elsewhere, a red rosette stays beside you for twenty paces. It does not move. Its position in the landscape remains fixed; the path simply continues to be adjacent to it. When at last you leave it behind, there is a small sense of release in the ankles, as though you have stepped off a surface that was very slightly adhesive.

The vegetation follows these peculiarities closely. Dense thickets gather along places where many distances meet. Sparse, broad-leaved plants occupy regions that are internally much larger than they look. One low tree may shade an area sufficient for a grove. Its shadow lies neatly beneath its crown, but you can spend several minutes crossing it.

There is little sound.

Your footfalls have a dry, flat quality on the mineral plates. Among the ground cover they become softer, though the leaves do not rustle unless you actually touch them. There is no wind threading the crowns. No insects tick or whine. Once in a long interval, a clear note sounds from somewhere underfoot, quiet enough to mistake for a ringing in your own ear. It comes from a root junction settling into agreement with another junction far away.

No second note answers it.

The rare animals are easiest to notice in the open spaces. A small, pale creature may stand among the red rosettes: six slender legs, a narrow body, a head with two glossy patches on either side. It holds its limbs at angles that seem delicate but remain perfectly steady. You might take it for a shed husk until it turns to face you.

The turn happens without visible motion. One moment its head points toward the grove; the next it points toward your foot. Nothing blurs. Nothing clicks.

If approached, it is eventually found a few yards farther away. There is no leap to watch. Its departure leaves the place as quiet as before. These creatures feed on flaws in the plants’ internal arrangements, removing tiny inconsistencies that would otherwise accumulate. Their mouths never need to break a leaf.

The whole region seems newly made. Mineral ridges still carry fine, blade-like crests. There are no fallen trunks, no leaf mold, no worn tracks between the groves. Yet the freshness does not imply that anything is growing rapidly, or growing at all. A curled shoot remains curled. A half-open flower keeps the same narrow gap between its petals. Beside it, mature leaves spread in complete, immaculate fans.

You can return after an hour and recognize the position of every filament.

The landscape is not frozen. It responds to contact. A stem bends beneath your hand and straightens when released. Dust rises beneath your heel and settles. A broken leaf exposes a bright interior which folds shut, restoring the edge without producing a scar.

But left to itself, the plain has nowhere further to go. Its vegetation is lush because nothing is lost, not because anything is continually replaced. Its warmth does not gather or dissipate. The clean edges of the stone are preserved without becoming brittle. There is no season preparing behind this one.

In a wide clearing, the mineral surface bears a shallow depression shaped like a basin. It is empty. Along its rim, dense green stems lean inward, their leaves almost touching above the center. The arrangement evokes a place where water ought to collect, but the basin has never held any. Beneath its pale floor, roots meet across impossible distances. Their minute adjustments sustain the crowded life around it.

You can sit there comfortably, with the sun on your shoulders and the dry stone warm beneath your palms. Across the clearing, a copper-fringed crown has a gap through which you see another grove, then a white strip of plain, then the low horizon.

Nothing moves in that gap.

After a while, you notice that the distant grove is visible between two of the leaves beside your knee as well. The trees are the same size in both openings. Their trunks align exactly.

You shift your hand, and one view closes. The other remains.
