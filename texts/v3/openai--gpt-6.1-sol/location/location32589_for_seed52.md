The city begins well before its first buildings, in the spacing of the trees.

Across the low plain, groves stand at regular intervals: dense, extravagant masses of foliage separated by long stretches of pale, almost bare ground. Their leaves are broad and dark, with copper undersides that show when the air moves. Vines hang through the branches without reaching the soil. Each grove occupies a shallow depression, and each depression holds enough moisture to sustain this abundance, though between them the earth is dry enough to powder beneath a foot.

At a distance, the groves resemble neighborhoods. Closer in, some of them are.

Buildings stand inside the vegetation, their walls visible as narrow verticals among the trunks. Others rise alone from the plain, surrounded by several hectares of unoccupied ground. They are tall without being monumental, slender towers with rounded chambers attached at uneven heights, their surfaces the color of unglazed porcelain. Beneath them, streets cross in broad, empty bands. There are curbs, junctions, numbered entrances, shelters where someone might wait for transport. The scale is unmistakably urban. Yet one can look down an avenue and see, beyond three intersections and a solitary public building, an uninterrupted strip of grass extending to the horizon.

The city was planned for more inhabitants than have arrived. Or for inhabitants who require more room than people do.

There is no clear daylight. The sky has the dim, warm luminosity of a room with its lamps hidden behind curtains. Clouds are difficult to distinguish from the atmosphere around them; occasionally a darker thickness travels overhead and the towers lose what little shadow they possess. Beneath the trees, small lamps burn among the roots. They illuminate neither paths nor doors, only particular leaves, particular knots of bark.

The plain itself is new.

Along road cuttings, the exposed earth has not yet settled into layers. It is an intimate confusion of black mineral glass, pale clay, and porous stone with clean, brittle edges. Low ridges run across the city, sometimes passing beneath buildings, sometimes breaking through the pavement. They are only waist-high in places, but their crests are sharp. Rain has not had time to round them. In the undeveloped districts, flakes of stone stand upright in the ground like pages fanned in a closed book.

Every few weeks another ridge appears.

It begins as a straight discoloration, perhaps a dark seam across a plaza. The paving separates neatly along it. For several days there is a faint warmth underfoot. Then the ground lifts, slowly enough that a bench can remain balanced on it, quickly enough that residents do not plant permanent things nearby. The municipal maps acquire a new contour. Walkways withdraw from the seam on hundreds of small joints, leaving a strip of exposed soil.

No one calls this an earthquake. Nothing shakes.

The land is still being made, and the city has been built to accommodate the work.

Much of its engineering is concealed in these accommodations. A tower beside a forming ridge will move a little west during the night without disturbing its foundations. A stairway will have one fewer step in the morning. In the long transit halls, trains arrive with no sound of approach. The track is a polished trough containing nothing visible, and the cars seem to become present along its length, first as an interruption in the dimness, then as solid surfaces with doors.

Passengers wait until the condensation appears on the windows. Before that, the cars are not safe to touch.

Moisture is sparse but carefully held. There are no broad rivers here. Water travels through the city in narrow channels, most of them roofed with fern fronds or ceramic covers. It can be heard under certain intersections: a soft, rapid running that stops when someone opens a service hatch. The canals feed the groves, then disappear below ground.

Rain comes briefly, usually in the warmest part of the cycle. Large drops fall widely spaced, striking the pavement with distinct sounds. A street may receive only twenty or thirty of them before the cloud passes. Each dark mark spreads farther than it should, following hairline patterns in the paving until it reaches a planted hollow.

After rain, the air smells of crushed stems and warm chalk.

There is more water on the buildings than in the weather. Their lower walls sweat at irregular hours. Pearls of condensation form on surfaces that are not cold, join into threads, and run upward toward narrow collecting gutters. People leave cups in recesses beside their doors. By morning, these contain a small measure of clear water, tasting faintly of stone.

The plants exploit every such source. Around a faulty gutter, a hanging garden can establish itself in a month. Roots enter doorframes, swell inside them, and emerge as smooth cords on the other side. Maintenance crews rarely cut them away. They fit the entrances with broader frames and teach the locks a new position.

In the richest groves, the vegetation has acquired a vertical geography. Dry-loving shrubs occupy the ground around the edges. Broad-leaved plants crowd the central hollows. Higher up, in the persistent damp surrounding the buildings’ upper chambers, soft mosses cover entire balconies. Flowers grow there that never open at ground level: pale tubes with heavy, nodding heads, each holding a teaspoon of liquid.

Almost nothing comes to drink from them.

Animals are uncommon enough to interrupt attention. A single narrow-bodied bird moving between towers can keep everyone in a transit shelter looking upward. Small, six-legged creatures sometimes emerge from the new ridges, dusty and slow, carrying fragments of mineral on their backs. They vanish into the groves before the edges of their birthplace have cooled. Whether they lived beneath the plain or were made with it is unresolved.

At night—or during the darker interval people use as night—something large occasionally moves through the fern channels. It leaves no footprints. In the morning, stems on opposite banks lean inward, and water has been drawn from several neighboring gardens. The city closes those paths for a day. The closure notices do not name an animal, only a maintenance condition.

Most sounds carry a long way.

A dropped tool rings through an entire residential district. One hears leaves rubbing together high above a deserted courtyard, a door closing somewhere beyond the next grove, the dry click of a ridge shedding its first fragments. The transit system has a low vibration that can be felt through the soles but rarely heard. During certain hours the towers exchange signals as faint descending notes, each answering another too distant to see.

People speak quietly outside. There is no rule requiring it. The spaces make louder conversation seem unnecessary.

Toward the southern edge, construction thins into a region of unfinished foundations. Huge rooms lie open to the sky, furnished with drains and doorways but no walls. Young trees grow in places marked for columns. Beyond them stretches a fresh expanse of dark ground, so flat that the city’s scattered lights reflect from it as if from water.

It is dry. Walking across it leaves shallow prints, each with a bright rim.

Here the newest streets end in clean cuts. Their buried services project a short distance beyond the paving: bundles of translucent rods, sealed pipes, slender filaments that turn slowly toward the unoccupied land. Some mornings there are survey markers farther out than anyone remembers placing them.

The air remains warm. A few leaves tumble from the last grove and stop against a rise too slight to see in the dimness. Somewhere beneath the plain, water takes a route that did not exist yesterday.

Behind you, the city stands widely spaced among its gardens, with rooms lit on distant floors and a train becoming solid at an empty platform. Ahead, a thin seam crosses the bare ground. It is straight for a hundred meters, then curves gently east.

By tomorrow it may be high enough to cast a shadow.
