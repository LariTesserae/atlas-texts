The valley is broad, low, and almost black beneath the storm. Its far side appears only when cloud lifts from the lower slopes: a long shoulder of rock, a strip of pale snow, then darkness again. Most of the land between is green. Dense willow scrub follows the watercourses, spruce crowds the gravel rises, and the hollows hold sedges so thick that the wind moves their tops without exposing the ground.

Nothing is still long enough to take in at once.

Rain comes across the valley in hard, slanting bands. Between them, sleet rattles through the branches. The temperature hovers just below freezing, but the ground releases enough heat to keep most of the water moving. Ice forms on the windward sides of stems, on fence wires, on the lips of stones. Under the vegetation, the soil is black, wet, and soft. Water appears beneath a boot before the footprint is complete.

The river cannot be seen from much of the valley floor. It is hidden behind brush and fresh banks, but it can always be heard. Its roar fills the low country, swallowing the smaller sounds until you approach them: rain hammering broad leaves; gravel ticking down a collapsing cut; a spruce trunk knocking against another trunk as both bend. Near the main channel, the sound becomes a pressure in the jaw. People who live here carry hand signals into ordinary conversation. Even inside a house, with the shutters closed, they tend to sit close together.

The valley has only recently been uncovered. Less than a generation ago, ice occupied the central basin. Its retreat left a chain of shallow lakes, loose moraines, and steep-sided deposits that have not yet settled into durable shapes. Every flood redraws some part of the map. The river cuts a bank overnight and exposes trees that grew in a summer, then carries them away with their root mats still green. Side streams abandon their beds, spill through the scrub, and find lower ground. Fresh stones have sharp edges. A broken slope can look almost quarried, though no one has touched it.

Vegetation has come quickly. The first low mats of moss and flowering heath are still visible on exposed gravel, but behind them the growth is already deep and tangled. Dwarf birch and alder occupy terraces barely older than their trunks. Ferns crowd the warmer seepages. In sheltered places, spruce saplings rise so close together that their branches interlock from the ground upward. Old ice is sometimes found beneath this new forest: a dirty blue slab under roots, wasting into a cavity that fills with cold water.

The lushness is uneven. It follows warmth, shelter, and stable footing rather than sunlight, of which there is little. The growing season has long dim days; now, late in the year, the sun scarcely clears the southern ridge. Cloud reduces noon to a gray interval. Leaves remain on some of the shrubs because the roots are warm, although their outer branches are glazed with ice.

Much of that warmth comes from installations scattered through the basin.

From a distance they resemble field shelters: low roofs, narrow doors, foundations sunk into gravel. There are only a few dozen, separated by stretches of marsh or woodland, and none is large enough to dominate the view. Their walls are made of a dull, dark material that holds neither lichen nor frost. Around each building, the ground stays thawed in a rough oval, often crowded with vegetation. A few have porches, tool racks, boot scrapers. One has a kitchen window and a row of battered mugs hanging over the sink.

Beneath these small buildings are the machines that keep the valley inhabited.

They transfer heat through paired foundations with no pipes between them. They hold selected patches of ground against subsidence, divert momentum out of a flood surge, and keep a crossing anchored when the gravel beneath it has gone downstream. Their visible workings are modest: status strips beside doors, inspection plates, a pale ring embedded in a concrete sill. The immense work happens below the surface, beyond anything that can be explained by the thickness of a wall or the size of a cable.

The limits are apparent, though. A stabilized house can stand while its garden falls into the river. A bridge may remain level over a channel that has shifted twenty meters sideways. The engineers protect particular places, not the whole valley, and there are mornings when the safe route from one building to the next has to be established anew.

There is an abandoned station on the eastern flats. Its roof is intact. Its windows are dark. Floodwater has stripped the ground from around its foundation, leaving the building on a narrow pedestal of compacted earth. Willow roots trail from the sides. The station still warms the soil beneath it, and several small birds shelter there in the green cavity under the floor.

Wildlife is everywhere, though often only a movement at the edge of sight. Ducks huddle in the lee of reed beds, lifting together when debris bears down on them. Small rodents use the raised seams between flooded hollows; their paths pass beneath roots and through dense grass, sometimes ending abruptly at new water. Deer keep to the firmer ground along the valley margins. They emerge from the trees soaked and dark, feed quickly, then withdraw when the gusts strengthen.

In the quieter backwaters there are fish. Insects hatch above the warm seepages even in this weather, and the birds work those places hard. A dipper stands on a stone in a tributary, vanishes into the torrent, and returns to the same stone. Its song cannot be heard. Farther out, ravens cross the valley low, pushed sideways by the wind, correcting with abrupt movements of their wings.

The storm makes the animals difficult to count. A flock may be concealed in a single alder thicket. A dozen deer can pass through the scrub without showing more than a back, an ear, the pale flash of a rump. The landscape feels occupied even where nothing is visible. Leaves shake against the wind’s direction; water ripples beneath an overhanging bank; a narrow tunnel in the moss is newly open.

Toward the estuary, the valley widens. The river divides into channels across a dark gravel fan, and the freshwater marsh gives way to salt grass. Low islands rise only a little above the tide. They carry heaps of driftwood, reed beds, and occasional survey posts bent by ice. Beyond them lies the sea, almost indistinguishable from the sky except for the moving white edges of breakers.

Here the noise is greatest. Surf strikes the outer bars while floodwater rushes seaward between them. On an incoming tide the two meet in steep, confused waves. Logs turn end over end. Foam collects behind islands and is torn loose in long streaks. The wind lays the grass flat, lets it rise halfway, then drives it down again.

A narrow causeway crosses the upper marsh. It is made of ordinary gravel, repaired often, with several short spans held by the buried systems. White markers show where its edges should be. In bad weather, some of the markers disappear behind spray or reeds, and travelers stop rather than guess. There are insulated waiting huts at either end, stocked with dry clothing, food, and hearing protection.

At night, the scattered buildings show very little light. Windows are shuttered against flying grit. Door lamps shine downward onto wet steps. Maintenance crews use hooded lights, revealing brief sections of the valley: glossy leaves, a pale boulder, water running over a road that was dry that afternoon. Beyond the beam, the terrain continues shifting in the dark.

Sometimes a bank fails close enough to hear through everything else. There is a deep crack, then a heavy rush, then the continuous river again. In the morning a new bluff stands above the channel. Its face is raw clay threaded with roots. Water beads on it and runs down in thin lines. Already, small pieces are falling from the edge.

The place is cold and fertile, recently made and constantly disturbed. The woods are young, the channels provisional, the buildings carefully anchored to ground that does not stay put. From a sheltered doorway you can watch an entire slope of willow thrash in the rain, each branch moving differently, while behind it the river tears at the bank. By daylight, however little there is, someone will go out to see what remains connected.
