The first difficulty is finding anything that can be called empty.

From a distance, the upland appears white: a high, brilliantly exposed continent lifted into air too cold to carry much moisture. Its edge is a succession of fresh cliffs, some higher than a weather system. Clouds lie against their lower faces, and the summits rise out of them with the clean, broken outlines of stone that has not yet learned to crumble. There are no rounded foothills. Mountains meet the plateau through abrupt shelves and blade-like ridges; the valleys between them are narrow, deep, and mostly without rivers.

But the whiteness is not snow, except in the shadows. It is movement too slow to distinguish from a surface.

Almost everything visible is alive.

The plateau carries forests whose crowns press together so tightly that, from above, they resemble a single pale hide. Their leaves are thick, upright fins, silver on one face and dark green on the other. Most are held edge-on to the sun. When a stand turns its leaves—a response that takes several days—a dark patch travels across the plateau, sometimes hundreds of miles wide. The trees do not grow tall by lowland standards, but they grow from every available seam. They grip the sheer cliff faces. They fill fractures before those fractures have accumulated soil. Roots enter the sharp new stone and branch through it like veins in a tooth.

Between the trees there is another forest, knee-high, and beneath that another, measured in the thickness of a finger. Cushions, scales, tiny branching cups. Nothing sheds readily here. Dead leaves remain attached for years, becoming pale scaffolds for smaller plants. A fallen trunk does not open a clearing; it is intercepted by neighboring branches, suspended, rooted into, occupied before it has finished falling.

At ground level, the lushness is almost oppressive. There is no open path between stems. The ways that look passable belong to animals, and an animal is usually still in them.

The cold is severe enough that a breath freezes against the nearest leaf. Yet the light has weight. It pours down from a sky so clear and shallow-looking that the mountains on the opposite side of the continent can occasionally be seen, lifted by refraction into a second, inverted range. Stone, foliage, shell, and frost return the glare from below. Shadows are intensely blue and sharply bounded. Within one, the temperature can drop enough to make a wet joint crack.

Much of the life here wears translucent armor. Under the armor is pigment; under the pigment, black tissue that absorbs the sun. A leaf may have a surface colder than ice while its interior remains warm enough for a slow circulation. An animal can lie motionless beneath a bright crust, its organs working in a narrow pocket of heat.

There is little rain on the high country. Moisture arrives in brief events: a cloud driven over an escarpment, an hour of needles striking the leaves, a mist that freezes as soon as it touches a shaded face. The forest collects all of it. Water slides along grooves, enters collars at the bases of branches, and disappears into sealed reservoirs. At dawn, there are beads beneath the lowest leaves. By noon, those beads have been taken back up.

You can travel a long way through damp vegetation without seeing open water.

Then, unexpectedly, there is a pool.

It lies in a fresh crack in the rock, only a few feet wide, black despite the light. The banks are congested with organisms waiting for access. Some have held their places for so long that neighboring roots have grown around them. A pale animal shaped like a folded shield lowers one mouthpart into the water. Around it, smaller bodies draw moisture from the vapor rising off its shell. Below the surface, transparent swimmers are packed upright, each scarcely thicker than a blade of grass. They move aside by fractions of an inch. The water itself seems reluctant to stir.

The pool is warm. Far beneath it, the continent is still rising.

This is young land, though almost nothing upon it behaves quickly. The uplift proceeds in long, uneven increments. Every few generations of the small leaf-dwellers, a ridge acquires a new fracture. A valley floor tilts. Roots tighten across a gap. Fresh mineral surfaces appear, glossy and unworn, and the forest enters them before frost has had time to soften the edges.

In certain places the stone is warm enough to feed the whole mass above it. Elsewhere, life draws on light, on the electrical difference between heights, on the slow grinding of loaded rock. The plants are connected below ground by dense conductive roots. Their dark fibers carry charge from sunlit crowns into shaded depths. At exposed breaks, a blue discharge crawls along the grain. Animals feed there with insulated tongues.

The sound of the plateau begins with this current.

It is a low, continuous vibration, felt first through the soles and then through the teeth. Above it are harsher notes: contacts snapping, plates chattering, the long metallic rasp of feeding parts drawn across mineral seams. Forests on opposite slopes hold slightly different pitches. Where their root systems meet beneath a valley, the tones interfere, producing beats so slow that a listener might mistake them for changes in the weather.

Closer by, there are knocks, wheezes, grinding pulses. An enormous number of animals are breathing, but few breathe freely into the air. They push warmth and vapor through folded chambers, using pressure to drive limbs, lift protective plates, crush food. A resting herd sounds like machinery operating behind a wall.

There are no walls, no housings, no roads. Nothing has been installed. The valves are cartilage. The flywheels are rings of dense bone suspended within bodies. The cables are tendons braided with mineral fibers. What sounds like a works stretching to the horizon is an ecology.

On the upper ridges live animals broad enough to bridge fissures. Their backs are low and segmented, crowded with vegetation rooted in a permanent layer of dust. Each carries rows of feet beneath its sides, though it seldom moves more than a body length in a day. When one lifts itself, the sequence of muscular locks engaging can take an hour. The sound travels for miles: a succession of deep clunks, followed by the release of trapped air.

They graze by lowering a front margin onto the forest and advancing it very gradually. Plants survive behind them because the mouths take only the warm inner tissue. The silver outer leaves remain, empty but upright, and new growth uses their protection. Smaller animals gather along the feeding edge. Some eat fragments. Some drain the grazers’ condensation. Some tend colonies in the grooves of their armor and never touch the ground.

It is difficult to decide where one animal ends. A ridge grazer may support several hundred species, including creatures larger than a person, all fitted into its seams. Beneath each plate, bodies overlap in layered galleries. Transparent worms conduct heat from organ to shell. Blind, many-jointed animals clean the pressure chambers. Small white predators remain fixed beside openings, waiting for prey to pass within reach.

The abundance is not hectic. It is crowded and deliberate.

Across a square yard of bark there may be thousands of animals, each occupying a precise portion of the surface. Some feed once a season. Some turn toward the sun so slowly that their tracks can be read only by comparing one week with the next. A winged creature may remain braced against a trunk for an entire winter, its wings deployed as collectors rather than instruments of flight. When it finally detaches, the noise is startling: a hard flutter, a little shower of ice, then the broad hum of its passage.

Above the canopy, the largest flyers scarcely flap at all. They are pale and wide, ribbed underneath, with long streamers hanging into the trees. Rising air supports them where sun strikes the cliff faces. They drift along those faces in processions, entering the same updrafts generation after generation. Their streamers comb spores and small animals out of the crowns. Occasionally a body turns, and sunlight passes through it, showing the dark pumping organs laid out in repeated rows.

Their shadows take a long time to cross you.

There are regions where the packed forest gives way to packed animals. On south-facing shelves, shell-bearing creatures settle side by side, their backs forming an apparently continuous pavement. Foliage emerges through the gaps. Farther down, on a slope too steep for ordinary soil, thousands of long-bodied grazers cling vertically with their heads buried among roots. Their breath escapes from vents along their flanks and freezes into short white plumes. From the opposite ridge they look like another kind of tree.

Even the bare stone is not bare. Small mineral-colored bodies fit into hollows left by crystals. They can remain still for decades. A hand set against the rock might find that a portion of it is yielding slightly, accommodating the pressure.

Night does not quiet the place. The current moves deeper into the roots. Warm animals draw together, and the forest settles by minute degrees, releasing a long series of creaks. Beneath the canopy, translucent organs glow where stored charge passes through them. For a few hours the upland has an underside: a faintly lit network of chambers, mouths, wet folds, and threads connecting one body to another.

Then dawn reaches the eastern cliffs.

The leaves brighten before the ground does. Frost flashes along the armor of the resting herds. A pressure chamber opens somewhere far away with a sound like an immense door being dragged across stone, though there is no door, and nothing behind it but another animal beginning its day.

By full morning the plateau is white again. The new mountains stand in their old-looking forest. The pools remain hidden. A grazer has advanced perhaps a yard. High overhead, one of the broad flyers turns into the light, and for several minutes the shadow of its ribs lies across miles of tightly occupied land.
