The first impression is of a plateau covered in something too dense to be a forest and too continuous to be a city. There are no gaps where ground shows through. The ground is part of the growth: a black, fibrous pavement, hot enough to soften the soles of a boot, folded here and there into low ridges. Those ridges become walls. The walls divide into stems. The stems broaden into roofs, and the roofs carry another thickness of stems.

Everything is alive, and everything has been made.

The plateau stands high above the surrounding country. At its western edge, the living pavement rolls over a parapet and descends in a sheer face of braided roots. You cannot see the bottom. Warm air rises out of that darkness with the smell of scorched seed husks, electrical insulation, and old flour. Far across the drop, other terraces appear as faint horizontal interruptions in the black. Occasionally one shows a dull red seam. Then it closes, and the distance becomes indistinguishable from the sky.

There is very little light here. The roof of the world—cloud, canopy, or the underside of some greater construction—is close and opaque. What illumination exists comes from inside the plants. It leaks through joints, shines in the mouths of ventilation flowers, or shows briefly when a thick trunk splits. Most surfaces are visible only by the warmth they seem to hold against the eye. A ribbed wall may stand an arm’s length away without being seen until it moves.

The heat has no daily rhythm. It comes up through the floor and out through the vegetation, a sustained industrial heat with cooler currents circulating inside it. A narrow passage can feel almost tolerable until a row of shutters opens overhead. Then air pours down that has just crossed the furnaces below, and the passage becomes difficult to breathe.

The shutters are leaves. They are thick, blunt-ended, and hinged along pale lines of flexible tissue. Some are larger than houses. Their undersides carry arrays of fine ceramic hairs that comb dust from the air. On the upper surfaces, where almost no one can look, soot accumulates in a smooth black nap.

There is scarcely any water. A little survives deep inside certain sealed tissues, hoarded molecule by molecule. Nothing drips. Nothing steams when cut. A broken stem releases hot powder and a bead of amber grease that hardens before it falls.

These plants have outlived the chemistry that once made plants possible. Their leaves take energy from differences in temperature. Their roots move charge through beds of dry salts. Growth travels through a branching network of pressure, electricity, and dense resins. A tree can build a new limb by drawing carbon from the air and stripping minerals from the body of its dead neighbor. The distinction between feeding and demolition is difficult to make.

Much of the plateau is occupied by long production groves. They follow old geometric divisions, though the divisions have sagged and wandered over time. One grove grows sheets: broad leaves extruded between paired trunks, flattened by rollers, toughened in hot air, then cut loose along a line of weakness. Another produces hollow stems of identical diameter. They emerge slowly from swollen rootstocks and advance into racks made of older stems. Elsewhere, rows of low shrubs manufacture a fine granular substance that accumulates beneath them in neatly bounded heaps.

No one carries these things away. The grove itself consumes them. Sheets become shutters and floors. Pipes become conduits. The granules are fed into roots that need to stiffen, or poured into cavities where some failing structure must be supported before it settles.

You can find evidence of an original plan in the spacing of certain trunks, in repeating junctions, in the shape of doorways that no longer lead anywhere useful. A few passage openings retain a shallow recess at shoulder height, as though a hand was once meant to be placed there. Those recesses are polished, but not by hands. Small feeder roots slide through them now.

The place is immensely old. Its oldest walls have lost every sharp edge. Staircases have worn into slopes; slopes have flattened into warm shelves. Entire towers survive as broad mounds beneath newer growth. Where an old load-bearing trunk has slowly collapsed, the surrounding plants have grown over it so many times that the depression resembles a shallow valley.

Yet nothing stays still for long.

Across a district perhaps two miles wide, growth proceeds in sudden crowded waves. A lane open at the beginning of a shift may close before the heat in it changes. Roots swell sideways into usable space. A roof loosens, sinks, and is supported by a fresh crop of columns pushing upward from the floor. Several buildings can exchange their contents through their common walls, leaving the passages between them in entirely different places.

The changes usually make little noise. There is a soft rasp as a sheet of bark slides past another. A seed casing gives a dry click. Somewhere behind a wall, a long vibration stops, and its absence becomes noticeable only after several minutes.

Then a whole block begins to move.

It happens first in the paving. Closely spaced ridges rise underfoot, each no thicker than a finger. They progress down the lane like a slow disturbance through heavy cloth. Ahead, the walls lean away from each other. A canopy folds inward. Tens of thousands of leaves turn edge-on at once, uncovering a red glow deep below the district.

Hot dust lifts in a dense sheet.

By the time it settles, the lane has been swallowed. A new avenue runs across its former course, bounded by young walls whose surfaces are still soft and faintly luminous. Somewhere beneath them, the old corridor remains intact for a while, packed with displaced air and the objects it could not digest quickly enough.

No animals live here. There are no calls overhead, no movement startled by your footsteps. The small forms that pass between stems are plant structures: mobile buds drawn along conductive fibers, pollen packets migrating toward heat, knots of root tips searching for an unoccupied seam. They do not look for you. They respond to shade, pressure, mineral content, and warmth. Standing still too long makes you part of several local calculations.

There are safer places to pause. At the eastern rim, a broad apron of old bark has become so polished and chemically exhausted that little will grow on it. From there you can look across the upper surface of the plateau. It extends farther than expected: factory groves, low domes, ventilation fields, districts sunk almost level with the pavement. Nothing quite reaches the proportions of a skyline. The tallest structures are continually being dismantled for material; the oldest have gradually spread their weight outward.

In the darkness, brief red openings mark where work is happening. One opens nearby, another far away, then six together along a buried conduit. Above a distant grove, the canopy rises visibly, held up by the pressure of hot air. It stays raised for a moment before lowering over a changed arrangement of roofs.

The apron is warm beneath your feet. Behind you, a new root touches its edge, withdraws, and touches it again.
