The hollow lies below the root collar of a tree, though from inside it is difficult to believe in the tree. There is no trunk overhead, no crown, no obvious direction in which the ground must be. There is only a pale green chamber, warm enough for a bare arm, with light arriving evenly through its walls.

You could cover the entrance with your palm.

Inside, you can walk for some time before reaching the opposite side.

The floor is alive. So are the walls and the high, loosely vaulted ceiling. Nothing has been excavated: the hollow opened when two layers of growing tissue pulled apart, and it is still opening. Along its edges, new surfaces unfold with the clean precision of leaves emerging from a bud. There are sharp green ridges, thin as knife backs, and translucent flanges that have never been touched by wind or weather. Older portions are cream-colored and faintly ribbed. They yield a little underfoot, then recover after you have passed.

What first looks like bare ground resolves, when you kneel, into tightly fitted leaves. They have no free edges. Each grows into the next, making a continuous skin, and their veins sink into the floor rather than rising above it. Beneath that skin, something moves slowly toward the far wall: a thickening, a transfer of sap, perhaps the beginning of a root. It passes under your hand with the firmness of a muscle.

Yet the chamber feels almost empty.

There are no thickets or hanging tendrils. No objects interrupt the long green distances. A single stalk, pale and jointed, grows near the center, but even this is so slender that you can lose sight of it against the wall. Everything visible is plant, and almost nothing occupies the air.

The air is loud.

At first the noise seems to come from outside: a river behind the walls, heavy rain on a roof, branches beating against one another. Then you notice that it has no single source. A dry seam in the ceiling releases with a crack that rolls across the chamber. The floor tightens and gives out a deep, wooden boom. Somewhere beyond the living skin, water is drawn through a passage narrower than a hair, making a high, sustained whistle.

The sounds are too large for their causes. A bead of moisture detaching from the ceiling lands with the report of a stone dropped into a well. You feel it in your chest, although the bead itself is barely enough to wet one fingertip.

There are intervals when all these noises coincide. The hollow fills with a vast rushing, and the thin stalk at its center trembles without bending. Then the processes separate again, and you can hear the small dry clicks of leaf cells locking into place along a newly formed ridge.

Most of the time, there is little water to be seen. The warmth is dry, with a scent like the inside of a snapped bean. Moisture gathers only in certain places: along a narrow seam at the base of the wall, beneath a folded lip of ceiling, in the print left by your hand. These patches do not spread. The tissue around them drinks inward, leaving a dark border that gradually pales.

Once during each growth cycle, the hollow sweats.

Pearls appear on the walls. They grow until their weight draws the wall slightly inward, then break free and fall sideways toward the central stalk. For several minutes, rain crosses the chamber almost horizontally. You can stand in it and remain mostly dry; the drops avoid your skin unless you hold still long enough to be mistaken for part of the place.

When a drop does strike you, it is warm. It smells of cut grass and contains a minute, folded leaf.

The stalk catches this rain along its joints. What it cannot catch continues past it, slows, and eventually settles on the far wall. That wall becomes the floor for a while. The chamber does not turn. Your feet simply begin to understand another surface as down. The change comes gently, with a faint pressure behind the ears, and you find yourself standing where, a moment earlier, you had been looking upward.

There is no fixed map of the hollow, though its few features are easy to recognize. The pale stalk. The damp seam. A shallow crease shaped like a closed eye. A place where three fresh ridges meet.

You can walk from the crease to the stalk in twelve steps. Returning may take forty. If you hurry, the distance usually increases. If you stop to examine the floor, you sometimes discover that you have already arrived.

The light remains ordinary through all of this. It reveals color plainly and casts soft, credible shadows. Your hands look like your hands. At the brightest part of the cycle, the chamber resembles a room beside a large window on a mild afternoon.

But there is no window, and the ground above is thick with roots and soil.

The light travels through the plant in the same channels as nourishment. Near the damp seam it has a yellow cast, as if some fraction of sunlight has been kept there for several seasons. In the newly opened tissue it is almost white. Occasionally a bright patch passes beneath the floor, tracing the branching route of a root. Its passage is followed by a wave of warmth and, much later, the muffled sound of leaves moving high overhead.

Night is brief. The walls dim to the green of a leaf held against closed eyelids, and the noises become more distinct. Growth continues. You can hear each ridge extending, each dry membrane lifting away from the tissue behind it. The chamber gains space in these dark intervals. A surface that was within reach at dusk may be several paces away when the light returns.

Only one animal seems to live here.

It is about the length of a grain of barley, with six pale legs and a body transparent enough to show the green fluid inside it. You see it rarely, usually at the edge of a fresh split in the wall. It carries a curved thorn between its front legs. With this it scrapes the tender surface, opening a groove from which a little sap wells.

Its work is slow and deliberate. Scrape, wait, drink. Turn the thorn. Scrape again.

When the groove begins to close, it wedges the thorn into the opening and rests beside it. There are no stored tools, no nests, no heaps of discarded material. Once, you find two thorns laid across one another beneath the folded ceiling, but by the next cycle they have put out roots.

The animal makes almost no sound. In the immense noise of the chamber, this silence gives it a peculiar presence. You can sometimes locate it without seeing it: there is a small place along the wall where the rushing, cracking, and whistling seem to have stopped.

It does not approach you. If you cast a shadow over it, it waits until the shadow has grown past.

Near the entrance, the living tissue is thin enough to reveal a little of the outer world. Not a view exactly, but scattered particulars: the cold outline of a buried stone, a root pressing through soil, a thread of fungus passing across the opening. These seem enormous from within. A particle of earth can remain visible for hours while the tree shifts around it.

Then a tremor comes down from above.

The ceiling bows. A loud green pressure sweeps through the hollow, flattening the central stalk and driving the few loose drops into the seams. New ridges rise from the floor. For a moment, the entrance is plainly visible, small and dark, tucked beneath a curled flap of tissue.

Beyond it, something has stepped close to the tree.

The pressure passes. The stalk lifts again. The floor releases its held breath in a series of booming clicks, and the entrance disappears among folds that were not there before.

Afterward, the hollow smells freshly opened. A new sheet of pale tissue peels from the wall and extends over the empty space, stopping just short of your shoulder. Light enters it slowly, vein by vein. At its far edge, a drop gathers and hangs.

You wait for it to fall.
