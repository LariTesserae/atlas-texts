Behind the lowest inspection hatch in the pumping house is a room scarcely large enough to stand in. The hatch opens beneath a stair, where the wall appears to be solid all the way down. You have to kneel to find its handle. Inside, the floor lies a hand’s breadth below the water table, although there is no earth here and no groundwater: only the maintained level of the reservoir on the other side of the wall.

Everything is made. The floor is cast concrete, the walls are ceramic-lined concrete, the low ceiling is the underside of a machinery bed. Even the porous bank along the back wall began as a strip of filter blocks, manufactured to catch sediment. There is no stone that was not quarried and cut, no soil except what roots and water have assembled from the gradual loss of other things.

The room is mostly empty.

A shallow sheet of water covers its floor. Nothing interrupts it except one broad pedestal, worn almost level with the surface, and a drain slot too narrow to admit a finger. The pedestal once supported something heavy. Its bolts have been ground down to dark, flush circles. There is no longer a useful distinction between its corners and its sides.

Above it, nearly a metre of open air separates the water from the ceiling. In so small a place, that unobstructed space feels considerable. You can see the whole width at once: pale water, pale wall, a band of green at the far edge. No loose equipment, no fallen pipe, no heap of debris. The water has carried away everything it could move.

It has been doing this for a very long time.

The age is clearest in the absence of sharpness. A seam in the floor survives as a shallow valley. Lettering on a ceramic panel has become a few depressions that hold slightly darker water. Where a pipe enters the wall, the original square opening has worn into an oval, and the oval has softened until it resembles the mouth of a small burrow. Nothing here is newly broken. Even the damage has finished becoming part of the room.

Water enters through that opening in a clear, continuous ribbon. It runs down the lining without splashing, spreads across the floor, and leaves through the slot. The inflow and the drain are matched so closely that the level appears fixed. A thin brown line along the wall shows where the surface has been for longer than anyone working upstairs can remember.

The plants occupy almost none of the room, but where they are, they are abundant.

They grow on the filter bank and in two joints above it: soft, overlapping leaves, fine fern fronds, roots so densely braided that the manufactured pores are no longer visible. The bank is only as wide as a shelf. Its greenery hangs over the water in a continuous fringe, lush enough to conceal its own support. Beneath the larger leaves are smaller leaves, and beneath those a close nap of moss. They have no reason to reach farther. The useful moisture, the trapped silt, and the light all meet along this one narrow edge.

Their roots enter old filter cloth pressed into the joints. Some of that cloth still shows—a grey, hairy material indistinguishable at first from dead roots. The first plants probably arrived in water used to rinse the reservoir. Now their fallen parts provide most of what the next leaves need. Very little is wasted. Whatever escapes the root mat becomes a faint stain on the floor before the current takes it away.

A sealed work lamp illuminates everything with plain white light. It is mounted in a recess above the hatch, out of reach of the spray. There is no picturesque gloom. You can see the mineral crust on its cover, the tiny bubbles under a fern stem, the screws holding the cable conduit. The leaves are an ordinary, healthy green. The water is clear enough that its surface sometimes disappears, leaving the submerged floor apparently dry until your hand touches it.

The air is warm and thoroughly wet. Not hot enough to steam; warm enough that a sleeve pressed against the wall comes away damp. Condensation covers the ceiling in a film. At its lowest points, drops remain attached, swelling against the vibration until they release. The surfaces smell of wet cement, living roots, and the faint metallic warmth of machinery.

The sound is enormous.

A pump stands immediately above the room. Its casing is bolted to the slab that forms the ceiling, and its continuous roar passes through the concrete, through the water, through the bones of anyone crouching inside. There is a deeper note from the delivery main behind the wall. Together they fill the little chamber so completely that its own sounds are unavailable. Water falls, leaves tremble, a drop strikes the floor, but none of these can be heard.

Even a shout would scarcely occupy the space. You feel your voice in your throat and hear almost nothing of it.

The vegetation quivers without swaying. Fine roots vibrate under the water. The pedestal, the lamp, the softened wall seams all share the same minute motion. Nothing travels anywhere except the water. The room has movement everywhere and almost no event.

Animal life is limited to a few pale snails tucked beneath the lowest leaves. Often only one is visible, attached to the ceramic just above the waterline. There is little here to support anything larger, and no quiet interval in which a wingbeat or a footfall would matter.

Upstairs, workers keep the reservoir at its marked level. They replace bearings, check gauges, clean screens. Over the years, successive machines have taken the place of earlier machines on the same heavy bed. Their purpose has remained the same, and the neglected room beneath them has settled around that purpose.

Here, the overflow must stay open. The lamp must stay lit. The filter bank must stay wet. Those few conditions account for nearly everything.

Once the hatch is closed, no part of the room requires a witness. The white light continues to fall on the empty centre. Water holds its depth. The dense little margin of green maintains itself against the worn lining, while the pump sends its unbroken weight of sound through the ceiling.
