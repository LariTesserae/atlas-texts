The first thing that looks like stone is not stone.

It is a gray shelf projecting from the hillside, rounded at its edge, its upper surface fretted into shallow cups. Frost has gathered in the cups. Beneath it, where the slope has torn away, there are layers of compressed stems so old and tightly knitted that their individual shapes have almost disappeared. A pale root enters one layer and emerges several yards farther down. The shelf bends slightly under the weight of the frost.

All the ground is like this. The ridge, the basin below, the long descending shoulder that vanishes into cloud: living material weathered into the forms of a mountain.

There may be rock underneath. Nothing here exposes it.

The country is high enough that the clouds sometimes pass through the valleys rather than above them. Beyond the nearest ridge, more ridges stand in ordinary daylight, blue-gray under a white sky. Their crests are broad and worn. There are no dramatic young peaks, no fresh cliffs. The shapes have been rubbed down by centuries of cold wind, by the weight of successive growths, by the slow slipping of one rooted layer across another. From a distance it is an old, settled upland.

Up close, almost nothing holds still.

Across a slope the size of a small town, pale leaves turn over in successive patches. One patch darkens, another shines. Dense mats of ground-cover buckle upward, opening narrow seams; the seams close somewhere else. A depression fills, not with water but with a rising cushion of small jointed shoots. Farther down, a bank loses its support and pours into the valley in a slow green avalanche. By the time it reaches the bottom, the fallen mass has begun to disentangle. Its roots feel for purchase. Its uppermost stems turn toward the light.

The movement is nearly soundless. Everything is soft at the point of contact. Branches slide over waxy leaves; stems fold along prepared joints; roots withdraw through sleeves of wet fibre. There is no snapping wood. Even a large collapse is heard chiefly as a change in the air, a faint pressure against the ears.

You could watch a whole hillside rearrange itself and hear only your own breathing.

The cold is constant without being absolute. In shadow, the vegetation carries a thin glaze of ice. On exposed ridges, leaves are small and thick, crowded so closely that wind cannot get between them. They are dull olive, silver, the purple-brown of an old bruise. Down in the sheltered folds, taller growth rises in loosely spaced columns, each column composed of many plants clasping one another. There are gaps broad enough to see through. The landscape is full, but not uniformly tangled: it has courts of air, long corridors beneath leaning stems, open bowls lined with low, close growth.

Moisture arrives intermittently. A cloud lodges in a valley for an hour and leaves every surface beaded. A brief rain darkens the ridges, then stops. Most of the water disappears immediately into leaf folds or narrow mouths in the ground. What remains collects in small pools held above the valley floor, in the cupped crowns of broad plants. The pools are clear, shallow, and often roofed by a skin of ice.

There are no permanent streams visible from above. Water travels through the country internally.

Where an old terrace has peeled open, you can see the channels: translucent tubes as thick as a wrist, branching among the packed roots. Fluid moves through them in pulses. Some carry clear water. Others carry a cloudy suspension, green or amber, dense enough to leave deposits at every bend. A swelling travels along one tube, pauses at a junction, and divides. Nearby, a cluster of roots tightens around a swollen sac. The sac empties. A second fills.

Once you notice this, the hillsides become difficult to read merely as slopes.

The long ridges collect light. The shaded hollows accumulate and break down fallen tissue. Broad-leaved fields unfold at midday, then retract as the temperature drops, delivering their stored sugars into buried conduits. Mineral grains, lifted from whatever lies beneath, are sorted into pale nodules. Old fibre is softened, drawn apart, and spun through new growth. There are acres devoted to each stage, although no boundary is permanent. A basin that was digesting dead stems yesterday may be lifting a fresh canopy today.

The place has the throughput of an industrial district. It processes whole hillsides of matter, continuously, at a scale too large to follow. But there are no buildings, no machinery standing apart from what it acts upon, no abandoned tools. The pumps are swollen roots. The valves are membranes. The conducting lines lie under the green skin of the ground, where brief electrical discharges coordinate openings and contractions across distances of several miles.

Occasionally a faint blue point appears in a torn seam, even in daylight. It lasts less than a second. Then, far across the valley, a band of vegetation folds flat.

The motion does not spread evenly. It hesitates, backs up, starts again. Two surges meet and produce a raised knot. A dark patch advances uphill against the prevailing movement while fresh silver growth streams downhill around it. Sometimes a whole field remains motionless for hours, its leaves lifted and its channels full. Then it releases all at once. The adjoining ground bulges. Curtains of trailing stems withdraw into the ridge, revealing a hollow that was not there a moment before.

This is where the turbulence lies: in crowded flows, interrupted transfers, sudden changes of use. Nothing needs to move quickly to change everything.

The oldest forms persist through it. A blunt spur remains a blunt spur after the plants composing it have shifted and been replaced. Certain terraces are always terraces. Growth follows their worn contours, and slipping material settles into the same shallow basins. New tissue takes on old shapes almost immediately. Along the windward crest, fresh stems emerge flattened and crooked, fitted to grooves left by generations of predecessors.

Only in a few places does the surface fail to accommodate its own activity. Here, enormous blisters rise from the turf. Their skins are thin enough to show dim moving shadows inside. Some slowly deflate. Others split along a line of tiny leaves, releasing folded shoots that spread across the surrounding ground. The emptied skin lies there for a while, silver and creased, before the lower growth draws it down.

Animals are scarce. A small white moth may sit on the warm lip of a vent. Something narrow sometimes moves beneath a low canopy, leaving a trail of displaced frost. You might wait all afternoon and see nothing else. There is little loose food, little unclaimed shelter. Even the dead leaves are tethered; even a fallen branch is already being reclaimed from within.

The finest view is from a ridge at late afternoon, when the light comes beneath the clouds.

Then the valleys separate into depth. You can see the pale workings under the darker surface growth, the wet gleam of an opened seam, the miles of leaves turning at different angles. Cold air settles in the hollows. The exposed crests whiten. On a far slope, a broad section sinks almost imperceptibly, and another rises beside it, maintaining the familiar outline of the hill.

Nothing lights up for the evening. There are no roads to pick out, no windows, no distant engine note. The daylight simply weakens over the moving country.

Before dark, a small pool near your feet drains through its floor. Its thin lid of ice remains suspended for a moment. Green tips press upward beneath it, lift it whole, and carry it into the thickening leaves.
