The city lies below the level of the plain, in a depression so broad that its far side is usually lost in the dimness. From above, it looks like a break in the earth: a long, shallow mouth edged with worn limestone. The descent is gradual. You can walk down without ever feeling that you have entered a cave, and only much later look up and discover that the sky has narrowed to a pale strip between overlapping shelves of rock.

There is no water in sight. No runnels on the slopes, no wet shine on the stones, no dark margin where a pool has recently vanished. Even the deepest hollows hold loose dust. The air is cool and thoroughly dry; it takes the warmth from your hands without leaving anything on your skin.

The floor has been settling for a very long time.

What might once have been a broken landscape has become an arrangement of low, rounded rises. Fallen blocks have shed their edges. Gravel has worked its way into the spaces between larger stones, and powder has filled the gravel. Here and there, a pale ridge emerges from this soft material like the back of something almost buried. The limestone is full of shells, though most are visible only as faint changes in texture: a curve beneath your thumb, a small fan of parallel lines. Their sea is older than the hollow, older than the buildings, older than anything still moving here.

The buildings stand in loose districts along the sides.

They are made from the same stone as the surrounding slopes, which makes them difficult to recognize at first. A wall looks like a ledge until you notice its corners. A ledge becomes a row of thresholds. Long terraces follow the natural contours, with broad stairs connecting them at intervals. There are courtyards, roofless chambers, covered passages still intact beneath great slabs. Most structures are low. Nothing rises much above the rock that shelters it.

It is a city without a skyline.

Its oldest streets have worn into shallow channels. Doorsteps are smooth, their centers slightly lower than their ends. Some entrances have become oval through the slow loss of their corners. Elsewhere an entire building has subsided into a mound, leaving only a lintel exposed and a cool, black opening beneath it.

No machines remain. There are no pipes humming in the walls, no lamps, no wires crossing the streets. Whatever work made this place is finished. Its passages are kept open now by feet, paws, wings, and the occasional collapse that clears one route while closing another.

Between districts there is considerable emptiness: wide courts of packed mineral dust, bare inclines, stretches of stone pavement with no upright wall left beside them. You can see a hundred doorways across such a space and not see anything occupying the ground between. The city feels spacious, almost sparsely inhabited, until you listen.

The sound is overwhelming.

It comes chiefly from the upper galleries, where birds occupy the long shelves beneath the rock ceiling. Their nests crowd the lintels and the undersides of broken vaults. Pale droppings descend in broad streaks over the walls. Every available projection seems to hold a bird, yet their bodies are small enough that from a distance the colonies resemble roughness in the stone.

They call continuously. Short cries strike the opposite wall and return lengthened. Wingbeats gather into a heavy, rushing flutter. Under these sounds are others: the dry chatter of insects, claws scraping masonry, the dense rustle of bats packed into deeper chambers. A sudden disturbance in one gallery spreads along it, nest by nest, until the whole terrace is shouting.

In the narrow streets, the noise becomes physical. It fills the space around your head. You can feel its lower notes through the soles of your feet when a large flock leaves an enclosed hall. Speech travels badly here—not because the place swallows it, but because it already contains too much sound.

Beyond the busiest galleries, however, the ground remains quiet. A lizard may spend an hour on the same step. A tortoise moves through a court so slowly that you notice the change in its position only after looking away. Beetles cross the dust in deliberate lines, each carrying a grain or a dry fragment considerably larger than itself.

The vegetation gathers where the stone gives it shelter.

It does not form a continuous carpet. There are open expanses with scarcely a stem, followed by a stairwell so full of growth that the steps cannot be seen. Thick-leaved plants fill the mouths of courtyards. Their leaves are matte green, gray at the edges, folded or cupped to expose as little surface as possible. Some grow in dense rosettes close to the ground; others have short woody trunks and crowns that overlap into low, uneven canopies.

From the brighter cracks overhead, roots descend through the city. Some are no thicker than cord. Others have broadened against the walls, occupying the old joints between blocks and pressing them gently apart. Far above, on the plain, there must be shrubs and small trees. Down here, you see their undersides: branching, patient structures that have followed the smallest opportunities through the rock.

The richest growth is beneath the bird colonies.

There the dust is darkened by generations of fallen nest material, feathers, shells, and droppings. Tough vines spread across the low walls. Narrow leaves crowd the rims of buried pots. Small shrubs rise out of abandoned hearths and split the paving into irregular islands. In one sunken court, green branches fill every level from the floor to the surviving roof beams, though the surrounding streets are almost bare.

The leaves seldom move. There is little wind below the plain, and what reaches the plants has spent itself passing through cracks and turns. Their stillness makes the insects easier to see. Grasshoppers sit along leaf edges. Moths rest in the folds. Small birds disappear into the foliage and emerge with something pale in their beaks.

Nothing here grows quickly. A branch may need years to extend across a doorway. A root thickens around a stone until the stone becomes part of its shape. The abundance is cumulative: a great deal of life added in small increments to places that have remained undisturbed.

Light enters unevenly.

Near the descent, it is the ordinary weak light of a shaded valley. Farther in, it comes through fissures in the rock overhead, laying long, diluted patches across roofs and floors. Most of the city exists in reflected light, limestone passing a little brightness from surface to surface until it runs out in the deeper rooms.

At midday, one broad court becomes visible almost in full. Its paving is intact beneath a thin coating of dust. There is a low platform at one end, a row of square sockets along another, and a central stone basin filled not with water but with leaves, shed skins, and the husks of insects. Around its rim are the polished places where animals climb in and out.

As the light shifts, the court disappears by degrees. First the corners lose their shape. Then the sockets become indistinguishable from stains. The platform remains a while longer, pale against the floor, before it too settles into the general dimness.

The largest changes are almost impossible to witness. A ceiling slab lowers by a fraction. A wall opens along a seam already full of roots. Dust accumulates behind a threshold until the doorway is no longer a doorway but an entrance through the top of a room.

Occasionally, far away, a stone falls. Its impact is brief, but the echoes travel through adjoining passages, and birds erupt from several terraces at once. For a minute the city is louder still. Then the flocks return to their shelves, insects resume their work in the debris, and the new stone begins its long residence on the floor.

Toward the lowest district, the buildings are nearly buried. Only their upper courses remain above the pale sediment, making a pattern of low enclosures across the basin. Plants occupy some of these; others are empty except for animal tracks. Beyond them, the depression continues under a broad roof of natural rock.

You cannot see its end.

There is enough light to make out another terrace, another scatter of rounded walls, a dark mass of leaves growing around a broken arch. Beyond that are faint surfaces without clear edges. The sound continues farther than the light: wings, calls, the crowded scrape and rustle of animals living in rooms no one has entered for a very long time.
