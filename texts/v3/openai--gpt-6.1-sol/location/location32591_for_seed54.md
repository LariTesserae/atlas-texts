The city lies high enough that the clouds arrive from below.

They gather against the underside of the plateau, pressing into its fissures, and emerge along the streets as cold vapor. Most days there is no horizon. There are the nearest walls, wet enough to be found by touch, and the next few bridges receding into a darkness slightly paler than the sky. Occasionally the cloud floor lowers. Then, through gaps in the paving, a whole valley opens beneath the city: hanging reservoirs, the pale backs of enormous animals, a scatter of towers reaching upward from ledges that have not yet become part of the mountain.

The mountain is still being made.

Its youngest surfaces are smooth and black, with shallow wrinkles where they cooled. New stone rises beneath older stone at a rate too slow to watch, but fast enough to trouble foundations. Survey marks set into a wall a century ago now describe a gentle arc. A staircase has acquired three additional steps without anyone installing them. Along the eastern edge, a road ends at a warm, soft ridge that will eventually harden into another district.

There has been little time for weather to work here. The cliffs have sharp lips. Fallen blocks retain the shapes of the chambers in which they formed. Nothing has rounded into gravel. Underfoot, the ground is either a single unbroken surface or a mass of angular fragments, each with wet edges bright enough to cut skin.

Water runs everywhere.

It comes through ceilings, beads along cables, gathers beneath the membranes that enclose the transit galleries. It rises in wells whose bottoms are higher than their mouths. Some of it is ordinary meltwater, freezing into transparent shelves wherever it leaves the protection of a conduit. Some is a dense mineral brine that remains liquid in the cold, collecting in black pools with skins of loose ice. The city keeps these waters separate where it can. Their meeting produces a brittle white precipitate that grows inside machinery.

Every doorway has a gutter. Every gutter has a second gutter beneath it for the months when the first freezes solid.

There are no gardens. No moss softens the walls, no roots occupy the cracks, no fallen leaves block the drains. The few green surfaces are copper salts. The branching shapes inside the window panels are stress fractures. From a distance, the broad dark masses filling the lower streets might be mistaken for vegetation, until one contracts and a passage opens through it.

Animals occupy almost all the space the buildings leave available.

They lie folded beneath bridges, cling to the exterior walls, stack themselves in drainage shafts. Some are no larger than a fingernail: translucent plates with a muscular rim, adhering to stone in overlapping ranks. They turn the walls into thick, restless scales. Others are the size of rooms. These lodge between buildings with their bodies shaped by the available gap, pressing a soft flank against one façade and anchoring a row of blunt feet to the other.

The largest are visible only in parts. A ridged back crosses a square. A feeding veil hangs through six levels of an abandoned lift shaft. Far below the upper city, something bears a service road across its shell. The road has expansion joints for when it breathes.

Nothing moves quickly.

A wall animal may take an afternoon to lift one edge of its body. A herd crossing a causeway can close the route for nine days. The small plated creatures advance in slow collective folds, the rear ranks climbing over those ahead until the entire colony has shifted a hand’s width. Even their alarm responses are prolonged: a tightening, a deepening color, the gradual withdrawal of delicate organs into armored sleeves.

Yet the city is loud enough to make conversation difficult.

The animals call through stone.

A low note enters the feet before it becomes audible. It travels through a district, sets water trembling in cups, and emerges from the roofs as a metallic roar. Smaller creatures answer in dense clicking bands. The clicks overlap until they resemble heavy rain on sheet metal, though the actual rain, when it comes, is nearly silent beside them.

There are pressure gates booming shut, ice cracking in the overhead channels, the hollow knocks of transit capsules docking several streets away. Whole buildings hum. Some were designed to; others have acquired the habit through prolonged contact with their inhabitants.

The loudest hours are called the settling.

For several hours, sometimes for most of a day, the deep rock relaxes beneath the plateau. Foundations shift by fractions of a millimeter. The animals sense the change before the instruments do and begin calling to one another. Their notes descend through the city’s structure into the young stone. At the same time, the oldest machinery wakes in the cellars, producing a slow counter-rhythm that can be felt in the teeth.

People stop working during the settling. They secure loose objects, close the inner shutters, and wait in rooms whose floors hang independently of the walls.

The buildings are densely packed but seldom touch. Narrow clearances run between them, filled with water, cable, ice, or living bodies. Bridges pass through bridges. Houses are entered from below as often as from the street. Some façades have no doors at all; their residents arrive through shafts that bring them upward in warmed capsules.

Heat is conserved with a care that makes every inhabited room feel secret. Outside, a bare hand begins to ache almost immediately. Inside, warmth is held close to the body by fields that barely warm the surrounding air. A person sleeping in an alcove can leave frost on the wall beside their face.

The machinery is mostly concealed. What shows is oddly modest: ceramic rings embedded in stone, dark filaments crossing the underside of a bridge, shallow basins full of liquid that never quite touches their edges. Transit capsules move without rails through corridors too narrow for them, compressing and recovering their shape. Workers carry folded braces that unfold into load-bearing structures larger than the buildings they support.

At the youngest edge of the city, engineers plant foundation seeds in the cooling rock.

For years these remain no more than dull studs surrounded by condensation. Then a stud opens. Fine ribs extend downward and sideways through the stone, measuring, gripping, negotiating space with the mineral growth. Above ground, a room begins as a low curve that catches snow. Its walls thicken through successive winters. The first residents may move in before it has acquired an upper floor.

The animals arrive earlier.

They gather around new foundations to drink the seepage and feed on the chemical gradients between fresh rock and freezing water. Many carry colonies of smaller organisms in their throats and undersides. Others attach directly to the city’s power filaments. Their mouths close around insulated cable without piercing it; somewhere inside the mouth, energy passes across a boundary the builders understand only well enough to regulate.

Old neighborhoods have learned which species can be tolerated.

The broad, blind ones distribute weight and damp dangerous vibrations. The narrow jointed ones obstruct valves. A certain pale animal, flat as a discarded glove, must never be removed from a doorway in winter: beneath it, the threshold remains flexible. Children are taught to recognize these distinctions by texture, since light is scarce and most eyes adapt poorly to the deeper streets.

The city uses little illumination. Lamps attract swarms that cover them until they fail. Most public markings are audible or tactile. A handrail changes its pattern before a junction. A safe bridge gives three clear tones under a footstep. At an unsafe one, the far anchorage answers late.

There are places where a little light persists: inspection lamps inside sealed tanks, the soft red indicator beneath a pressure door, occasional sparks traveling along the backs of feeding animals. From a high balcony these appear scattered through the darkness at many depths. Some drift so slowly that they can be mistaken for lights in distant windows.

Above the roofs, the unfinished plateau extends for miles. Its ridges carry crowded colonies, relay towers, and reservoirs covered by thick flexible lids. Beyond these, the bare stone is slick with condensation. Survey ropes disappear into fog. At their ends stand instruments listening to the mountain form.

A walker out there hears the city long after it has vanished: its gates, its continuous choral pressure, the slow exchange of calls between animals lodged in streets and animals sheltering in the cliffs.

Farther on, even those sounds weaken. Water ticks beneath a skin of ice. Fresh rock gives off a little heat. In the darkness, something immense shifts enough to let a warmer current pass beneath its body.

By morning, the current has cut a shallow channel. By the following winter, small armored animals will be lining it. Eventually a surveyor will follow the channel back toward the city, looking for a place to lay a pipe.
