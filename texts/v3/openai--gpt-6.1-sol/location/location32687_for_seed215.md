The first thing you notice is how far apart the buildings are.

From the edge of the station terrace, the city looks like a few misplaced structures in an immense upland: a tower on a dark shelf of rock, a low white building beside a lake, several rows of windows almost lost beneath the next ridge. There are whole weather systems between them. Rain hangs over one district while another lies in clear afternoon light. Farther away, a bank of cloud rests on the plateau, concealing everything except the upper floors of a residential cluster.

Only after you have watched for a while do you begin to see the connections. A line in the ground is a covered street. The pale seam crossing a hillside is a freight route. What seemed to be an isolated cliff has doors along its base, and the dark stripe beneath the lake is the roof of a concourse.

The city is mostly below the surface. Above it, there is room.

The upland extends beyond the horizon in broad, unfinished terraces. It has few sharp summits. Its heights are long swells, flat-topped rises, shallow basins separated by ridges so wide that crossing one feels less like climbing than travelling into a different season. Snow remains on the northern slopes, though lower down the air is just warm enough for rain. The wind smells of cold water and freshly broken stone.

Much of the stone is freshly broken. Some of it has never been exposed before.

This land is still being raised. Deep beneath the inhabited levels, pressure lifts immense sections of crust at a rate small enough to live with and fast enough to measure in a human lifetime. The city’s foundations accommodate it. Buildings settle on adjustable footings; transport tunnels lengthen at joints; lake outlets are moved when their gradients change. Survey marks on the terraces record elevations that are already obsolete.

The engineering that steadies the ground is almost entirely hidden. Occasionally there is a service shaft with a warm rim, or a fenced circle where frost refuses to form. In some districts, the rock rests under stresses that ordinary stone could not sustain. Instruments maintain those conditions continuously. People walking overhead encounter nothing more extraordinary than a level pavement.

Beyond the managed ground, the plateau is raw. Water has not yet cut deep valleys into it. Rain gathers in slight depressions, spreads into sheets, and finds temporary routes between blocks of stone. Streams divide and reunite across slopes that have no settled drainage. A channel used all summer may be abandoned after a winter uplift. There are pools so new that their edges are still clean mineral, without mud or a stain of algae.

Older hollows are softer. Fine silt lies beneath clear water. Moss grows in narrow bands at the flood line, and low plants occupy sheltered cracks, their leaves small, dark, and close to the ground. In places, reddish sedges make a thin fringe around a lake. Elsewhere there are only scattered cushions of vegetation, each with a skirt of grit deposited by the wind.

From a distance the plateau seems nearly bare. Close up, every protected surface has something living on it.

Beetles work through the moss. Pale larvae curl beneath stones. Tiny crustaceans graze the undersides of submerged ledges, and fish move through water too shallow to hide them. The lakes are connected not only by streams but by seepage through porous rock; small animals pass along these submerged routes and appear in basins that seem isolated.

Birds are everywhere, though seldom gathered where you first look. Shorebirds stand in the wet gravel, almost indistinguishable from it until they run. Dark diving birds leave circles on the lakes. On the lee side of a distant ridge, thousands of pale wings lift at once, turn, and settle. The flock is so far away that none of its noise reaches the terrace.

Larger animals travel the open ground in loose groups. They are thick-coated grazers with broad feet suited to wet gravel, following the patches of sedge and the low green growth around seepages. Their paths curve through the city without much interruption. A row of residential windows may face a migration corridor; a public stair may descend beside a place where animals rest out of the wind. Predators follow at a distance, using the shallow folds of the terrain. Sometimes the only sign of a hunt is a sudden movement across a slope, then a gathering of scavenging birds an hour later.

There is little sound to accompany all this activity.

Near the station, rain ticks on a railing. Water slips under a slab with a faint, hollow gurgle. A bird gives a single call from the roof and waits so long before calling again that you forget it is there. Vehicles move beneath the ground. Ventilation exhaust is dispersed across large areas rather than forced through loud outlets. Even the construction sites are quiet from a distance: machinery braces itself against the rock and works slowly, without impacts.

The quiet makes the scale difficult to judge. A building across the basin looks close enough to reach in a few minutes, but the walk takes most of the afternoon. Along the way, its blank wall resolves into hundreds of windows. The low ledge beneath it becomes a cliff. What looked like a dark patch of gravel becomes a herd.

The city’s exposed architecture is plain and substantial. Pale ceramic cladding, dark glass, sheltered entrances, broad roofs that release snow into designated hollows. Buildings have few ornamental projections to catch the weather. Their doors open into warm, dry rooms where coats drip onto heated grates and the windows look out at distances that still feel excessive after years of living here.

Some districts are joined by enclosed passages. Others rely on transport that arrives beneath small shelters set far apart on the open ground. A shelter may serve three towers, a research station, and a settlement hidden inside a ridge. Waiting there, you can see no road and hear no approaching vehicle. Then a door opens in the floor-level wall, spilling ordinary interior light onto the wet paving.

New districts are prepared before anyone moves into them. Beneath an empty slope, rooms are excavated, water systems tested, foundations balanced. The surface may remain untouched except for a handful of survey posts. Eventually windows appear in a rock face. A lift pavilion is uncovered. Footpaths begin to show between the entrances and the nearest shore.

The lakes change through the day. In the morning their margins are filmed with ice; by midday the ice breaks into transparent plates and drifts toward the outlets. Afternoon rain raises the water over the moss. At dusk, cold air descends from the higher terraces and mist collects first in the excavations, then along the streams, until the lower city disappears.

Above the mist, the windows come on.

They do not form a continuous glow. Each district makes its own small constellation, separated from the next by kilometres of dark ground. Birds still pass between them. The last grazers descend toward sheltered basins. Underfoot, pumps adjust the pressure in foundations, trains cross the plateau, and instruments register another minute change in the height of the land.

By morning, water will have found a new way down one of the slopes. Someone will mark it on a map. Somewhere farther out, beyond the occupied districts, a shallow pool will overflow for the first time and begin a stream.
