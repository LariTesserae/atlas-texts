The first impression is of a plain, though it stands higher than most worlds’ mountains.

It is so broad that its far edge disappears into the curvature of the planet. Under an ordinary white sun, the ground is pale brown, with patches of iron-red and a faint violet cast where the rock has broken recently. There are no summits nearby. Everything that once rose above this surface has been worn down: ranges, volcanic crowns, the walls of immense craters. Their roots remain as bands of different stone, exposed side by side, smooth enough in places to resemble grain in a wooden tabletop.

The horizon looks level until you walk toward it. Then another horizon appears below it.

The plain is a shelf. Beyond its edge lies clear air and, very far beneath, another shelf, darker and broader, with its own distant escarpments. Lower still are dim continental surfaces that cannot be distinguished from shadow. The drop is too large to judge. A pebble pushed over the rim remains visible for a surprisingly long time, flashing in the sunlight, before it becomes too small to follow. You never hear it land.

Up here, the air is thin but breathable if you move slowly. The sun warms the front of a coat while the cold reaches through its back. In the shade of a waist-high stone, the ground stays chilled all day. There is no snow, no frost, no dampness under rocks. The few clouds visible are below the plain, flattened into long white scraps against some lower layer of the atmosphere. They never reach this height.

At first the country seems perfectly still.

Then a dark patch several kilometers away slides sideways.

It is a sheet of dust, scarcely thicker than paper, moving across the rock. It passes over shallow grooves without settling into them. Along its leading edge, individual grains leap and race ahead, returning in brief, intricate spirals. Another sheet approaches from the opposite direction. Where they meet, the dust rises into a low wall, folds over itself, and collapses into a moving star of narrow arms.

There is almost no sound.

Most of the plain’s weather takes place within a meter of the ground. The upper air moves slowly; close to the stone, currents turn, split, reverse, and accelerate with violent suddenness. A loose strap may hang still against your leg while grit streams past your boots fast enough to polish their buckles. In some places the dust travels uphill. In others it stops along a line so exact that one side of a stone is scoured bare and the other remains powdered.

The lines change position. This is what makes the plain dangerous.

A shallow depression can become a center of converging currents in the time it takes to cross it. Dust that has lain undisturbed for decades lifts in a single motion. A hundred square kilometers of ground darken, then brighten as the lifted material rolls away. Far off, a column forms, clear at its center and brown around its circumference. It leans, divides, and comes apart before reaching any great height.

The stone itself participates, more slowly.

Much of the surface is made of overlapping mineral plates. They are immense, thin in proportion to their width, and worn smooth at their margins. The oldest have rounded edges and surfaces stippled by impacts too numerous to distinguish. Their movement is usually imperceptible. But occasionally one shifts a hand’s breadth, exposing a black seam that runs beyond sight. A tremor passes through the soles of your feet. The seam closes somewhere behind you and opens farther ahead.

There are no fresh mountains to be built here. The plates have been sliding against one another for so long that every ridge they produce is already shaped to be worn away. Low wrinkles rise, shed their burden of grit, and subside. The country changes continually within a narrow range of forms, as though all the extravagant possibilities were exhausted long ago.

Life occupies the exceptions.

In sheltered cracks grow small gray cushions, widely separated. They look like fragments of dirty felt pressed into the rock. Each is a colony of short, stiff stems with translucent tips. During the brief hours when sunlight enters a crack at the right angle, the tips become faintly green. The colonies gather traces of vapor from the air and draw mineral salts from beneath them. A mature patch may be smaller than a hand and older than the nearest building.

Their roots bind the loose edges of a fissure. Around the largest colonies, dust collects in narrow crescents. Tiny black bodies sometimes move there—six-legged scavengers, flattened almost to the thickness of their own shadows. They spend most of their lives beneath the felted stems. When a current changes, they emerge together and hurry to another side of the colony, carrying white eggs underneath their bodies.

Larger animals are rarer.

Once or twice in a day’s travel, you might see a low shape crossing an exposed stretch of ground: a pale, long-bodied creature moving on four widely spaced feet. It walks only when the surface air is quiet. At the first tremor it lowers itself into a groove, fitting its flexible ribs against the stone. Its skin is the color of old mineral dust, except for dark lids that close sideways over its eyes. Tracks last until the next passing current, sometimes seconds, sometimes years.

The buildings are easier to miss than the animals.

They stand days apart, usually at the intersection of several plate boundaries. Most are squat shelters made from a dull ceramic that has weathered less than the surrounding rock. Their corners are rounded. Their doors face away from the prevailing ground currents, although the currents have changed so often that this precaution is now unreliable.

Inside, the rooms are empty. There are shallow sleeping recesses, a work surface, hooks worn bright by use. No visible machinery. No screens. A bowl-shaped hollow in one wall sometimes contains a little dust, and sometimes contains nothing at all, even when every other surface is coated.

Below each shelter is an anchor.

You cannot see it directly, but you can find its influence. Set a small stone on the floor and it takes slightly longer than it should to come to rest. Drop it outside and it falls normally. Close to certain walls, a hanging thread inclines toward the building rather than toward the ground.

The anchors reach into the deep structure of the plateau. They keep the largest plates from separating altogether. Whatever made them could alter the local direction of weight and distribute a load through volumes of rock rather than through pillars or foundations. It did not make the surface stable. Perhaps that was never the intention. The shelters protect only a few square meters each, leaving the rest of the continent to shift.

Some anchors have failed. Around those sites, the plain is lifted into shallow, enormous blisters. Broken buildings ride their crowns. Through openings in the stone you can look down into spaces that have no visible bottom, with other mineral sheets suspended across them, their undersides lit by sunlight entering from distant cracks.

At evening, the light becomes warmer without the air becoming warmer. Long shadows reveal the smallest relief: a ridge no higher than a thumbnail casts a dark line across several steps of ground. The gray plant colonies shine at their tips. Dust moves in copper-colored ribbons.

This is also when the largest disturbances begin. Heat leaves the exposed plates unevenly. Boundaries tighten. Whole fields of stone shiver, and the near-ground currents race outward from them. Across the plain, disturbances meet and interfere. Dust rises in separate patches a hundred kilometers apart, then connects in a web of moving fronts.

From a shelter doorway, the landscape can seem to be boiling.

Yet the sound remains slight: an occasional dry tick from the ceramic wall, a few grains brushing the threshold, the soft scrape of a strap dragged across the floor. Beyond that, immense motion proceeds in silence.

By morning, a familiar route may be gone. The shelter’s step may open onto a seam instead of solid ground. A distant red band may have disappeared beneath a pale plate, and a colony you passed yesterday may now stand alone on a small island of stone.

The sun rises over a level horizon. The air is clear. You study the ground before taking the first step.
