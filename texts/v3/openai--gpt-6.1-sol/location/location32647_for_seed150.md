The place occupies a fold in the mountain, small enough that a person standing on its upper rim could count the larger roofs. There are perhaps thirty, although it is difficult to decide where one ends and another begins. They overlap, lean against each other, send supporting roots through one another’s walls. Beneath them lie lower roofs, older chambers, passageways that have narrowed to the width of a shoulder. The whole settlement is no larger than a hamlet. It has the crowdedness of a city.

Nothing in it is cut.

The roofs are made of living stems, bent inward by their own weight and held together where they have grafted. Thick evergreen leaves lie over them in close, downward-pointing ranks. At the edges, the leaves grow smaller and harder, almost scales; their pale undersides show where the wind has lifted them. Moss fills every seam. The walls are bundled trunks, swollen roots, and the interlocking branches of trees that never grow much taller than a person. They have spent their height on thickness instead.

From above, the settlement resembles a dark green knot lodged in a gray cleft. From within, it is a succession of close rooms.

There is little sky between the roofs. What light enters has passed through cloud, then through needles, then through a hanging fringe of wet lichen. It reaches the lanes as a dull green illumination in which water is easier to see than stone. Droplets shine along the edges of leaves. A gutter will briefly brighten from end to end when a pale opening travels across the clouds, though the sun itself never appears.

The cold is steady. It lies in the walls and comes through the floor into the soles of the feet. On the upper side of the settlement, where the mountain shadow lasts longest, ice remains inside curled leaves well into the afternoon. Elsewhere it thaws just enough to loosen its grip. Little transparent cuffs slide down twigs and break against the moss without much sound.

The vegetation grows densely here because there is nowhere else for it to go. Above the fold, wind scours the slopes down to gravel and brittle cushions of alpine plants. Below it, the ground falls sharply into cloud. But in this sheltered pocket, water emerges along a band of newly exposed rock, and every available surface is occupied. Roots grip other roots. Ferns rise out of pockets in the roofs. Tiny pale plants live in the damp forks of larger ones, and threadlike shoots run through the moss beneath them. Even the apparent floor is alive: a compressed weave of fine roots under a short, dark nap of leaves.

A footstep does not sink far. It leaves a pale mark that slowly darkens as the leaves turn upright again.

The lanes follow the water. There is a main passage, curving from the high seep toward the lower edge, and several narrower passages that feed into it. Along each runs a shallow channel lined with slick stems. The channels carry no more than a finger’s depth of water, except after rain, when they fill almost silently and the lowest rooms begin to flood. There are thresholds raised above them, bridges formed by broad roots, and little sheltered basins where sediment settles before the water passes on.

The arrangement is remarkably orderly. At junctions, the walls pull back to make space. A broad, low chamber stands near the middle, where five lanes meet. Its roof is held up by several trunks grown together at the crown; beneath it, the floor is almost level. Around the chamber’s edges are deep dry hollows, each large enough for a crouching body. They look recently vacated.

There are no hinges, fastenings, pipes, lamps, or discarded implements. The openings have grown smooth lips of bark. Several have narrowed over time, leaving oval scars where a doorway once was. In one lane, a root crosses at knee height and has begun to join the opposite wall. Anyone returning to this place after a long absence would have to find a different way through.

Most of the plant growth is dark: blue-black needles, green bark, red-brown leaf stalks. New growth is unexpectedly pale. It shows beneath the eaves as a fringe of cream-colored tips, soft and translucent, tucked safely out of the weather. In the coldest weeks these tips remain tightly folded. During a thaw they lengthen, sometimes enough in a single day to alter the outline of a window.

The landscape is young in ways that are easiest to notice at its margins.

Beyond the uppermost roofs, a wall of exposed stone rises in fresh angular steps. Its faces have not yet become rounded. Water runs down bright fracture lines, and flakes occasionally detach, landing on the upper leaves. The plants catch these fragments and hold them. Some lie clean on the surface; others have already acquired a rim of moss. Below them, loose stones suspended in roots make hard knuckles in the soft walls.

At the lower edge, the settlement ends abruptly. A whole shelf has broken away from the mountainside. The hanging roots are fresh at their tips, and several still bleed clear sap that freezes into small cloudy beads. Far beneath them, water falls out of sight. The vegetation has already begun to extend beyond the break: thin roots reaching downward, shoots turning into the empty space, leaves opening over places that do not yet have a floor.

Here the scale of the mountain becomes briefly apparent. A few steps back into the lanes, it disappears again.

There are very few animals. Once in a while a small gray bird works along a roofline, probing beneath the leaves. It moves with the concentration of a creature searching a difficult place for very little food. Its claws make faint dry ticks on the hard stems. In a sheltered basin near the central chamber, dark larvae cling beneath submerged roots. They stir when water from a new thaw arrives, then become still again.

No insects cloud the air. Nothing rustles continuously in the undergrowth. The quiet has room for individual sounds: a drop striking a curled leaf; the soft scrape of ice against bark; a short creak as two load-bearing stems shift.

When rain comes, it is absorbed in stages. First the upper leaves begin to tap. Then the moss darkens and swells, closing gaps that were open a moment before. Water gathers under the eaves, falls into the lanes, and starts to move through the channels. The scent changes from almost nothing to a faint mixture of resin, wet bark, and bruised green tissue. At the lower thresholds, leaves turn edgewise to the current. The settlement drains for hours after the rain has stopped.

At dusk, the cloud descends into the fold. It enters through the upper openings and collects beneath the roofs, making the close passages seem longer. The pale growth tips remain visible after the darker walls have vanished. Water continues to tick somewhere ahead.

By morning, frost has joined the outer leaves into rigid fans. The lanes are still open, sheltered by their low roofs, and a thin current runs beneath a skin of ice. In the central chamber, new shoots lean toward the five entrances. Along the broken lower edge, the roots have grown a little farther into the mist.
