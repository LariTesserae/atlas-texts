The plain lies only a little above the sea, though the sea itself is hidden. You know it is there by the salt on your lips and by the way the water in the hollows rises without rain.

From a distance, the land resembles a dark, crowded town. Thousands of stone blades stand across it, some no higher than a knee, others twice the height of a person. They lean, overlap, split into narrow pairs. Their edges are so fresh that pale seams still show where the rock has opened. Between them, shallow channels carry water the color of tarnished pewter. There are no trees to establish a familiar scale, no grass to soften the distances. What seems to be a nearby ridge may be a line of stones several miles away.

Above everything hangs a low ceiling of cloud. Its light is sufficient to show surfaces, seldom enough to cast a shadow. Wet faces gleam. Dry faces absorb what little brightness reaches them. Farther out, where the plain dips toward the unseen shore, stone and cloud become the same gray.

The rock is young. The plain has risen recently, by the standards of land, exposing a bed of dark mineral layers that seawater is only beginning to dismantle. Some layers part cleanly into slabs. Others swell when wet and push their neighbors aside. You can find places where a stone has cracked within the last few days: the interior is pale, almost blue, with a smell like rain striking a hot hearth, although nothing here is warm.

Water occupies every small uncertainty in the ground. It beads along fractures, sits under loose plates, seeps through gravel without forming a visible stream. A seemingly firm patch gives beneath a foot and fills slowly around it. There is little mud. Most of the ground is chips, scales, and angular grains, moving against one another with a faint gritty sensation.

The plain is loud.

At first the sound seems to come from the cloud: a sustained, uneven roar, deep enough to be felt in the chest. Then, walking among the blades, you hear how divided it is. One cleft whistles. Another produces a hollow knocking. A stone with a circular hole in its side gives a low, breathy note that continues long after the air around it has become still.

Beneath the plain are flooded chambers. Their roofs are thin, and many of the upright stones are open at their bases. As the hidden sea rises, water enters the chambers and drives their air upward through the rock. As it falls, the same passages draw air in. A tidal breath takes hours. There is no visible commotion to match the noise: only a pool deepening by the width of a finger, a dark line climbing a wall, bubbles collecting beneath a ledge.

The sound changes as you cross the basin. In the narrower fields it is crowded with high notes, almost metallic. In the broad hollows it settles into a heavy rushing. Occasionally a deep chamber empties far below, and a single report rolls through the ground. Loose flakes tremble on their shelves. Water shivers in cups of stone. Then the long breathing resumes.

Nothing green grows here. Even the sheltered cracks are bare.

There are films in the water, clear until viewed at an angle, when they show a dull violet sheen. They gather where fresh mineral surfaces meet saltwater. If disturbed, they fold into threads rather than breaking into scum, and over several hours the threads creep back toward the stone. They leave a waxy deposit that makes certain ledges dangerously smooth. On old surfaces the deposit has whitened into thin crusts, marking former water levels.

The animals are scarce enough that their traces are more often encountered than their bodies.

Across some crusts run paired grooves, close together, interrupted wherever the creature lifted itself over a sharp edge. In still pools you may see a pale oval resting beneath a shelf. It has a low, flexible shell and a fringe of short limbs. It feeds on the mineral films, scraping very slowly, with long pauses between movements. When exposed, it withdraws into deeper water without hurry. Its shell is translucent at the rim; the shadow beneath it shows through.

There are larger creatures somewhere in the basin. Their shelters remain on raised islands of rubble: squat rings of fitted stone, each with one narrow entrance facing away from the channels. They are scattered too widely to make a settlement, but near enough that another is usually visible from the first.

Some have collapsed into ordinary heaps. Others are intact, roofed with overlapping plates around a central opening. Inside, the floor is worn smooth. The stones nearest the entrance bear a greasy gray polish at the height of a human ankle.

No tools lie beside them. The slabs have been chosen rather than cut, hauled into place and wedged with smaller fragments. A few still carry tide crusts on faces now turned inward. Whoever builds here works patiently, using the shapes the ground provides.

One shelter stands on a rise scarcely higher than the surrounding plain. Around it the stone blades thin out, giving a view over acres of wet, broken ground. Channels converge below the rise and disappear beneath a flat slab. Beyond them are more blades, another pale-roofed shelter, a long pool too dark to show its depth.

The entrance stone is warm.

Only slightly: enough to notice after the cold water and the chilled surfaces outside. The warmth persists under your palm. Somewhere beneath the shelter, perhaps, a deeper fracture reaches into rock that has not yet cooled. Condensation gathers on the underside of the roof and drops onto the smooth floor, one drop at a time.

From this small rise you can watch the landscape move at its own pace. A submerged notch emerges. A strand of film stretches across a pool. Far away, a blade sheds a sliver from its edge; the fragment falls into the water with a brief pale flash. The channel below the shelter reverses its flow so gradually that you notice only when a floating scale of mineral begins traveling the other way.

All around, the rock continues to breathe. The open distances are full of its sound.
