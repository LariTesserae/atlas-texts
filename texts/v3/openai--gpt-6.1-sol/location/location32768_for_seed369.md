The hollow lies high on the mountain, just below the place where the trees become short. It is small enough to cross in a few minutes, though there is no straight way across it. Bare stone interrupts the ground cover, and the ground cover conceals water. You go around the wet places, then around the places that only look dry, until the opposite rim seems farther away than it ought to be.

Above that rim there is mostly sky. The lower country is hidden by the convex shoulder of the mountain. Occasionally a cloud rises from beyond it, showing first a bright edge and then its whole soft body, already coming apart in the warmth.

The hollow is sheltered from wind. Its air feels warmer than the height should allow, especially near the ground, where broad leaves hold the sun and black soil keeps the previous day’s heat. There is a smell of crushed stems, damp mineral, and something faintly sweet fermenting under roots. The light is ordinary daylight, generous and revealing. Water shines where it is exposed. Shadows have green edges.

Much of the hollow is open.

The plants grow lavishly, but in separate places: a great fern beside a seep; a low tree with a crown wider than its height; a cushion of thick, rounded leaves occupying one shelf of rock and stopping exactly at its edge. Between these growths lie stretches of pale stone, shallow gravel, and dark earth with nothing growing from it. The bare intervals make each plant seem almost solitary. A single fern can have thirty fronds, each long enough to lie across a person’s shoulders, yet stand several paces from the next living stem.

There is no obvious reason for some of the empty ground. It is moist. It is warm. Soil has collected there. Seeds must fall into it.

But the roots do not enter.

Along the edges of these patches, you can sometimes see roots turning back on themselves beneath a thin layer of water. They make pale loops in the mud, as neatly curved as the tips of sleeping fingers.

The stone is old and softly worked. Nothing here has the sharpness of a recent break. Low ribs of gray rock rise through the hollow, their surfaces pitted, their corners rounded, their upper faces polished by weather that arrives gently and keeps arriving. Some ribs are covered with a translucent skin of algae. Others are clean except for an orange stain below a crack.

Water comes from those cracks throughout the day.

It does not pour. It gathers into beads, joins into threads, and travels down the rock until it finds a basin. The basins are usually no larger than a wash bowl. Their bottoms hold fine black sediment and a few pale leaves that have become almost transparent. One basin spills into another through a notch deepened over a very long time. Then the water disappears beneath a mat of vegetation.

There is no stream leaving the hollow.

At the lowest point, where an outlet should be, stands a smooth bank of stone with warm moss on its face. You can hear water behind it.

That is the first sound you notice, and eventually the one you hear beneath everything else: a broad, low rushing within the mountain. It is too loud for the little seeps that show themselves. It sounds like a river passing through a large room underground. Sometimes it swells enough to fill the hollow, making the open air feel enclosed. Sometimes it retreats, and individual drops become audible again.

The plants contribute their own smaller noises. Water falls from the tips of the large leaves onto the stiff leaves below, producing hollow taps. A seed case expands in the sun and releases a slow series of clicks. Now and then a frond, heavily loaded with moisture, slips against its neighbor with a sound like wet cloth being pulled from a table.

Nothing happens quickly enough to explain all the noise.

Even the water seems reluctant to move. A bead can hang from the underside of a fern for several minutes, growing broad and clear. Inside it the whole hollow appears upside down: the open sky at the bottom, the stone ribs above. When it finally falls, the leaf rises a little. That motion passes inward along the stalk, and the plant settles again.

Animals are scarce. There are narrow tracks in the mud near one pool, made by something with three toes. The tracks arrive from beneath a bush and return beneath the same bush. On a sun-warmed slab there may be a small, flat-bodied creature the color of the stone, visible only when it opens its dark mouth to drink from the film of water. It drinks without approaching the pool; moisture gathers along its lower jaw.

Occasionally a bird crosses above the hollow without landing. Its call is brief and startlingly thin against the underground rush.

Most of the leaves remain uneaten. There are no clouds of insects over the pools. Decay nevertheless proceeds. Fallen stems soften into dark ribbons; old leaves lose their green, then their substance, leaving branching veins spread over the mud. Beneath these remains, new roots advance with a slowness that gives the impression they are listening before they choose a direction.

The hollow has one change that is easy to mistake for weather.

In the late afternoon, the empty patches begin to darken. Moisture rises through them without bubbling, until the soil has a level gloss. At the same time, the pools sink slightly and their stone margins emerge. The fern tips turn toward the bare ground. The underground noise becomes deeper.

Then, over the course of an hour or more, the water withdraws again.

No footprint survives this rising. A mark pressed into the bare soil will soften and close, though a mark beneath a plant may remain for days. Loose stones shift a little, usually toward the nearest exposed rib. Leaves that have fallen into the empty patches are found afterward at their edges.

There are no walls, paths, cut stumps, or old hearths. The few stone flakes among the gravel could be broken by frost or by a hand; nothing else gives them a history. A person could shelter beneath the spreading tree, drink from the upper seep, and find dry wood inside a dead branch. But there is little here that invites improvement. The plants already occupy the sheltered places. The water already chooses its basins.

Toward evening, light leaves the hollow unevenly. The western rim holds the sun while the pools below become gray. Warmth continues to come from the soil. The leaves smell stronger. Small droplets appear on the bare stone, even where no crack is visible.

For a while, the underground rushing quiets.

In that interval you can hear the hollow at its actual size: a drop striking mud a few paces away, the slight settling of a frond, water entering a shallow basin beside your foot. Then the deeper sound returns from behind the mossy bank, and continues long after the last bright edge of cloud has passed overhead.
