Below the plateau, the ground opens into another kind of country.

It is not a narrow cave with a passage leading onward. It is a long, low basin under a roof of stone, broad enough that the far side is lost in its own dimness. The floor descends in shallow terraces, rises around heaps of fallen limestone, and descends again. Looking across it, you can distinguish something like hills and dry valleys, but their horizons meet the ceiling rather than the sky. In several places the roof has broken through to the surface. These openings are small in relation to the space beneath them. They admit a diluted gray light that reveals one slope, one face of rock, a pale strip of floor, then leaves the rest obscure.

The stone is sharp. Slabs lie tipped on their edges, with corners that have not yet been rounded away. A fracture running through a wall exposes white limestone beneath the older, smoke-colored surface. There are no deep river channels, no smooth beds of gravel. Most of the floor is a mixture of angular chips, chalky dust, and larger blocks resting where they fell. The basin has the unfinished look of a place opened by a few great breaks rather than patiently hollowed into every detail.

Yet nothing appears precarious. The slabs are seated deeply in their heaps. The walls hold. Even the thin ledges projecting above the floor seem firmly part of the mass behind them. Whatever violence made this space has ended.

Water is present chiefly as a condition of surfaces.

The underside of a particular shelf is dark with condensation. A crack has a wet seam that can be followed for twenty paces before it disappears into dry rock. Beneath one roof opening, rain has left a shallow hollow lined with fine brown sediment. There is usually a little water there, too little to reflect more than a fragment of the opening overhead. Most of the basin is dry enough that a footfall would raise pale dust.

In the lowest ground, however, the dust clumps. Stones lifted there would be cool and damp underneath. Water gathers in narrow pockets between buried slabs, beyond the reach of the light, and seeps through them so slowly that there is no visible current. One pool has a black surface interrupted by the pale tips of submerged limestone. Another is scarcely a pool at all: a film spread over flat rock, with an irregular rim of mineral deposit.

The air is cool without being cold. Its temperature belongs to the depth, not to the hour. Near the openings there may be a faint suggestion of the weather above, but farther in, the same coolness lies against the skin everywhere. There is little wind. Smells persist: wet chalk, dry animal fur, a strong ammoniacal edge, and, in certain hollows, the dense smell of organic matter breaking down.

For a while, the space can seem almost empty.

There are long reaches where nothing interrupts the rock except another rock. The light is too weak to give the eye much purchase, and the few green things are gathered beneath the breaches in the roof. Ferns grow in pockets of black soil behind fallen blocks. A thin moss darkens the shaded side of a rain-washed slope. Near one opening, several pale grasses bend toward the same source of light. Their roots occupy a crack rather than a bed of earth.

Beyond these small patches, there is no vegetation at all.

Then the ceiling resolves.

What looked like a roughness in the stone is fur. The darker seams beneath the vault are occupied by bats, packed close in some places and hanging in scattered clusters in others. They fill fissures too narrow to see into. They cling beneath shelves and along the backs of arches. Entire sections of the roof have a granular texture made by their bodies.

Their numbers are difficult to grasp from below. A patch that seems modest stretches farther into darkness than its visible edge suggests. A single pale face of stone divides two colonies, and then another colony begins beyond it. The large, empty distances are principally distances between places where animals gather.

The sound is correspondingly large.

It is not one continuous roar, though at first it may seem so. There are layers: the fine, crowded squeaking overhead; the dry scrape of claws; wings brushing wings; a sudden leathery flutter that travels through a cluster and subsides. From the openings come the harsher calls of rock pigeons. They occupy shelves nearer daylight, their gray bodies hard to distinguish from the limestone until a head turns or a white wing catches the light.

The pigeons’ calls strike the opposite wall and return with their edges blurred. Bat voices collect under the lower vaults. In some parts of the basin, the echoes overlap so thoroughly that the source cannot be located. A noise directly overhead may seem to come from the far slope. Even a small falling pebble acquires a disproportionate presence.

There is scarcely a silent place, but there are places where the sound thins enough to separate its parts. Behind a massive fallen slab, close to the floor, the ceiling colonies become a distant agitation. There you can hear beetles working through dry debris.

The life below is less immediately visible and no less abundant.

Under the principal roosts, the floor is covered with guano, sometimes loose and powdery, sometimes dark and compacted by moisture. Beetles run through it in numbers. Small pale larvae occupy the damp layers. Mites gather on scraps that the eye first takes for stains. At the edges of these deposits, spiders have stretched low webs between stones, and crickets stand with their long antennae reaching into the air.

Turn over almost any piece of limestone near a roost and its underside is occupied.

Away from the colonies, the animals become scattered again. A centipede follows the protected seam between two slabs. A small mouse keeps to the wall, stopping beneath each projecting stone. Beside a shallow pool, a toad sits in a depression just large enough to hold it. Its skin has nearly the same mottled color as the damp sediment. It need not travel far: insects come to the water.

This is a country fed largely from outside. The bats range above the plateau and return to the same deep shelter. Birds bring seeds, feathers, and fragments of nesting material to the upper ledges. Rain carries a little soil through the openings. What arrives is caught, used, and passed downward. The sparse green patches account for very little of the life here.

There are no paths except those worn by repeated animal passage, no walls except the broken walls of the basin itself. Nothing has been placed to make the terrain easier. A route across the floor would have to accept the stone as it lies: climb a heap, squeeze between blocks, double back from a damp cleft that looked passable at a distance.

The place offers few signs of change. The rain hollows keep their shapes. The colonies occupy their established portions of the roof. The same ledges catch the light, and the same low ground holds its small reserves of water. Animals move continually, but their movement does not alter the character of the basin. It fills an already settled arrangement.

From the center, the nearest roof opening looks surprisingly remote. There is a small patch of ordinary sky, with grass growing around its rim. Below it lie the pale slopes, the damp pockets, the almost bare stone. Farther out, the ceiling disappears into a darkness full of voices.

The scale is in the distance between openings, the length of the shadowed terraces, the time it would take to cross from one wall to the other. The abundance is mostly overhead and underfoot. Between them, cool air fills the open space.
