The hollow is behind the pumping house, down three shallow steps that disappear beneath warm water. From the service road you would not know it was there. A bank of pale spoil hides one side; the other is the blank rear wall of the building, damp to halfway up its brickwork. Between them lies a little basin, scarcely wider than a room, with a sheet of water across its floor and three islands of newly hardened mineral.

Nothing here has had time to become rounded.

The islands have thin, projecting edges, like plaster spilled onto a table and allowed to set. Their upper surfaces are cream-colored, interrupted by rusty fans where a different seep has run over them. Underwater, they turn amber. In places the water has deposited a lip around its own margin, so that a pool is held above the surrounding pool by a wall no thicker than a fingernail. The smaller pools brim but do not quite overflow. Every few minutes a drop gathers on the lowest point of a rim, swells, and falls.

The heat arrives from several directions. There is ordinary afternoon heat, held between the bank and the brick wall. There is the heat of the water, which comes up through a narrow fissure beneath the steps. And there is a deeper warmth in the ground itself: even the dry patches are uncomfortable to kneel on. A hand placed on a loose stone feels warmth on both sides, sunlight above and something conducted upward from below.

The water is clear enough to see the floor, but the floor gives little sense of depth. Pale mud, fresh crust, and reflected sky share almost the same color. Only a bolt lying under the surface makes the shallow water apparent. Its threads are furred with white deposit. Its head remains dark.

There are no insects skating over the pools. No larvae hang beneath the surface, and no bird comes down to drink. The basin has the ingredients of a watering place without becoming one. A few plants survive along its upper edge, where rain briefly dilutes the seep: two clumps of wiry grass, a low reddish plant with thick leaves, and something green growing from a crack in the mortar. They stand far apart. Between them are broad stretches of bare bank, marked by the sharp tread of a maintenance boot that has held its shape for weeks.

The sound is much larger than the place.

Beyond the wall, pumps run continuously. Their lowest note is felt through the soles before it is heard, a heavy vibration that seems to occupy the water and masonry together. Over it comes the loose metallic knocking of a guard panel, then a higher, unwavering whine. Speech has to be raised here. Even so, the hollow seems still. The machinery moves elsewhere; its noise enters without bringing any visible hurry.

A narrow pipe passes through the wall just above the waterline. It is wrapped in split insulation and ends in a downward-facing elbow. From it comes a steady thread of hot discharge, no thicker than a pencil. Where the thread strikes, bubbles form beneath the surface and travel a short distance before vanishing. The stream has built its own small terrace. Successive layers spread from the outlet, each one ending a little farther out than the last.

The pumping house is old; this ground is new. Six months ago, perhaps, there was only a gravel sump here. The pale mineral has since buried most of the gravel, enclosed the lower stair, and begun climbing the foot of a ladder bolted to the wall. Its rungs are still bare. Its two feet have disappeared into smooth white mounds.

There are other signs of maintenance, but few: an overturned bucket on a dry shelf; a length of cable hanging from a hook; a rectangular cover painted blue, its corners orange with rust. Someone has scratched a line into the brickwork and written a date beside it. The water now sits a finger’s width above that line. A newer mark has been made farther up, without a date.

Rain changes the surface more than it changes the place. Drops hammer the shallow water and darken the spoil bank. Cooler runoff arrives down the steps carrying grit from the road, but the fissure keeps feeding warmth from beneath. For an hour or two after a storm, vapor hangs low in the basin. Then it thins, the suspended grit settles, and the hot clear water returns.

In full daylight, everything is plainly visible. The wall casts a straight shadow. The ladder casts a thinner one. Light reflected from the pools trembles faintly on the underside of the pipe, although the water seems motionless when watched directly. Near the outlet, the white crust is still soft enough to take the impression of a fingertip. A few inches away it has become brittle and breaks with a clean little snap.

The place grows by almost imperceptible additions. A rim thickens. A buried stone loses its last exposed corner. Water finds a slightly lower route and abandons another, leaving a pale channel across the amber floor. The noise continues at the same volume through all of this.

At the far end, where the spoil bank nearly touches the building, a narrow strip of shade stays wet all day. Here the newest deposit is translucent rather than white. It lies over a brick fragment and preserves the fragment’s broken shape beneath it. Warm water moves across this surface slowly enough that a floating flake of rust takes several minutes to travel the length of a hand.

Past it, the basin ends in an ordinary drain grille. Only half the grille is visible now. The rest has been sealed into the growing floor, and the remaining slots carry the water away with a low, continuous sucking sound, almost lost beneath the pumps.
