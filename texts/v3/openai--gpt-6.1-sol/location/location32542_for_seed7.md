The place is one level below the desert, shallow enough that a few roots have reached it, deep enough that daylight arrives only as a gray seam at the top of the western wall. It occupies the bottom of a subsidence: a piece of ground that has lowered almost intact, leaving a chamber the size of a small station hall beneath the crust.

The roof has not yet learned to be a roof. Its fractures are clean. Pale stone hangs in squared plates, and the edges of the opening still fit the shapes of the fallen blocks below. Nothing has been rounded by a stream or softened by centuries of dripping. Even the dust seems new. It lies in fine, separate fans beneath the cracks, each fan preserving the angle at which the last grains fell.

It is very hot.

At first the heat seems to come from the earth, but after standing still you can distinguish its direction. The southern wall warms your face. The floor warms the soles of your feet. A narrow trench near the center gives off a heat so concentrated that the air above it bends what little light there is. Elsewhere, the darkness is quite steady.

There was an installation here before the ground sank. Much of it sank with the ground.

Three concrete footings stand at unequal heights. A length of insulated pipe enters one wall and leaves through another, taking a route that no longer makes sense. Near the eastern end is a small metal shed, its door open just far enough to reveal a wheel valve and the corner of a stool. The shed has no roof; that remains overhead, bolted to a beam in the original ground level. Between roof and walls is a dark gap full of hanging roots.

The machinery still works, though there is little to identify what work it is doing. A buried motor turns too slowly to make a recognizable hum. You can feel it through the pipe if you rest your hand there. Every several hours, a valve changes position with a soft click. Warmth moves from one section of the floor to another. Some areas cool enough to gather moisture while others dry.

The vegetation follows these changes.

From the entrance, the chamber looks almost empty: exposed stone, a broad ash-colored floor, three or four heavy masses of growth separated by open space. Closer in, each mass proves astonishingly dense. Leaves overlap in thick, wet layers. Pale stems pass between them, divide, and join again beneath the surface. Roots spread over concrete, then disappear into seams too narrow to admit a finger.

The largest growth surrounds the pipe where its insulation has split. Its leaves are long and blunt-ended, black-green when seen by the entrance light. Their undersides are the color of an old copper pan. They stand nearly upright during the hottest part of the cycle, leaving air between them. As the pipe cools, they lower, one after another, until they form a shallow basin.

Condensation gathers there.

There is never much water at once. A drop grows beneath an exposed joint, stretches, and falls. Another forms on the cool underside of a roof plate. Along the north wall, dampness darkens the rock for a hand’s breadth on either side of a crack, then vanishes before reaching the floor. The open ground is dry enough to powder beneath a shoe.

But the leaf basins keep what they receive. Their rims close by small increments, trapping beads of water in the folds. Beneath them is a damp interior you cannot see into without lifting several layers. The air that escapes smells of warm roots, iron, and cut melon.

These plants need little light. Much of their nourishment seems to come from the seams where mineral-rich vapor escapes the damaged pipes. A few low leaves glow along their veins, faintly enough that you notice them only after the entrance has disappeared behind you. The glow does not illuminate the chamber. It gives you fragments: the edge of a concrete footing, a root crossing a bolt, the shape of a leaf held out over the trench.

Where the vapor is too hot, nothing grows. Around each vent is a bare ring of newly deposited material, yellow near the opening and white farther out. The deposits are brittle, porous, and sharp. They break under almost no pressure. Beside them the vegetation is lavish, pressing against a boundary it cannot cross.

There are no animals. No wings pass through the seam overhead. Nothing scratches inside the shed or moves under the leaves. Water held in the plant cups has no larvae in it. The dust preserves fallen seeds and fragments of insulation without a single track among them.

The quiet makes small events easy to hear. A drop striking metal is distinct from a drop striking stone. A leaf, releasing its stored water, makes a brief soft patter. Occasionally the pipe expands against a bracket with a dull tick. Afterward, there may be a long interval in which the only sound is your clothing shifting as you breathe.

Near the shed, someone has laid narrow ceramic tiles across the floor. They lead to the valve, though a section of the path now ends against a fallen slab. The tiles remain cooler than the surrounding ground. Moisture has collected beneath them, and fine white roots enter at their corners. If a tile is lifted, those roots come with it in a thin, carefully fitted sheet.

There is a bucket beside the door. Its handle has rusted through on one side. Mineral crust fills the bottom, and a single broad leaf grows from it, its stem passing through a hole no larger than a nail head. The leaf has bent toward the wheel valve. It nearly touches the rim.

The chamber changes slowly, but it is not settled. Each cooling cycle leaves a little more salt around the trench. Each warm cycle opens another hairline fracture in the new stone. Dust falls from overhead and covers the exposed roots; the roots rise through it, making low ridges in what had seemed a level floor.

After a long time inside, you begin to notice the different ages of things. The concrete is old and stained. The machinery is older than the green growth around it. The broken edges of the chamber are very young. Some still have the color of stone that has never met air.

Above the western wall, the gray seam gradually brightens, then fades. Very little of that change reaches the leaves. They keep their own time, opening over a cooling pipe while the desert overhead enters the hottest part of its day.

At the edge of the trench, one drop is gathering. It has been gathering since you arrived. Its underside catches the faint green of a leaf vein, and for a while it hangs there, trembling almost imperceptibly in the heat.
