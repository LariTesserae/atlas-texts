The place occupies a notch near the top of a mountain, so high that the lower clouds have their own weather far beneath it. From the outer edge you can sometimes see lightning inside them: a brief white branching, without thunder. There is another mountain across the gulf, but only its last few hundred feet are visible. Its summit appears to float at the same height as the notch, close enough to make the intervening distance difficult to judge.

The notch itself is small. You could walk its length in a few minutes if there were a dry path. A wall of black rock rises at its back, leaning slightly outward; two narrow spurs enclose its sides. Between them, a shelf of pale mineral ground supports a grove, a pool, and a low industrial building with a corrugated roof. Beyond the grove the ground ends abruptly. Water crosses that edge in several thin falls, pulled apart almost immediately by the wind.

The rock has not had time to become rounded. Its fractures are clean, its corners sharp enough to cut a hand. Some faces have the faint metallic sheen of broken glass. Others are coated in yellow-white deposits where hot water has run down them and dried. The whole summit was raised comparatively recently—not in anyone’s lifetime, but recently enough that the drainage still seems undecided. Water collects in blind cracks, fills hollows with no outlets, then discovers a seam and abandons them. A few dead pools lie under the leaves, their rims marked by successive water levels.

Much of the ground is warm. Even where the air moving over the rim is cold, heat comes steadily through the soles of your boots. Near the rear wall, a hand laid on the stone feels the warmth of something that has been in sunlight all afternoon, though that wall receives little direct sun. Fissures release vapor without urgency. It thickens among the plants and beads on the undersides of leaves.

There is almost always water falling somewhere, but very little of it is rain.

It falls from the overhang in large, widely spaced drops. It runs down the stems of the grove’s tallest plants. It gathers on exposed pipes, on the corrugated roof, on the old cable that crosses between the side walls, and drips from all of them at once. The air is not foggy enough to conceal things. In clear daylight you can see each thread of water and the wet shine on individual stones. Yet nothing left outdoors stays dry. A folded coat becomes heavy by noon. Paper softens at its edges. Metal kept in a sheltered cupboard acquires rust on the side facing the back wall.

The vegetation grows most thickly over a mound between the building and the pool. There are glossy shrubs with long, downward-pointing leaves; pale ferns that root directly into cracks; a few squat trees whose trunks divide almost at ground level. Their branches have grown together so tightly that the grove has an interior. From outside it looks no larger than a cluster of roadside trees. Underneath, it feels like a succession of low rooms, each floored with damp litter and bounded by stems, roots, and hanging foliage.

The leaves nearest the ground are enormous. Some have been split by falling mineral grit, but the tears have browned and healed along their margins. Moss fills the cups where branches fork. Fine roots descend from those cups into the air and sometimes reach the ground. Other roots disappear into warm fissures and come out several feet away, thicker and reddish, as though briefly cooked.

There are flowers, but not many bright ones. Most are green or cream-colored, and open close to the trunks. Their smell is strongest beside the pool: sweet, damp, faintly fermented. You have to look for insects. Once in a while a small dark fly lifts from a flower and settles again. There are no birds in the grove, no continuous scraping or calling from hidden animals. The plants occupy nearly every available surface without the busy traffic that ought to accompany them.

A few broad-shelled snails live near the warm channels. They leave clean tracks through the soft mineral film. On some mornings a single pale lizard lies on the concrete apron outside the building, its body aligned with a buried pipe. It stays there long enough that a visitor can mistake it for a scrap of insulation.

The building is a pump house. It has two windows, both cloudy from within, and a door swollen at the bottom. Its foundations are drilled straight into the summit rock. Behind it, three heavy pipes enter the mountain; in front, another pipe passes beneath the grove and disappears over the cliff.

The installation is small, but its machinery is not quiet.

A motor runs continuously inside, giving out a hard, low roar. At the rear wall a pressure valve adds a high hiss. The sound fills the notch, caught between the rock faces, and makes the space seem more crowded than it is. Close to the building, speech has to be delivered into someone’s ear. Beneath the trees the leaves soften the higher noises, leaving a deep vibration that can be felt through a palm resting on a trunk.

Every twenty minutes or so, a mechanism inside the pump house strikes once with a heavy metallic knock. Then the roar changes pitch, very slightly. Water trembles in the pool. A few drops fall from leaves that had been holding them.

Nothing much follows.

The motor turns, the pipes hold their pressure, and the grove continues to drip. The noise suggests haste, but there is none. The machinery maintains a slow circulation through the mountain’s hot interior. Water drawn from the shelf goes down through one bore and returns warm through another, bringing dissolved minerals with it. A pressure vessel takes something useful from the cycle before the surplus is sent over the edge. No one living here now remembers whether the grove preceded the pump house or grew because of it.

There are other marks of work, nearly hidden. A square concrete pad lies under the ferns, with four cut bolts at its corners. Porcelain insulators remain fixed to a rock face above head height. Beside the pool is a short flight of iron steps, descending only into water. Their lower treads are white with deposits; the top one has been polished by occasional boots.

Someone still visits. A narrow passage through the vegetation is kept open, and the grease on the door hinges is fresh. Tools hang inside the pump house in carefully spaced outlines. Outside, a battered enamel mug sits upside down on a railing. It has been there long enough for a fern to grow through its handle, but not long enough for anyone to remove either mug or fern.

At midday the light is ordinary and strong. The roof shines. Leaves throw distinct shadows onto other leaves. Sunlight reaches the pool in a broken rectangle and reveals a bottom crowded with pale stones. Small bubbles rise from between them, each following almost the same route as the one before. The water is clear until disturbed; then a milky sediment lifts and takes hours to settle.

The height becomes apparent only in certain places. At the end of the concrete apron, the railing stops against empty air. Through a gap in the grove, a cloud passes below rather than above. A loose piece of corrugated metal near the cliff has been tied down with three separate lengths of wire. The trees lean inward, away from the rim, though the air among their trunks is usually still.

Toward evening the distant summit loses its detail. The notch remains warm after the sun has left it. Condensation gathers more quickly, and the falls at the edge thicken a little. The motor can be heard from every corner.

Under the grove, a new root has reached a length of pipe and begun to turn around it. A crack in the rear wall holds a narrow wedge of fresh moss. On the iron steps, below the waterline, another thin layer of white mineral is forming over yesterday’s layer. None of these changes is visible while you watch. There is only the water moving through the leaves, the vibration in the ground, and the occasional heavy knock from behind the closed door.
