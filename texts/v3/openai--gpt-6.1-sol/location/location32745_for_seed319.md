The city occupies a high shelf of pale stone, several kilometers wide, with no clear boundary between its streets and the surrounding country. Roads continue beyond the last buildings as shallow grooves in the rock. Drainage walls become terrace edges; terrace edges become natural ledges. From above, the place would look less like a concentration of buildings than a system laid across a basin: long roofs, circular reservoirs, stairways, yards, and broad stretches of exposed ground, all fitted to the same slow descent toward the southern escarpment.

There is water almost everywhere, although very little of it looks like a river.

It lies in films on the paving. It beads along the undersides of handrails. It gathers behind retaining walls and comes through their faces in dark, horizontal seams. On warm afternoons, when the sun has dried the upper surfaces of the stone, the cracks remain wet. A person sitting on a low wall will eventually find a damp patch through their clothes. The water is not particularly cold. It has spent a long time near the surface, passing through rock that holds the day’s heat.

The city smells of wet limestone, machine oil, and mineral deposits warming in sunlight.

Most of the buildings are low and substantial. Their walls are thick, their windows deeply set, their roofs gently pitched toward central gutters. A few taller structures rise out of the gaps: pump houses, settling towers, an old hotel with seven floors of open balconies. They stand far enough apart that each keeps its whole silhouette. There are streets where you can see three districts at once, across empty sorting yards and shallow reservoirs, without seeing more than a handful of people.

This is not an abandoned place. Curtains move behind windows. Laundry hangs under covered walkways. A bakery opens before daylight beside a valve station, and a bus runs the ridge road every forty minutes. But the city was built around facilities whose size has little relation to the number of people required to operate them. A single worker may cross a yard as large as a neighborhood to inspect a gauge.

Everything has room around it.

And everything is loud.

The first impression is of an immense waterfall concealed behind the buildings. Then, as the ear begins to distinguish layers, the sound separates into heavy water running through masonry channels, turbines below street level, the rough continuous breath of cooling stacks, and the deeper vibration of pumps fixed to bedrock. There is a metallic component that is not quite a ringing: large plates trembling against their bolts, continuously, year after year.

The noise does not arrive in events. Nothing needs to happen for it to become deafening. It is the ordinary condition of the afternoon.

A door closes without an audible click. Someone pushes a cart over broken paving, but the wheels make no sound you can pick out. Two people meeting in the street stand close enough to touch sleeves. They speak directly into each other’s ears, or point, or use the small economical gestures everyone here learns as a child. Inside shops, counters have writing slates. Houses have little padded rooms at their centers, with double doors and no windows, where families go to talk.

Outdoors, the city moves in a strange visual quiet. A bus passes. A sheet lifts from a balcony. Water slides down a flight of steps. All these small motions occur inside the same vast, nearly unchanging roar.

The industrial works draw water from beneath the shelf. The supply rises through faults under considerable pressure, and the oldest machinery was built simply to keep it from lifting the street foundations. Later generations found uses for what had to be moved anyway. There are mineral-separation halls, washing plants, ceramic kilns, and workshops that manufacture the seals and bearings consumed by the pumps. Broad pipes run beside pedestrian paths, their insulation patched with successive generations of different-colored cloth.

At intervals, the pipes cross a depression on squat concrete piers. Water collects beneath them in pools the color of cloudy glass. Pale sediment surrounds each pool in overlapping rings, so that even a small puddle records years of filling and retreat.

The landscape is heavily worked, but its forms are old and settled. The cliffs do not look recently broken. Their edges have rounded. Exposed steps dip in the middle, worn partly by feet and partly by the thin water that follows the same route down them. Iron brackets have stained entire walls orange. Lime has grown over forgotten fittings, leaving their shapes visible beneath smooth mineral skins.

Repairs rarely erase what came before. A new channel is cut beside an old one. A damaged arch is filled halfway with brick, leaving a smaller passage through it. The city has learned to alter itself without pretending to begin again.

Despite the wetness and warmth, little grows.

The water leaves salts behind wherever it evaporates, and most of the soil is a shallow accumulation of dust between stones. Vegetation survives in particular places: a line of stiff reeds where a reservoir leaks into a patch of clay; gray-green shrubs on the shaded sides of spoil banks; small, fleshy plants in roof gutters that are difficult to reach. There is one district where trees have been planted in raised beds supplied with collected rainwater. Each tree stands in its own square enclosure, and the beds are tended with the seriousness given elsewhere to machinery.

Beyond that district, a single green branch leaning over a wall can be visible from the far end of a street.

Animals are uncommon enough to draw attention. Now and then a small dark bird appears at a settling pool, walks quickly along the rim, and departs. Pale insects live in the wet recesses behind inspection doors. In the quieter service tunnels there are tiny translucent creatures that graze the mineral film; workers find them clinging to gloves and return them to the wall with a fingertip. Nothing large can live comfortably near the main works. Even the dogs kept in some houses sleep in the padded inner rooms.

The strangeness of the place is easiest to notice in those tunnels.

Below a certain depth, water does not always follow the slope. A damp line will climb a wall, pass over the crown of an arch, and descend on the other side. Droplets gather beneath a ceiling and travel toward a drain that was installed above them. The engineers have names for these behaviors and tables predicting where they are likely to occur. Their diagrams are practical, carefully dated, and not entirely consistent with one another.

Older houses have ceiling gutters as well as floor drains.

No one makes much of this. A tenant wipes a wet lampshade, checks the upper gutter for blockage, and goes back to preparing supper. What would be astonishing elsewhere is another maintenance obligation here. Occasionally a building develops a new wet seam, and for several months its residents keep bowls in unexpected places while surveyors determine whether the water’s route is becoming permanent.

Change is measured on that sort of timescale.

A reservoir may take a season to lower by the width of a hand. A mineral crust advances across a yard over decades. The main turbines turn continuously, but their housings are so large and their motion so well contained that nothing about them feels hurried. Even the busiest facility appears to be holding a condition steady rather than producing something.

Toward evening, the western light reaches under the long roofs and illuminates the wet ground. The roofs themselves remain dull: concrete, oxidized metal, coarse waterproof cloth. Below them, every thin puddle acquires a bright edge. Steam from the cooling stacks drifts across the spaces between buildings, briefly hiding a staircase or the bottom half of a tower.

At the southern overlook, the shelf ends in a broad, worn cliff. Hundreds of outlets discharge through its face. Some release only a trickle; others pour steadily from mouths large enough to admit a truck. Far below, their flows meet in a flat valley divided by embankments and salt-white roads. More industrial roofs lie down there, smaller with distance. Beyond them are low ridges, bare except for dark seams of growth along their sheltered sides.

You can stand at the parapet and watch one thread of water descend until you lose it in spray.

The stone under your palms is warm. Your sleeves slowly become damp. Behind you, a worker turns the wheel of a gate valve, moving it a fraction at a time. The city’s immense sound changes slightly, too little to name, and then holds its new pitch.
