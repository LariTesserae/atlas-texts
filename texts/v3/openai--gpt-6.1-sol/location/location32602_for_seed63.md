The hollow lies just below the summit, where a shoulder of black rock has folded over itself and left a chamber open on one side. Outside, the mountain falls away into miles of pale, hot air. Inside, there is scarcely enough sky to see by: a narrow fracture overhead, a dull seam at the entrance, occasionally a star caught between two leaning slabs.

The floor is about the size of a village square. It is difficult to judge this at first because almost none of it is visible.

Animals cover it.

They occupy the walls as well, the undersides of ledges, the cracks between stones, the stones themselves. Some are no larger than a fingernail. Others are long enough to bridge the passage from floor to ceiling, though these are mostly folded away in crevices, their joints overlapping like the ribs of closed fans. In the dark, their bodies resemble loose shale, pale mineral crust, or the rounded nodules of an old lava flow. Then a surface moves against the grain of the rock, and the distinction becomes possible for a moment.

The heat comes from below. The mountain has been cooling for so long that its outer slopes are weathered and crumbling, but something deep in it still shifts. Narrow vents breathe into the hollow. They do not whistle. Their mouths are broad, worn smooth by generations of bodies passing over them, and the gas rises with the soft insistence of warmth from an oven door.

There is no water to collect anywhere. No seepage in the cracks, no dampness beneath a stone. The white deposits around the vents are salts laid down by gas. When disturbed, they lift in little dry clouds and settle quickly in the thin air.

At the entrance, an old step has been cut into the rock.

There are two more beyond it, nearly buried. Farther inside stands a low arrangement of flat slabs, perhaps a shelter once, or a boundary around something that mattered. Its opening is too small for an adult person to enter upright. The roof has sunk at one corner, and the whole structure is thick with animals. They have polished its edges with their passage and packed its joints with shed skins until it looks more grown than built. There are no tools left beside it, no metal, no writing. Only that first step retains the unmistakable shape of an intention.

Nothing here grows toward light.

The smallest inhabitants gather around the hottest cracks. They have six blunt legs and bodies like flattened beads of smoked glass. Their undersides are porous. For hours they remain pressed to the mineral crust, taking something from it that the rock continually renews. Then, with no apparent signal, hundreds detach and move a few inches along the fissure. Their old positions are occupied before they have finished leaving.

A newly fallen stone can remain bare for several minutes. That is a long vacancy here.

Larger animals browse among them. These are soft-sided beneath hard backs, with narrow mouths that comb rather than bite. They pass slowly over the beadlike creatures, lifting some, leaving others. A feeding animal makes almost no sound except a faint brushing, like a sleeve drawn over grit. Its back is usually occupied by smaller passengers, some feeding on the crust lodged in its seams, others waiting to be carried across a gap they could not cross alone.

There is no clean division between the ground and what lives on it. Beneath a foot, what appears to be gravel may separate into a dozen retreating bodies. A ledge may be solid at its center and alive along its entire rim. Dust moves uphill in lines.

Even so, this is a mostly quiet place.

There are no calls, no wingbeats, no rustling leaves. The air is too hot and dry for many of the familiar small noises of life. Feet have pads. Shells meet without rattling. Predators enter a cluster of prey with such gradual pressure that the cluster loosens around them before they strike.

Only close listening reveals the chamber’s activity: the minute scrape of mouthparts; a tick as a cooling flake parts from the wall; the brittle unfolding of a new limb. At long intervals there is a deeper noise beneath the floor, a single muted knock. The animals nearest the vents flatten themselves when it comes.

They feel the mountain before they hear it.

When the underground heat strengthens, the hollow rearranges itself. The change begins in the cracks, where the smallest creatures release their hold and climb. The grazers follow. Bodies that have seemed fixed for hours lift on pale jointed legs. Bands of movement spread outward along the floor, turning aside at the old slab enclosure, climbing its roof, flowing down the opposite wall.

For a few minutes, places that were crowded become bare. The exposed rock is stained in overlapping circles and long smooth tracks. There are hollows worn to the shape of resting animals, hollows inside those hollows, and beneath them older shapes no living occupant quite fits.

The chamber has had this traffic for a very long time.

Its sharpness is mostly gone. Even the broken stone has been rounded by grit, heat, and the passage of bodies. At the edges of the floor, fine debris has accumulated in sloping banks: shell fragments, mineral powder, thin discarded plates, the hard remains of things eaten incompletely. Here the animals are paler and more densely packed. Some live entirely within this material, surfacing only as a brief line of disturbance, a ridge traveling through dry refuse.

The largest creature in the hollow lies beneath such a bank.

You might see one end of it and take it for the base of the wall. Its outer surface is dark, pitted, and incrusted with the same salts as the surrounding rock. Smaller creatures shelter in those pits. One of its limbs extends across the mouth of the old shelter, where generations of them have used it as a bridge.

It feeds infrequently. When it does, a seam opens along its lower edge and several square feet of the bank subside. There is no snap, no lunge. Loose shells slide down. Living animals scatter from the sinking patch, though not all of them escape. Afterward the seam closes, and the surface is occupied again.

Above it, in a pocket beyond the reach of the vent gases, hang clusters of amber-colored capsules. Each is fastened directly to stone. Most are empty, slit neatly down one side. A few contain a tightly folded animal whose limbs are visible through the casing. When one opens, the creature stays beside it until its new surface hardens. Other inhabitants touch it as they pass, testing it with mouths or feet. It survives by holding absolutely still.

There is very little light, but much of the hollow is sensitive to it. A clear night puts a faint strip of stars along the overhead fracture, and the animals directly beneath it withdraw into adjacent darkness. During the day, reflected light sometimes reaches the entrance from a distant white slope. Then the threshold empties. What remains visible is a scatter of cast-off armor and the first cut step, its center worn shallow.

Wind reaches that step, too. It arrives heated by the outer cliffs and carrying powder from the summit. Most of the powder falls near the mouth of the hollow. A few grains travel farther, settling on backs and into joints. Animals pause to clean one another, prying grit loose with delicate hooked feet. The discarded grains join the banks at the wall.

Beyond the entrance, the open mountain looks almost empty. Nothing moves on its exposed ridges. The sun strikes old volcanic stone, and the distance trembles.

A few paces inside, darkness closes around an abundance too dense to take in at once. Every usable surface is occupied; every body supports smaller bodies; every withdrawal makes space that something else is already approaching. The floor slowly migrates beneath its covering of life.

And under all that patient motion, the mountain gives another quiet knock.
