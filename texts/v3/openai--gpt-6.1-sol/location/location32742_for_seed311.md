The city is inside the hinge of a floodgate.

From outside, there is almost nothing to see: a dark iron knuckle below the waterline, a seam bright with new ceramic, a few green filaments bending in the current. The floodgate itself is much older, built to hold back a river that no longer has distinguishable banks. Its upper edge lies somewhere above, in the pale, restless light. Its lower edge is buried in silt. But the hinge has recently been repaired, and the repair contains a place.

Enter through the seam and the water becomes clear.

It remains water. There is no airlock, no dry chamber, no miraculous breathable atmosphere. Water fills the streets, the stairwells, the narrow spaces beneath balconies. It presses into the hollow stems of the few plants that grow here. Yet the murk of the surrounding flood stops at the entrance as sharply as a shadow stops at a window frame. Fine sediment gathers there in a trembling brown curtain. Occasionally the curtain bulges inward, and a row of silver teeth emerges from the ceramic to comb it flat.

Inside, the city occupies a cavity about the size of a small room. It is difficult to determine how many streets it contains.

From any one position, the distances seem ordinary. A passage is narrow enough to cross with an outstretched hand; a square has room for a dozen doorways around it. But turning a corner reveals another block where there should have been the iron wall of the hinge. Looking back, you can still see the entrance. The city does not conceal its size. It simply declines to add its distances together.

Its ground is low and nearly level: a shallow basin of pale gray material, ribbed by drainage channels that have never drained anything. The channels carry denser water instead, cold streams running close to the floor. They are visible by the slight distortion they make in everything seen through them. Where two meet, the pavement blurs, and a dark bead of grit can hang motionless above the intersection for hours.

The ground is new. Corners have not softened. Steps are edged like cut glass. Along the base of the buildings, surplus material has set in thin, sharp flanges, the way batter hardens at the rim of a mold. Nothing here has had time to acquire the comfortable damage of use.

Nevertheless, everything is crowded.

Buildings lean against buildings, though none appears to need support. Conduits fill the lanes overhead in bundled layers: translucent pipes carrying white light, black pipes carrying pulses you can feel in your teeth, braided threads along which little angular shadows travel. Open spaces are occupied by machines whose purpose is not immediately apparent. One square contains a stack of shallow basins, all full, each surface slanting at a different angle. Another is packed with vertical plates so close together that their gaps look painted on.

There are doors, too many doors. Some are scarcely wider than a finger. Some open onto solid walls. Some have hinges on all four sides and can fold inward without choosing an edge. Above them, small panels display shifting arrangements of blue marks. The marks are crisp, brightly lit, and never repeat while you watch.

No one comes through.

There are no fish. No drifting larvae, no little mouths working at the seams. No shells cling to the old iron beyond the entrance. The water has the peculiar cleanliness of a place where animal life has been excluded for so long that the absence has become a physical quality. There is nothing quick to startle, nothing soft hiding beneath a stone.

The plants are sparse and carefully confined. At three or four intersections, translucent green ribbons grow out of square sockets in the pavement. They have no leaves in the ordinary sense, only a branching internal mesh through which bubbles move slowly downward. Each ribbon bends against the local current rather than with it. When a pipe overhead flashes, the plants draw themselves taut, darken briefly, and release a milky trace into the water.

The trace is collected almost at once by a grille.

Elsewhere, a low crust of blue-green growth occupies the underside of a stair. Its margin advances visibly, perhaps the width of a hair each minute, until a passing line of light cuts it back. The severed fringe dissolves. Growth and pruning continue without pause, leaving the same irregular edge in nearly the same place.

The light is bright enough to show all of this plainly. It arrives from above through long clear wells that appear, from below, to end in ordinary daylight. Ripples cross their openings. Clouds dim them. Sometimes the broken silhouette of the floodgate passes overhead, though the gate cannot be moving so often.

White walls throw the light into the lanes. Pale tiles return it from beneath. Even under the densest knots of machinery, you can see the little tool marks on a bracket, the faint green tint at the thick edge of a glass panel, the lone black fleck suspended in a clear conduit.

The water is cool. Metal keeps the cold longer than everything else. The spaces beside the hinge’s outer shell are colder still, and the city has laid narrow heated strips along its walls to prevent crystals forming there. Every so often, one strip fails. A white needle grows from a bolt head, splits into a fan, and reaches a few inches into a street before a maintenance arm unfolds from the pavement and crushes it. The fragments do not melt. They are swept into a pipe with red fittings.

The sound is enormous for so enclosed a place.

Water carries every operation directly into the structure. There is a deep pounding from the gates outside, irregular enough that you keep expecting it to stop. Closer in, pumps chatter behind the walls. The stacked basins produce a continuous bright hiss. Plates strike against unseen stops with the flat report of stones clapped underwater. At intervals, the entire floor gives a low note, and the smaller sounds briefly fall into its rhythm.

Nothing seems idle. Street panels lift and settle. New ceramic extrudes along a roofline in a shining thread. A bridge withdraws into one wall while another pushes out below it. The cold channels change course, turning some passages opaque and clearing others. Construction takes place without scaffolds: a bead appears, stretches into a rod, branches, and becomes a railing before its wet surface has lost its shine.

There are signs of accommodation despite the emptiness. Recesses shaped for hands. Benches with softly rounded seats among all the fresh sharp edges. A cupboard containing seven clear cups, each secured against the current by a fine wire. Beside it, a tiled alcove is warmer than the rest of the city.

At regular intervals, the cupboard opens. Water enters the cups, though they were already full. The wires tighten. The alcove light brightens.

Then the cupboard closes, and the warmth slowly fades.

Near the entrance, the sediment curtain shivers again. Outside, the flood presses brown and cold against the hinge. Inside, a fresh street is forming along the cavity’s lowest wall. Its lamps switch on one at a time, illuminating pale steps, narrow doors, and a green ribbon already stirring in its socket.

The pounding continues. The new doors remain closed.
