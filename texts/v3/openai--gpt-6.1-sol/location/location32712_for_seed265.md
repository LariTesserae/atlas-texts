The plain is warm enough that the water never feels cold, even where it comes out of the ground.

It lies low beneath a darkness without stars or a visible sky. There is no horizon to look toward, but the openness can be felt: wind travels a long way before it reaches you, bringing the smell of brine, hot iron, and mud from places several miles apart. A footstep has room to disappear. Occasionally a sound returns from so far away that you have forgotten making it.

Most of the land has emerged recently. The mud still holds sharp edges. Low ridges run across it like seams left by a casting, their sides steep enough to crumble under a thumb but not yet softened by weather. Between them are shallow basins, narrow channels, and broad shelves covered by an inch or two of water. There are almost no rounded stones. What lies underfoot is angular black mineral, pale clay, fragments of shell, and something grainy that feels like furnace slag.

Water seeps from the ridge tops as readily as from the hollows. It gathers on their upper edges, swells into beads, and trickles down both sides. Some ridges have a groove at the crest where two opposing currents meet. Put your hand there and the water presses upward against your palm.

Rain falls infrequently, but everything is wet. Moisture collects on skin, on tools, on the undersides of stones. Fabric grows heavy before it grows dirty. The air is crowded with droplets too small to fall, and breathing it leaves a warm mineral taste at the back of the throat.

There is very little tall vegetation. Clumps of fleshy reeds occupy the firmer shelves, widely spaced, their leaves thick and almost cylindrical. They have no rustle. When disturbed, they bend slowly and remain bent for several minutes. Around their roots, a thin, greasy mat spreads across the mud. Elsewhere the only growth is a low crust that can be mistaken for silt until it contracts beneath a foot.

Animals fill nearly every part of the plain.

The channels are crowded with soft-bodied swimmers, some no larger than a fingernail, others long enough to bridge two basins. Their backs feel segmented, but their movements are continuous. Flat crabs occupy the undersides of mineral plates. Pale worms rise through pinholes in the mud and hold themselves upright, tasting the air. In the shallows, fish press so closely together that a probing stick encounters a yielding, muscular surface before it finds the bottom.

Almost none of this life calls.

There are no choruses, no insects whining in the warm air. The animals communicate through pressure and contact. A crab tapping one claw against a buried pipe can alter the behavior of creatures half a mile away. Schools of fish change direction when a bank settles. On still stretches of water, a slight depression moves outward from an unseen body, passes through a stand of reeds, and continues across dry ground.

Larger animals walk between the basins. You usually learn of them through displacement: water rising against your ankles, a ridge of mud compressing beside you, warm breath arriving from above shoulder height. They place their feet carefully. Some have broad padded soles; some leave a row of narrow punctures. They seem indifferent to people, though they will wait for a person to step aside rather than push past.

A few are very large. One may stand near a pumping station through an entire shift, shifting its weight once or twice. Workers identify these animals by scars felt on their legs. Nobody has established whether they have heads.

The industrial structures are scattered widely enough that each possesses its own stretch of country. A concrete pump house stands on short pilings in one basin. Farther out, a gantry straddles a channel that has not yet formed. There are valve yards, intake towers, small engine sheds, and platforms with stairs descending below the mud. Pipes connect some of them, although the connections cannot always be followed.

The machinery runs quietly. Engines turn at such low speeds that an exposed shaft appears motionless until you rest a finger against it. Pumps make a deep, intermittent compression felt in the chest. Chains pass over wheels one link at a time. The loudest ordinary sound is water dripping into an empty metal cup.

What the installations produce is uncertain. Their gauges register weight, salinity, elapsed time, and several quantities represented by shallow grooves instead of numbers. A station may draw warm water for months, then begin drawing clay. Another delivers a steady stream of clean steel washers from a pipe that has no upstream opening. These are collected in bins and taken away on low, rubber-wheeled carts.

There is work here, but no bustle. Operators feel the gauges, grease bearings, clear animals from intake screens. They carry tools with thick handles and wear boots wide enough not to cut through the young surface. No one uses a lamp. Lamps brought onto the plain continue to consume fuel or electricity, and their housings become hot, but they do not illuminate anything.

People navigate by textures laid along the ground. A strip of perforated rubber leads to a pump house. A row of ceramic studs marks a safe crossing. At junctions, the studs have different shapes, worn smooth by hands. The routes are dependable for a time. Eventually the plain places them somewhere else.

This happens slowly.

A channel shifts a few inches in a season without disturbing its banks. A ridge lengthens from both ends. Two distant platforms draw nearer until their handrails touch, while the journey between them still takes several hours. Workers stop using a route when the ground becomes too crowded with such discrepancies. They establish another by dragging a weighted chain across the mud, then return after a few weeks to find out where it leads.

The water is less reliable than the land. One shallow pool may be crossed easily at its narrow end and require a boat at its broad end, though the depth everywhere is the same. Nets lowered into certain basins come up dry, filled with shells and fine dust. In others, a hand pushed below the surface encounters another surface facing upward. Beyond that second surface there is warm air, reeds, and sometimes the leg of an animal standing on the other side.

These places are marked with a ring of rough rope. The rope must be replaced often. Small creatures gather along it and eat the fibers.

For all the crowding, the plain feels spacious. There is always another stretch of water beyond the pumping station, another low embankment beyond that, more animals standing patiently in the wet darkness. Its distances are substantial even when they refuse to agree with one another. You can walk all night without climbing more than a few feet.

If you remain still long enough, the nearby life begins to settle around you. Fish resume moving against your boots. Worms emerge beside your footprints. A heavy animal lowers itself into the water with such care that the surface barely rises. Far away, a pump completes its stroke.

The new ground holds. Moisture gathers on the fresh edges. Beneath the mud, something broad turns by a fraction, and the channels begin, almost imperceptibly, to drain in another direction.
