The mountains rise through the water, but few of them reach the air.

Their summits are visible from far away: dark green points beneath a pale, level brightness. Between them lie broad saddles, branching ravines, and slopes so steep that the vegetation seems to hang rather than grow. The whole range stands on a high plateau. Beyond its outer rim, the land falls toward distant lowlands; here, near the clouds, a warm sea fills the valleys.

There is no beach. At the basin’s edges, water meets vertical stone or passes straight into a thicket of floating roots. Where a summit breaks the surface, it does so abruptly—a wet black tooth surrounded by leaves, with perhaps a little moss exposed to the air. Clouds sometimes settle low enough to touch these islands. Rain falls into rain-colored water, and for a while the boundary between the two is difficult to find.

Most of the landscape is below that boundary.

The water is clear, with a faint green-gold tint. Sunlight reaches deep into the upper valleys. It lies across the slopes in broad, slow-moving bands, illuminating submerged groves and the sharp edges of newly broken rock. Beneath an overhang it becomes blue. In the deepest clefts it disappears altogether, though the darkness begins much farther down than expected.

These are young mountains. Their ridges still have corners.

Some rock faces are almost bare, split into long facets by the pressure that lifted them. Others shed thin plates that rest against the slope like fallen leaves. A recent fracture reveals stone the color of bruised plum, untouched by roots or mineral film. Even underwater, there is a freshness to it: an edge that has not yet been softened, a shelf with no sediment, a crack whose two sides still plainly belong together.

Warmth comes up through these cracks. There are no boiling vents or violent plumes, only patches where the water becomes warmer as you pass through it. The vegetation is thickest around them.

It grows in layers.

Close to the stone, there are dense mats of small, round leaves, each leaf standing on a translucent stalk. They cover ledges and creep into fractures, so tightly packed that the rock beneath is visible only where a piece has fallen away. Above them rise bushes with flexible, jointed branches. Their leaves are long and narrow, but not limp; they maintain their own slight curve in the water, like blades of grass held upright by an almost imperceptible breeze.

Higher still are the groves.

Their trunks are pale, swollen at the roots and slender above. Some climb from the valley floor almost to the surface. Others grow sideways from cliffs, bending upward only after they have cleared the shade of the ridge. Their crowns spread into broad shelves of foliage, one above another, with curtains of fine roots hanging between them. Seen from a distance, a grove resembles a green cloud caught in the terrain.

The leaves are darkest on top and silver underneath. When a current moves through, hundreds turn together. A whole hillside briefly pales, then settles back into green.

There is remarkably little current.

The water moves, but on a scale too large and slow to register at first. A strand of root changes its position over several minutes. A fallen leaf ascends through one valley and descends into another. Warm water gathers against a sunlit slope until it begins a gradual journey over a saddle. You can follow the movement by watching small particles of pollen, luminous in the light, each taking its time.

The surface overhead is often smooth enough to reflect the submerged crowns back at themselves. In shallow places, foliage and reflection meet, and the eye loses track of which branches are real. A leaf may seem to continue upward into a second leaf, into a second tree, into another mountain descending from the sky.

Some of the plants hold air.

It gathers beneath their leaves in flat, bright pockets, or fills clear bladders along their stems. The larger bladders are the size of fruit. They lift branches toward the light and give the groves their tiered shape. When one breaks free, it does not hurry upward. It rises with bits of tissue attached, turning slowly, carrying a tiny inverted view of the valley on its shining underside.

Occasionally one catches beneath a rock lip. Air accumulates there over years, making a dry chamber no larger than a cupped hand. Fine white roots enter these pockets and branch through them. Their tips bead with moisture. There are entire thickets growing across the underside of a ledge, their leaves submerged and their roots hanging in trapped air.

Almost nothing swims among them.

Once in a long while, a narrow animal passes between the trunks, transparent except for a dark line along its body. It moves with a few unhurried folds and vanishes against the silver underside of a leaf. Small grazers live on the oldest stems, but they are easy to mistake for buds. Their passage is marked by clean trails through the soft green coating.

The abundance here is mostly growth, not movement. Every available height has its own vegetation; every warm fissure has its crowded margin. Yet a valley can remain visibly unchanged for hours.

It is quiet, too. There is no continuous snapping or clicking from the groves, no busy chorus from hidden creatures. Far above, heavy rain sometimes makes a soft, sustained patter. A stone loosens from a cliff and strikes a lower ledge with one clear knock. The sound travels a surprising distance through the water, then leaves the surrounding stillness intact.

Along the outer escarpment, the sea ends at a wall of pale mineral. Water seeps through it rather than spilling over. On the far side, high above the lowlands, thousands of slow drips darken the cliff. The mineral wall continues to grow, sealing some passages and opening others, holding the basin at nearly the same depth. There is no great outlet, no rushing river to scour the valleys bare.

So the mountains remain sharp, and the groves keep their fallen leaves.

In the sheltered hollows, those leaves collect in deep, loose beds. New stems rise through them. Fine sediment settles on exposed stone. A fresh ridge acquires its first green film, then a fringe of roots, then shade.

From one summit you can see these stages spread across the submerged range: bare purple fractures nearby, densely wooded slopes beyond, distant peaks blurred by miles of warm water. Above them all is the bright surface, close enough to make the whole sea luminous, still far enough away that nothing below it needs to emerge.
