The first thing you notice is how little shade the leaves provide.

They are broad enough: leaves the size of sleeping mats, leaves overlapping in glossy tiers, leaves held horizontally on thick green stalks. But the light gets underneath them. It shines along their lower surfaces and fills the spaces around their stems, so that standing beneath a tree is like standing beneath a green roof in a room with white walls. The air is hot there, too. Moisture gathers on your upper lip. Even the faint breeze feels as though it has passed over a body.

Ahead, the land opens into a low, spacious basin. Groves stand far apart across it, each dense enough to conceal a house, with long stretches of pale ground between them. Narrow watercourses divide the open spaces. Farther off are terraces, a shallow lake, and a ridge no higher than a village wall. Everything is bright enough to examine. Nothing disappears into haze.

The ground is made of fitted pieces.

You can see the joins wherever the green surface has not been laid over them: broad clay panels, each slightly bowed, pressed together with cords of dark fiber. Some bear the heel of a hand or the parallel marks of fingers dragged through wet material. There are no stones beneath them. Where a panel has been lifted, the opening reveals another fitted layer, and below that another, with the seams offset. The entire basin rests on workmanship.

The ridge is built the same way. From a distance it resembles a fresh earthwork; close up, its sharp crest is a row of triangular blocks stitched together through holes near their edges. The blocks are baked red on one face and glazed green on the other. No weather has rounded them. Water beads along the stitches without soaking the cord.

You can follow that water downhill.

It collects in shallow channels impressed into the panels, crosses a walkway through a notch exactly the width of a wrist, then enters a pool bordered with folded leaves. The pool is warm and perfectly clear. Its bottom is a woven tray, visible through the water, with pale roots running between the strips. Small fish occupy the rootwork. There are dozens of them, perhaps hundreds, each no longer than a finger, their bodies marked with a seam from mouth to tail.

They are alive. They turn their eyes toward you and move into the spaces between the roots. Their fins are thin pieces of translucent membrane bound onto tiny ribs. One rests near the surface with its mouth open. Inside, instead of gills, you glimpse a neatly pleated red chamber.

Nothing splashes.

Across the basin, water is everywhere but seldom gathered deeply. It sits in the hollows of leaves, shines beneath the walking surface, runs down the inner faces of walls. The broad paths are dry in their centers and wet along their edges. When you kneel, you can feel warmth coming up through the clay, while the damp binding between the panels is cool enough to soothe your fingers.

There has been no rain recently. There is no sign that rain is needed. Drops swell at the tips of leaves, fall into the same small cups below, and travel away through channels no deeper than a fingernail. Each receiving cup is worn into the shape of a drop, though nothing else here seems worn at all.

The vegetation grows in islands.

One grove is full of tall, hollow trunks wrapped in green cord. Another consists of low bushes with heavy waxy leaves and fruit the color of new bricks. Beds of creeping plants cover entire terraces, rising in folds over the edges and hanging down without reaching the ground. Between these masses are open courts of clay, sometimes wider than the groves themselves. A lone palm stands at the center of one court. Its leaves are plaited so finely that you must touch them to distinguish the individual strips.

Every plant has been assembled, but assembly has not prevented growth. At the tip of a vine, a new leaf is half unfolded. The narrow fibers of its stalk divide, cross, and fasten themselves through one another. It is making its own basketry.

You wait to see the leaf open farther. It does not.

Elsewhere, a flower holds its petals around a dark throat. Pollen has been applied inside it in soft yellow strokes. An insect crawls over the pollen, collecting it on the brushes tied to its legs. The insect’s abdomen is lacquered, its antennae two fine threads. You hear a faint click as it passes from one petal to the next.

That click is unusually loud.

For all the creatures here, the basin is quiet. Birds stand among the fruit clusters. Long-bodied animals lie on the raised margins of the pools. Beetles gather beneath the wet seams of the ground. A broad-backed creature moves slowly through the shallows, carrying a patch of reeds rooted in the woven plates of its shell. There are animals wherever you look closely, but they do not call to one another, and their movements scarcely disturb the leaves.

At intervals you hear the small sounds of fitted things: a reed rubbing against a reed, a claw touching clay, the tightening of a cord. Water passes through the channels with a sound too low to follow unless you place your ear near it.

The buildings stand farther apart than the groves.

They are low, open-sided structures raised on bundles of poles. Their roofs are thick with living foliage, held in place by lattices of split wood. You can see the tool marks on every support: cuts made with a narrow blade, holes bored from both sides, bindings drawn tight with wedges. No nail or hinge is visible. Doors, where there are doors, are screens that can be lifted out of their sockets.

Inside one building are a shallow bowl, a polished stone blade, and a wooden frame holding a half-finished section of ground. The clay has been spread over a mesh of fiber and pressed into place. A groove for water stops halfway across it.

Beside the frame lies a roll of green material. Up close, it resolves into thousands of small leaves joined edge to edge. Some have roots dangling from their undersides. The roll breathes gently, expanding and settling without making a sound.

There are no people at the workbench. There are no people on the paths, though the basin is laid out for walking and the buildings are made to be occupied. Cups stand beside pools. Resting platforms overlook the lake. At the entrance to a grove, a pair of sandals has been placed neatly on a dry panel.

The lake lies almost level with the surrounding courts. Its banks are fresh, square-edged, and faced with overlapping scales of fired clay. Long rafts of plants divide its surface, leaving broad lanes of clear water. Out in the middle, white posts rise from the shallows. Birds perch on them at widely separated intervals.

From the bank you can see the underside of the water.

It has a faint crosshatched texture, as though the lake were a transparent cloth stretched over its basin. Fish pass beneath it. A bird lowers its beak and draws up a strand, holds it for a moment, then lets it settle back among the other strands. The surface closes without a ripple.

Beyond the lake, a staircase climbs the low ridge. Each step is immaculate, with no grit lodged in the corners. At the top stands a pale wall, waist-high on one side and shoulder-high on the other. It encloses nothing. Through its openings you see the continuation of the basin: more scattered groves, more warm pools, more low roofs standing in brilliant air.

The sky, examined from here, is also made.

It is a smooth pale surface held at an immense distance, with faint seams crossing it from horizon to horizon. The light comes through the whole surface evenly. There is no sun to follow, no cooler direction to face. At one seam, a short length of loose fiber hangs down. You cannot judge how long it is.

After a while, you begin to notice what does not happen.

The fruit remains heavy on its branches without ripening or falling. The exposed clay stays clean. The pool margins never soften. Water flows, but no channel widens. The birds shift their feet on the posts, and yet the arrangement of birds is the same whenever you look back.

Nothing is rigid. Leaves yield to your hand. Fish turn and swim. The heat makes you thirsty. You can drink from a cup beside the water, and the water is fresh.

But the cup is full again when you set it down.

You return past the workbench. The unfinished groove still stops halfway across the clay. The rolled leaves are still breathing. Outside, the half-open leaf at the end of the vine holds exactly the same shape, its fine bindings exposed to the light.

The whole basin has the look of something newly completed, with the tools not yet put away. You walk across its clean panels between the widely spaced groves, listening for footsteps other than your own. Beneath you, water travels through the handmade ground. Around you, the leaves are lush and warm, and every small creature has somewhere prepared to rest.
