The hollow is small enough that a person could call across it, though there is seldom anyone at the other side.

It lies just below the summit ridge, cupped between three pale fins of rock. Beyond their tips there is nothing nearby for the eye to rest on: only air, a second range far beneath, and clouds moving through valleys whose floors cannot be seen. The hollow itself is perhaps the size of a hamlet that was never built. One end narrows into a cleft; the other opens onto a drop. Its floor tilts gently toward that opening, so that everything loose—seed husks, flakes of stone, the little bones of birds—eventually works its way toward the sky.

The mountain has risen recently. There are old rocks in it, but they have only lately been brought into this arrangement. Pale seams still meet exactly across fractures, as though the cliffs might be pushed back together. The edges are sharp enough to cut a mitten. Gravel lies in small, sorted spills beneath each wall, with no soil to soften it except where plants have begun their patient trapping of dust.

In full daylight the place is almost painfully bright.

Sunlight strikes the surrounding snowfields and enters the hollow from several directions at once. Even the undersides of stones are faintly illuminated. The white rock contributes its own glare; quartz grains flash when the wind moves a stem across them. Shadows exist, but they are blue and shallow, and a person seeking relief must put their face close to a crack in the wall.

The air remains cold. In shade, water freezes on a fingernail. In sunlight, a dark cushion of moss can become warm enough to steam.

This difference sustains almost everything.

Vegetation occupies little of the hollow’s volume, but wherever it has gained a foothold it is thick. The floor is scattered with islands of green: low, dense cushions separated by bare stone, each one edged with silver hairs. Creeping shrubs spread against the ground rather than upward. Their branches are no thicker than cord, their leaves small and leathery, their trunks older than their size suggests. Between them grow rosettes with crimson undersides, tiny yellow flowers, and moss deep enough to conceal a whole world of moving bodies.

Seen from the ridge, the hollow appears nearly empty. Kneeling down changes that.

Beetles push through the moss like dark boats. Springtails gather along the wet margins of leaves. Pale larvae graze the algae growing beneath translucent flakes of stone. Spiders have fastened short webs between stems so low that frost can touch both the silk and the ground beneath it. In the warmest patch, beside a black boulder, small flies emerge in such numbers that the air trembles an inch above the flowers.

None of this makes much noise.

The flies stay close to the earth, where the wind cannot take them. The beetles have heavy bodies and reduced wings. Even the birds are mostly silent here. They arrive singly or in pairs, settle on the ridge, then drop into the hollow to feed. Their calls are short and used sparingly. Sound carries too well across the exposed slopes; there is no advantage in announcing a place where insects gather.

A flock may be present without looking like a flock. One bird stands on the upper fin, another works beneath a shrub, a third waits at the mouth of the cleft. Minutes pass before the eye discovers them all.

Water comes irregularly, and mostly without rain.

Clouds sometimes rise from below and fill the hollow for half an hour. Their droplets collect on plant hairs, fur, webbing, and the rough faces of the walls. Then the cloud passes on, leaving every surface beaded. The sun returns with such force that exposed moisture vanishes quickly. Under the cushions it lasts longer, held in moss and accumulated leaves.

There is one shallow pool, no broader than a sleeping animal, where meltwater gathers against a slab. Its surface freezes at night. By noon the ice has loosened from the edges, and tiny creatures move beneath it while the remaining sheet turns slowly in the water. Nothing drains visibly from the pool, but the slab below it is stained green, and a line of unusually vigorous plants follows a hidden seam toward the cliff.

The daily thaw is the hollow’s busiest hour.

Flowers open. Insects climb into the light. Birds descend from the ridge. A small, round-eared mammal comes out of the cleft and carries clipped stems back beneath the rocks, laying them on a dry ledge before storing them. Above it, a hunting bird passes without a wingbeat. Every visible animal stops moving. When the shadow has gone, activity resumes in separate places, cautiously at first.

At the same time, frost is loosening the mountain.

A faint tick comes from one wall. Later, a grain falls. Then a thumbnail-sized chip drops onto the gravel, ringing once against another stone. The collapse of a larger piece is rare, but fresh scars show where it happens. Plants advance over surfaces that may disappear beneath them next winter.

Near the cleft stands a shelter made from six slabs and a roof of overlapping turf. It is too low to stand in. The doorway faces away from the drop, and a flat stone can be drawn across it from inside. There is no chimney; smoke would have to find its way through the roof, and the plants above the entrance are browned from occasional use.

Inside are a bed of dry stems, a wooden bowl repaired with a strip of hide, and a digging stick with a polished handle. A few lengths of cord hang from a peg driven into a crack. Whoever comes here travels lightly and does not stay through the deepest cold.

Their other marks are slight. Three stones have been set around the warm boulder to keep feet off the flowering ground. A shallow groove leads condensation from one wall into a covered cup. At the edge of the hollow, a hand-high stack of flat rocks marks the safe descent. Beyond it, the path is only a sequence of scuffs on stone.

Evening does not bring darkness quickly. The sun leaves the hollow, but the opposite snow slopes continue to shine into it. Flowers close in borrowed light. The insects retreat below the leaves. Birds depart, and the small mammal makes one last journey with a mouthful of grass.

Cold settles first in the open spaces between the green cushions. Water beads stiffen. A web becomes visible as a delicate arrangement of white points. Beneath the plants, there is still warmth enough for things to move.

For a while the whole hollow looks motionless. Then the pool’s new skin of ice shifts against the slab, making a small, clear sound. Somewhere in the wall, a crack opens by less than the width of a hair.
