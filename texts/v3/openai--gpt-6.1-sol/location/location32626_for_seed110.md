High inside the mountain, just below the last reach of its summit, there is a hollow scarcely larger than the space beneath a table.

Its entrance is a seam behind a leaning slab. Nothing marks it from outside. A person finding it would have to turn sideways, put one arm through first, and lower their head into the warm air coming from the stone. Then a knee, then the other shoulder. There is room inside to sit, but not to stand.

No light enters. Even at noon, even when the summit outside is bare beneath the sun, the bend in the seam keeps the hollow dark. You learn its shape by touching it, and touching is difficult: almost every surface is wet.

The floor is not quite rock.

It has the firmness of clay packed beneath a shallow pool, though there is no clay in it. Press a thumb down and a slight hollow remains. Press harder and something solid answers from below. The upper layer is mineral matter that has gathered but not finished setting, smooth in some places, grainy in others, full of small rounded elevations that might become stones if the process ever continued. Here it does not. The floor has reached this degree of firmness and keeps it.

Along the walls, ribs project into the room. Some are broad enough to brace a palm against; others are thin partitions with yielding edges. They divide the hollow into smaller recesses, each holding its own quantity of water. The water stands so close to the top of its basin that a fingertip laid on the rim touches it.

There is no gravel, no sand, no accumulation of worn material beneath the walls. Nothing has yet broken away. The ledges have rounded ends, but not because they have been abraded. They formed that way.

In the largest recess, warm water covers the floor to the depth of a knuckle. It has a faint mineral thickness. When a hand is withdrawn, the wetness clings between the fingers before separating. There is no current, and the submerged surface is as clean as the dry one. A small depression near the wall is deeper than the reach of a finger. Its sides narrow gently, making it impossible to determine whether it is a hole or merely a fold.

The air is warm enough that an outer coat becomes uncomfortable within minutes. There is no hot spring boiling underneath, no obvious source of heat. The warmth belongs equally to the water, the walls, the air. Put your cheek against the stone and there is no shock of cold.

Moisture covers the ceiling in closely spaced beads. They hang above you without falling. Many touch one another, separated only by the shallowest creases in their surfaces. A raised hand can break several at once; water runs briefly down the wrist. Left alone, the beads remain suspended.

The chamber is crowded, though almost nothing lives here. Between the mineral ribs are shelves, knobs, membranes of stone, little enclosed pockets that can be entered only with two fingers. There is scarcely a plain surface the size of a palm. The whole mountain seems to have begun forming again within this small space, trying out several shapes at once.

Most of the growth is beyond sight, beneath the floor or inside the walls. You can feel it as a resistance that differs from place to place. One knob is hard at its center and soft around the edge. Another is firm outside but yields slightly under sustained pressure. Neither collapses.

There are a few living things.

A dark, rootless growth occupies a wet shelf near the entrance. Its short filaments lie flat against the mineral, sparse enough that the surface can be felt between them. Farther inside, a second patch grows beneath a ledge. Neither reaches toward the seam.

An eyeless animal lives in the deepest accessible pool. It is narrow and pale, about the length of a fingernail, with tiny limbs held close beneath its body. Sometimes it rests against a submerged rib. Sometimes a touch finds it elsewhere. It eats something too small to distinguish in the water. There is no visible burrow, nest, or trail.

The animal makes no sound.

Most of the hollow is equally quiet. A sleeve brushing a wall is loud here. So is the movement of water displaced by a knee. When those sounds cease, there is a faint pressure in the ears, the sensation of listening inside something tightly closed.

People have been here, although there is little evidence of what they wanted.

Three pegs of dark wood are wedged into cracks above the shallow pool. A cord passes between them, supporting a palm-sized shelf. On it sits a cup made from a seed shell, with a chipped rim and a handle tied on separately. The cup is dry inside. Someone has taken care to keep it that way.

Beside the entrance lies a flat stone worn smooth on one face. It was brought from elsewhere; nothing in the chamber has been worn. A shallow groove crosses its upper edge, perhaps for holding a cord, perhaps for working a strip of fiber. In the soft floor nearby are two heel impressions and the place where a hand was set down.

Those marks remain clear. Water fills the deeper heel print, but has not softened its outline.

The hollow permits disturbance without seeming to develop from it. A displaced pool becomes still. A touched bead gathers itself again on the ceiling. An impression stays where it was made. There is no season in which the floor dries, no colder interval when the mineral hardens, no flood that carries the little cup away.

Outside, the mountain is high enough to have snow in its cracks and thin air over its ridges. Inside this concealed pocket, there is only the close warmth, the wet stone, and the unfinished floor.

If you sit on the brought-in stone, your knees nearly touch the opposite wall. Your shoulders brush two of the ribs. Above your head, the suspended water is within reach.

It is easy to imagine that the chamber will eventually close, or open, or become a spring. But nothing in it gives that impression. The small ledges are firm enough to persist. The pools are full enough. The stone has begun, and here, in the dark, it stays at its beginning.
