The city is below the salt, in a chamber so wide that its far districts look like low hills. There is no sky, but there is distance. Streets diminish toward a pale horizon; towers become stems, then scratches, then disappear into the brightness rising from the floor.

Everything that can be stood on is alive.

The paving is a close-grown mat of ivory roots, pressed flat while young and hardened into a surface like unglazed porcelain. Along the curbs it retains a little give. A loaded cart leaves shallow impressions that lift out again over the course of an afternoon. Buildings rise from the same mat, their foundations continuous with the avenues, their walls made of trunks trained into sheets. Window openings are held apart by permanent grafts. Staircases thicken at the places most often stepped on.

The city has few inhabitants for its size. A broad boulevard may be empty from one end to the other except for a maintenance worker, a slow freight vehicle, and a group of animals moving along the warm seam beside a power conduit. Many buildings stand far apart, surrounded by bare expanses of living pavement. Their empty floors have already grown railings, sockets, and doors. Some of the doors open onto rooms that will not finish forming for another decade.

Here, vacancy is planned as carefully as occupation.

The air is cool and extraordinarily dry. A bare hand laid against a wall feels the chill first, then a faint inward pull as the wall draws warmth through its surface. Nothing sweats. Nothing drips. Even the deepest channels contain no water: they carry powders, dry gas, or bundles of filaments sliding slowly through protective sheaths.

The plants belong to an old, non-aqueous lineage. Their sap is a pale, oily fluid that stiffens in cold weather without freezing. Beneath the bark it travels in narrow insulated vessels; exposed to air, it becomes a brittle amber crust. Broken branches do not bleed for long. They seal with a thin glassy cap, under which the repair proceeds.

Their leaves are mostly below the surface of things.

Peel back a loose paving scale and there are thousands of them, folded into the underside, each no larger than a fingernail, overlapping like the gills of a mushroom. They absorb charged dust from the air passing through the street’s ventilation pores. Larger leaves line the interior walls of factories, where machines comb them for the silver salts that collect along their veins. The visible city is almost leafless, but every wall has a dark, densely pleated interior.

Light comes from shallow growths spread across the ceiling.

They resemble immense flat seedheads, pale green at their edges and white toward their centers. The oldest are several streets across. Their light is steady enough to read by and bright enough to cast shadows, although the overlapping sources soften the edges. A person walking beneath them may have three or four faint shadows traveling beside their feet.

At the beginning of the working period, each seedhead brightens from the center outward. Later, narrow dusky bands move across it. Different districts keep slightly different hours. From a high bridge, one can watch morning advancing over an industrial quarter while the residential terraces beyond it remain in the gray of late evening.

The ceiling itself is being made.

There was once only a narrow crack beneath the salt field. The city’s ancestral roots entered it, laid down ribs, and began lifting the burden overhead. They are still lifting. At the outer limits of the chamber, the roof is low enough to touch. White shoots crowd through fissures, bind loose crystals, and extend joint by joint into the unoccupied dark.

The salt does not come away in great falls. It is taken apart a grain at a time and packed into cells. Much of the city’s stiff, pale tissue is reinforced with it. A piece of old wall, cut and polished, shows branching white crystals held within translucent fibers.

There are no worn mountains or ancient riverbeds here. Relief is an early draft. A ridge begins as a swelling under the pavement. Over several days it develops a crease, then an opening, then a row of blunt shoots. Workers arrive to decide whether it will become a loading platform, a windbreak, or a street that climbs gently toward another district. Survey marks are cut into the young bark. Some are later swallowed; others remain visible high on completed walls.

The machinery is substantial, though it rarely makes much noise.

Factory halls contain rows of broad black wheels turning against belts grown from flexible vine. Shafts pass through rings of living tissue that tighten around them and keep their alignment. The largest engines are sunk into the floor, with only their flywheels showing. They run on the difference between warm air brought down from the salt field and the cold air of deeper chambers. When the temperature difference is small, they slow almost imperceptibly, and the entire district schedules its work around that loss.

A factory in full operation sounds like someone breathing in a distant room.

Its products emerge through slots in the walls: coils of cable with a living insulating skin, transparent panels, lengths of rigid hollow stem, sacks of mineral meal, small identical housings for electrical switches. The machinery trims and sorts what the building grows. Nothing is sent out quite dead. A windowpane will close a scratch if supplied with heat. Cable laid into a new conduit puts down hair-fine roots and anchors itself.

Freight travels on low, six-wheeled platforms. Their motors are electric, their wheels faced with resilient bark. They move slowly because the streets sometimes adjust beneath them. Ahead of each platform, a little rake drags across the paving and reads its tension. A newly rising root can be felt before it breaks the surface.

Between the districts are open tracts called commons, although there is little on them to distinguish one place from another. They stretch for kilometers: smooth gray-green floors, scattered ventilation chimneys, low folds concealing supply trunks. In the quiet, one hears a dry click now and then as a pore opens. A fine stream of dust rises, catches the light, and vanishes.

Animals are plentiful, but most stay near those openings.

Small pale browsers with broad feet scrape the powder from the rims. They have narrow mouths and deep cheek pockets, and spend much of the day sitting motionless while their jaws work. When a freight platform approaches, they move just far enough to let it pass. Some have learned to travel beneath the platforms, feeding on particles shaken from the cargo.

There are also long-bodied creatures that live inside the walls. Their presence is betrayed by a traveling bulge no wider than a wrist. They eat unhealthy tissue, following changes in electrical resistance through the building. A wall with too many of them begins to look restless. Maintenance workers listen against it through flat wooden discs and coax the animals out with warmed mineral paste.

The predators are harder to see. They cling beneath bridges and stair landings, their fur the color of unlit bark. They wait for the browsers to gather, then descend on flexible limbs without a sound. After a kill, the scraps are pushed into designated feeding slits in the pavement. The street closes over them.

There is almost no smell of decay. The air has a faint resinous sharpness, strongest around fresh construction, and sometimes the warm, bitter odor of an engine house.

People wear soft-soled shoes and carry lamps even in the bright districts. A lamp is useful for looking into a pipe, but it also declares that one is awake and working. Buildings respond to that small local warmth. A door may relax at the approach of its occupant. An unfamiliar door remains tight, its seam barely visible.

Certain rules are posted on enamel-colored plaques grown directly into the walls. Do not warm an unfinished arch. Do not leave loose metal in a feeding channel. Report persistent movement beneath sleeping rooms. These are practical warnings. Heat can persuade a young structure to grow toward the wrong place, and the city absorbs unattended objects with very little discrimination.

At the newest district, the streets lose their names.

There are stakes, cables, a few temporary lamps, and broad empty squares marked on the floor. Behind a safety fence, the foundation of a station is rising. It currently resembles a cluster of low white knuckles. Between them lie the first grooves of tracks, still shallow, still flexible. Every few minutes a grain of salt drops from the roof and lands with a tiny audible tick.

Workers pass through the site in pairs. One measures, one adjusts the heat. Farther out, beyond the last lamps, blind branches turn slowly through the cold air, testing the room they have opened. Their tips spread, withdraw, and spread again.

By the following morning, one will have found the position of a future pillar. The surveyors will set a ring around it. Traffic will be diverted, power drawn closer, and a new patch of ceiling will begin to brighten above the work.
