From a distance, the high country looks bare.

It rises above the haze in long ochre shelves, with broad valleys between them and a few immense, widely separated shapes that might be trees. Sunlight falls plainly across it. There is no luminous mist, no permanent twilight: at noon the shadows are short and hard, and the heat makes the far escarpments tremble.

Only when you climb onto the first shelf do you discover that the ground is alive.

The ochre surface is a rind, several feet thick, grown by a plant whose extent nobody has measured. It resembles cork worn smooth by generations of feet. In places it breaks into overlapping scales; elsewhere it has the grain of old timber, though the grain bends around depressions and continues over ridges. Beneath this dry outer layer lies a cool, fibrous tissue. A cut made with a sharp shell slowly beads with clear sap, then closes over several days, leaving a pale seam.

There is no exposed earth. Even the dust is vegetable: powdered rind, fragments of seed casing, the golden meal produced by burrowing animals. What looks like gravel consists of hard, round nodules shed from the undersides of roots. Some can be planted. Most have been rolled so long that they have lost the ability to grow.

The plateau extends beyond any journey people remember completing. Its uplands stand high enough to catch moving weather, but they are hot. Warm air arrives from the lower world and climbs the escarpments all day, carrying the smell of distant flowering basins. It crosses the open shelves in gusts that feel as though they have passed through an oven.

Water comes irregularly. A storm may spend three afternoons gathering over one ridge, then move away without raining. When rain does fall, the ground darkens almost at once. Thousands of pores open in the rind. The water disappears with a faint, pervasive sucking sound, and within an hour the surface is dry enough to sit on.

The dependable water is hidden.

It gathers beneath overhangs, inside folded roots, in deep bowls where the living ground has sagged. Some hollows hold no more than a cupful, replenished by the night’s condensation. Others contain green, warm pools, their surfaces crowded with larvae and little grazing mouths. The animals know which folds will be wet before dawn. By midnight, paths that seemed deserted are full of movement toward them.

The great trees are not numerous. Sometimes a day’s walk separates one from the next.

Each stands on a rise made by its own buried body. Its trunk may be half a mile across at the base, tapering into a crown that appears, from below, like a small range of mountains suspended in the sky. Branches carry broad pads of tough foliage rather than delicate leaves. Their undersides are pale; when the wind lifts them, a whole crown flashes silver.

Between these trees lies an extraordinary openness. You can watch an animal cross the ground for hours. You can see a storm moving on a shelf two days away. The distances feel clean and empty until you listen.

The sound is almost unbearable.

The rind is full of animals. They scrape inside it, chew through its dry layers, drum on thin-walled chambers, and call along passages that carry a voice much farther than open air. Some calls emerge a hundred yards from the creature making them. Others travel through the ground and enter the body through the soles of the feet.

Above this underground noise are the flying swarms. Hard-winged insects rise from fissures in columns broad enough to shade a valley. They fly low in the heat, millions of wing cases rattling against one another. Birds follow them—long-necked birds, birds with heavy cutting bills, little birds that live most of their lives in the wake of larger ones. Higher still, broad-winged scavengers circle on the updrafts.

At the great trees, the noises gather into something almost solid. Animals occupy every fold of bark and every junction of branches. Troops of pale climbers move through the crown. Flower-feeding creatures hang beneath the foliage, each larger than a person, their wings beating slowly enough to count. Shelled grazers spend decades ascending the trunk. Their descendants complete the climb.

A tree heard from several miles away sounds like machinery, though nothing here has ever been powered except by muscle, wind, and growth.

The largest ground animals travel singly. They have low, heavily plated bodies and long, flexible mouths with which they strip the corky surface. Behind them they leave pale tracks, sometimes broad enough to become roads. Smaller creatures follow, feeding on the exposed tissue before it hardens. Predators follow those. A fresh grazing trail can carry a moving community from horizon to horizon.

Older trails settle into shallow valleys. The ground heals, is eaten again, heals again. Over centuries the routes become permanent features, with polished shoulders and rounded beds. Wind works on them too, blowing rind-dust through the same channels until every projection is softened. The landscape has the worn assurance of something that has endured a very long time without becoming still.

People build where these old routes meet.

Their settlements are small and far apart, usually hidden beneath the lifted edge of a root or tucked into the sheltered side of a tree’s basal rise. A dozen dwellings may house everyone who lives within several days’ walk. Walls are woven from shed fibers and coated with resin. Roofs are made from overlapping rind plates, weighted with dense seed nodules.

Nothing is driven deeply into the ground. Pegs are pulled and reset as the substrate grows. A neglected house can slowly tilt, or find its doorway narrowed by a thickening fold.

The tools are bone, shell, horn, and pieces of extremely hard wood taken from dead branches. Cutting living tissue is skilled work. Too shallow, and a water pocket stays sealed; too deep, and the wound drains into inaccessible chambers. Water keepers learn by watching the color of the rind and feeling its tension with the heels of their hands.

At the settlements, conversation takes place close to the ear. People have gestures for ordinary exchanges and percussion signals for distances: a mallet against a hollow root, three strikes on a suspended shell. The surrounding animals sometimes repeat these signals. A summons is trusted only when it arrives with the right pauses.

There are a few abandoned structures on the open shelves. Their roofs have blown away, but their woven walls remain, half absorbed into the surface. You might mistake one for a low bush until you see the neat rectangular opening, still holding the shape of a door. On certain mornings, condensation gathers along its frame.

Toward evening the heat loosens. Shadows from the great trees extend for miles. Flying insects descend, and the ground-dwellers emerge through holes that were invisible in daylight. Their bodies are glossy, jointed, furred, translucent; their numbers seem impossible for so sparse a view.

Then the high country changes its sound.

The daytime rattling gives way to deeper pulses. The living ground contracts as it cools. Air is pushed through old channels, making the valleys moan. Far apart, the great trees release their stored heat, and each crown lifts a small cloud above itself.

Under those clouds, moisture begins to collect.

There is a brief interval before the crowds arrive when you can kneel beneath an overhang and see the first clear droplets swelling on its green underside. Each contains an inverted piece of the plateau: the empty reach of ground, a distant tree, the ordinary sky.

Then a thousand feet begin scratching in the dark, and you move aside.
