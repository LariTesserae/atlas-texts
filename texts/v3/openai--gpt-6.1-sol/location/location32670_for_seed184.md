The district lies twelve meters below the surface, in a hollow no wider than a market square. From above, on the rare days when the water clears, it looks like a dark knot caught in the pale bed of the lagoon. Roofs overlap roofs. Pipes cross the alleys at different heights. A grove of submerged trees grows through the central pumping house, their crowns pressed against the underside of a glass canopy that has not held air for generations.

Everything is flooded. There is no dry chamber at the end of a passage, no sealed door behind which the old town survives intact. Water fills the cupboards, the stairwells, the spaces between the pages of the ledgers. It has entered the smallest parts of the machinery and become necessary there.

The water is warm, though the surface above is cool. Heat rises from the works beneath the district in such weak currents that it can take an hour for a strand of weed to lean from one side of a doorway to the other. Near the bottom, the warmth gathers against the skin. Higher up, a swimmer passes through thin, cooler layers, each distinct enough to feel like crossing a threshold.

There is little open ground. Even the alleys are occupied: by cable bundles, root curtains, collapsed balconies, storage racks whose contents have grown into single mineral masses. To move through the district, one follows clearances rather than streets. A narrow route passes beneath a tram axle, between the branches of a submerged fig, then through the upper half of a window. The lower half has been filled by a dark, glossy cushion of vegetation. The plants keep this route open. Their leaves turn away from the slight current left by passing bodies, and over many years the habit has become a corridor.

Most of the buildings are brick, close-grained and almost black when wet. Their corners have softened. Mortar has worn back into shallow seams occupied by roots as fine as eyelashes. Brass plates remain beside the doors, their letters legible beneath a growth that can be rubbed away with a thumb. Ceramic insulators hang from beams. There are porcelain sinks, enameled switchboards, iron staircases with broad perforated treads. The materials seem chosen for immersion, though much of the town appears to have been built before anyone expected it.

At its center stands the pumping house, a squat, square building expanded so many times that the original walls now survive inside it as partitions. Its glass roof is divided into hundreds of small panes. Some are green with growth; some are opaque with lime; a few admit narrow portions of the distant daylight. Beneath them, the trees rise out of the old intake beds.

They have thick gray trunks and soft translucent leaves. The leaves grow directly from the branches in overlapping fans, without stalks, and collect what little light reaches them. Here and there a leaf has wrapped itself around a warm pipe. Its veins have changed direction to follow the pipe’s curve.

The trees do not flower often. When they do, the flowers open inside closed buds, and the buds become small, cloudy globes. These eventually detach and drift against the canopy. Nothing eats them. After several months they dissolve, leaving a faint yellow stain on the glass.

Beneath the roots, the engines still turn.

Their movement is difficult to notice. A flywheel the height of a doorway takes most of a day to complete a revolution. Piston rods extend by fractions, pause, then withdraw. The bearings are lined with a pale fibrous growth that feeds on heat and sheds a substance like clear oil. Along the pressure housings, colonies of dark algae seal the joints. Where a seal begins to fail, the colony thickens; where the metal cools, it retreats.

The works produce a slow circulation through the district. Water is drawn down through the former market cellars, warmed in the deep galleries, and released through the upper windows of the pumping house. The flow is gentle enough that silt remains on exposed shelves. Yet it reaches everywhere. A loose thread hung from a banister reveals it. So does the uniform direction of the moss inside a locked room.

This circulation keeps the town from filling completely with sediment. Beyond its perimeter, sand has buried the surrounding houses almost to their chimneys. Here, the lanes remain hollow, the doorways recognizable. There is no wall at the boundary. The district ends where the water ceases to move.

Very little can be heard. Sound carries clearly, but few things make it. Once in a while an engine releases a muted click, felt as much in the jaw as heard. A seed globe brushes a pane. Somewhere in the pipework, a trapped bubble changes chambers with a single soft note.

The loudest sound is usually one’s own clothing moving through the water.

There are animals, but they are scarce and easy to mistake for parts of the place. A small white eel lives inside the switchboard of the southern exchange. It emerges at intervals that seem unrelated to hunger or daylight, travels the same length of handrail, and returns. Flat, transparent snails occupy the undersides of ceramic tiles. One large fish, the color of old copper, sometimes rests beneath the tram platform. Its fins move so slowly that its shadow seems more alive than it does.

There are no shoals. Nothing darts across the square.

At the edge of the market stands a public clock with two faces. Both are dimly illuminated from within, though the cables leading to the clock have broken. One face shows the hour at the surface. The other moves with the engines. Its minute hand has crossed half the dial since the last generation of trees took root.

Around its base are small houses with exterior galleries. Curtains hang in some of their windows, thickened by algae until they resemble woven mats. Cups remain on tables. A chair has been pushed back, leaving just enough room for a person to rise, though roots now descend through that space from the ceiling.

No inhabitants are visible. Nevertheless, certain signs persist. A brass handle has a bright patch where a hand would grip it. One stretch of the market rail remains free of growth. In the pumping house, beside a pressure gauge, a pencil rests in a groove worn into the metal. Its wood is swollen, its graphite exposed, but it has not drifted away.

Near the northern boundary is the only open space: a courtyard no larger than a room, floored with green tile. The warm current passes beneath it, and the water there is almost perfectly still. Above, through the tangled galleries and branches, a narrow shaft reaches the surface. Light descends through it as a dim, wavering column.

A bench stands against the courtyard wall. Fernlike plants have filled every gap around it, but its seat remains bare. From there, the whole district is close enough to sense: the heat in the pipes, the crowded rooms, the faint pull toward the intake beds. Once during the afternoon, the slow shadow of the flywheel passes across the tiled floor. By the time it has gone, the light from the shaft has shifted, and a leaf beside the bench has begun to unfold.
