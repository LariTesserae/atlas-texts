High on the mountain, above the last place where snow can hold its shape, there is a pale seam in the rock. From below it looks like an unimportant flaw: a thumb’s width of yellow between two faces of black stone. Even close to it, you might mistake it for a vein of sulfur.

The opening is warm enough to sting a fingertip. Behind it is a landscape with a horizon.

It is not a large landscape. Its entire floor could fit inside a cupped hand, although looking across it takes the eyes a little time. Distance is still being established here. The far edge sometimes recedes when you try to count the plants, then returns while you are looking elsewhere. There is no trick of mirrors. The hollow is learning how much room it has.

Its ground consists of fresh mineral: cream-colored plates, translucent amber blisters, small ridges of metallic gray. Nothing has been weathered. Edges are sharp without being broken. The shallow depressions have not been scooped out; they are simply places where the surface has not yet arrived. In a few of them, you can see through to a darker, earlier state of the mountain.

Heat rises from these unfinished places. It has a dry, close smell, like a hot motor wrapped in crushed herbs.

There is no water in the hollow. A drop introduced from outside would hold together for an instant, brightly foreign, then become a tiny white stone. The plants would leave it alone.

They grow in four widely separated stands.

The nearest is a luxuriant tangle of copper-green leaves, each leaf thin enough for sunlight to pass through and heavy enough to hang straight down. They overlap in curtains. Their margins curl into tight spirals, and their undersides carry dark, precise ribs. At the base, the stems thicken into translucent bulbs through which a golden substance moves very slowly. It is not sap. It appears to be warmth made viscous, gathered during the day and fed upward through the plant when the surrounding stone cools.

Beside this thicket, the ground remains bare for what feels like several paces, though the actual gap is narrower than a fingernail.

The second stand is blue-black and low. Its foliage grows in fans from single points, opening layer after layer until each plant resembles an extravagant fern. Between the leaflets lie filaments finer than hair. When the light shifts, faint sparks travel along them. A spark may take an hour to cross a frond.

The third stand is visible only from certain angles. Seen sideways, it is dense with narrow red leaves. Seen directly, it is mostly empty air, with occasional scarlet edges suspended in it. Its roots extend into a portion of the floor that has not yet acquired depth. The plants have been waiting there for a long time.

At the far end grows one broad-leaved specimen, pale as a peeled almond. It has more foliage than all the others together, yet stands entirely alone. Its canopy fills a shallow recess without touching the walls. Under it, the light is greenish and hot. New leaves emerge already fully veined, then spend months deciding how to unfold.

Nothing moves among them. There are no insects, no burrows, no eggs, no grazing marks. Fallen leaves remain where they land until their inner structures cease to hold, at which point they separate into bright grains. Those grains are drawn through the mineral floor toward living roots. The process leaves no rot and almost no smell.

The mountain’s ordinary sunlight enters through the seam. It is clear daylight, sometimes harsh enough to make the amber ground look white. Dust outside casts brief shadows across the entire place. At noon, a cloud can bring an evening that lasts three seconds.

But sunlight does not account for all the energy here.

Deep beneath the surface, natural processes have taken on the exactness of machinery. Mineral bands rotate inside other mineral bands. Crystalline chambers accumulate pressure, release it, and refill. There are contacts that close when heated, veins that carry current, narrow channels in which powdered stone advances by increments. No hand has arranged any of this. The systems grew with the rock, as casually as rings grow inside a tree.

Occasionally the floor gives a single quiet click.

Much later, a leaf opens.

The relationship is not always clear. Some clicks precede changes by days; others seem to complete changes that have already happened. If you watched long enough, you might begin to distinguish several processes running beneath the vegetation: one separating metals, another making the amber substrate, another feeding the slow electrical pulses into the dark fans. Their working parts are buried. Here and there a transparent patch exposes a shaft turning so gradually that its movement is evident only from the changing position of an inclusion.

The hollow has the heat and faint mineral tang of a foundry, but almost none of its noise. Put your ear near the opening and you hear the wind outside, thin at this altitude. Beneath it lies a lower sound that may be coming through the stone rather than through the air. It stops for long intervals. During those intervals, the silence feels unusually complete.

The ground changes less by wearing away than by coming into existence. A ridge may slowly rise beneath an unfallen leaf. Two plates may meet without a crack ever having separated them. Once in many years, a depression gains a bottom, and the roots suspended above it begin to descend.

Gravity is reliable near the entrance. Farther in, it follows the mineral veins. Loose grains rest against the sides of ridges or gather beneath an overhang. The solitary pale plant drops its oldest leaves upward into the recess above its canopy. They remain there, slowly disassembling, like a second crown.

Nothing in the hollow seems troubled by this.

Outside, the high mountain is exposed to wind, immense temperature swings, and a sky that darkens early. Inside, the heat scarcely varies. The foliage remains abundant in its isolated patches. Between them, the young ground is clean and empty, with room for several more stands should the place eventually make them.

For now, a copper leaf hangs over a bright, unfinished edge. Beneath it, something turns in the mountain. The leaf lowers by an amount too small to see, and the edge becomes a little more real.
