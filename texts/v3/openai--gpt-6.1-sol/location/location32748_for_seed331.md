The hollow is small enough that you can see its far edge through the trees, though getting there takes longer than you expect. There is no straight crossing. Water occupies the lowest ground in broad, shallow sheets, and the places that look dry are sometimes only skins of roots stretched over it. A path follows the firmer margins, wandering around pools, returning almost to itself, then passing through an opening you could not see from the other side.

Nothing rises much above your head except the trees. Even the enclosing ridge is low: a pale stone lip, rounded and interrupted, like the rim of a vessel that has been handled for a very long time. Its rock has no fresh fractures. Where it emerges from the soil, it is smooth without being polished, worn into shallow hollows that hold rainwater, fallen petals, translucent eggs. Some hollows have worn through altogether. Water passes through these openings in clear, finger-thick strands.

The stone is warm. So is the water. Kneeling beside a pool, you feel heat coming from the ground rather than simply from the sun. There is little difference between the warmth of the shallows and the air above them. The distinction is mostly texture: one slips around your hand; the other settles on your skin and remains there.

Light fills the hollow almost continuously.

There is a sun, visible through breaks in the high foliage, but it does not account for all the brightness. Under leaves, beneath overhangs, inside the open mouths of old drains, you can still make out the smallest details. Shadows remain, soft and greenish, but they never become obscure. A beetle under a stone is as legible as one crossing the path. At midday the pools are difficult to look at directly, not because they glare, but because every part of them seems equally illuminated—the surface, the suspended fragments, the mud below.

The trees stand well apart. Each has a low, broad crown and several trunks that have gradually merged, leaving narrow vertical seams. Their leaves are thick and glossy, some larger than your palm, others no bigger than fingernails along the same branch. Between the trees lie stretches of open water, pale mud, and short vegetation dense enough to conceal your feet. Lushness gathers at particular edges: a bank crowded with ferns; a fallen limb furred with orchids; one square of wet ground packed with soft, jointed stalks. A few steps away there may be almost nothing but water and the gently rippled impression of sand.

At first, the place seems sparsely inhabited.

Then your eyes adjust to its movement.

Every leaf has something on it. Clear-bodied animals cling along the veins, their internal organs appearing as tiny yellow knots. The undersides carry flattened creatures that move by loosening one edge of themselves and fastening another. In the pools, fine threads of larvae hang vertically, each with a dark head and a body almost invisible in the bright water. Shoals pass through them without tangling. On the mud, what seemed to be a dusting of seed is a multitude of small shelled animals, each lifting and settling its body by a fraction.

There are larger creatures too, though you notice them later. An animal the color of wet wood lies along a root, its six feet folded so neatly that they resemble old branch scars. A soft-backed grazer stands belly-deep in a pool and feeds on floating leaves. Behind it, dozens of narrow fish work over the places where its feet have disturbed the bottom. Pale birds occupy the scattered trees, sometimes four or five to a branch, facing different directions. When one opens its wings, the feathers separate into long translucent strips before closing into a smooth surface again.

None of this makes much noise.

There is the occasional touch of a foot entering water, the minute tearing sound of a grazer taking a leaf, a single dry click from somewhere inside a trunk. Insects travel without the familiar electrical whine. The birds communicate by changing the colors of their throats, showing brief patches of blue or dull orange beneath their bills. Even the predators are quiet. A stalk among the reeds bends farther than the wind has bent its neighbors, unfolds a set of delicate jaws, and takes a passing insect. Then it resumes the shape of a stalk.

You can stand still for several minutes and see more animals than you could count, yet hear mostly the water passing through stone.

Near the center of the hollow are three built things.

One is a small roof on four slender supports, sheltering a bench. The roof is pale and thin, with rounded corners. No lichen grows on it, although its supports have become enclosed in roots. Water gathers along its underside in orderly rows of droplets. Every so often all the droplets rise at once, pass through the roof, and disappear. The bench beneath stays dry.

The second structure resembles a doorframe standing in the shallows. There is no wall beside it, no paving leading up to it. Its lower half has sunk into the ground. Through the opening you see the same pool and trees that you see around it, but a few moments earlier. A bird you have just watched take flight is still sitting on its branch within the frame. Rain falling in the hollow sometimes continues inside the opening after it has stopped everywhere else.

Animals cross it freely. The difference does not seem to trouble them. A fish enters on one side and comes out on the other with its mouth still working around a morsel it has not yet caught.

The third structure is harder to recognize. It is a disc under the water, wider than a table, covered by a thin layer of silt. Its edge shows in places: a dark material with no grain, no corrosion, and no growth attached to it. Across its face, small exposed marks change position too slowly to watch. From morning to evening, a line becomes a circle, or a circle becomes several disconnected strokes.

The disc regulates something. You learn this from the vegetation around it. When a mat of roots spreads too far into the open pool, a strip of ground elsewhere sinks a little and fills with water. When a tree falls, warmth gathers under the gap in its crown, and seedlings unfold within days. The hollow changes constantly, but its proportions remain much the same. There is always open water. There are always intervals between the trees.

Whatever made these structures understood how to leave room.

A rain begins without darkening the sky. Large, widely spaced drops strike the pools, each raising a brief crown. Animals emerge from the soil before the ground has properly wet: rounded worms with fringed mouths, small amphibious things carrying leaves over their backs, a broad flat creature that squeezes out of a crack far narrower than its body. They move toward the fresh water running over the stone rim. Some feed there; some release clouds of eggs; others simply let the current pass across them.

For a while the whole hollow seems to flow in several directions at once. Rain descends. Droplets climb the shelter. Water seeps sideways through the ridge. Beneath the mud, burrowing animals lift grains that settle a few inches away. The grazer leaves its pool, opening a channel behind its legs that fills with fish.

Then the rain passes. The air stays warm and bright. The birds smooth their disassembled feathers.

At the edge of the path lies a stone worn into the shape of a shallow bowl. It contains water, three fallen leaves, and a tiny animal slowly eating one of them. Along its rim are several faint parallel grooves, perhaps made by a tool, perhaps by something that once crawled there repeatedly. The grooves have almost disappeared. The bowl itself will last much longer, deepening imperceptibly each wet season, until one day it admits water from below.

There is no exposed height here left to collapse, no sharp slope demanding to be carried downhill. The land has reached a form that can endure its own weather. What changes now is intimate: a root dividing a seam, a mouth removing the edge of a leaf, a pool gaining the width of a hand.

You find a dry place beneath the shelter and sit. Across the water, the hollow’s far boundary remains plainly visible. Between you and that low rim, in the generous spaces among a few trees, life keeps appearing.
