The high country is still rising.

It has no foothills. Beyond the last level stretch of pale mineral ground, the land lifts in great, abrupt shelves, one above another, until the uppermost edges disappear into a black sky. Some of these shelves are as broad as valleys. Others are narrow enough that their undersides can be seen from far away: dark slabs held up by columns of newly hardened rock, with red heat showing through the joints.

There is no snow at this height, and scarcely any cold. Warmth comes from below. It passes through the soles of the feet, gathers beneath overhangs, and escapes from fissures with the dry, faintly bitter smell of scorched stone. In places the ground is too hot to touch. A few paces away it is merely warm, although the two surfaces may look exactly alike. Nothing has yet acquired the stains or crusts that would make such differences readable.

The air is thin. Distance is unusually clear when the dust settles, but there is little light to reveal it. A dim copper band along the horizon gives shape to the lower ridges. Higher up, the cliffs are visible by their own intermittent glow. Most of the country remains black on black: a nearer edge hiding a farther edge, a shaft of darkness opening under what had seemed to be solid ground.

This is not an old mountain range being worn away. Its slopes have no softened shoulders, no fans of gravel, no channels where runoff once found the easiest descent. Their faces are sharp, sometimes unnaturally clean. A cliff may expose a single sheet of dark glass several hundred feet tall. Beside it stands a wall of rough yellow crystal, each point intact, with no dust caught between them. The two were formed within days of one another. Neither has existed long enough to look ordinary.

Across the upper basin, ridges appear in parallel ranks. Their spacing suggests some deep pressure moving beneath the surface, lifting the crust as a hand might lift cloth. But the crust does not bend readily. It breaks. Every few hours a new line opens somewhere in the basin, and the blocks on either side begin to move apart.

The motion is usually too slow to watch directly. Its effects are not.

A gap that could be stepped across before darkness becomes impassable while one is looking elsewhere. A leaning pinnacle straightens. The flat ground at the foot of a escarpment develops a shallow tilt, then a steeper one; loose grains start to creep, and suddenly the whole plain is a slope. Far off, two ridges that have stood separately begin to overlap. There is no reliable skyline.

Despite this activity, the place is mostly quiet.

Much of the breaking happens deep underground. What reaches the surface is a low pressure in the chest, followed by a small dry click from nearby stones. Sometimes the earth gives a long, strained creak. It seems insufficient for the scale of the movement. A massive wall rises a little higher, scattering a thin curtain of fragments, and the sound is no louder than a door opening in another room.

The louder events are brief. A column splits from crown to base. A glass face bursts outward. The report travels through the thin air, loses its sharpness, and is followed by a delicate tinkling that can continue for several minutes. Then silence returns, with the changed landscape still standing.

There is no surface water. The hollows contain powder, angular chips, or smooth deposits left by gases escaping from the rock. These deposits are white in the faint horizon light and yellow near the hot seams. In a sheltered crack, they may grow into fine branching forms that resemble roots. They are brittle salts. Touch one and an entire miniature thicket collapses.

Actual life, if that is what the scattered dark patches are, stays close to the warm fractures.

The patches lie flat against the stone, no larger than a spread hand. They have no leaves, stems, or visible pores. Their surfaces are dull purple, almost black, and slightly granular. On a newly opened seam they sometimes occur in a broken line, each patch separated from the next by several yards of bare rock. Their edges slowly advance toward the escaping heat.

They do not cover the ground. There are miles with none at all.

When a vent cools, the patches become pale and lift at the edges. Eventually they detach as thin, weightless scales. A passing gust carries them into a crevice or out over the precipice. Whether this is death, dispersal, or simply another part of their existence is difficult to tell. There are no animals to eat them, no insects to visit them, no tracks leading to them. Their only evident relations are with stone, heat, and the direction of the air.

Wind arrives without much warning. For a long time the air may be still enough that the heat rises vertically from a fissure. Then a pressure change draws a gust along the cliff tops. It lifts the newest dust but leaves the larger fragments untouched, laying pale streamers across the dark basin. The dust is abrasive and warm. It smells metallic. Where it passes over glass, it makes a soft whisper; where it passes through the salt branches, a faint ticking.

Below the eastern rim are several structures.

They are widely separated, small enough to miss until one stands above them. Each is made of fitted stone, though “fitted” may be too generous a word: broad slabs set upright, narrower slabs laid across their tops, a few wedges driven into the gaps. There is no mortar. There are no carvings or polished surfaces. The builders used pieces already close to the shapes they needed.

One structure has three walls and a low roof. Another is only a ring of standing stones around an empty square. A third has split in two with the ground beneath it, leaving half its roof on either side of a widening cleft. All face away from the basin’s hottest vents. Their interiors are cooler than the open ground and almost completely dark.

Nothing inside identifies their purpose. There are no vessels, tools, ashes, or stored provisions. A shallow depression in one floor could have been made by repeated use, but it could also have formed as the slab cooled. Some of the stones are older than the ledges on which they stand. They may have been carried upward from lower ground. They may have arrived there when the mountain lifted.

The shelters offer little reassurance. Their roofs are sound, but the land under them is not. Beside one doorway, a narrow crack runs through the floor and disappears beneath the back wall. Warm air rises from it. The wall has begun to lean inward.

At the basin’s center is a broad open area of black, smooth stone. It reflects the dim sky so poorly that it looks like an absence in the landscape. From a ridge above, its scattered projections resemble islands: isolated fins, squat pillars, and one tall blade with a glowing line down its side.

Down on the plain, those features are much farther apart than they appeared. The blade is several hours away on foot. Between here and there, the surface varies from rippled glass to dull, porous rock. Some stretches ring faintly under a footstep. Others absorb the sound entirely.

There are fresh breaks everywhere, but almost no loose rubble. New material keeps welling into the cracks and binding their edges. A fissure opens, shines for a while, then skins over. The skin darkens as it cools. Before it has fully hardened, pressure from below wrinkles it into low ridges.

Occasionally the surface rises in a shallow dome. The dome holds for a minute or an hour. Then its crown tears, releasing a little heat and a plume of mineral vapor. What remains becomes another small hill, sharp-edged and empty.

From the western heights, these changes can be seen across the whole basin. A red line appears near the center. Another answers it beneath a distant wall. Dust lifts from a ridge whose summit has just moved out of sight. Along the northern rim, a row of pillars slowly separates from the cliff behind them.

For a while, all of this happens together.

Then the lights fade. The dust passes. Only a few warm cracks remain visible, and the high country seems still again.

By the time the copper band returns to the horizon, there is a new shelf beneath the northern rim. Its surface is bare, unmarked, and steeply tilted toward the basin. No salt has formed on it. No dark patches have reached its seams. It holds nothing yet but the heat of its making.
