The path reaches the high country without reaching a summit. After the last steep ascent, the ground opens into miles of shallow basins, wooded ridges, and low hills with rounded shoulders. There are still peaks to the east, but they look worn down rather than broken off. Water shines between them. From here, most of the world is below sight.

It is very hot.

The heat is not confined to the sunlit rock. It comes through the soles of your shoes and hangs under the trees, where the air smells of wet bark and ripe fruit. Even the wind is warm. When it passes over a pool, it brings a little relief from evaporation, followed by a heavier wash of humidity. Clothes dry on their outer surfaces while remaining damp against the skin.

At this altitude there ought to be frost, thin soil, exposed stone. Instead, there are tree ferns taller than houses. Their old trunks lean over the path, furred with moss and rooted into cracks full of black humus. Broad leaves overlap at several heights. Vines hang from them in loops so thick that some have acquired their own gardens: small shrubs, orchids with waxy brown flowers, nests, pale fungi. Where a tree has fallen, the opening fills with reeds before its trunk has finished sinking into the mud.

The land is not a continuous forest. It opens repeatedly into marshes and stretches of bare, gently sloping rock. These give long views across the plateau. You can see several distinct belts of trees, the silver backs of pools, a ridge with three solitary structures on it, and beyond that an immense bank of ordinary white cloud. Much later, at the western edge, you discover that the cloud is far below the plateau. What looked like a distant horizon was its upper surface.

The edge is difficult to approach. The ground stays level almost to the drop, but the vegetation becomes lower and more tightly rooted. Shrubs spread flat against the stone. Their branches grow into one another, making a springy mat that covers holes without filling them. Beneath it, water runs toward daylight.

At the brink, the plateau ends in a dark wall descending through cloud. You cannot see its foot. There are ledges thousands of feet down with forests on them, and waterfalls passing those forests without appearing to touch them. Some falls spread into mist. Others narrow as they descend, as though the water is being drawn through a funnel too large to see.

The nearest waterfall makes a sustained, coarse roar. It is part of the sound everywhere here, even deep inland: sometimes a distant rumble beneath the insects, sometimes a vibration in a hollow trunk. Nothing about it suggests hurry. The water falls continuously, and has done so long enough to wear the brink into a smooth lip.

Inland, most water moves much more slowly. Pools overflow through channels worn only a few inches deep. In some places a broad sheet of water travels across bare rock so evenly that its motion is visible only where it encounters a fallen leaf. The leaf rotates once, drifts a hand’s breadth, and catches against a tuft of grass.

The stone is dark green when dry and almost black when wet. Its surfaces are rounded, not freshly fractured. Old cracks have widened into slots with polished sides; hollows have filled with silt; roots have settled into the joints. The hills seem to have arrived at shapes they can keep. Even where a slope has slipped, the scar is old enough to support trees, though their trunks still curve at the base.

Water also comes out of the rock.

On a shaded face, droplets gather in rows that follow no visible seam. They swell, merge, and descend. A hand placed there finds the surface warm. The water is cooler, with a faint mineral taste. Along some walls, this seepage feeds hanging moss several yards thick. Birds disappear into it. Their movements shake out drops long after they have gone silent.

The plateau’s small buildings stand in these wet places rather than above them.

One is a narrow tower on six legs, half enclosed by a strangler tree. Another resembles a roof set directly on the ground, its curved surface shedding water into a ring of planted trenches. There is a pavilion on an island that has no visible bridge. They are far enough apart that each appears to be the only structure in the landscape until you climb the next ridge.

None has windows in the usual sense. Pale panels interrupt the dark material of their walls, and the panels sometimes become transparent when no one is looking directly at them. The effect is easy to miss. Walking past, you catch a glimpse of an empty chamber, a column, a floor covered in shallow water. Stop and turn, and the wall is opaque again.

Their edges remain remarkably clean. Moss grows up to them but does not cross onto them. Roots follow the foundations for a few inches, then bend away. The material feels neither metallic nor ceramic. It has the warmth of skin left in sunlight, and gives slightly under pressure before becoming firm.

At the tower, six thin streams descend from six outlets near the top. Five fall normally into the marsh. The sixth rises.

It rises slowly, intact for perhaps twenty feet, then divides into beads that continue upward until they become too small to follow. The birds avoid that narrow column. Insects do not: they gather beside it, where tiny winged bodies occasionally drift upward with the droplets, then fly free.

There is no hum, no pulse of light, no obvious machinery. A small maintenance hatch near the tower’s base has been left open. Inside is a smooth recess containing a few dry seeds and the shed skin of a lizard.

Farther on, the path crosses a band of ground where your weight decreases. It is not enough to lift you, but each step carries farther than expected. Fern fronds stand more upright there. Rain falling into the band slows before reaching the soil, making the shower look unusually dense. Outside it, the drops fall at their ordinary speed.

These bands have fixed boundaries, marked by changes in growth. They cross the country without regard for slopes or streams. Whatever maintains them has been here long enough for plants and animals to find uses for it.

Large browsing animals favor the lighter ground. They have narrow heads, blunt horns, and broad, soft feet that spread over the marsh. In the hottest hours they stand belly-deep in pools, chewing floating stems. Little white birds walk along their backs and probe the folds behind their ears. When the animals move, they do so deliberately, testing each patch of mud before shifting their weight.

Other lives are less conspicuous but much more numerous. The pools contain shoals of finger-length fish with dark bodies and silver mouths. Crabs shelter beneath exposed roots. Beetles the size of plums make a knocking sound as they strike the undersides of leaves. A long-tailed animal sleeps in a fork overhead, its fur so heavily inhabited by moss that only its breathing distinguishes it from the branch.

The air is full of calls. Some birds make harsh, short cries; others produce low notes that carry across the marshes and seem too large for the bodies making them. Insects sustain a rattling chorus through the afternoon. Frogs answer from holes in the stone. Close to a pool, you hear bubbles breaking among the reeds, the scrape of feeding mouths, the sudden slap of a fish taking something from the surface.

For all this noise, there is little rapid movement. A flock may spend an hour working through one tree. Cloud shadows travel slowly across the open basins. Fruit drops, splits, and lies where it fell until small animals gather beneath it. The great browsers follow trails pressed into the mud by generations of broad feet.

Toward evening, the sunlight becomes lower and yellow, perfectly familiar. It catches the wet faces of leaves and the pale walls of the scattered structures. The heat eases only a little. Vapour begins to collect in the hollows, first as separate wisps, then as a low, continuous layer through which the trunks stand dark and clear.

From a ridge, you can watch that vapour spread over several miles of country. The pools disappear beneath it. The tops of reeds remain visible. Here and there, the mist gathers against something you cannot see, piling into a steep bank in the middle of an otherwise empty marsh.

Below the plateau, the clouds turn grey. Above it, the sky remains blue for a while longer.

Water continues to rise beside the tower. Water continues to fall from the cliffs. Between those two motions, the high country stays warm and crowded with life, its old channels filling at the same unhurried rate, its worn stone receiving another evening’s rain.
