The height is apparent first in the light. Shadows have hard edges, and even the shaded sides of stones are bright with light reflected from the ground. The sky is a deep, almost bruised blue. Far below the plateau’s rim, clouds lie in separate banks, their upper surfaces visible through the gaps between headlands. Rain falls from them into country that cannot be seen.

Up here, the land is cool, pale, and mostly bare.

It extends through a succession of shallow basins, broad shelves, and rounded ridges. Across the largest basin, several miles away, a dark outcrop looks close enough to reach in an hour. Between here and there are hollows concealed by the low angle of the ground. Walking toward anything takes longer than sight promises.

The stone is a gray-white limestone threaded with darker, harder seams. In places those seams stand a hand’s breadth above the surrounding surface, making long ribs that cross the shelves and disappear beneath gravel. Elsewhere the limestone has worn into smooth-backed humps, each with a skirt of small, flat fragments. No peak has a fresh edge. The cliffs at the plateau’s boundary are immense, but their faces are softened by fluting, old hollows, and ledges deep enough to hold shadow all day.

Loose material has largely found its resting place. Fine grit fills depressions. Larger stones lie in shallow beds fitted so closely that a boot disturbs very little. The exposed ridges have been scoured clean. It is an old landscape, still receiving weather but no longer looking unfinished by it.

There is almost nowhere to shelter from the sound.

A wind moves continually across the plateau, although near the ground it is often no more than a cold pressure against the ankles. Its noise comes from beneath. The limestone is hollow in many places, pierced by narrow shafts and long chambers connected through cracks too small to enter. Air crossing their mouths draws out a tremendous, sustained roar. Some openings produce a low note felt in the chest. Others hiss, whistle, or emit a rough vibration like stone being dragged over stone.

The sounds overlap across the basins. At certain points they reinforce one another so strongly that the teeth seem to hum. A person could stand beside another person and see their mouth moving without catching a word. Behind a ridge, one layer of the noise drops away and another becomes audible, but there is no quiet.

At first the plateau appears empty of animals. Then a bird rises from what seemed to be a crack in the ground, and another follows, and fifty more lift from a ledge beyond it.

They are small, dark-winged birds with pale bodies and blunt faces. Their nests occupy narrow slots in the cliff and shallow cavities under stone lips. Whole colonies can be hidden within a slope that looks bare from twenty paces away. When they leave to feed, the air above that slope briefly fills with wings; when they settle, the open space returns.

Other birds remain high over the basins, using the same reliable currents. Broad-winged scavengers turn slowly without flapping. Swift, narrow birds cut below them, taking insects lifted from the hollows. Their calls are shrill enough to pierce the cavern noise, though only at close range.

The ground is alive at a smaller scale. Beetles shelter beneath flakes of limestone. Wingless crickets occupy fissures, their pale legs braced against both walls. Mites gather around the bases of plants. In some hollows, lifting a stone reveals dozens of small bodies moving away from the light.

Food is concentrated rather than plentiful everywhere. Across long stretches of exposed shelf, nothing grows. Then, at the foot of a slight rise, there is a mat of silver-green leaves no larger than an open hand. Beyond it lies another, and another, each rooted where fine soil has accumulated.

The plants keep close to the ground. Their leaves are thick and hairy; their stems run horizontally through gravel before sending up new growth. A few bear tiny flowers, yellow or violet, held inside cups of leaves that protect them from the wind. Lichen makes dark orange stains on the harder stone. From a distance these scattered growths are almost invisible. Up close, each has its own small population feeding, hunting, sheltering, and laying eggs.

Moisture gathers in similarly restricted places.

Most mornings, condensation beads along the shaded sides of cracks. It darkens the limestone by a few shades and runs down into pockets beneath the surface. Some pockets contain only damp sand. Others hold a thumb’s depth of water, clear enough that the waterline is easier to see than the water itself. Narrow channels carry the excess toward deeper cavities.

Once in a while a cloud rises high enough to brush the rim. Fine rain sweeps over one or two basins, briefly giving the pale ground a soft gray color. Much of it drains straight into the stone. By the time sunlight returns, the exposed shelves are dry, but beneath them the cracks remain wet.

The small mammals know those cracks. They emerge from burrows under the larger slabs: compact, broad-footed creatures with dense gray coats, black noses, and short ears. They move in quick runs between feeding patches, then stop with their bodies pressed low. Some dig for roots. Others carry seeds into the chambers below.

One sits beside a flat stone, holding a shell-covered seed in its forepaws. It strikes the seed against an edge, turns it, and strikes again. There is a scatter of broken shells nearby, and the stone’s edge is polished where generations have used it. Elsewhere, a bird probes a deep fissure with a stiff grass stem, withdrawing it with a grub attached.

These are the only signs of manufacture: a selected stem, a favored stone, objects used and dropped within the place that supplied them. There are no paths except the brief runs between shelter and food. No wall interrupts the basins. No smoke rises.

Standing here for a long time reveals how much happens without altering the landscape. Birds depart and return. A mammal crosses a bare stretch. Insects work through a flowering cushion. Water appears in a hollow and sinks out of sight. The same currents support the same wide circles overhead, and the underground roar continues through every small event.

Even the light seems to hold its position longer than it should. There are few tall objects by which to judge its movement, and the pale ground throws brightness into every exposed surface. Only the deep cracks preserve a convincing darkness.

Toward the outer edge, the shelves narrow and the cavities open into the cliff. Here the roar is joined by thousands of birds calling from ledges below. The air is cold against the face, warm where sunlight touches a sleeve. Beyond the brink, clouds drift through a depth too great to estimate.

Behind you, the plateau continues: rounded stone, open basin, a distant patch of leaves. It is difficult to tell whether the dark marks on the next ridge are birds or fissures until one of them moves.
