The district is only six streets wide, and all six streets end at water.

Not a river, exactly. Water occupies the narrow spaces between retaining walls, spreads beneath loading platforms, and stands in the courts of buildings whose ground floors have been surrendered to it. There are channels with names engraved on municipal plates, but their names do not describe where they go. A channel called North Drain enters a wall facing south. Another returns, warm and faintly green, from beneath the street it was supposed to empty.

The streets themselves are broad, mostly vacant, paved with dark ceramic blocks. Their centers have settled into shallow troughs. After rain, water runs down them in sheets thin enough to expose the raised makers’ marks on each block. Even now, several hours after the last shower, the paving is wet. The walls are wet. The undersides of handrails carry rows of drops. Nothing dries completely here except the pale circles immediately above certain buried pipes.

Overhead, a concrete reservoir stretches across the district on immense square legs. It covers nearly everything without quite enclosing it. Between its slabs are slots through which daylight falls in gray bands. You can see the sky from some crossings: a narrow strip of heavy cloud, briefly brightening, then closing again. Most light comes reflected from water or filtered through leaves. Beneath the reservoir, noon has the dimness of an early evening that refuses to cool.

The heat rises through the soles of your shoes.

It comes partly from the weather, partly from the machinery below street level. In the pumping houses, motors turn behind louvered doors. Their sound fills the little district: a deep, sustained labor overlaid with sharper knocks, the rattle of loose sheet metal, and the tremendous brief rush of a valve opening. Conversations would have to be conducted close to the ear. There are no conversations now. At intervals an electric bell sounds twice, waits, and sounds once more.

The plants occupy the spaces left around this work.

Broad leaves grow from beds built into the retaining walls, from broken roof gutters, from the silt caught behind ladder rungs. A single bed can be so lush that it seems to have swallowed an entire stairway, yet beyond it there may be twenty meters of bare pavement. Large empty courts alternate with dense green corners. Nothing spreads evenly.

The most vigorous growth is near the warm drains. There the leaves are almost blue-black, thick and glossy, held on stalks the color of old copper. New leaves emerge tightly rolled, and by the end of an afternoon some have opened to the size of serving trays. Fine white roots descend into the channels. The current combs them all one way; when the pumps reverse, they slowly comb the other.

Higher up, where the water is less reliable, vines make loose curtains between balconies. Their lower lengths drip steadily onto the street. A few municipal trees stand in square wells, widely spaced, with trunks swollen at the base and roots lifting the rims of their concrete planters. They have been pruned around pipes and cables for so many years that their branches hold the shape of the machinery even where machinery has been removed.

There are no birds in them. No insects gather at the lamps. Nothing small scurries from the wet footprints left by a maintenance worker. On the water, drifting seeds and strips of bark can be watched for a long time without anything approaching them.

The only fluttering is mechanical: a torn belt slapping a guard, a strip of survey tape snapping in the exhaust from a ventilation shaft.

At the center of the district is a sunken square, perhaps thirty paces across. Its edge is lined with closed workshops, a tiled public washhouse, and a glass-fronted control room. Stone steps descend to the square from four sides. The lowest three steps remain underwater, furred with short, dark growth. The others are rounded by use and by decades of overflow. Their worn hollows collect clear water even when the square is nearly empty.

A gantry crosses overhead. Suspended from it are four rust-red sluice plates, each large enough to block a street. They are raised and lowered by chains running into the control room. Through its steamed windows, small indicator lights can be seen burning beside a motionless desk fan.

When a sluice rises, the square begins to change.

Water first appears along the seams in the paving. It wells up without bubbles, darkening the ceramic in straight lines. Then the drains cough. Water pours from openings beneath the workshop doors, meets in the middle, and spreads into a shallow, fast-moving sheet. Leaves lying flat on the ground lift and sail toward the washhouse. The sound is suddenly much louder, almost solid between the walls.

The process lasts several minutes. Once it ends, the square drains again, leaving fresh silt, displaced fragments of vegetation, and occasionally an object washed out of the old service passages: a brass fitting, a blue tile, a chair leg softened to the texture of cork.

People have placed these finds on the washhouse steps. They are arranged without labels, above the usual waterline. There is room for many more.

The place has been doing this for a long time. The buildings lean slightly but have stopped leaning further. Mineral deposits have bridged cracks in the masonry; roots have thickened around their supports. Replacement pipes run alongside older ones rather than taking their place. In some passages, there are three generations of pipe, each with a different kind of joint, each sweating into the same drain.

And there is one movement for which the control room has no instrument.

Near the western edge, water climbs a wall.

It gathers at the base of a blind alley, rises through a band of porous brick, and emerges twelve meters above in a row of overflowing stone cups. From there it spills into a gutter and returns to the pumps. The cups were built to receive it. Their rims are worn smooth. The alley’s warning sign concerns slippery paving, not the ascent.

On especially hot afternoons, the rising damp passes through the brick in visible pulses. Each pulse takes roughly as long as a person would take to climb the adjacent stair. Leaves rooted in the mortar turn toward it. The motor noise falters for a second, then resumes.

Beyond this alley, an iron gate opens onto a brighter road. From inside, the brightness looks almost white. Yet the district’s heat follows you to the gate, and even the latch is damp.

Behind you, a bell rings. A chain starts moving. Somewhere beneath the paving, a large quantity of water changes direction.
