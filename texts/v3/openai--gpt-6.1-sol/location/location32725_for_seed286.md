The sea stands above the clouds.

Its lower edge is visible from a great distance: a dark green line across the sky, level for thousands of miles, with pale weather gathered beneath it. There is no cliff holding it up. The water rests in the crown of a living mass whose roots descend through the cloud layer and disappear toward a ground too far below to see. What looks like the shore is a thickened margin of leaves, folded together and sealed by their own wax. Beyond that margin lies an ocean, cold and clear, suspended at the elevation of mountain summits.

Nothing spills over. The edge has the rounded, worn appearance of something that has held this weight for an immense length of time.

Within the sea, the light is ordinary daylight. It comes down broad and white through the surface, loses its warmth quickly, and reaches surprisingly far. There is no black abyss. Even in the deepest basins, the water retains a pale green illumination, enough to distinguish a distant vertical shape from its reflection. The cold is not sharp enough to freeze anything. It is the steady cold of deep shade, of stone that would never warm in the sun—except that there is no stone here.

The bottom is plant.

So are the rises, the shelves, the long shallow valleys. The apparent gravel consists of small, hard seed cases seated in a living membrane. The dark outcrops are compressed wood, their grain running through them in enormous curves. What might be taken for a layer of sediment is felted root hair, dense enough to bear weight, with older fibers polished into a smooth brown skin. Every visible surface is alive, but very little looks newly grown.

The vegetation has finished becoming this place.

Across the bottom lie broad, weathered plains with low ridges spaced miles apart. Their surfaces have been scoured into channels and rounded into humps, then settled into those forms. There are no broken twigs, no loose leaves, no debris awaiting decay. Even the exposed fibers have a clean, permanent fray. Here and there, the grain opens into a shallow hollow containing the outline of an animal: a long back, a folded fin, the faint impression of a head. It is difficult to tell whether these are resting places or shapes the substrate has always possessed.

Although everything is vegetal, the sea feels spacious.

The great plants stand far apart. A column may rise from the floor to the surface, wider than a city, with nothing comparable for another hundred miles. Between columns are expanses of open water so broad that distance begins to look like weather. A faint cloudiness halfway across a basin may be a forest of transparent fronds, or merely the accumulated green of water viewed through water. Farther off, the upper branches of another column appear as a dark horizontal seam.

Up close, a column has no single trunk. Its surface is a union of roots, cables, flattened stems, and layers of bark grown tightly into one another. Some strands are as fine as hair; others are large enough to contain a river. They enter the mass at deliberate-looking angles, although nothing has arranged them. Wide flanges project from the sides. Between them are chambers lined with tough, translucent tissue.

This is where the noise comes from.

The landscape is loud with work.

A deep bass note fills the water, accompanied by a harder, metallic register that can be felt in the teeth. Near the columns, the sound separates into several distinct tones: a transformer hum, the sustained rush of a great intake, a dense choral rasp like machinery heard through the wall of a factory. There are no engines to find. There are no buildings, no housings, no manufactured parts. The work is being done by living membranes.

Inside the thick stems, mineral-rich fluids pass through stacked tissues. One layer separates salts. Another draws metals into dark conducting veins. Broad sheets exchange charge across surfaces many miles wide. The luminous seams in the bark are electrical, a dull blue-green visible even in daylight. Elsewhere, rows of swollen nodules hold gases at pressure. Their outer skins are ridged and reinforced, and the sea around them carries a clear, high note.

The scale is industrial. The anatomy is entirely native.

A whole basin may be involved in one continuous conversion: cold water taken into a porous plain, dissolved material separated across a thousand square miles of tissue, useful compounds passed into the columns, the water returned through fine slits along the distant rim. There is no waste heap, no exhaust plume, no increase in output. The system operates at exactly the rate the place can sustain.

Even its noise has reached equilibrium. It does not pulse or falter. There is no irregular knock to wait for, no brief silence in which a smaller sound can be heard. The great tones fit together and remain there, making every open reach of water feel occupied without filling it with objects.

Animals inhabit these reaches.

They are most visible beside the bright seams: pale bodies with broad fins held open, their translucent skins revealing red branching vessels. Smaller creatures occupy the underside of the surface, where their dark silhouettes resemble seeds caught against glass. There are long, narrow animals threaded between root fibers, and flat ones spread over the living floor with their edges tucked into grooves.

They are abundant in the way stars are abundant. Any small patch may seem almost empty; look across enough space and there are more bodies than can be counted.

None is caught in the act of arrival or departure. A fish beside a column remains beside that column. Its fins are fully extended, its mouth slightly open. A ribbon-shaped animal lies through a corridor of roots with the whole elegance of its length visible. The tiny grazers on the basin floor are fixed at the edge of their grazing.

At first, this looks like stillness imposed on a moving world. Then the absence of motion becomes more complete.

There is no suspended silt slowly settling. No bubble rises from the nodules. The light does not tremble. The leaves nearest the surface do not bend, though the membranes beneath them maintain their vast throughput. No animal needs to complete its gesture. Nothing is waiting for the next beat.

The processes continue, but they have no stages.

Water occupies a flow without a parcel progressing along it. Electricity occupies the conducting veins without a discharge advancing through them. The great sound fills the sea without a crest of vibration passing your ear. These are conditions of the place, as depth and cold are conditions. There is work everywhere and no event.

The older-looking surfaces are especially strange. They bear all the evidence of duration—abraded wood, smoothed ridges, exposed grain, roots rounded by currents—yet no further wear occurs. The sea possesses its weathering as it possesses its elevation. Whatever history produced these forms is no longer accessible from within them.

High overhead, the surface holds the sky in an unbroken plane. Through it, white clouds can be seen below the outer rim, while above it lies a clear, pale blue. At the edges of the ocean, the supporting leaves become so broad and thin that daylight passes through their veins. Beyond them is an immense aerial distance, and far beneath that, the suggestion of another world.

There is no path down. No fallen branch connects one level to the other. The roots enter the clouds whole and emerge, if they emerge at all, somewhere beyond sight.

From the middle of a basin, the nearest column is only a dim vertical stain. The floor slopes gently away beneath you, its woven surface disappearing into green light. Pale animals occupy the intervening water at widely separated depths. The sound remains immense.

Nothing approaches.

Nothing recedes.

The place offers no moment in which it becomes more itself. It is already there: the cool sea held high, the broad living floor, the scattered pillars carrying their continuous load, and all that power passing through a landscape which will never look different.
