Near the summit, where the mountain narrows to a blade and casts no shadow at noon, there is a hollow no larger than a sleeping animal.

You would miss it from the ridge. Its entrance is a seam between two flakes of white stone, fringed with ice needles. Nothing pours out. Nothing suggests that the seam opens into water, or that the water inside is moving violently enough to crush a finger against the wall.

The hollow is completely submerged. There is no surface, no air trapped beneath the roof. Cold, clear water presses against every part of it, lit by the sun through a thickness of translucent rock. Even its deepest recess receives a pale ordinary daylight. A grain of sand casts a distinct shadow. The few green things turn toward the west wall in the afternoon.

Outside, clouds snag on the lower peaks. Inside, the water behaves as though it has several miles in which to gather speed.

A current crosses the chamber from nowhere to nowhere. It narrows at the center, twists, strikes the floor, and recoils in a white burst of bubbles. Those bubbles do not rise. They travel toward whatever stone has most recently formed, crowding against it until they vanish with a sharp report. Hundreds vanish each second. The resulting sound fills the hollow so thoroughly that there is no quiet interval in which to hear the next sound arrive.

It is a grinding roar, threaded with cracks and high whistles. Through the water, you feel it in your teeth. On the ridge, with your ear pressed to the entrance, it resembles a whole valley of falling timber.

Yet there is almost nothing here.

Most of the chamber is clear water. The floor has three projections, widely separated: a blunt white knob, a thin blade tilted against the current, and a translucent mound with a shallow cup in its top. Between them lies smooth, unfinished stone. No gravel. No silt. Nothing has had time to be broken down.

The mountain is still making this part of itself.

Along the floor, new mineral gathers in trembling membranes. A sheet appears so thin that you can see the ridges beneath it, then thickens from milk-clear to white. Sometimes it folds before it hardens. Sometimes the current lifts it, and a new ledge is left standing almost vertically, with its wet lower edge still wavering. Watching for an hour, you might see a basin acquire a lip, lose it, and acquire another farther inward.

These are not old rocks being worn into shapes. They are shapes trying to become rock.

The fresh surfaces have the soft look of cut wax, though they are hard enough to nick a bone tool. They carry no scratches except those made deliberately. Their edges are perfect and uncomfortable to look at: the edge of the tilted blade is so new that light seems reluctant to cross it.

There are a few plants.

On the western wall grows a patch of green no broader than a thumbnail, rooted in six little pits. It consists of flat, jointed strands that lie tightly against the stone when the current approaches. Afterward they lift one at a time, cautiously, as if testing whether the disturbance has finished. Their color is dull olive at noon and startlingly bright toward evening, when sunlight enters nearly sideways.

Elsewhere, a single transparent filament stretches between two points on the ceiling. Small green beads travel along it. When one reaches the far end, the filament lengthens a little. Its progress is extremely slow. A stone shelf forming beneath it has already overtaken and enclosed part of the strand.

The animals are harder to find.

One lives in the cup atop the translucent mound: a soft gray creature with a ring of hooked feet. It faces into the current and opens a pair of narrow fans to catch whatever the water brings. Most of the time the fans are empty. Occasionally a speck touches one, and the whole animal contracts.

Another may be living inside the white knob. At irregular intervals, two black points appear beneath its skin. The knob then turns slightly, although it has no visible joint and is continuous with the floor. The black points withdraw. For a while afterward, the current bends around it.

There are signs that someone has worked here.

Near the entrance, three wooden wedges hold apart a crack that would otherwise close. The wood is dark and swollen, cut with an uneven blade. Each wedge is bound with hair-thin fiber to a small perforated stone, so that if it slips loose it will not be carried away.

Farther in, a strip of woven bark has been stretched across the narrowest passage. It shudders continuously. The weave is loose, and the water goes through, but the strip catches the thin mineral skins before they harden. Several have accumulated on it. Someone has peeled them away in the past; little crescents remain along the fibers.

Beside this screen rests a spoon made from a split seed shell. Its handle is polished by use. A shallow groove in the wall fits it exactly.

There is no dwelling, no hearth, no store of food. Only these few provisions for returning: a way to keep the entrance open, a way to catch the new stone, a tool put down where the current cannot take it.

At the back of the hollow lies the thing the spoon is used for.

It is a small depression, usually empty. During the strongest surges, a bead of water gathers there that does not mix with the rest. It has a faint yellow cast and a sharply defined outline. Other water strikes it, divides, and rushes on. The bead remains.

If left alone, it becomes stone before sunset.

If lifted while still soft, it stays soft.

The groove beside the spoon bears traces of several such liftings: round impressions, each smaller than a pea, with a smear leading toward the entrance. On the ridge outside, tucked beneath an overhang, are four little yellow stones threaded onto a cord. They are smooth and faintly warm even when the cord is frozen stiff.

Inside, the noise continues. A ledge grows over an old tool mark. The bark screen pulls taut, slackens, pulls taut again. Green strands flatten against the western wall, and the gray animal closes its empty fans.

Then the current changes direction.

For a moment every loose thing moves together toward the entrance. The spoon rattles in its groove. The wooden wedges creak. A mineral sheet lifts from the floor and sails across the chamber, clear enough to disappear except where its edge catches the sun.

It strikes the opposite wall and fixes there.

By evening, there is a new wall inside the hollow, and beyond it a narrow space full of bright, roaring water that nothing has yet entered.
