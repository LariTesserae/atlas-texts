Below the oldest market street, one level deeper than the shop cellars, there is a pumping court that never quite dries.

It is not large. From the iron stair at one end, you can see the far wall through the steam: three bricked-up arches, a service door, and a strip of pale concrete where an extension was begun and then stopped. The court is roughly the size of a small town square, but roofed with brick vaults and crossed by pipes. There are no shopfronts here. The addresses belong to valves, electrical cabinets, and inspection chambers. Numbers have been painted above them in white, repainted often enough that some stand slightly proud of the wall.

The heat comes from several places. A district-heating main passes along the north side, its insulation swollen and split at the joints. Two pumps stand in a recessed bay, pushing warm drainage water up toward the street sewers. Beyond the unfinished wall, ground water enters through clay that has held the city’s summer heat long after the air above has cooled. There is no breeze to carry any of this away. Even with the pumps idle, the air is hot enough to make the stair rail unpleasant to hold.

Water occupies the court without forming one continuous pool. It lies in shallow trays between concrete ribs, beads along the undersides of pipes, runs down a wall in five or six established threads. An overflow channel skirts the room. Its surface looks still until a piece of grease or a dead insect reveals the current. Farther in, where the floor was never finished, water darkens the clay almost to black.

The dry ground is a raised maintenance walk, two equipment plinths, and a scatter of brick islands left by some earlier arrangement of the drains. Most are only a pace or two across. Between them, the wet floor stretches out under the low light with surprisingly little upon it.

There is evidence of construction everywhere, though almost none of it looks recent. Bolts wait in rows for a machine that never arrived. Reinforcing rods project from the edge of the abandoned slab, each wearing a small orange crust. Behind temporary mesh fencing, a mound of excavated earth still bears the squared cuts of a spade. Heat has dried its upper face into plates, while seepage keeps the base soft. Occasionally a plate slips down and settles without a splash.

Along the warmest leak, pale mineral deposits are beginning to join the wall to the floor. They are low and blunt, more like spilled mortar than stalactites. Some have formed around washers and cable ties dropped years ago. A broken ceramic fuse is already half buried in a cream-colored ridge. The deposits grow too slowly to watch, but the maintenance workers know which ones can no longer be kicked loose.

There is very little green.

At the foot of a ventilation shaft, daylight falls on a patch no bigger than a tabletop. A few grass blades have rooted in grit there, brought down as seeds on shoes or in street runoff. They are long and thin, leaning toward the shaft. Beside them is one tough weed with dusty leaves and a stem bent around the wire guard. Elsewhere, algae stain the shallow channels a dark olive. Nothing grows on the hot pipework. Nothing leafy survives beyond the reach of that small, dirty opening to the street.

The animals make use of almost everything else.

Cockroaches gather in the cabinet hinges, beneath the rims of inspection covers, inside the empty bolt holes. On a quiet visit, the walls appear lightly freckled. When a pump starts, some of the freckles move. They withdraw into seams or hurry across exposed brick, their feet making a faint dry tick beneath the larger sounds.

Midges rise from the cooler sumps. They hang in loose columns under the lamps, drifting upward until the heat from a bulb pushes them aside. Flies rest on the warm walls in such numbers that each has only a narrow space around it. Spider webs occupy the cable brackets, the gaps behind signs, and the underside of the stair. Most are heavy with dust and small carcasses. New strands gleam among the old ones whenever a worker’s torch passes.

Rats travel the ledges above the overflow channel. Their routes are clean where repeated passage has polished the brick and rubbed the dust from pipe elbows. A person standing still will see one emerge from beneath the stair, pause, and continue toward the pump bay. Soon there will be another. They do not usually run. There is food washed down from the market, warm shelter behind the lagging, and little reason to spend energy crossing open ground quickly.

The smallest creatures are concentrated at the waterline. Larvae stir the silt in puddles that look empty from a standing height. Worms show briefly where water crosses the clay. Under a brick lifted from the floor, pale insects scatter through galleries pressed against its underside. There are so many animals, but much of the court remains visibly bare: broad wet surfaces, empty stretches of wall, long distances between the few large objects. Life occupies the edges.

Three lamps illuminate the place. One burns steadily, one has yellowed behind its sealed cover, and one comes on only when the pumps draw power. Their light is enough to find the walkway, read a gauge, recognize a moving rat. It does not reach far into the arches. Condensation catches it in small, shifting points overhead.

The loudest sound is the pump, but it runs slowly. Each stroke produces a heavy knock, followed by water moving through a pipe somewhere out of sight. There is a delay between the two sounds. Once learned, the rhythm is hard not to anticipate. In the pauses come the hiss of a leaking joint, the patter of drips on sheet metal, and the rough humming of an extractor fan whose blades are furred with dirt. From the street above, cart wheels and footsteps arrive as brief dull disturbances in the vault.

Changes take place here over weeks and seasons. After rain, the channels rise by a few centimeters and the rats use the upper ledge. In dry weather, a margin of silt is exposed and acquires tracks. A leak thickens its mineral collar. A patch of algae reaches another brick. The unfinished earth settles against the mesh.

Once a week, someone descends with tools. They scrape a gauge clean, oil a bearing, lift the clogged screen from the sump. For a while the water runs more freely. The walkway is swept, the insects retreat, and fresh chalk marks appear beside the valves.

By the next visit, the warm damp has softened the chalk. The screen has begun to clog again. Beneath the lamps, the midges are turning in the same small spaces.
