The place is easiest to find at low tide, though it is not quite part of the shore. From above, it looks like a dark slit in a shelf of pale clay: something the last winter’s frost opened and the spring water has not yet learned to fill.

You have to kneel to see inside.

Below the slit is a hollow scarcely larger than a room, with a floor of black water and one narrow bank. Its roof is the underside of the clay shelf, held together by roots. Beyond the entrance, the roof rises enough for someone to stand bent-backed, but there is nowhere useful to stand. Most of the hollow is empty air. A little stone, a little vegetation, water; then the considerable space between them.

The clay broke recently. Its exposed faces still have corners. Thin plates project from the walls, sharp enough to scrape a knuckle, and the pale fracture surfaces have not acquired the green skin of older ground. Some carry the impressions of roots that were torn away when the shelf shifted. Others show small, dark inclusions: pebbles, fragments of shell, a leaf folded into the sediment so long ago that only its black outline remains.

Water comes out of these walls rather than running over them. It beads along the fresh fractures, gathers beneath the projecting plates, and falls. Each drop is large and slow to form. The clay around it darkens first; then the drop swells until it holds a tiny upside-down version of the entrance. When it lets go, the impact travels across the pool and touches every wall.

There is usually another drop already falling somewhere else.

The water is cold enough to hurt through a glove. It is also less deep than its blackness suggests. Near the entrance, you can see the bottom under a few inches of clear water: a smooth bed of silt, three pieces of pale gravel, the wavering track of a small animal. Farther in, the floor descends by no more than the height of a boot, but the roof cuts off what little light reaches it. That part appears bottomless.

Nothing grows there.

All the green is gathered on the narrow bank, where fresh seepage enters above the reach of the tide. Moss makes thick cushions around the exposed roots. Small ferns lean almost horizontally into the hollow, their fronds held low beneath the roof. Between them stand wet blades of grass, unusually broad and dark, flattened by drips and then lifting again. There are only a few square feet of this growth, but within them almost no clay is visible. The plants overlap, root into one another’s old leaves, and hold a quantity of water disproportionate to their size.

A fern tip touches the pool. As the water rises, it bends farther down. When the water falls, it comes up carrying a line of bubbles.

The air smells of clay, salt, and crushed stems. The cold here has little to do with the weather above. On a mild afternoon, when sunlight warms the exposed shelf, the hollow stays chilled by seepage and evaporation. Breath condenses under the roof. Water forms on the roots themselves, travels along them, and drips from their lowest points. Some of these points hang well away from any wall, so that rain appears to begin in empty air.

Outside, on the other side of the estuary, machines work through most of the day and much of the night.

You cannot see them from inside. There is no metal here, no pipe, no discarded bottle, no constructed edge. But the sound crosses the water and enters the clay. The hollow receives it differently from the open shore. The sharp noises disappear. What remains is a low, forceful vibration: engines under load, pumps drawing, the repeated strain of something being lifted and set down.

It is loud enough that the drops seem to fall inside it.

At intervals the pitch lowers, and fine grains shake loose from the roof. They make little dimples in the pool. Sometimes a loose clay plate trembles against its neighbour, producing a dry chatter that is surprisingly distinct in all this wetness. You can put a hand on the wall and feel the working machinery several miles away.

The animals live mostly at the margins.

Small translucent shrimps hover beside the bank, close enough together to look like bits of moving glass. They retreat when a shadow passes over the entrance, then return one by one to pick at the moss where it dips into the water. Beetles occupy the cavities beneath exposed roots. Pale mites move across the undersides of leaves. There are worms in the clay, and their narrow openings leak darker water than the surrounding wall.

At first the place seems almost uninhabited. Then, after you have held still for a while, every patch of green begins to move.

A larva folds itself around a grass stem. A beetle shifts under a root, pushing out a crumb of clay. Something too small to name crosses the skin of a hanging drop without breaking it. Along the pool’s edge, tiny snails have gathered where the fresh water mixes with salt. Their shells are the colour of the wall, and it takes time to distinguish them from grit.

The middle remains empty. No weed reaches across it. No animal stays there long. Occasionally a shrimp swims out, makes two or three abrupt turns, and returns to shelter. The blank water gives even that brief excursion a surprising distance.

The tide changes the hollow without entering as a visible wave. First the water at the back loses its stillness. A strand of dead root turns slowly. Then the level begins to rise along the walls, covering the pale gravel, reaching the moss, lifting old leaves from the bank. Salt water is coming through passages in the clay beneath the pool.

On large tides the whole green margin goes under.

The beetles climb. Air gathers beneath fern leaves. The shrimps move up into places that, an hour earlier, were only wet ground. Worms withdraw and close their openings. For a while the chamber looks simpler than before: water, roof, a few roots projecting down.

Then, as the tide ebbs, everything reappears in stages. The tallest grass first, streaming and bent. The fern tips. The moss, shedding water so quickly that it seems to be draining a much larger country. Tiny channels open in the bank and run for several minutes before dwindling to drops.

This is when the loudest sound comes from the hollow itself. Water pulls through the buried passages and takes air with it. There is a gulp, a pause, then a rough sucking roar. It can start beneath your feet and move to the back wall. The distant engines continue, but for a little while it is hard to tell which sound belongs to them.

The walls are still changing. After a freeze, there may be a new plate of clay on the bottom. After heavy rain, one of the seep holes widens and sheds a fan of pale sediment. A root that was buried yesterday hangs exposed today, white where the clay has fallen away. Nothing has been worn smooth for long. Even the rounded lip at the entrance owes its shape to softening and collapse rather than years of abrasion.

By late afternoon the light inside has a faint green cast from the bank. The entrance is bright enough to show each drop crossing it, while the rear of the chamber remains dim. If the machinery stops between shifts, you hear a thin rustling among the plants, the ticks of falling water, and the small crack of clay letting go.

The quiet does not last. An engine starts across the estuary, the wall begins to tremble against your palm, and a shrimp leaves the moss to hang briefly over the open pool.
