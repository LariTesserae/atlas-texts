The city occupies the upper half of a plateau, where the rock has been worn into broad steps. From the lower roads it looks less like a settlement than a system of pale seams: streets cut into the escarpment, long rooflines following the contours, a few chimneys standing widely apart. Above it there is only dark weather. Below it, far beyond the last retaining wall, a hot gray plain disappears into haze.

There are no towers crowded together, no tight knots of houses. The builders seem to have had more land than they knew what to do with. A single workshop may face a square large enough to hold a district. Roads divide and rejoin across empty terraces. Staircases sixty steps wide descend to one narrow door. The whole city is connected, supplied, drained, and almost entirely unoccupied.

Water makes it difficult to tell what has been abandoned.

It runs in the street channels, warm enough to redden skin. It beads on iron rails and gathers beneath the lips of stone benches. Walls seep even where there is no soil behind them. In some rooms, shallow water covers the floor without reaching the threshold, as if the doorway marks the limit of a basin deeper than the room itself.

Nothing dries completely. A footprint remains dark for hours, then fills from below.

The heat comes through the ground. At dawn, which is only a thinning of the cloud, the stone is already too warm to sit on. By the middle of the day, certain streets cannot be crossed in soft-soled shoes. The air above them trembles, though there is little sunlight to account for it. Far underground, boilers or furnaces continue to work. Their exhaust rises through square shafts distributed across the plateau, carrying a smell of hot metal and something faintly sweet, like plaster being mixed.

The cloud ceiling is low but seldom touches the city. There is a band of clear, dim air beneath it, enough to see several terraces away. Through this band, the chimneys release columns of vapor that rise neatly until they meet the cloud and spread sideways. Sometimes a darker shape moves within the overcast, tracing the route of a buried conduit.

When rain comes, it is heavy and brief. It strikes the empty squares with the sound of a crowd arriving, runs silver along the curb stones, and leaves the air hotter than before.

The buildings were made to survive this. Their lower walls are smooth ceramic, stained cream, brown, and green by mineral water. Above shoulder height, brick takes over, then sheet metal under the eaves. Roofs are steep on the uphill side and almost flat downhill, so that whole blocks resemble shallow scoops fitted into the slope. Each has a cistern, a spillway, an inspection ladder. Many have no windows at all.

There are signs of ordinary habitation, but they are sparse and oddly distributed: a cooking grate outside a pumping station; six chairs lined up along a parapet; a blue enamel cup beside a valve wheel large enough to require two people. In one neighborhood, doorways have been numbered in three different systems, each painted over the last. In another, all the doors stand open except one, whose handle has been wrapped carefully in cloth.

No washing hangs anywhere. Nothing grows in the gutters.

What looks from a distance like moss is a rough deposit of copper salts. The dark patches in the shade are iron-rich crusts. They spread slowly along the wet stone, lifting paint in delicate blisters. Occasionally a crust opens along a fine crack and releases a little clear fluid. Whether this is chemistry or a kind of life has never been settled, and there is no one nearby to ask.

The few animals keep to the cooler infrastructure. Small translucent things inhabit the overflow tunnels, clinging upside down where water passes above them. They have too many joints to resemble fish, and their bodies become almost invisible when submerged. Once in a while one appears in a street channel, walking against the current on pale hooked feet. It stops at every junction, touching both streams before choosing.

Higher up, along the windward wall, there are birds—or something with the same economy of movement. Usually only one can be seen. It stands on a chimney cap for most of a day, narrow and soot-colored, with its wings folded so closely that it resembles a piece of burnt metal. When it takes off, it drops first, vanishing below the terrace before returning on a current of hot air.

The city is quiet enough for these movements to matter.

A drip hitting a hollow pipe can be heard across an entire square. Valve chains rattle behind locked doors. Somewhere below the civic baths, an engine turns through six uneven strokes, pauses, and begins again. It has done this for so long that the nearby masonry has worn into shallow grooves wherever loose fittings touch it.

The larger machines are nearly silent. They work by weight and pressure: water lifting a float, a float releasing a gate, a descending counterweight drawing another quantity of water uphill. There are miles of pipes, but few exposed moving parts. You may watch a reservoir empty for an hour without seeing where its contents go.

Then, on a terrace above you, a fountain begins.

These are not ornamental fountains. Each is a blunt stone mouth set low in a wall, pouring into a trough polished by centuries of use. Most remain dry. When one wakes, others cease. The flow passes through the city in a slow sequence, neighborhood by neighborhood, as if only a limited number of streets can be wet at once. Yet the walls continue weeping everywhere.

At the highest edge lies a wide district of roofless chambers. Here the city’s organization becomes difficult to read. Corridors open onto slabs of bare bedrock. A row of factory bays ends against a cliff without any sign that the cliff was ever quarried. Rails enter a basin filled with milky water and emerge on the other side at a different spacing.

The basin is shallow near its edges. Its floor can be seen clearly: white tiles, dropped bolts, the shadow of a handrail. Farther out, the bottom appears to tilt upward until it meets the surface, although the water never spills over that rising plane.

In the late dimness, warmth moves beneath it in long bands. The tiles blur and become distinct again. Occasionally a bolt shifts a few inches uphill.

People who still use the city do not drain this basin. They maintain the walkways around it, replace damaged warning lamps, and leave everything in the water where it lies.

Beyond the chambers, the plateau opens into bare, weathered stone. Here there are no buildings, only survey markers and a broad gutter cut across the rock. Its edges have rounded with age. Hot water travels along it without hurry, carrying grains of black grit toward the city.

Standing there, one can see how little of the plateau has actually been built upon, and how much labor has gone into connecting those few places. A road crosses half a mile to reach a shed. A pipe follows the road, then continues alone. Farther away, a retaining wall holds up a terrace on which nothing stands.

The water keeps arriving. The gates lift and settle. Under the cloud, small lamps come on in empty pump houses, each illuminating a gauge, a wet floor, the underside of a stair.

By night, the city is visible mostly through its work.
