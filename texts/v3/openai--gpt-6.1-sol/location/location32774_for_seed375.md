The settlement fits inside a shallow depression, no wider than a village square. From its rim you can see nearly all of it: three narrow streets, a few roofless courts, a pale tower scarcely taller than a house. Beyond the depression lies a flat expanse of gray mineral. Nothing on that plain is high enough to hide another settlement, and no other settlement is visible.

There is no road leading in.

The rim is only knee-high. Its outer slope is smooth, but the inward-facing edge is newly broken, edged with translucent blades. They have not yet lost their sharpness. Here and there a blade has split lengthwise, revealing a faint yellow interior. The fractures are so clean that they seem recent, although there is no rubble below them.

The same yellow shows in the bases of the buildings.

At first the place looks constructed from thin stone slabs: walls set close together, rectangular openings, recessed thresholds. Then the details become difficult to account for. No two walls meet in quite the same way. Some corners are folded, others thickened like the joints of a stem. A lintel merges into its uprights without a seam. The stairs inside one doorway have broad, shallow treads that grow narrower toward the top, where they disappear into an unbroken wall.

There are no tools, fittings, or fastenings. No marks where anything has been cut.

The town grows here.

Its buildings rise from the floor as hollow mineral bodies, each one gathering itself around a pocket of empty space. Their arrangement is unmistakably urban. Fronts align along streets; passages connect courts; small chambers cluster around larger ones. Yet the order does not come from a plan. A wall forms where two growing hollows press against one another. An alley remains where neither can occupy the ground. Given enough neighbors, every chamber acquires an entrance.

The entrances face away from the nearest light.

That light comes from a low, featureless sky. It is neither daylight nor moonlight, but a dim illumination spread evenly through the air. Shadows are soft and close to their objects. Under the roofed passages there is still enough brightness to distinguish a hand from the wall, but not enough to see the color of either.

The air is cool. Stone takes warmth from the skin, steadily and without the damp chill of a cellar. There is no smell of moisture. No dark patches beneath the walls, no condensation in the deepest recesses. A shallow basin in one court is filled with dry, angular grains. Its edges are sharp all the way down.

Nothing here has been rounded by water.

Even the dust is unfamiliar. It consists of minute plates that lie flat until disturbed, then turn edge-on and hang in the air for a long time. Walking through a room leaves a dim glitter behind you. The particles settle without drifting toward the doorway. There seems to be no wind, though the air is not entirely still: its pressure changes slowly, enough to make your ears feel briefly full.

Along the shaded foot of certain walls grow sparse black plants.

They have no leaves. Each is a short stem with two or three flattened branches, thin as paper and rigid as mica. Their roots spread over the stone rather than entering it, following the yellow seams. The largest patch would fit beneath a person’s outstretched hand; most plants stand alone, separated by several empty rooms.

They feed on the walls as the walls form.

A fresh surface carries a weak charge. The plants draw it through their roots, and their branches gradually curl inward. When the charge fades, the roots release. The whole plant folds into a small, many-edged shape and rolls a few inches toward newer stone. This takes hours. A plant that seemed dead when you entered a court may have crossed its threshold by the time you return.

You can tell the older rooms by the black root marks near their floors. The marks are fine and branching, never more than a handspan above the ground. There are no great ruins. Once a room becomes old enough to stop growing, its walls thin from within. Eventually they come apart into flakes, leaving a clean rectangular vacancy among their neighbors.

The flakes lie for a while, then are taken back into the floor.

This is how the streets change.

In one lane, a long seam has opened down the middle. New material pushes up through it in two parallel lips. Already the passage is too narrow to walk comfortably. Around the corner, a former wall is becoming an arch: its center has thinned to the translucence of a fingernail, while both ends remain thick. Through it you can see the dim outline of a court that was enclosed a short time ago.

None of this makes a sound.

There is no grinding as the stone shifts, no crack when a surface parts. The material yields along lines too fine to see. Dust loosens and hangs. An entire doorway can lower itself by the width of a finger while you watch, quietly enough that you hear only your own breathing.

The few animals are easier to miss.

One clings beneath an overhanging stair: a pale, six-legged creature about the length of a thumb, with a body flattened almost to a membrane. Its feet grip the stone with broad pads. It remains still until a black plant begins to fold, then descends and places its mouth against the exposed roots. For a moment the plant’s movement slows. The animal swells slightly, releases it, and retreats beneath the stair.

Elsewhere, a small line of empty skins lies against a threshold. They are clear, delicate things, each with a slit along the back. There are only four. In this nearly vacant place, four of anything looks like a gathering.

At the center stands the pale tower.

It is hollow and open at the top. Its interior is scarcely large enough for two people to stand together, but its walls are thicker than those of the surrounding buildings. Narrow vertical chambers run through that thickness. Looking into one, you see a yellow glow far below, under the level of the square.

The tower is still rising. A fresh band around its base is soft enough to hold the impression of a fingertip. Higher up, the stone is hard and cool. At its top, a lip of translucent material bends inward, slowly narrowing the opening.

The rooms nearest it are moving outward. Their floors remain level, but their doorways tilt toward the streets. A court on the eastern side is widening; on the western side, two houses are pressing together and losing the passage between them. The whole settlement adjusts around the tower’s growth, making room without becoming larger.

There is very little here, and almost nothing stays where it is.

If you sit on the rim long enough, the place begins to look less deserted. Not inhabited, exactly, but occupied by its own slow affairs. A plant reaches a new seam. A wall admits the light. A room closes before another opens.

When you descend again, the doorway you used before is still recognizable. It has become too narrow for your shoulders. Beside it, where there was solid stone, a new opening gives onto a small, cool chamber with an untouched floor.
