The place is a shallow hollow in the bed of a cold lake, wholly underwater. From rim to rim it is perhaps thirty paces across: small enough to comprehend, large enough that the farther side softens in the water. A shelf of pale limestone encloses it on three sides. The fourth opens onto a level expanse of silt.

There is no shore within the scene. Above it, several metres of clear water separate the hollow from the surface, which appears less like a ceiling than a bright region without detail. Daylight reaches the bottom comfortably. It has lost its warmth and most of its yellow, but there is enough of it to distinguish a green leaf from a brown one, to see the grain of the rock and the fine sediment gathered inside its cracks. Nothing requires a lamp.

The limestone is grey where freshly exposed, cream where a thin mineral coating has settled over it. Its forms are old and rounded. Broad grooves descend the inward-facing shelves, remnants of water moving through this stone long before the lake covered it. Some grooves have widened into shallow troughs; others end in smooth cups holding a darker grade of silt. There are no sharp ledges. Even the loose stones on the floor have worn edges, and most are buried deeply enough to seem part of the floor rather than objects resting on it.

Near the centre lies a single slab, almost flat, tilted slightly toward the open side. One end disappears beneath sediment. Along the other, the water has exposed a band of fossil shells within the limestone: small pale curves, mineral now, neither inhabited nor loose enough to move. The slab gives the hollow a centre without dividing it. There is still plenty of empty bottom around it.

Vegetation occupies only a few patches. Short pondweed rises from a seam where the silt is deep enough to hold roots. The plants are widely separated, their narrow leaves held close to the stems. Farther up the shelf, a dull green film follows the rougher parts of the rock, disappearing wherever the surface becomes too smooth. Much of the stone is bare. There are no dense beds, no drifting mats, no vegetation reaching all the way to the light.

The water is cold enough that contact with it would become an ache. Here, however, cold has no dramatic expression. There is no ice, no vapour, no glittering frost. It is present in the clarity, in the restrained growth, in the way the few leaves remain firm and almost still.

No fish cross the hollow. No snails cling beneath the slab, and no small bodies stir the sediment. The spaces between the plants remain empty. What first looks like movement beside a stem resolves into a slight difference in brightness on the stone behind it.

The sound takes longer to notice.

At first there seems to be none. Then a low, continuous vibration becomes distinguishable, more readily felt through the limestone than heard in the water. It comes from machinery far beyond the hollow: pumps and turbines in a waterworks on the other side of the lake. There are no pipes here, no cables, no concrete faces or discarded fittings. The machinery is present only through its steady transmitted bass and through the unusually constant level of the water.

This part of the lake lies below ordinary wave disturbance. The waterworks holds the lake within a narrow range, and a slow exchange with cold groundwater keeps the hollow nearly uniform in temperature. There is little current to bend the pondweed or lift the silt. Water passes through, but so gently that its passage has no visible direction.

Fine particles lie in the grooves where they settled. The slab’s outline stays clear. A thin dusting on the open floor grades into deeper sediment at the base of the shelf without a ridge or plume marking the transition. Everything has had time to find a resting place.

Looking upward provides no glimpse of the industrial shore. Looking outward provides only more lakebed, pale at first and then blue-grey with distance. The hollow remains a natural pocket of stone, plants, and water, quietly held within the operating range of a much larger system.

Its scale is modest, and its details do not multiply indefinitely. Once the grooves, the buried slab, and the scattered plants have been noticed, there is mostly space left to look at. Light rests on the bare limestone. The low vibration continues. The water remains clear enough that the far rim is still there, softened but legible, enclosing the same small area of cold, undisturbed bottom.
