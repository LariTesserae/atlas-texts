The town occupies a shallow depression no wider than a few minutes’ walk, though crossing it can take most of an afternoon. Streets change their drainage while you are on them. A dry passage opens beside a flooded one; a stair softens into a ramp; the house you meant to visit draws back its doorway and presents an unbroken, dripping wall.

From the rim, it looks spacious. Six or seven low buildings stand well apart, separated by broad green courts and sheets of open water. Nothing rises much above the height of a fruit tree. There are no towers, no sharp rooflines, no distant skyline beyond this little basin. The surrounding country is almost level, with long, exhausted folds that might once have been hills. Warm mist drifts over them. Here and there, a darker bulge indicates a settlement like this one, too far away to distinguish from an island of vegetation.

Everything underfoot is alive.

What first appears to be paving is a dense, fine-grained growth, green where light reaches it and the color of wet cork beneath overhangs. It yields slightly under a heel. In places it has worn smooth enough to reflect a face; elsewhere its surface is roughened into ribs, providing purchase beside the water. A cut edge reveals no earth beneath, only successive layers of pale fiber, translucent vessels, and older material compressed almost to stone.

There must once have been soil here. There must have been rock. Neither is accessible now. The town’s deepest well ends in a layer of living tissue that closes around the instrument lowered into it.

The age of the place is difficult to reconcile with its activity. Nothing has a fresh edge. Even the newest growth emerges rounded, its seams already softened, as though wear were part of the process that makes it. Doorsteps sag into gentle hollows. Roofs have the profiles of stones rolled for ages in a river. A broken wall does not show a wound for long: water passes over it, fibers swell, and by evening the break has become a shallow saddle, entirely plausible as something that has always been there.

Yet the town never stops rebuilding.

Near the center, a public hall is slowly exchanging positions with a pool. Its western chambers are sinking, empty windows descending toward the water. Along the eastern margin, columns come up in dark, glistening bundles. Their tops spread and join. A roof gathers between them, thin at first, trembling with the impacts of rain, then thickening until small animals can run across it. People carry benches from one end to the other. They appear mildly inconvenienced.

The town is not crowded. Most of the courts are empty most of the time. From a doorway you can see a single person crossing a distant causeway, or two figures waiting under an eave while the path ahead of them finishes forming. There is space around every building, broad intervals of warm air and unobstructed light. But there is almost no exposed surface that is not occupied by plant life. Even the water carries a transparent skin of tiny leaves, visible only when it wrinkles. The vegetation has become floor, shelter, drain, furniture. It leaves the air clear.

Rain falls frequently, sometimes from a sky that seems too bright to contain it. It arrives in short, hard bursts, filling the courts with bouncing droplets. Afterward, the buildings continue to shed water for hours. Some walls weep in a regular pattern of vertical lines. Others sweat all over. Beneath one long eave, droplets remain suspended in a level row, shivering when someone passes. They are part of the town’s waterworks. When the drinking cistern needs filling, the whole row slides sideways through the air and enters an opening in the wall.

The mechanism is invisible. There are no pipes to trace, no obvious reservoirs above the roofs. Water sometimes climbs a dry surface without wetting it, traveling in a narrow, rounded band. In the square, a fountain lifts a quantity of water about the size of a large animal and holds it waist-high, where people dip their cups. Its underside ripples against nothing. Fish occasionally enter it through a rising thread connected to the pool below, remain for a while in the suspended water, and descend again.

Much of the town’s engineering has this unshowy character. A footbridge keeps one bank dry while allowing the other to flood, although both banks lie at the same height. Certain rooms are larger inside than their roofs could cover, but only during heavy rain. The inhabitants keep marks beside their doors indicating which furniture must be brought inward before the rooms contract. On clear days, the surplus chairs sit outside in orderly rows.

Warmth comes from below as well as above. Bare feet feel it through the paving. After a storm, pale vapor rises from seams in the courts, carrying the smell of crushed stems, stagnant water, and something mineral that has no visible source. The air is humid without being oppressive. There is almost always a breeze, though it does not reliably agree with the movement of the clouds.

Animals are everywhere once you begin to notice them.

Flat-bodied fish browse the undersides of the floating leaves. Crabs, or creatures close enough to crabs, work along the drainage channels, holding their legs wide on the slick surfaces. Small bronze frogs occupy niches in the walls. When a doorway is about to move, they leave it several minutes in advance. Residents watch for this and take down the hanging shelves.

Above the open courts, pale insects fly in loose columns. Their shadows occasionally separate from them and travel a little farther before rejoining. Birds with narrow bodies and broad, ragged tails plunge into these gatherings. They nest in roof pockets that deepen around the eggs, then loosen and open when the young are ready to fly.

There are larger creatures in the deeper pools. You hear them more often than you see them: a splash with the weight of a thrown sack, followed by a low vibration through the ground. Once, beside the hall, a broad back rises above the water. It is covered in short green filaments, with two clean tracks where something has been grazing. The creature turns under the surface, and the pool pushes against its banks hard enough to lift a nearby bench.

The sound of the place changes abruptly. A quiet court may fill with the roar of water passing beneath its floor. Several buildings answer one another with hollow knocks as their internal passages realign. Frogs call in metallic bursts from the walls. During a heavy shower, every roof has a different note: a deep drumming on the hall, a dry rattle on the market shelter, a soft hiss on the sleeping houses.

At intervals, all this is interrupted by a cracking sound from underground. It is loud enough to stop conversation. The paving flexes. Water jumps in cups. Then a few people go to inspect the sluices, and everyone else continues what they were doing.

The inhabitants maintain a small market along the highest strip of ground. It is mostly empty stalls, widely spaced, each shaded by a single spreading leaf. Goods hang from hooks grown out of the stall posts: food wrapped in membrane, polished tools, bowls with seams that heal after breaking. One vendor sells lengths of root that will become handrails if fixed in the right position. Another has shallow trays of dark seeds, each kept apart from the others. No one touches these without permission.

The market is periodically dismantled by the weather. An afternoon downpour may send water across the high strip, loosen the stalls, and carry them into separate courts. By morning they have anchored themselves again. The vendors find them by the calls of the animals living underneath.

Near the basin’s edge is a quiet place that seems older than the town around it: an oval clearing, a low seat, a pool scarcely larger than a doorway. The ground here has a gray-green sheen and is harder than any of the streets. No shoots emerge from it. Rainwater crosses its surface very slowly, dividing into fine streams that follow grooves too shallow to feel with a fingertip.

People come here when the town is changing too quickly. You can sit with your back against the warm seat and watch roofs lift, passages close, and sheets of water shift across the courts. The activity is constant, but from this edge it acquires scale. A building moves the width of a room. A channel opens and takes a different route. Three birds follow the newly exposed water.

Late in the day, ordinary sunlight reaches beneath the cloud cover. The town becomes unexpectedly clear. Each wet wall shines. The wide spaces between buildings fill with gold air, and for a few minutes you can see the basin whole: small, low, nearly worn flat, with its inhabitants crossing from one uncertain doorway to another.

Then a tremor passes through the ground. The shallow pool beside the seat spills over its rim. Somewhere across the court a new passage opens with a long, tearing sigh, and warm rain begins again.
