The district is about the size of a market square, though there is no point from which you can see all of it. Buildings stand almost wall to wall, leaving narrow passages where daylight falls in pale, straight strips. Their roofs are level with one another. Their foundations are not.

Under the eastern houses, the ground has only recently become solid.

It is a gray, fine-grained material, cold enough to numb the hand through a glove. In places it resembles limestone; elsewhere it has the dull sheen of compressed ash. There are no pebbles in it, no fossils, no buried layers. A broken piece is uniform all the way through. If left on a windowsill for several months, it begins to acquire grain, then tiny internal seams. Eventually it may become recognizable as a kind of stone.

The older foundations rest on darker ground, already divided into thin strata. Along one exposed edge, beneath a laundry stair, a shallow hollow is slowly developing. Water has not carved it yet. Nevertheless, rain collects there, as though the hollow has begun to tell the rain where it will be.

The whole district lies low. Beyond its boundary walls is a flat, colorless expanse, usually lost in cold haze. No hills interrupt the horizon. There is no evident reason to have built here, and the city’s tightly packed structures suggest that whoever founded it expected a severe shortage of space.

Perhaps they were right. Inside the walls, distances are dependable. Outside, they are not.

A road leaves through the south gate. From an upper window you can watch it cross the plain for half a mile, turn behind a low bank, and emerge at the north gate. Walking it takes anywhere from twenty minutes to the better part of a season. The inhabitants use it only to deliver things that are not urgently needed.

Most supplies arrive through cupboards.

These are substantial fixtures, built into the oldest houses: shallow cabinets lined with ivory-colored ceramic, each with a brass latch and a small, fogged pane. Their interiors are ordinary until the door has been closed for a sufficiently long time. Then a second depth becomes available. Bread comes from one cupboard, machine parts from another, folded cloth from a third. Deliveries are wrapped, labeled, and sometimes warm. Nobody here knows the route they have taken.

The buildings themselves are meticulously maintained. Their narrow facades are faced with pale tiles, ribbed metal, and a black composite that catches daylight without reflecting an image. Pipes occupy the corners. Some are no thicker than string; others disappear into the paving like roots. Most carry neither fluid nor current, at least not in forms that can be measured locally. They keep rooms adjacent to their doors, preserve the direction of staircases, and prevent the upper floors from settling into earlier versions of the ground.

At each crossing stands a short post with a translucent cap. Something inside it moves very slowly, like a bubble rising through syrup. When it reaches the top, a street has been secured for another interval. A maintenance worker comes, removes the cap, and turns the chamber over.

There are many such small tasks. A city with so little room has no tolerance for a corridor becoming longer overnight.

Despite the machinery, the light is unremarkable. On clear days the square receives several hours of weak sunshine. You can distinguish the cream tiles from the gray ones, see dirt beneath a railing, notice the red wool of a coat hung outside a window. Cloud passes overhead in broad, ordinary sheets. Shadows are generally faithful to the things that cast them.

Only at the edges of the district do they become uncertain. The shadow of the boundary wall sometimes continues across the ground after the sun has gone behind a building. It appears to be waiting for a different object to finish casting it.

Cold occupies everything. It is not a blustering cold. There is rarely enough wind to stir a sleeve. It lives in door handles, in the spaces beneath cupboards, in the center of an apple brought indoors. Rooms are heated by flat disks fastened to the walls, but their warmth travels poorly through the air. People sit close to them. A chair left halfway across the room remains icy.

Outdoors, damp gathers rather than flows.

The undersides of handrails bead with water. Thin seams of ice line the meeting of two paving slabs. Once or twice a week a light rain falls, making the buildings briefly darker and producing a clean mineral smell from the young ground. Most of the rain is caught in gutters and stored in narrow tanks between the houses. What reaches the street gathers in shallow pockets, seldom large enough to reflect an entire window.

One pocket lies at the foot of a blue door. It has remained liquid through winters that froze the tanks solid. Its surface is black and still. If you bend over it, you see the blue door reflected from inside the house, with its latch on the other side.

Nobody drinks from that one.

There is vegetation, though it has to be looked for. A yellow-green crust inhabits the damp joints of the northern wall. Three small shrubs grow in a planter above the central passage, their branches cropped short to keep them from touching the opposite building. They carry tiny leathery leaves through every season. The roots have entered the planter’s drainage mechanism, and each year the shrubs produce a few transparent buds that are carefully removed with insulated tongs.

In the warmer stairwells, a moss the color of old pennies grows around the heating disks. Residents leave it alone unless it covers the controls. It takes many years to spread the width of a finger.

Animals are rarer. There are pale, jointed creatures beneath some of the drain covers, seen chiefly when a cover is lifted for repair. They withdraw without haste. A single bird visits the roofs at long intervals. It is small and dark, with the shape of a wagtail, but it never lands in the same moment as its shadow. First the shadow settles on a parapet. A little later the bird arrives and fits itself over it.

Its call is a dry double note. In this quiet place, people look up.

The district is inhabited more densely than its silence suggests. Windows are close together, and behind them are kitchens, workrooms, sleeping alcoves, shelves of tools. A person crossing a passage may pass within arm’s reach of six households. Yet little sound escapes. The walls take in voices and release their vibrations slowly through the foundations.

Occasionally, resting your hand on the stone beside a doorway, you can feel the cadence of a conversation held there years before.

Most audible sounds are small and present: a latch lifted, a brush on a doorstep, the faint tick of cooling pipework. In the square, a public clock has no face. Its mechanism stands exposed inside a glass shelter, turning through a succession of intricate positions. Once each afternoon, two parts meet with a soft click. Shops close at that sound.

The people have practical rules for living here. Do not open both doors of a vestibule at once. Do not follow a passage that has lost its drain. If a room appears empty when you know someone is inside, knock on the wall rather than enter. Leave cracks in the paving unfilled until their depth has been measured twice.

A crack may be damage. It may also be the first sign of a valley.

The geology is still deciding what kind of place this will be. Beneath the dense buildings, pressure slowly sorts the new material into beds and joints. The district’s engineers monitor this process through panels set into cellar floors. They adjust the weight of houses by fractions too small to feel, encourage a firm shelf beneath a stair, discourage a fault from passing through a water tank.

Construction and geology work on similar schedules here. A wall is built, and over decades the stone beneath it learns to bear the wall. A drain is installed, and the surrounding ground gradually develops a slope.

In one courtyard, enclosed by four tall houses, there is a patch of bare earth no larger than a dining table. It is kept uncovered. Nothing is planted there, and nobody is permitted to tread on it. Its surface is slightly convex, dusted white with frost most mornings.

For thirty years it has been rising.

The increase is barely visible. Residents compare it with marks on the courtyard wall, then return to their rooms. There is some disagreement about what it will become. A low hill, perhaps, or the bed of a future stream. The heating pipes have been routed around it. Windows facing it have been made a little taller.

Late in the afternoon, ordinary sunlight reaches down between the houses and rests on this small, unfinished piece of terrain. Condensation brightens on the pipes. A drain creature pauses below its iron cover. Somewhere a cupboard is closed to await tomorrow’s bread.

Then the light leaves the courtyard, and the ground continues its slow work beneath the frost.
