The district occupies a hollow in the seabed, no larger than a town square. From above, it looks like a dark knot of machinery caught in a shallow stone bowl. Nothing rises far: a few chimney stumps, a crane with its neck folded, the domed roof of a pumping house. Beyond them, the bottom opens into pale mud. Here, every available space has been used.

Water fills the streets, the buildings, the narrow intervals between pipes. It lies inside the clock faces and behind the enamel signs. There is no shore within sight, no surface light reaching down to establish a direction. You know which way is up because loose particles climb slowly past the windows.

The water is almost at freezing. Metal left exposed has the hard, faintly blue look of something that would take skin from a bare hand, though there are no bare hands here now. Salt has entered every seam. In the colder recesses, where a pipe elbows beneath a stair, thin plates of ice form and persist despite the pressure. They have grown around bolt heads and cable bundles, clear at their edges, milky where they enclose rust.

The streets are barely wide enough for two people to have passed. They bend around boiler housings, descend beneath loading platforms, end at doors whose bottom panels have disappeared into sediment. Each frontage has been built over another frontage. Brick shows beneath riveted sheet metal; stone lintels survive inside newer concrete walls. A gutter that once carried rain now supports a thick conduit, and the conduit has acquired its own brackets and smaller pipes. In places the accumulated infrastructure leaves only a person-sized passage through the water.

Nothing has a sharp edge. The steps are shallow dishes. The corners of buildings have been rounded by long movement of grit, and iron railings taper where the current has worked them. Even the breaks are old. A fallen wall has settled into its rubble so thoroughly that its shape resembles a bank of shingle, with the remaining window arch emerging like a worn bone.

The district is still operating.

Its activity is difficult to see at first. A broad piston moves behind the pumping-house window, crossing the opening over the course of several minutes. Nearby, a chain descends into a slot in the pavement, each link turning fractionally as it passes. On the crane, the unloaded hook rises the width of a finger, pauses, then lowers again.

These motions produce almost no sound. The engines are enclosed in thick housings, and their wheels turn through flooded bearings packed with a pale grease that has never entirely dispersed. A valve closing somewhere beneath the street makes a small knock. Long afterward, another answers from a different building. Between them is the quiet pressure of the surrounding sea.

The old system takes its power from that pressure. Water enters through the grates at the district’s outer edge, travels into buried chambers, and is admitted to deeper reservoirs in measured amounts. Counterweights lift as the chambers fill. When the current outside reverses, the weights descend and drive the shafts.

There must once have been louder machinery. Exhaust flues lead nowhere now; furnace doors stand open on rooms packed with mud. But the waterworks beneath them have continued, shedding whatever required attention. Belts have rotted away. Unnecessary pumps have seized. The surviving parts turn slowly enough to polish their own contact surfaces, and the grit they produce is caught in settling boxes before it can return to the bearings.

The settlement has become smaller without becoming less crowded. Silt occupies the ground floors. Collapsed roofs press down on workshops. What remains open is a layered network of passages: the streets above, service corridors beneath, low channels below those. Water can find routes that a body cannot. Sometimes a trail of sediment slips into a crack beside a doorstep and reappears several buildings away, emerging from a pipe in the wall of an upstairs kitchen.

There is little vegetation. At the warmest discharge mouths, short brown fronds cling to the metal, each scarcely longer than a fingernail. Their stems lie flat until the current changes, then lift together. Elsewhere there are only translucent films on glass and a sparse fur along the sheltered undersides of rails.

Small pale crustaceans occasionally gather at a grate. They stand motionless for so long that their arrival is noticed only when one is absent. Once in a while, a narrow fish passes through the district, following the open corridor between the crane and the pumping house. It does not explore the side streets. Its body catches a trace of green light, then becomes indistinguishable from the water ahead.

That light comes from the inspection lamps.

Most are dead, their heavy glass lenses clouded from within. A few still show dim green discs. One illuminates a pressure gauge behind a window; another hangs above a hatch whose locking wheel has fused to its frame. The lamps brighten when the buried counterweights reach the bottoms of their shafts. For a few seconds, balconies and pipework become visible farther down the street. Then the light subsides, leaving their distances uncertain again.

In those brief intervals, the windows show something peculiar.

Rooms that are plainly full of sediment reflect clear interiors. A workshop window, filmed with salt and facing a wall only an arm’s length away, reflects a long aisle between benches. In the pumping house, the glass sometimes shows a second piston moving behind the real one. Its stroke is shorter. It reaches the end first and waits.

The reflections do not follow an observer. They remain attached to their panes, visible from one angle and gone from another. Some have been obscured by mineral deposits; others have slowly changed as the glass has bowed inward. There is a stairwell window in which the reflected stairs now climb into the ceiling.

No one is present in any of them.

At intervals, the district releases its accumulated sediment. A row of narrow covers lifts along the lowest street, exposing black slots. Water draws downward. Mud loosens around the doorways and slides in soft folds toward the openings. For a little while, the thresholds are visible, along with boot scrapers, drainage grooves, the patterned tiles of a vestibule.

Then the covers settle. The street clouds over. Fine material begins collecting again in the same sheltered places.

The boundary of the district is marked by a low stone curb, worn almost flush with the bottom. On one side lies open mud, unlit and sparsely inhabited. On the other, conduits crowd together, gauges hold their needles steady, and old valves continue opening into the cold. There is no fence or gate. Only that modest line around a place which, even underwater, still keeps its water moving through streets.
