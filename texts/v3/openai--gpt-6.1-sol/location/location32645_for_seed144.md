The height is apparent first in the shadows of the clouds. They travel across the lower country far below, dimming whole valleys without ever touching the plateau. Up here, the sun remains unobstructed. Its light falls on pale stone, on the dull red backs of ridges, on bands of black rock polished by dust. Beyond the plateau’s broken rim, the horizon is a succession of blue distances.

It ought to be cold at this altitude. It is not.

Heat comes through the soles of your shoes. In some places the ground is merely warm; in others, you cannot rest a hand on it. The plateau is a thick, cracked lid over something still burning. Along its faults, dry air rises with enough force to make loose threads stand upright. Even at dawn the hollows are hot. By afternoon, the ordinary sun and the buried heat meet halfway, and the landscape seems to have no cool side.

There are mountains above it, though they do not look much like peaks. They are broad stacks of stone, terraced by old collapses, their tops flattened or scooped out. Between them extend shallow basins miles wide. From a distance these basins resemble empty flats. Close up, there is scarcely an unoccupied surface.

Mineral crusts overlap like old paint. Rust-colored nodules crowd the cracks. Loose slabs stand on edge, held upright by smaller stones packed beneath them. The exposed ground is webbed with the trails of animals, split by narrow drainage grooves, stitched with roots wherever a root can survive. Little heaps of shed wings collect in the lee of every projection. Bones, almost all small, lie in sheltered crevices where the wind cannot carry them away.

The plateau is densely inhabited without being lush. Its abundance is low to the ground, tucked into joints, folded beneath things.

Vegetation appears as exceptions. A shrub with grey, finger-thick branches grows out of a seam beside a vent, its few leaves held vertically against the sunlight. On the northern faces of boulders there are dark pads of moss no larger than a palm. A kind of grass grows in stiff circular tufts around damp depressions; the outer blades are dead and bleached, while the centers remain green. From above, these tufts look like little scorched targets.

Nothing grows tall enough to give useful shade.

The animals provide most of the movement. Flat-bodied lizards occupy the warm slabs in such numbers that a patch of rock may shift when you approach it. They rise on their toes to cool their bellies, then lower themselves again. Small pale beetles pass between them, carrying fragments of dried moss. There are burrowing mammals with broad, naked ears, and long-legged birds that move so slowly through the basins that at first they resemble stakes driven into the ground.

Higher up, dark birds circle in the rising air. They hardly flap. Sometimes a dozen hang above a single fissure, each at a different height, forming a loose column. Their shadows slide over the stone, small and precise in the hard light.

Almost none of this makes a sound. The insects do not chirr. The birds call chiefly at night, and even then infrequently. Footsteps are louder than the living country around them. A lizard’s quick departure produces a brief scrape, a bird’s landing two dry clicks. Most of the time there is only the faint friction of wind against your ears.

Water occurs in places that seem to have kept it by mistake.

A seam in a cliff stays dark all afternoon. Beneath an overhang is a basin of warm, clear water with a white rim. Several miles farther on, an entire gully may be damp underfoot, though no stream is visible. You can follow the moisture uphill until it ends at a patch of gravel no different from the gravel around it.

Much of the water comes out of the ground as vapor. It rises invisibly from the hottest cracks and condenses against stone that has cooled overnight. At first light, the undersides of certain ledges are hung with droplets. Animals arrive before the drops begin to fall. Beetles climb headfirst down the rock; birds stand beneath the lip, tilting their beaks; the little mammals press their mouths to wet seams. By midmorning the stone is dry again.

There is rain as well, but it comes in narrow traveling curtains. You can see one advancing from the far side of a basin, darkening a strip of ground perhaps half a mile wide. Often it never reaches you. Rain striking the hottest surfaces vanishes in a pale breath, while the cooler channels carry a sudden rush of brown water. For an hour the old drainage system becomes visible: tributaries wake, pools fill, tiny falls pour between terraces. Then the flow thins, leaving silt combed around pebbles and a sharp mineral smell.

The erosion here has had a long time to find its shape. The gullies are not raw wounds. Their banks are rounded, their bottoms fitted with stable beds of stones. There are ribs of harder rock that every flood goes around, and hollows that fill in the same order each time. Even the great fallen blocks have weathered shoulders. Fresh fractures stand out startlingly against all this settled form, pale and angular, as though someone has broken a piece of pottery in a room full of old furniture.

Human work is sparse, but the machinery reaches farther than the buildings.

Across several basins run cables supported on short iron trestles. They follow the contours rather than taking the shortest route, bending around vents and crossing gullies on suspended spans. Once or twice a day, small freight carriers move along them. Each is little more than a metal bucket beneath a wheel assembly, carrying mineral cake, replacement parts, or tightly rolled lengths of pipe. They move slowly enough that animals step aside rather than scatter.

There are isolated pump houses, their sheet-metal roofs bleached nearly white. Beside each stands a condenser: a tall bank of narrow metal fins, angled away from the midday sun. Pipes descend into the earth. Others run to covered cisterns sunk beneath stone platforms. The machines draw their power from the heat below and collect their water from the vapor they release.

You hear them only when you are close. A low hum comes through the walls. Now and then a valve closes with a soft knock, followed by a long pause. Outside, the fins tick as they expand in the morning warmth.

Most of the installations seem unattended. A pair of boots may stand beside a doorway. A cup hangs on a nail. Someone has planted three struggling shrubs in a trough made from half a pressure cylinder, and watered them carefully enough that salt has formed a white line around the roots. Maintenance paths lead away across the rock, marked not by paving but by the repeated absence of sharp stones.

Around the condensers, the animals gather. Drains feed little patches of greenery. Lizards lie against the water pipes rather than the steam pipes. Birds learn which valve releases a few drops before sunset. The pump houses have become fixed points in their daily routes, much as the old wet cliffs are.

Elsewhere, the heat moves.

A crack that has been cool for years may begin to breathe. Its moss yellows; insects leave it; a new mineral stain appears around its mouth. Across the basin, an active vent may fade until the birds stop circling above it. The changes are slow enough to notice over weeks, but sometimes there is a quicker disturbance: a tremor, a brief rise of dust, a pool suddenly clouded from below.

The workers test the ground before placing a new trestle. Old ones stand abandoned beside their replacements, their feet sunk in crust that has grown around them. A cable can be rerouted. A building is harder to move.

Toward evening, the light becomes warmer without losing much of its strength. Every small relief casts a long shadow, and the crowded surfaces resolve into separate things: scales, stems, shells, pebbles, the curled edges of salt. The animals begin crossing the open ground. There is more movement now than at noon, but still little noise. Hundreds of bodies pass between the same stones, following cool margins and damp seams invisible to a standing person.

Then the lower valleys disappear into haze. The distant mountains retain a narrow band of sunlight, and the plateau remains warm beneath your feet.

Under the first stars, drops begin forming beneath the ledges. One falls into a stone hollow with a sound clear enough to hear from several yards away. A small animal emerges from a crack and waits beneath the next one.
