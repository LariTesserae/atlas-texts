The water is warm enough that entering it would not feel like crossing a boundary. It is the temperature of skin, slightly warmer in the narrow passages, cooler over the broad flats. There is no shore within the basin. Its edges are submerged shelves of dark limestone, and beyond them the water deepens without changing color, because there is almost no light by which to see a change.

A little reaches the highest structures at certain hours: a gray dilution of darkness, sufficient to separate a roofline from the water above it. Far overhead, floating silt catches the daylight and keeps it. Down here, the day is known mostly by changes in the current.

The settlement occupies a low depression several miles across.

From above, if there were light enough, it would resemble a crowded town built around a network of canals. There are thick-walled enclosures, open courts, arches linking one mass to another, and broad avenues that converge on deep circular wells. Some districts are closely packed; others consist of long, low terraces divided by sediment. Nothing rises very high. The tallest towers lean outward at their bases, their upper portions worn down into rounded stumps.

No one designed the whole of it.

Small colonial animals have been building here for so long that their separate habits have acquired the scale of planning. Each colony adds a lip, a partition, a sheltered cavity. It follows the movement of food through the water. It leaves gaps where a current is too strong and thickens its walls where the current hesitates. Neighboring colonies meet, seal together, and continue growing along the same favorable edge. After generations, a feeding passage becomes a street. After centuries, the street has side courts, covered approaches, and drains.

The builders themselves are nearly invisible: soft brown bodies withdrawn into pale mineral tubes, each with a small crown that opens into the water. Their old chambers remain below them. Most of the city is dead foundation supporting a thin occupied surface.

Its materials are shell, limestone, hardened secretion, and mud.

Fresh growth has a faint waxy translucence. Older surfaces are cream-colored beneath a coating of charcoal silt. Where currents rub against corners, the stone is smooth enough to feel polished; where the water hardly moves, it carries a close nap of bacteria. Loose plates of shell collect in the alleys. They settle edge to edge, lift during a strong pulse of water, and settle again in different arrangements.

There are no sharp ruins. Even collapsed walls have been worked into stable heaps. The city has suffered floods within the flood, long episodes of burial, seasons when the warm springs changed course. Its damage is old enough to be inhabited. Broken arches provide better shelter than whole ones. A sunken district, half-filled with mud, supports more animals than the high terraces ever did.

Vegetation is scarce. On the uppermost roofs, where that weak daylight sometimes reaches, a few dark strands trail from cracks. They are short, tough, and widely separated. Farther down, the living films on the walls feed without sunlight, using what the springs bring up from the rock.

The springs do not erupt. Warm mineral water seeps through the basin floor and rises in slow, wavering columns. A hand held over a vent would feel the temperature increase before finding the opening. Around these places the buildings thicken. The colonial animals crowd close to the nourishment, and their shells take on yellow stains. Fine white bacterial threads extend across nearby stone and tremble when something passes.

These are the busiest districts, although they are almost silent.

Small blind fish travel along the underside of arches, keeping their bellies near the ceiling. Pale crabs occupy the joints where two walls meet. Shrimp gather in rooms with weak circulation, so numerous that the room is full of delicate contact: antenna against antenna, leg against shell, bodies continually making space for one another. A long eel moves through them, and a channel opens ahead of its head, closes behind its tail.

Nothing here needs to hurry all the time. Food arrives in pulses. Between them, animals wait.

At the mouths of the deeper wells, large filter-feeders cling in layered ranks. Their valves open slightly, revealing a dark seam of flesh. Along the open avenues, rays lie beneath the sediment with only the soft outline of their backs showing. They rise when prey passes close enough, lifting a sheet of mud that drifts between the buildings and temporarily makes every passage narrower.

The darkness is densely occupied.

If you stopped in one courtyard, you might first notice only the warm water and the walls. Then something would touch your ankle and withdraw. A roof would prove to be covered with small resting bodies. What seemed like a ragged seam in the masonry would flex, exposing the feet of an animal flattened into the crack. Above the court, fish would be circling without light, keeping their spacing through pressure changes.

Occasionally there is a brief green point. A shrimp turns, disturbed; a soft-bodied hunter brushes against a wall. The flash illuminates a patch of shell and two or three nearby faces, then disappears. Most creatures produce no light. Those that do seldom spend it.

Sound travels far here, but there is little to carry. A dry little click comes from a crab’s claws. Shell touches shell in a current. Deep in the foundation, a trapped pocket of gas releases a few bubbles; they rise through successive chambers with soft, irregular knocks. Once in a long while, a section of unsupported wall gives way. The sound spreads across the basin as a low grinding, followed by the faint patter of fragments finding the floor.

The larger changes are felt before they are heard.

Weather passes somewhere above. A storm alters the weight and movement of the water, and several hours later the avenues begin to draw more strongly. Suspended food descends. Feeding crowns unfold from thousands of small tubes. The city’s surfaces become feathery, though in the darkness this transformation is chiefly a change in texture, in the resistance offered to the flow.

Predators leave their hollows. Crabs climb to exposed corners. Fish enter streets they avoid during still water.

Silt pours gently into the lower districts while the upper ones are swept clean. A courtyard may fill to its thresholds over a season, forcing its inhabitants into the adjoining rooms. Elsewhere, a newly scoured opening connects two passages, and the changed current makes both of them useful again. Construction follows. Fresh tubes appear along the opening’s rim.

Outward from the center, the buildings become shorter and farther apart. There are broad fields of buried shell, a few low ridges, isolated colonies with wide clear water around them. Beyond these lies the basin wall, softened by age and crossed by the paths of bottom-dwelling animals. The city has no final boundary. Its outskirts end where feeding becomes too difficult to sustain another layer.

In the oldest quarter, almost everything living is above the original streets. Beneath the present courtyards are chambers filled with fine mud, then sealed arches, then earlier foundations. Warm water still moves through some of those spaces. Animals enter by openings too small to recognize as doors and emerge far away.

A spring under that quarter is slowly changing its course. One wall has begun to grow faster than its neighbors. Its feeding crowns remain open longer; shrimp collect along its base. Across the passage, an older colony is withdrawing into fewer and fewer tubes.

There is no visible mark of the change yet. The street remains broad, its floor smooth, its ceiling low and dark. But the water now presses more firmly against one side, and the next layer of the city is already forming there.
