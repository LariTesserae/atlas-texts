The ground is still learning how to be ground.

In the middle of the basin, it has hardened enough to hold your weight: dark, warm stone with a fine-grained surface, damp as the skin beneath a bandage. Toward the edges it becomes less certain. A heel sinks a little. A shallow footprint fills with clear water, then rises slowly from beneath until the print is gone. There is no mud here. What looks like mud is rock that has not finished deciding where its surface belongs.

The whole place is scarcely larger than a village square, suspended high above a belt of cloud. Three low ridges enclose it, with a fourth side opening onto the drop. Through that opening, the cloud sometimes parts to reveal a distant country of pale folds and moving shadows. There is no path down. The ridges are too new to have paths across them, too new even for loose stones. Their crests are rounded, seamless, and warm.

Above them hangs a thick, lightless-looking sky. It does give light, but grudgingly: a diluted pewter glow, bright enough to make water shine and too dim to cast a dependable shadow. Occasionally something enormous and geometrical passes behind the cloud. You see a straight edge advancing through the brightness, then an angle, then nothing. The animals stop calling while it passes. Their silence makes its size more apparent than the shape does.

Most of the time the basin is very loud.

Water clicks in hundreds of places. It beads directly onto the stone, swelling until each drop snaps loose and strikes another surface. Beneath that is a low, irregular drumming from inside the ridges. The sound travels through your soles. Sometimes it runs rapidly from one end of the basin to the other, like someone dragging a knuckle along a hollow wall.

And over everything are the animals.

They are difficult to count because so few remain visible. The open ground can appear almost empty, with three black birds standing around a puddle and a pale jointed creature working its way up a wall. Then a seam widens, and a hundred small bodies pour out.

Some are flat, six-legged, translucent at the edges. They travel in abrupt little rushes, stopping together whenever the stone drums beneath them. Others resemble shrimp with thick, folded wings, and spring from pool to pool without ever quite flying. There are soft white animals that extend only their mouths from holes in the rock, each mouth surrounded by a fringe that trembles in the damp air. When rain approaches, the fringes begin to hum. A whole ridge can hum at once.

The birds feed on whatever the seams release. They have long toes, blunt bills, and glossy bodies no larger than a fist. Their wings open into unexpectedly broad brown fans. They take off often, but rarely leave the basin; the warm air rising from the ground holds them just above it, so that much of their hunting takes place in a wavering hover.

The rain itself is warm. It starts without a change in the sky, usually as a few large drops with several seconds between them. Then the air becomes thick with falling water. Steam rises in low sheets where it hits the darker stone. The birds settle. The winged shrimp flatten themselves against the ground. The white mouths disappear.

Rain lasts four or five minutes. During that time, the basin acquires a geography.

A depression becomes a pond. Two ponds join. Water finds a low saddle between ridges and slips through it in a silver rope. Little islands emerge, each carrying a few startled animals. There are no established streambeds to guide any of this. The water spreads, hesitates, doubles back. You can watch a shoreline move across the ground faster than you could walk beside it.

Then the ground rises into the water.

At first this is visible only as a dimming of the pools. Pale mineral threads gather beneath their surfaces, branching upward. They thicken, knit together, turn smoky, turn solid. A puddle becomes a shallow dish. The dish grows a rim. Water spills over that rim into a neighboring hollow, where the same process has already begun.

Nothing here has been worn into shape. Every hollow is a pause in growth. Every ridge is something arriving.

There is vegetation, though you might cross the basin without recognizing it. A few reddish blades stand beside the coolest pools, thick as bootlaces and slick with condensation. Their tips are weighted with little clear sacs. When touched, the sacs contract and pull the blade down into the water. Elsewhere, a gray mat occupies the sheltered side of a stone lip. It grows so slowly that the lip has begun to lift it into the air. Its exposed roots hang like short, wet threads.

These small patches attract a disproportionate amount of life. Beetle-sized grazers work between the red blades. The birds wait nearby. A long animal, almost invisible except for the movement of water around its back, rests beneath the gray mat with its head pointed toward the feeding ground. Once in a while its jaws close with a sharp wooden clap. Afterward the grazers keep feeding, but farther apart.

There are three structures.

One stands close to the drop: a narrow shelter of pale panels, open on two sides, with room for perhaps four people to sit. Its roof is dry even in heavy rain. Drops stop a finger’s width above it, spread into trembling disks, and slide sideways into a gutter that is not attached to anything. At the gutter’s end, the water vanishes with a soft hiss.

The second structure is only a door.

It stands upright on the highest ridge, mounted in a frame of dark metal. Through the frame you can see the sky. Open the door, however, and there is a dim interior with a red floor and a railing leading down. Warm, stale air comes out. Somewhere below, a machine turns with a steady knocking sound.

No one seems to use it. Small animals shelter behind the frame, but none crosses the threshold. The birds will land on the top edge and look down into the opening, their heads tilted, before retreating to the ridge.

The third structure lies nearly buried at the center of the basin. It consists of six porcelain-colored posts surrounding a metal disk. The disk is embossed with lines too fine to follow, and its center remains perfectly dry. Each post has a notch near the top. Something connects the notches, though it cannot be seen directly: rain breaks against it; a flying insect turns aside; your hair lifts when you stand too close.

When the posts begin to sing, the ground becomes active.

Their note is low at first, almost lost in the dripping. It rises until loose water shivers across every exposed surface. The birds lift away. The jointed animals retreat. Beneath the pools, the mineral threads grow visibly longer.

You can see how carefully the machinery works. A rising ridge stops just short of the red blades. New stone curls around the buried shelter footing without covering it. Openings remain where the white animals have withdrawn. The basin is being made with its inhabitants still inside it.

Not all of them survive the work. Sometimes a seam closes before the little translucent bodies can escape. The birds gather at such places afterward. Sometimes a new pool forms over an old breathing hole, and the white mouth beneath it sends up a single necklace of bubbles.

There are older marks on the shelter’s inner panels: scratches at several heights, a handprint in dark pigment, a row of small drilled holes. Someone has set a cup beneath one of the drips. It is almost full. Beside it, a folded seat adjusts its angle whenever the ground tilts, preserving a level place to rest.

From that seat, the changes are easier to watch.

The space remains small enough to know intimately. You can recognize an individual bird by its damaged bill, follow one pale animal from ridge to ridge, notice that a particular pool always forms first. Yet by evening the hollow you watched that morning may be a mound, and the mound may have split to release a stream of warm water.

As the sky darkens, light appears beneath the newest surfaces. It is faint and amber, scattered in patches where the stone is thin. The grazing animals gather over it. Their translucent legs glow. The long predator moves from pool to pool, leaving a wake that shines for a moment and goes dark.

Beyond the open edge, clouds rise until the distant country disappears. The shelter gutter hisses. The door knocks quietly on its frame though there is no wind.

Then the six posts take up their note again, and beneath all the crowded cries and clicks, the basin answers with the deep sound of another layer coming into place.
