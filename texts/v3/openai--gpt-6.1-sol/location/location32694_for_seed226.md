The town occupies a shallow cup near the top of the mountain, just below the height where trees cease to grow. You can walk its circumference in half an hour, though there is no path that follows the whole rim. In places the boundary is a low wall; elsewhere it is a bank of fern, a stand of pale trunks, or simply the point where the ground ends and the clouds become visible beneath it.

Above, the sky is clear enough to show blue between long, loosely woven clouds. Below, weather conceals the valleys. Sunlight falls on the town with the plain brightness of late morning, showing every knot in the timber and every bead of water along the grass.

There is much construction here, and very little to fill it.

Covered walkways lead between terraces. Stairs divide and meet again. Narrow bridges connect upper floors across gaps where there are no lower floors. There are public basins, roofed market bays, a long building with six entrances, and platforms clearly intended for houses. Most are empty. Through their posts you can see the open space at the center of the town: a wide, bare court with one low stone swelling through its floor.

The buildings have not fallen into disuse. Their beams are fresh. Peg heads still show the pale marks of a cutting blade. Bundles of split wood wait beneath roofs, weighted against the wind. Yet a person appearing at the far end of a gallery looks unexpectedly small, as though they have entered a place prepared for a much larger gathering.

The mountain is still making this cup.

Along the inner slope, new stone emerges in rounded folds, smooth and dark with moisture. It has none of the shattered, granular look of exposed rock. There are no screes beneath it, no gullies cut into its face. Its surfaces resemble the backs of large roots pushing up through soil. Some are cool and firm; some have a slight give beneath a thumb. Those softer places are fenced with cord.

Water beads from the stone, gathers in its shallow creases, and enters wooden channels. The supply is modest but persistent. Even after several clear days, the channels keep running. Their edges wear thick fringes of moss, and the soil nearby is black, springy, and wet enough to retain a footprint until evening.

People here do not quarry much. They set wickerwork around the places where rock is rising.

A low retaining wall begins as a woven frame filled with wet mineral paste. Stakes keep it upright; broad leaves are laid against it to prevent the paste from escaping. Over several seasons, the material firms. The wicker is cut away where it can be reached and left enclosed where it cannot. Older walls bear the imprint of reeds, the curve of a leaf rib, occasionally the outline of someone’s palm.

Nothing is quite square. A stair changes its rise by small amounts from year to year. Door lintels are lashed rather than nailed, so they can be lifted. The galleries stand on wooden feet that can be shortened or extended. Beneath several buildings, stone is slowly approaching the floorboards.

Much of the town’s work consists of noticing these changes in time.

The vegetation occupies the edges lavishly. Mountain alder leans over the outer parapets. Ferns crowd the stair landings where water splashes, their fronds nearly waist-high. Small-leaved vines spread across roof poles and hang down in cool curtains. Thick grass grows in troughs suspended from galleries, and beds of sorrel, cabbage, and dark blue flowers fill the narrow strips between channels.

From the court, though, all this green remains peripheral. It climbs, spills, and gathers along the walls, leaving the middle conspicuously open. There is very little overhead except sky. Even the walkways, seen from below, are slim lines of timber with large intervals between their supports.

The emptiness makes the sound surprising.

Water falls through a clay pipe behind the long building, only the width of two fingers, but its impact resounds through a cavity under the court. The mountain has hollows in it. Their openings are small and usually hidden by moss or fitted with wooden grilles; their interiors must be much larger. A dropped bucket makes a deep report. Footsteps on one particular stair carry across the entire cup. Wind entering a slit beneath the eastern terrace produces a sustained, uneven roar.

Conversation is difficult in the open. People lean close or step behind the heavy planted screens around the basins. From a distance, you see mouths moving without hearing words, while a mallet striking a peg on the opposite side of town arrives sharp and loud.

There are few animals. One grey bird comes to drink from the highest trough and leaves as soon as someone approaches. Slugs shelter under the channel boards. On warmer afternoons a handful of small flies appear above the garden beds, but today the air is cold, and nothing moves there except leaves disturbed by dripping water.

The people wear layered wool and keep their sleeves tied back while working. Their tools are simple: adzes, knives, stone weights, wooden wedges, a few narrow iron blades sharpened almost to nothing. Under one roof, a woman splits willow for a wall frame. Farther along, two people lift a water channel onto a higher pair of supports. They work slowly, stopping to look along its length before fastening it.

Beyond them are more roofs, more empty bays, more stairways ending at platforms open to the weather.

It is unclear how many of these places await occupants and how many have been built because the ground beneath them has begun to rise. On the sheltered side of a wall, a row of marks records the height of the central stone. The oldest marks are low enough to be hidden by a fern. The newest is scarcely a finger’s breadth above the last.

By afternoon, cloud begins to climb the outside of the mountain. It enters between the alder trunks, touches the undersides of the galleries, and leaves droplets on the hanging vines. For a while the court remains sunny. The wet roofs shine; the open doorways stay dark.

Then mist reaches the parapet. The town’s outer stairs disappear first, followed by the valley view between the posts. Water continues sounding beneath the bare court. Someone walks the channels with a wooden scoop, clearing leaves from each junction before the light goes.
