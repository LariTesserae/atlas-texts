The place is no larger than a small hamlet, though it has no streets that lead away from it. It lies low, in a shallow basin of warm red light, and is crowded almost to the point of touch: roofs against boughs, boughs against balconies, balconies against the backs of large, motionless animals. From one end you can see the opposite boundary, a wall of pale tiles rising just above the tallest trees. Reaching it takes most of an afternoon.

There is no water. Not under the paving, not in the sealed jars, not in the glossy fruit. A broken stem gives off a little heat and a smell like freshly opened walnut shells. The deep green of the foliage is not the green of wetness. It is the color that certain minerals turn when they are folded thin enough for light to pass through them.

Everything here was made by hand.

The floor is laid in broad clay slabs, each bearing the thumb marks of the person who flattened it. There is no earth beneath them, only smaller slabs, and beneath those, wedges, beads, hollow bricks, and the knotted cords that hold the lower layers together. You can see into this foundation where a paving stone has been lifted. It goes down perhaps three feet before meeting the underside of the floor again.

The workmanship is simple and extraordinarily patient. There are no engines, no wires, no devices more complex than a spindle or a hinged jaw. Stone knives lie beside reed-shaped clay knives. Wooden mallets have been replaced so often that some consist entirely of repairs. Wherever a surface would otherwise be blank, someone has scored it with a comb.

Most things are new. The corners of the houses are sharp enough to catch a sleeve. Fresh chisel strokes stand out on the doorframes, and chips remain beneath the smallest trees. Nothing has been softened by rain or a river. Even the dust is angular. It gathers in little fans at the bases of walls, where animals have rubbed against them or a branch has slowly ground on a roof.

Yet the place feels neither unfinished nor recently occupied. The inhabitants, if they are inhabitants, have always been doing what they are doing now.

In the central court, a woman is shaping a leaf.

She sits beneath the tree it will belong to, with a board across her knees and a bowl of green powder beside her. She rolls the powder into a sheet, folds in ribs made from fine splinters, and pinches the edge into a sequence of shallow teeth. The work takes a long time. Occasionally she holds the leaf against the light. When it is finished, she attaches it to a low branch with a loop of dark thread.

By evening the thread is gone. The branch has grown into the small hole she made.

This is how the grove accumulates.

Its trunks are thick, close-set, and heavily worked. Some are assembled from coils of fired clay; others are bundled reeds with bark fitted over them in narrow strips. A few are made from stacked rings of bone-colored material, each ring slightly larger than the one above it. Their roots fasten around paving stones, balcony posts, benches. They do not seek anything below. They seek things that can be held.

The foliage is abundant. Broad leaves overlap in deep curtains. Fine leaves hang in sprays so dense they resemble fur. There are low plants whose leaves are upright cups, all empty, and creepers that join one building to another with broad, fleshy ribbons. The ribbons are warm to the touch. Against the skin, they tighten very slowly, as if trying to learn its shape.

You have to lift branches to pass between the houses. Some leaves are so large that they serve as shutters. Others have been woven into roofs while still attached to their trees. Beneath these roofs the light is ordinary daylight, green at the edges and bright wherever a leaf has split. There is no luminous fog, no blazing sky. Shadows lie plainly across the paving.

Only after a while do you notice that they do not quite belong to what casts them.

A low shrub has the shadow of a ladder. A chair casts the outline of a sleeping animal. The woman making leaves has two shadows, both busy with their hands, though one has already finished.

The air is warm and dry enough that lips begin to feel like unglazed pottery. Shade offers relief, but only a little: the walls and trunks release heat throughout the day. There is no wind. Now and then a slow pressure passes through the court and every hanging leaf inclines the same way. It takes several minutes. By the time the last leaf moves, the first has returned to rest.

Animals occupy nearly every available ledge.

Small six-legged creatures, the color of burnt flour, browse along the leaf margins. They leave neat rectangular gaps. Their mouths are hard little punches, and the pieces they remove are swallowed whole. Later, beneath the balconies, they lay green pellets that someone gathers for the leaf-maker’s bowl.

Long-bodied animals lie inside the walls, visible through open joints in the masonry. Their skins look like worn leather. They have flat heads, no obvious eyes, and a habit of moving one forefoot at a time. A single step may take an hour. When they finally settle, the wall settles with them.

There are birds, or things that use branches as birds do. Their wings are pleated rather than feathered, and their feet fit the ridges of the bark with unsettling precision. They fly only short distances. Most of the time they sit in pairs, facing away from one another, their tails wrapped around the branch between them.

They never call.

The loudest sound is usually a seed casing opening: a small dry tick somewhere overhead. After that, a seed drops into the foliage and may take several minutes to reach the paving, tapping from leaf to leaf. Other sounds are close and easily missed—the rasp of a feeding mouth, the touch of a knife on a board, a cord stretching inside a beam.

Fruit hangs thickly in the lowest part of the grove, where the buildings are barely separate from the trees. The fruit is pale yellow, heavy, and faintly translucent. Cut one open and you find no pulp or juice, only tightly packed flakes arranged around a hollow center. The flakes dissolve on the tongue with a taste of toasted grain and something metallic.

The hollow center is not always empty. Sometimes it holds a tiny chair. Sometimes a ladder with four rungs. Sometimes a sleeping creature, folded so carefully that it cannot be removed without waking it.

These things are carried indoors.

The houses have shallow steps, low ceilings, and rooms arranged around patches of dense growth. Their walls are plastered over wicker frames. You can see where hands pressed the plaster into place, and in some rooms you can still make out the outlines of those hands beneath the final coating.

Inside, shelves hold unfinished animals. A jointed leg rests beside a carved jaw; a body waits for its second side. There are trays of eyelids, clay ear cups, bundles of antennae. None of the parts are grotesque. They have the quiet, practical look of useful objects awaiting assembly.

A newly completed animal stays on its shelf for a day or two. Then it changes position. Someone opens the door and puts a little food on the threshold.

No one seems to remember making the larger creatures. One occupies almost the whole of a narrow courtyard, its pale back supporting a stand of saplings. It resembles an ox only in the weight of its head and the patience with which it carries it. Its legs are out of sight. Once each season, it lifts its chin far enough for someone to sweep beneath.

Along the boundary wall, the construction is still proceeding.

Tiles are stacked beside a stool. A shallow basket holds pegs carved from seed husks. The wall’s newest section is unmistakable: crisp joints, fresh white surfaces, no grazing marks. A man works there with a mallet, fitting one tile at a time.

From the court, the wall seems straight. Up close, it is made of very small turns. Each new tile faces a slightly different direction. If you follow its base, the houses remain on your left, but the central grove occasionally appears on your right as well. A window you passed ten minutes ago opens ahead of you, now at ground level.

There is a gate. It is no larger than a household door and has a wooden latch polished by use.

Beyond it is a short passage lined with the backs of the same houses. There are baskets outside the doors, a tree leaning over a roof, a bird sitting with its folded wings. At the passage’s end you enter the central court, beside the woman’s work board.

The gate is useful chiefly for deliveries. People carry tiles through it, and bowls of green powder, and sometimes a sleeping animal too large to bring through a house.

The place changes slowly. A roof gains a little thickness. A root takes possession of a loose stone. Over weeks, an animal becomes less distinguishable from the wall in which it sleeps. Leaves acquire scars where they brush together, and these scars are copied onto the next leaves made beneath them.

Even the distances are adjusted by small amounts. A bench may be farther from a doorway than it was last year. A passage narrows without either wall appearing to move. In the densest corners, where branches and eaves and bodies press together, there are pockets of space that have never been used. They remain cool and dark. You can reach into one up to the shoulder, though from the other side it is no wider than your hand.

At dusk, workers put down their tools. The birds turn once on their branches. Warmth rises from the slabs and carries the scent of ground leaves through the low rooms.

The woman ties her last leaf into place. Nearby, a small animal steps out from beneath her stool, carrying a curl of green offcut in its mouth. She lifts her feet to let it pass.

Above her, the new leaf holds still. Then, while the rest of the grove remains motionless, it bends a little toward the light.
