The city stands only a little above the tide. From the seawall, its highest ground looks like an accident: a low ridge of old dredging spoil, rounded by rain, with a road laid along its back. Everything else is level enough that a spilled cup of water would have trouble deciding where to go.

Heat remains in the concrete after sunset. It comes through the soles of your shoes and rises from the walls of warehouses, carrying the smells of salt, warm iron, diesel, and the dark water beneath the quays. A storm has passed offshore, but its cloud still covers the sky. There is no bright horizon. The sea and the low ceiling of cloud are different shades of the same dull pewter.

The port extends for several miles: sheds, pumping stations, tanks, loading aprons, enormous shallow yards. Between its structures lie expanses of almost nothing. You can see a lone forklift moving on the other side of a basin and watch it for minutes before it disappears behind a building. Wide roads connect districts that seem too far apart to belong to one city. Their painted lanes have faded into the pale aggregate, and their gutters are full.

There are no trees. There is no grass along the fences, no vine on the disused stairways, no green fringe at the water. Salt dust, paved ground, and regular scraping have kept the port bare for generations. Where pavement has broken, the exposed soil is gray dredged silt, compacted and crusted. The few places that might hold roots are washed by brine from the salt works or scoured by water released from the flushing mains. Along the busiest roads, a maintenance tractor has recently left broad brush marks in the wet grit.

Yet almost every place where the water touches something is occupied.

Under the quay ladders, mussels crowd the rungs until their original shape is difficult to see. Barnacles make a rough white collar around every piling. Small crabs stand in the cracks of the seawall, their bodies nearly indistinguishable from the wet concrete until they move. As you approach, a whole stretch of wall seems to loosen and withdraw.

The storm drains are alive with mosquitoes. They gather beneath covers and in the lee of pump houses, thickest where fresh rainwater floats above the salt. At the lamps they become a fine, restless grain in the air. Geckos wait on the illuminated walls, spaced just beyond one another’s reach, and strike so quickly that you usually see only the swallow afterward.

The larger animals are less immediately visible.

Fish fill the deep basins. Their presence shows as pressure on the surface: long patches of water that wrinkle against the breeze, silver turns below a sluice, the sudden opening of a dark circular hole where a larger fish has taken something. When a pump begins discharging, small fish gather at the outlet. Bigger fish arrive behind them. Sometimes the water there looks crowded enough to stand on, but twenty yards away it is smooth.

The city’s emptiness is mainly above the waterline.

Most of its work happens inside metal housings, behind insulated doors, or beneath the streets. The desalination plant occupies three low buildings with no windows. Its intake runs out under the seabed; its pipes come ashore unseen. A distant vibration reaches the seawall through the ground. Near the pumping station, it is strong enough to tremble the puddles in the bolt recesses of an inspection cover.

Elsewhere there is very little sound.

A cable taps a mast. Water falls from a broken gutter into a flooded loading pit. Somewhere across the basin, a truck backs briefly, then stops. The heat seems to discourage unnecessary movement. Even the gulls sit widely separated on the roof ridges, bills slightly open, making only occasional complaints when another bird lands too close.

At the edge of the outer harbor, wading birds stand in the exposed mud. There are thousands of them, distributed across a distance large enough to make them look sparse. Each has a small area of its own, each walking, pausing, probing. Seen from the road, the mudflat resembles an empty surface stippled with punctuation. Through binoculars it is all work: bills entering burrows, little runs after escaping shrimp, arguments settled with a raised wing.

The ground here has been worn into forms that no longer change much. Rain follows shallow channels across the yards, channels too broad and smooth to be called cracks. Steps have dished centers. The corners of masonry buildings are rounded where generations of carts passed too close. Rust has swollen railings, split their paint, and then washed down the walls in overlapping stains.

Repairs are everywhere, but they are modest. A new square of concrete lies inside an old square. A stainless-steel valve has been fitted to a pipe whose flanges are thick with corrosion. Beside a warehouse door, fresh sealant follows a joint that has opened and been filled many times before.

The oldest waterfront street floods at high tide. No one attempts to keep it dry anymore. Its electrical cabinets stand on raised plinths, and the warehouse thresholds have low ramps for carts and boots. The tide enters through the drains, spreads a few inches deep, and carries tiny fish along the curb. As it retreats, crabs appear from beneath the parked trailers to search the wet road.

This evening the tide is still rising.

Water has reached the lower flight of steps at the ferry landing. A loose plank lifts, settles, and lifts again. Farther inland, the first drain covers begin to sweat salt water through their holes. Rainwater from the roofs is still arriving in the same system, so the channels briefly run both ways: pale runoff spreading across the darker tide, each carrying its own sediment.

Along the ridge road, the salt works is coming on for the night. Lamps brighten inside the sheds. A conveyor starts slowly, its belt emerging from a wall and crossing an empty yard on a line of steel supports. Wet salt rides beneath its hood. At the far end, a loader moves in a white interior haze, visible only when it passes the open door.

The cooling channels behind the works are warm enough to fog in the slightly cooler evening air. Little rises from them now, but moisture collects on the handrails and hangs beneath the pipes. The hottest discharge channel is almost bare. Farther along, where seawater mixes into it, shrimp flick through the shallows and fish hold steadily in the current. Herons wait at the change in depth.

After the lamps come on, bats begin working the spaces between the buildings. They pass low over the drainage trenches, then climb sharply around the light poles. Rats move along the wall bases and beneath the loading platforms, rarely crossing the open yards. From one dark warehouse comes the small, dry rustling of pigeons shifting on roof beams.

There is no single center of activity. The port keeps moving in separated pockets: a pump cycling, a belt running, fish gathering at an outlet, birds feeding ahead of the tide. Between them are long stretches of damp pavement where nothing seems to happen.

By the time the light has gone, the seawall is slick beneath your hand. The air is still hot. Across the basin, a worker opens a door, steps into a rectangle of yellow light, and empties a bucket into the harbor. The splash is brief. Something turns below it.

Then the door closes, and the water resumes its small, crowded movements against the pilings.
