Beneath a flat red stone, no broader than a sleeping mat, there is a hollow full of green.

The stone lies in an otherwise barren salt field. Nothing on its upper face distinguishes it from the thousands around it: the same dull crust, the same chipped corners, the same white dust packed into its shallow grooves. But its underside is lifted a hand’s breadth above the ground, and beneath that narrow lip the salt gives way to a descending seam. The seam opens into a chamber small enough that a person could touch both walls with outstretched arms.

It is warm down there. Not the fierce warmth of the surface at noon, but the close, settled warmth inside a loaf freshly broken. There is no moisture in it. The air has the dry, bitter smell of crushed stems, hot pottery, and something faintly medicinal.

The chamber’s floor is pale mineral, newly fractured. Thin plates overlap like scales, their edges still sharp enough to cut skin. Here and there a crack exposes orange crystal beneath the white. No grains lie at the foot of these fractures. No edge has rounded. The hollow seems to have opened very recently, though nothing suggests it is still opening.

Most of the floor is bare.

The greenery occupies five separate islands, with broad stretches of empty mineral between them. Each island is extravagantly dense. Leaves crowd leaves; thick stems fork into thinner stems, which carry frills, combs, fans, soft-looking tassels. One growth resembles a fern rolled tightly around a fist. Another has broad, dark blades stacked horizontally, with crimson undersides visible through the gaps. A third hangs from the ceiling in a swollen green braid, ending just above the floor.

None of these plants contains sap.

A broken-looking stem exposes a dry interior of fine golden threads. The leaves have the texture of thin leather on one side and powdered stone on the other. They hold their shapes without the least tremor. Their roots spread over the floor rather than burrowing into it, following the seams in flat, intricate patterns. In places the roots meet a crystal edge and divide into filaments so fine they look like a stain.

They seem to feed upon the arrangement of the minerals: the difference between one crystal face and another, the pressure of a joint, the particular angle at which a plate rests against its neighbor. Yet nothing is used up. The orange beneath the white remains orange. The roots remain full.

The light comes from no identifiable lamp. It gathers weakly along leaf margins and under the mineral plates, a greenish dusk sufficient to separate nearby shapes but not to reveal the whole hollow at once. Looking into a clump of foliage, one finds a succession of smaller spaces: a pocket behind a blade, a slit between two stems, a chamber enclosed by a curled frond. Each contains animals.

There are more animals than the first glance allows.

Flat, six-legged creatures cling beneath the leaves in overlapping rows. Tiny pale bodies fill the tassels. A narrow-backed animal, the color of fired clay, lies along a root with its forelimbs raised. On the ceiling hang clusters of soft, jointed things resembling seed pods until their many feet become visible. Their eyes are black and polished. Some have their mouths open. Some are bent over eggs. One holds a smaller creature between delicate hooked limbs.

All are motionless.

They do not look dead. There is tension in every posture, weight borne by every gripping foot. Their bodies are neither shriveled nor stiff. The little hunter has its abdomen lifted clear of the root. A wing is spread beneath a leaf, showing a fine network of green veins. These are living positions, complete in themselves, with no next position waiting to follow.

And the hollow is loud.

A deep, continuous note fills the space, felt first in the teeth. Above it lie sharp insect tones, close enough together to grate against one another. There is a sound like hundreds of dry husks being crushed, and beneath that, the heavy wooden resonance of something being struck in a much larger room.

No sound begins or stops. There is no beat to count, no call followed by an answer. The open mouths of the animals remain open; the raised wings remain raised. The noise is simply present, as heat is present. A hand held near the entrance feels its pressure against the palm.

Among the green islands are a few small constructions.

Three chips of salt stand upright around a shallow depression. A strip of dark fiber binds two stems together. Beneath the hanging braid sits a bowl no larger than a thumbnail, made from clay pressed thin by fingers. It contains a single black seed. Beside it lies a tool: a sliver of crystal fitted into a split twig and secured with a winding of root.

These things are crude but carefully made. The bowl’s rim has been smoothed. The upright chips face inward. The binding crosses itself twice before disappearing into the foliage.

There are no builders to be found. Perhaps they are among the animals; perhaps the chamber holds another hidden scale of life, too small to distinguish from the powder on a leaf. The little tool looks ready to be picked up. Nothing on it has gathered dust.

The longer one looks, the less the place resembles a moment arrested halfway through an event. There is no evident strain in it, no weather approaching, no growth pressing toward its next stage. The eggs do not await hatching. The fractures do not await collapse. The bowl is neither abandoned nor in use.

Everything has reached an exact, lasting condition.

Above, heat passes across the salt field and shadows change their length. Beneath the stone, the same five masses of foliage occupy the same clear intervals. The hunter holds its small prey. The black seed rests against the curved clay. The fresh mineral edges remain sharp in their faint green light.

The hollow continues to sound, densely and fiercely, without spending any of itself.
