At first the country looks like a plain beneath low clouds. Then a break opens in the cloud, and you see another plain far below it, dim and warm-colored, with an entire weather system moving across its surface. The place where you are standing is very high.

There are no peaks to announce this height. They have worn away. The plateau is the remnant of a mountain range so old that even its roots have been rounded: a cold, broad expanse of gray-green stone, shallow basins, and rises that take several days to cross without ever seeming steep. Here and there, a dark seam marks the former position of a cliff. It lies flush with the ground now. Water passes over it without falling.

The sky is usually bright enough to see by, though rarely clear. Clouds move at the height of a person’s chest, shredding around low stone walls and collecting in the hollows of worn paving. Above them there is pale daylight. Below the edge of the plateau, there can be thunderstorms whose flashes arrive without thunder.

Cold enters everything gradually. A tool left outside does not frost at once; first its handle becomes difficult to recognize by touch. Then a white grain gathers in the joints. Breath condenses on wool and wood. Even in the growing season, the underside of a stone is cold enough to stiffen a wet finger.

Yet the vegetation is rich.

It grows close to the ground, in deep beds separated by enormous intervals of bare country. From a distance, these beds look like spilled ink. Within them are layers of narrow leaves, glossy cushions, translucent stems, and broad plants whose folded surfaces retain a little warmth. Black peat fills depressions no deeper than a bowl. Roots travel beneath the stone crust and reappear a mile away, so that what seems to be an isolated garden may be the exposed part of a much larger organism.

Nothing needs an animal to carry its seed. Some plants cast grains into the low cloud; others extend runners along the old mineral seams. A silver creeper separates into pieces at the first hard freeze. Each piece opens slightly in daylight and closes at dusk, working itself across the ground over the course of a winter.

There are no birds above the beds, no insects among them, no tracks leading down to water. When a leaf has a hole in it, the hole is from ice, abrasion, or a fault in the leaf’s own growth. The absence becomes more noticeable the longer you stay. There is nothing to startle from cover.

Moisture comes intermittently. Weeks can pass under a dry, milky sky. Then condensation begins forming on the northern faces of stones, and the whole country darkens by degrees. Rain, when it comes, is fine and cold. It may cross the plateau in a band only a hundred paces wide, traveling for months without widening or breaking. People wait for these bands beside cistern mouths with wooden gutters already laid out.

The rivers are shallow and widely separated. Most occupy channels much broader than they need, polished troughs inherited from a wetter age. A narrow stream wanders through the middle, stopping in pools, losing itself beneath mats of vegetation, emerging clear. In places its current carries water uphill.

This is one of the first things a traveler must learn: the slope you can see is not always the slope the water obeys.

Some surfaces retain an earlier inclination. Rain runs along it, though the stone has since settled another way. In the oldest districts, builders test every foundation with a cup of water before placing a wall. A house erected according to sight alone may collect its cooking smoke in a lower corner or find that everything dropped on its floor rolls toward the ceiling beam.

The cities occupy relatively little of the country. Their streets do not.

Long paved ways cross hundreds of miles of open plateau, connecting clusters of houses, storehouses, gardens, and meeting courts. A settlement may consist of thirty buildings around a sheltered green hollow, with its next inhabited street two days away. Nevertheless, both belong to the same city, share the same water calendar, and maintain opposite ends of the same avenue.

The avenues are older than any living name. They are made of slabs too large to have been moved by the present inhabitants. Between them, smaller stones have been packed by hand. Generations of repair lie in the joints: pale wedges, dark river pebbles, scraps of fired clay, pieces of broken grinding stone. Where an avenue has shifted out of reach, a ladder or a few steps lead to it. Where it passes through a place that no longer permits continuous distance, a small shelter marks the interruption.

The shelters have no doors. Inside each is a bench, a covered water jar, and a board on which travelers leave simple marks: how many entered, in which direction, during which weather. A person can walk through such a place in an afternoon and emerge a season later. More often the crossing costs only a few hours, or returns a traveler to the same shelter with snow on the opposite shoulder.

No one builds over an interruption. The paving is kept clean right up to its edge.

Buildings are low, with thick stone walls and roofs of split timber covered by living mats. Timber is scarce. It comes from sheltered groves where dark, slow-growing trees spread sideways before they grow upward. A single trunk may support a dozen rooted arches. Woodcutters work with stone blades and small iron axes, taking only the dead sections; cutting living wood can open a path beneath the tree that was not there before.

Doors are felted. Windows are narrow and shuttered. Courtyards hold leaf beds, fuel bricks drying under cover, racks of clothing, and shallow ceramic vessels in which useful films are grown. Those films thicken into skins that can be peeled, dried, and stitched. Much of what people wear was cultivated in a basin.

There are kilns, hand looms, workshops with foot-powered wheels. No machine runs unattended. A turning wheel left alone may keep turning after its axle stops, wearing a groove into the air that has to be covered until it heals.

The workshops are quiet. You hear scraping, the measured knock of a mallet, a latch settling. Voices carry poorly in the open country but strangely well along certain walls. People learn where not to whisper.

Despite the stillness, the plateau is always in motion.

A broad rise may sink by the width of a fingernail in a night. Across a district, leaves turn together as a buried seam warms. Frost lifts the paving and lays it down again. Thin sheets of stone detach from slopes, slide without much noise, and come to rest a little farther out on the plain. The inhabitants mark these changes with upright rods and strings. In a city court, rows of such rods show the movement of the ground through several human lifetimes.

The plants respond more quickly than the people. Beds withdraw from newly unstable stone, leaving pale root-webs exposed. Other beds advance into it. Low flowering structures appear after rain, open for a few minutes, and release a powder that lies suspended close to the ground. At night the powder settles. By morning there may be a faint green line along a seam that had been bare for centuries.

Some of the changes are harder to chart. A basin fills with water, though no rain has reached it. A familiar row of houses becomes visible from the wrong side. A paving slab develops a shadow beneath it while still lying flat on the earth.

These events have practical names, local to each district. They are discussed at the cistern, alongside roof repairs and the condition of the fuel stores. The plateau has been behaving this way for longer than its inhabitants have kept records. Familiarity has not made it safe, but it has made careful work possible.

Far from the cities are areas where even that care is insufficient.

There the worn land becomes extraordinarily smooth. All the small distinctions vanish: grain, fissure, stain. Walking across it, you may discover that your footsteps are becoming fewer. Behind you, the line of your approach ends well short of where you remember beginning. Ahead, a patch of dark vegetation remains the same size through hours of travel.

People turn back when this happens. They return slowly, placing a loose pebble at each step, until the ground begins accepting distance again.

Beyond these places, visible on exceptionally clear days, stand vast fields of pale upright forms. They resemble towers at first, then cliffs, then the stems of some growth too large to resolve. They cast no shadows that reach the plateau. Sometimes cloud gathers around their bases; sometimes the forms remain visible through it.

No road is known to reach them.

By evening, the nearest city becomes easier to see. Shutters close. Small lamps burn behind cloth screens. Steam rises from a communal kitchen and bends toward a street where there is no wind. Along the garden margins, leaves spread flat to catch the last of the light. Water continues moving in the broad, almost empty channels.

A traveler standing beside one of the great avenues can see a few lit windows, a patch of cultivated green, and beyond them miles of bare stone. Somewhere out there, a rain band is passing. Somewhere a seam is opening, and roots are already turning toward it.

The road beneath the traveler’s feet is sound for the moment. Its joints have been packed recently. In the quiet, one can hear a worker farther down the avenue setting a small replacement stone into place.
