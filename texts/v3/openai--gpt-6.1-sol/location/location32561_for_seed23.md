The town occupies a hollow just below the mountain’s uppermost ridge. From above, it looks like something caught in a cupped hand: a crowd of pale roofs, dark slots between them, and water shining wherever there is room for water to shine. You could walk from one end to the other in twenty minutes, if the streets stayed where they were.

There are no trees. Three or four juniper bushes cling to the outer wall, growing sideways in the cracks, their exposed roots polished by ice. Elsewhere there is a little black moss, a crust of yellow lichen, and a grass so short that the grazing animals seem to be licking the stone.

The light is immense.

It comes down from a white sky and back up from snowfields, glazed roofs, pools, and the wet faces of the cliffs. Even beneath an arch, you can see the grains in the mortar. Shadows are thin and blue, pressed tightly against the things that make them. People wear narrow wooden visors with slits cut across the grain. Without one, it is difficult to tell where a wall ends and the air begins.

Everything is old enough to have lost its sharpness. Doorsteps are shallow bowls. Corners have become shoulders. The stones along the principal stair have been worn into overlapping scallops, each holding a clear spoonful of water. The town’s oldest buildings are not quite separate from the mountain: their back rooms were chipped into it, their outer walls assembled from the pieces. In places you can follow a seam of quartz through a house, across an alley, and into the opposite house, as though the buildings were still parts of one rock that had been persuaded to stand apart.

Water issues from that rock continually.

It beads along the quartz seams, gathers beneath lintels, drops from the undersides of steps. It rises between paving stones without bubbling. In some courtyards the ground is submerged to ankle depth; in others, a skin of ice carries the reflection of an upper window while something moves beneath it. The water is bitterly cold, but it does not all freeze. Some streams remain liquid far below the temperature at which the washing stiffens on its lines. Others turn to ice the instant they touch a particular stone.

The inhabitants know these stones. They mark them with loops of red wool.

A red loop on a threshold means you should not bring a wet foot across it. A loop around the handle of a public basin means the basin is safe to fill but not to empty. There are little hooked tools for lifting ice away from a spillway, and flat bone wedges for separating a frozen door from its frame. Almost every adult carries a mallet. Metal is scarce, used for knife edges and the tips of drills; most of the town is maintained with wood, antler, cord, and the patient replacement of one stone at a time.

There is a great deal to maintain.

The hollow is crowded with terraces, staircases, covered passages, little bridges, retaining walls, drying racks, raised animal pens, and channels crossing other channels on stone crutches. A gutter may run through someone’s kitchen at waist height. A bedroom may be reached through a neighbour’s roof. Beneath the public square are six smaller squares, each once open to the sky, now vaulted over to make room for the one above it. Water descends through all of them.

So do the animals.

The first thing you notice is the birds: white birds against white walls, visible mostly by their eyes and the flesh inside their beaks. They occupy every ledge. They squeeze between roof stones, hang beneath bridges, and stand in the shallow channels with their feet submerged. Their wings beat rapidly, but make only a dry brushing sound.

Then the other lives become apparent.

Small furred bodies thread the drain holes. Broad-backed beetles move under translucent ice. There are soft, many-legged things clustered around the warm breath of occupied doorways, and little black fish in pools no deeper than a hand. Long-eared grazers live on the roofs, crossing narrow planks between households. Their winter coats are so thick that snow rests on them without melting. They lower their heads into baskets of scraped lichen and chew with the grave concentration of creatures doing difficult work.

Nearly every surface is occupied. The underside of a lifted bucket is stippled with sleeping snails. A bundle of firewood contains three nests. The hollow beneath a stair is full of pale animals that retreat from a lamp but not from daylight. You learn to set things down slowly.

Yet the town is almost silent.

Water ought to roar here. There are drops falling twenty feet, torrents squeezed through narrow throats, whole sheets slipping off the higher terraces. But the sounds are small: a tick, a hiss, the brief tapping of loose pebbles. The porous stone takes sound into itself. A hammer struck against a wall is heard clearly by the person holding it; someone across the alley sees the blow and hears nothing.

In the deeper rooms, old noises occasionally come back out. A scraping sound may issue from a dry wall. A cupboard may murmur with a conversation too blurred to understand. People do not put their beds against newly repaired masonry. They prefer walls that have had a winter to settle.

The quiet makes the movement difficult to grasp. At first the place seems suspended, all brightness and cold. Then you look closely enough to see that almost nothing is still.

The channels are changing.

A thread of water running downhill will slow, swell, and begin to travel sideways. A pool lifts against one edge as though the paving beneath it were being tilted, though the paving has not moved. Droplets gather on a vertical wall until there are hundreds of them; then they all detach at once and cross the alley in a bright, level flock.

This happens throughout the day.

The inhabitants call it the turning. There is no bell for it. The birds give the first warning, leaving one set of ledges and crowding onto another. Children pull baskets from the streets. Shopkeepers raise their lowest shelves. Someone lifts the plank bridge beside the dye house; someone else lowers a bridge two doors farther on.

For a few minutes the town becomes feverishly busy.

Water climbs a stair. Ice flowers open in the joints of a wall and close again. The submerged animals pour through newly connected pools. Roof grazers push shoulder to shoulder along their planks. People lean from windows to catch floating trays, pass infants over channels, and knock loose the wedges that will allow a gate to swing in the other direction.

All of it happens in the same subdued register. A whispered instruction. Wood rubbing wood. The soft slap of a fish landing on a wet step.

Then the flow settles into its new arrangement. An alley is dry where a stream ran before; the bakery has water at its door. The baker brings out a narrow board, sets it on two stones, and resumes serving customers.

The walls have been rounded by centuries of these turns. Their niches, grooves, and bulges accommodate currents that return often enough to be familiar, but never in quite the same order. No house is built with only one entrance. No channel is expected to remain a channel all year.

At the far end of the hollow, behind the last houses, is a basin enclosed by cliffs. It is scarcely larger than the town square. The water there is milky with suspended stone, and animals crowd its margin so densely that the shore seems furred.

Here the changes are faster. Islands of ice form, split, and drift together. Gravel rises from the bottom in low mounds, spreads, and sinks again. Thin pillars of frozen spray stand for an hour and collapse without a crash. The cliff faces weep steadily, each drop catching the light.

There is a bench beside the basin, carved from a single block. Its seat is worn deep. From it you can watch the town’s roofs through the crowded movement of wings.

A child comes down carrying a shallow wooden tray. She waits until a strip of water withdraws from the stones, steps into the space it leaves, and sets the tray beside a crack in the cliff. Almost immediately, small translucent creatures begin to climb into it.

She waits for eleven. Then she lifts the tray and goes home, taking a different path because the water has already covered the one she came by.
