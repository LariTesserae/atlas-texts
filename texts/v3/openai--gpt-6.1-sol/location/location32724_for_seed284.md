The city is hidden behind a loose inspection plate on the highest tower of the mountain cableway. From outside, there is only a seam in the metal, a screw with its head worn smooth, and a little black dust caught beneath it. Beyond the tower, the mountain falls for nearly two kilometers without offering anything broad enough to stand on.

Inside the seam, the country is scarcely wider than a hand.

Its floor is a shelf of fractured ceramic, pale gray under a dim red service lamp. The ceramic was once a single insulating block. Vibration has opened it into ridges, gullies, and narrow, deep-sided slots. Some breaks are old enough to have darkened; others are white and sharp, with a fine powder still clinging to their edges. An overhanging fragment has split within the last hour. It rests on three points, rocking almost imperceptibly whenever a cable car passes far below.

Everything here is elevated twice: first by the mountain, then by the tower. Even the lowest hollow sits above most clouds.

There is no soil. The loose material is powdered insulation, iron scale, graphite, flakes of paint, and the translucent husks of things that have lived in the enclosure. These gather in sheltered pockets, sorted by the draught. Black grains lie close to the entrance; lighter white dust has traveled deeper. At the back, beneath a copper bracket, lies a drift so fine that an insect leaves a clean, steep-sided trench through it.

No rain reaches the cavity. Snow occasionally packs against the outer plate, but the plate is heated just enough to shed it. The melt runs elsewhere. Here the air arrives dry and cold, carrying the faint bitter smell of hot wiring from the lower equipment chambers.

The settlement occupies every surface.

Two rows of miniature maintenance machines stand along the ceramic ridges: housings no larger than seeds, stamped from dull nickel, connected by wire rails. These were installed to clean and reseat the tower’s electrical contacts. They have continued working through years of neglected inspections, extending their routes wherever a reachable surface remains. A cable passes over a ravine on four slender brackets. Beneath it, a chain of little carriers moves spent graphite toward a waste slot. Some carriers are empty. Some bear a load almost as large as themselves.

At the central junction, three repair arms surround a damaged terminal. Their movements are too small to notice at first. Then one lifts, turns a fraction, and lowers a bright metal shaving into place. Another presses it flat. The third holds steady against the tower’s shuddering.

There are streets, though they were not designed as streets. Insulated wires make causeways between blocks. Rows of solder beads offer a climbable route up the back wall. A folded copper sheet forms a covered passage, narrow at one end and broad at the other, where warm air spills from the transformer housing. Generations of traffic have polished its lip.

Most of that traffic is alive.

Small dark beetles move among the carriers, feeding on grease and the debris blown in through the seam. Mites collect beneath their wing cases. Pale moths shelter in the recesses of the wiring, their wings held upright to fit the gaps. A few have been here long enough to lose the powder from their edges. Tiny flies emerge from the remains of other insects. Spiders occupy the higher ledges, stitching short webs across routes too narrow for the cleaning machines.

On a warm contact cover, forty or fifty bodies can be crowded into a space the size of a fingernail. They touch, separate, climb over one another. There is constant negotiation at the openings. A beetle backs out of a tunnel when a carrier enters. A spider lifts its forelegs as a brush passes underneath. Moths cling against the ceiling while the machinery clears a drift below them.

The tower draws new inhabitants out of the air. In summer, updrafts bring insects from the valleys, lifting them far beyond any place where they could normally live. Some strike the metal and are lost. Some find the warm seam. The cavity receives them without distinction: a living arrival, a dead one, an egg caught on a strand of windblown silk. Nothing grows green here, but the influx rarely stops for long.

It is mostly quiet.

The great machinery of the cableway is insulated by thick steel and distance. Its sound reaches this chamber as a soft pressure, felt more than heard. The little carriers make a dry tick at the ends of their rails. A repair arm clicks twice before each stroke. Occasionally a wing scrapes metal with a sound like paper being shifted on a desk.

Then the wind changes.

A gust slips under the inspection plate and crosses the whole landscape in an instant. Dust lifts off the ridges. The sheltered passages become wind tunnels. Moths flatten themselves; beetles wedge their feet beneath wire; loose husks sail down the chamber and strike the mesh at its far end.

The service lamp dims as the tower takes a heavier load.

For several seconds, everything is moving. The ceramic fragment rocks. A spider’s web pulls nearly flat. One carrier loses its load, and three others halt behind it. A rain of graphite falls into a newly opened crack. The gust dies, leaving the chamber colder and rearranged.

Recovery begins at once. Brushes emerge. The stalled carriers reverse, each stopping short of the one behind. A beetle investigates the spilled graphite and finds, beneath it, a torn moth abdomen. The spider draws in its slackened threads. On the central terminal, the three arms continue their repair.

Such interruptions come dozens of times a day. The tower flexes with wind and passing loads; surfaces that were adjacent at dawn may be separated by evening. Routes close when a ceramic edge breaks away. New ledges appear. The machines test them with light taps before placing a rail. The animals explore more quickly.

At the outer seam there is a balcony of chipped enamel, cold enough that few things linger on it. From there, the nearest peaks appear level with the floor. Beyond them lies a vast blue-gray distance, and below, the cloud tops move across the valleys.

A beetle comes out onto the enamel. It feels the air with its antennae, turns, and goes back through the seam. Behind it, the red lamp reveals tiers of crowded ledges, a repair arm poised above its work, and fine dust beginning to slide toward the next fresh crack.
