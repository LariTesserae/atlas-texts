The hollow lies just beneath a sheet of pale rock, shallow enough that a person standing above it would feel warmth through the soles of their feet. Nothing on the surface marks its position except a damp seam and, in cooler weather, a little breath of vapor. Below that seam is a space no larger than a cupboard, with a low roof, a tilted floor, and a pool tucked into its deepest corner.

It is very bright inside.

Light collects on the undersides of the roof crystals, on the fine teeth of a newly broken ledge, on the trembling meniscus where water touches stone. The wet surfaces shine almost white. The dry ones are cream, sulfur-yellow, or the pale gray of fresh ceramic. In places the brightness makes it difficult to tell whether a surface is wet until a bead moves across it.

There is no lamp, cable, shaft, or opening wide enough to admit this much daylight. The light is delivered from elsewhere by a field that guides it through the surrounding mineral without heating the intervening rock. Whatever produces that field is far outside the hollow. Here there is only its local effect: a patch of ordinary sunlight, with its warmth and spectrum intact, falling through several meters of stone. The illumination weakens slightly when clouds cross the distant collector. A small shadow then becomes visible beneath every crystal point.

The heat belongs to the ground.

Warm groundwater comes up through a hairline fracture in the floor. It does not gush. It swells into a clear lens, pauses, and spills down a slope scarcely longer than a hand. Along the way it passes over three shallow shelves, each edged with a thin, brittle lip of mineral. These lips are growing. The newest is so delicate that the passing water bends it before it has quite hardened.

The pool is hot enough to make immersion painful. Near the inlet, tiny bubbles cling to the floor and release in loose groups, rising without much sound. Farther away, the water cools by a few degrees against the walls. That small difference organizes nearly everything that lives here.

There are no leaves, roots, stems, or floating green films. Much of the hollow is bare mineral. A few dull patches on the cooler stone are colonies of microbes, thin enough to be mistaken for stains. One is rust-colored; another has the gray translucence of skin over a healing scrape. They feed where the groundwater meets oxygen carried down through the surface seam. Their boundaries advance slowly, then retreat when a fresh pulse of hotter water washes over them.

The hollow itself is young. One wall still fits, almost exactly, the shape of the slab that dropped away from it. Angular fragments lie below, their corners unsmoothed. Water has begun to soften the smallest edges, but has not yet made channels deep enough to hold a fingertip. Instead it spreads in clear, wandering films, dividing around a grain of grit, gathering again a little farther down.

A single drop hanging from the roof can alter the whole visible drainage pattern when it falls.

For a moment there is a small impact crater in the film. Water leaps onto a dry patch. A previously isolated bead connects to the moving sheet and is carried toward the pool. Then the surface settles into a slightly different arrangement. In this little basin, rain has the force of a local flood.

Most of the moisture arrives from below, but some comes through the ceiling after weather passes over the surface. That water is cooler and faintly cloudy. It creeps along fractures before appearing inside as a row of beads. Sometimes the beads hold for hours. Sometimes they fall one after another, making a soft, uneven ticking.

Otherwise the sounds are few: the minute click of a grain breaking loose; a bubble opening; a low hiss near the hottest part of the inlet. The rock carries a distant vibration too deep to hear distinctly. If an ear could be placed against the floor, it would register as pressure rather than noise.

Animal life is uncommon and mostly transient. A pale mite occasionally comes down the damp seam from a cooler cavity above. It walks across the ceiling with a deliberate economy, testing each foothold. Near the hot pool it turns back. There is very little food here, and the bright field gives it no comfortable darkness except beneath the fallen chips.

One chip shelters a minute springtail. It spends long intervals motionless against a moist undersurface, then crosses a few millimeters to graze on a microbial film. Its movements leave no lasting track. Only when it touches the edge of a water bead does its presence visibly change the place: the bead dimples around a foot, stretches, and snaps back as the animal withdraws.

There is ample empty space between these small presences. A broad, spotless wall occupies most of the hollow. The pool has no inhabitants visible in its center. Across the brightest shelf, nothing moves except water.

Yet little stays fixed for long. Heat draws vapor from the pool. The vapor reaches the cooler roof and gathers there, returning as drops. Dissolved minerals settle out at the margins of the flow, making pale rims. An old rim cracks under its own growth and sends a flake sliding into the basin. A pulse from below raises the water level, drowning the lower shelf for an hour before it reappears, cleaner and more sharply outlined.

At times the warm inlet slows almost to nothing. Then the whole hollow seems to hold its breath. The pool becomes smooth; the ceiling drops grow larger without falling; a mite ventures farther down the wall. Eventually pressure builds in the fracture and the clear lens at its mouth rises again.

The renewed flow is not dramatic. It makes no roar. It simply crosses the floor, enters the pool, and begins to change the edges of the room.

Above, the surface remains nearly featureless. Below, in a space that could be covered by two hands, hot water is making a landscape—ledges, basins, precipices, sheltered shores—and erasing its first attempts while the light falls steadily through the stone.
