The hollow lies just below the highest point of the mountain, where two slabs of newly exposed rock have parted without falling away. It is a narrow place, perhaps sixty paces long, deep enough to hold a darkness of its own. Above it, the summit divides the clouds. Below it, through a slit at one end, there is sometimes a glimpse of another mountain so far down that its snow looks blue.

Very little sky reaches the floor.

The walls lean inward. Their upper edges nearly touch, and between them hang thin plates of stone, still attached along one side. Each plate has a sharp lower lip. The rock has had too little time in the weather to acquire rounded edges, soil, or the familiar softness of an old cliff. It is dark gray where dry, almost black where wet, with pale seams that continue from one wall to the other. The mountain split here recently enough that the two faces still look capable of fitting together.

At night, which occupies most of the day inside the hollow, those seams are the easiest things to see.

The floor is crowded with angular fragments. Some are as large as sleeping animals; some are no larger than teeth. They lie in unstable stacks, yet almost none of them moves. Walking would require a patience usually reserved for climbing: a foot placed, weight tried, a pause to hear what has shifted underneath. There is no path. There are several routes through the stones, but each belongs to an animal of a particular size.

Water arrives without much spectacle. Clouds passing over the summit press moisture into the upper cracks. It collects behind the leaning plates and emerges hours later, one drop at a time. There are days when it rains on the mountain and the hollow stays dry, then clear afternoons when every wall begins to drip.

The largest pool is no wider than a spread hand. It sits in a fresh fracture near the center, black and perfectly still, with a white mineral rim incomplete along one edge. Smaller pockets occur beneath stones and inside split crystals. A little water runs down the wall, loses itself in a seam, and reappears lower down. Its whole descent may take a week.

Despite the height, there is seldom ice on the floor. The stone retains a faint warmth from below. The air is cool enough to sting the nostrils, but sheltered from the wind, and the coldest surfaces are overhead. On certain mornings a translucent crust forms along the lips of the hanging plates. It loosens by degrees. A piece the size of a fingernail falls and breaks with an unexpectedly large sound.

The animals answer.

At first their noise seems to come from the rock itself: tapping, a low rasp, an intermittent rush like a sleeve dragged across rough cloth. Then the eyes begin to distinguish bodies.

Nearly every crevice is occupied. Small pale animals cling head-down beneath ledges, their broad feet flattened against the stone. Darker ones lie across fractures with their legs braced on opposite sides. In the dampest seams, translucent larvae pack themselves so tightly that each contraction passes into the next, making the entire seam pulse. There are winged creatures, but few fly here. Their wings are folded into narrow fans and used to steer a fall from one shelf to another. At this altitude, flight into the open air would carry them beyond any hope of return.

Some of the bodies are luminous. The light is weak and greenish, usually concealed beneath a joint or inside the fold of an abdomen. A moving animal briefly illuminates its own feet. Another turns against a wall and reveals the outlines of six others gathered around it.

The hollow is crowded far beyond what its sparse growth seems able to support.

There are cushions of nearly black moss where seepage persists, a few thumb-length plants rooted in cracks, and a leathery growth spread thinly over the warmer stone. Its surface looks dry until disturbed; then beads of moisture rise through it. Animals graze these patches with minute scraping mouthparts. Their work leaves pale arcs that slowly darken again.

Much of the food comes from above. The mountain catches creatures carried upward in storms: moths, seeds, scraps of lichen, flying insects frozen in the cloud. They settle between the summit slabs and gradually work down into the hollow. A single large moth can sustain a ledge for days. Its arrival causes no visible rush. The nearest animals turn toward it. Others begin moving much later, after some faint trace has reached their crevices. By the next night the ledge is densely occupied, and the moth has vanished beneath overlapping backs.

There are larger inhabitants as well. In the deepest end, where the walls pinch together, something the length of a forearm rests upright in a crack. Its hide has the color and fine grain of the surrounding stone. It remains so still that smaller animals crawl over its face. Occasionally an eyelid closes from the bottom upward.

It feeds rarely. When it does, its mouth opens sideways.

The other creatures keep a small clear space around that crack, though no boundary is visible. They cross the floor beneath it, but never linger. Even the luminous ones extinguish themselves there.

Elsewhere, the animals alter the place in small, persistent ways. Several kinds carry flakes of stone in their mouths. They wedge them beneath their bodies, raise them against drips, or use them to narrow the entrance to a resting crevice. A flat shard placed at the right angle can guide an occasional drop toward a brood. These arrangements resemble little accidents until one watches a creature return, test the water with an antenna, and push the shard a fraction farther inward.

Nothing rises above the scale of a foothold. The hollow has no structures except those the mountain made and these minute corrections.

Sound accumulates between the walls. A scrape from one end returns from the other with its pitch altered. The animals tap to locate one another, and the echoes overlap until the dark seems full of distant activity, though the source may be inches away. Beneath the tapping is a steady communal rasp: feeding, grooming, the slow abrasion of bodies against stone. Every few hours, something deep in the mountain gives a low, single knock.

That sound stops everything.

Feet remain lifted. Antennae hold their curves. The larvae in the wet seams cease contracting. For several breaths, the hollow is quiet enough to hear water gathering at the end of a crack.

Then one animal resumes scraping, and the others follow.

The place changes slowly, but it is not old. Fresh surfaces continue to appear. A wall sheds a wafer of rock, exposing a clean patch that has never held moisture or life. For a while it stands out as a pale shape in the gloom. Condensation reaches it first, then a wandering grazer, then the thin leathery growth. Eventually a creature rests there long enough to leave the outline of its body.

Once during the short afternoon, sunlight may enter through the slit at the lower end. It touches a single stone and warms it. Animals gather on the shadowed side, close enough that their limbs overlap. The light never reaches most of them.

Above, wind crosses the summit with nothing to obstruct it. Below, valleys open into distances too great to judge. Here, within the parted rock, a drop is forming. A small animal waits beneath it, feet planted, mouth raised. The drop swells for a long time before it falls.
