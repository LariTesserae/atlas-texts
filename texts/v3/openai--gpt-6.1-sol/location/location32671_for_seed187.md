The place is a shallow hollow, scarcely lower than the ground around it. You could walk across it in a few minutes, though there is no path and nothing at the other side that asks you to arrive. Its boundaries are a low rim of willow and alder, with pale daylight showing between their trunks. Above them lies a large, ordinary sky.

There is almost nothing in the middle.

No boulder, no solitary tree, no pool holding a piece of the clouds. Just a level surface of small living things: moss, low sedges, creeping stems, leaves pressed close enough together that the ground beneath them never appears. The plants are so short that, from the edge, the hollow looks empty. Only when you crouch does the surface separate into blades, rosettes, branching moss, and the fine red threads of new roots.

Beneath that is more plant material. The living skin rests on a deep accumulation of old growth, compressed and darkened but not yet reduced to anything like ordinary soil. A foot sinks slightly, then stops. Lift it, and the leaves slowly straighten around the impression.

It is cool enough that you keep your hands in your pockets while standing still. In the morning, moisture beads along the sedges and gathers beneath the overlapping leaves. By midday most of it has gone. The hollow is not a swamp: there is no standing water to step around, no broad wet shine. Dampness persists in sheltered places, in the base of a moss cushion or under a fallen willow branch already covered in green.

Now and then rain passes over. It makes the whole surface darker for an hour. Water disappears into the mat with almost no runoff, and afterward the hollow smells briefly of crushed stems and cold tea.

The land has had a very long time to lose its edges. Whatever once rose here has weathered down; whatever channels once carried water have widened, filled, and become indistinguishable from the rest. Even the rim is more a change in vegetation than a slope. There are no fresh cuts or bare banks. The few slight undulations are rounded so gently that you notice them chiefly through the way the light falls.

At the edge, the alders grow apart from one another. Their lower branches have died back, leaving open space beneath the crowns. The wood is silver-gray, patched with lichen and minute green films. Fallen limbs remain where they dropped, sinking into the ground cover. Some have become low, mossy ridges; others are visible only because a line of different leaves follows them.

The hollow is open enough for you to see almost all of it at once. Yet it takes a while to hear what is happening there.

A wren begins from somewhere in the rim, loud beyond any reasonable expectation of its size. Another answers. There are finches in the alder crowns, a woodpecker working a dead upper limb, and birds hidden in the sedges whose calls seem to come directly out of the ground. A pair of geese sometimes passes low overhead, filling the hollow with blunt, nasal cries.

Closer down, flies shelter among the leaves. Beetles move under the moss. Small mammals have made narrow tunnels through the plant litter, and their entrances appear as dark slots that close again when the sedges spring back. You rarely see the animals themselves. You see a stem tremble, or hear a short, brisk rustling travel several feet beneath the surface.

There is another sound, much larger than these.

Beyond the low trees, out of sight, machinery runs. It has a heavy, steady pulse and a thinner whine above it. At intervals comes a metallic clatter, followed by a long exhaust-like rush. The sound belongs to some industrial operation elsewhere on the flat land—a mill, perhaps, or a pumping station. From inside the hollow there is no way to tell. No roof is visible. No road enters. Nothing made has been left among the plants.

But the noise is close enough to be part of the place.

On still days it fills the open air, making the hollow seem like a large vacant room beside a working engine. The birds sing through it. Sometimes a call is lost in the clatter; sometimes it rises above the low vibration with startling clarity. When the wind changes, the machinery recedes and you become aware of leaves rubbing together, wings beating, the faint tearing sound of an animal feeding in the rim.

The light is plain and sufficient. Under cloud, each patch of green keeps its own color: yellow-green sedges, blue-green creeping leaves, nearly black moss in the shallow depressions. In sunshine, individual blades cast tiny shadows. There is no deep interior to enter, no dim center. You stand among plants with clear air around your body and sky directly overhead.

Little seems to alter the hollow for long. Rain is absorbed. Flattened stems rise. A dead branch settles another fraction into the mat. A bare place left by a digging animal fills from its edges. In winter the green dulls and the stems lie closer to the ground, but the shape remains. In spring there is fresh growth without much increase in height.

Its stability is visible at the margins. No young trees advance far into the open middle. A few seedlings survive for a season, held low among the sedges, then disappear. The alders maintain their loose ring. The moss keeps the shallow depressions. Each kind of plant occupies nearly the same small territory year after year.

If you stay, the emptiness becomes less simple. There are more movements than you first saw, more sounds than you can locate, more layers under your feet. Still, nothing gathers into a central object. Your eye travels across the low green surface and comes to rest nowhere in particular.

The machinery continues beyond the trees. A bird calls from a branch you can see but cannot find it on. Somewhere close to your shoe, beneath a leaf, a bead of water holds until the stem bends enough to let it fall.
