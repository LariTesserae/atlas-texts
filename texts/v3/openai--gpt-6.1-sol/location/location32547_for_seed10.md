The high country has worn down until it resembles a floor.

There are no peaks left, only immense, shallow rises whose summits disappear into cloud without becoming steep. Valleys lie between them like impressions left by bodies in a mattress. Everything is rounded, saturated, and cold. The distances are difficult to judge. A dark patch that seems close enough to reach before evening may be a belt of vegetation several miles wide; a pale ridge beyond it may be the far shore of the same pool you are standing beside.

Almost nothing here is stone anymore.

The ground is a living felt, gray-green on top and nearly black underneath, laid over what remains of the mountains. It is composed of plants too closely intergrown to separate: mosses with jointed stems, creeping leaves reduced to scales, pale roots braided through roots that have been dead for thousands of years and are still being used. There is no clear boundary between the living surface and the buried layers. Put a hand into a split in the turf and the warmth you expect from decomposition is absent. The whole depth is cold. Something grips your fingers very slowly, not as an animal would, but as a root closes around an obstruction over several growing seasons.

Here it takes a few seconds.

Water rests close beneath every surface. A footstep makes a shallow depression fill from below. The water is clear at first, then green, then opaque with drifting fibers. When you take your foot away, the felt rises again, carrying the footprint upward until it becomes a small mound. Behind a traveler, a line of such mounds persists for an hour or two. Eventually they open along the top and release narrow leaves.

The country can be crossed, but it does not remain crossed.

In the hollows are lakes with no visible banks. The felt thins into floating strands, and the strands thin into water, so that the transition is discovered by weight rather than sight. Some lakes are only knee-deep. Others are open shafts through the entire thickness of the plateau. You can look down into them and see dim green shelves descending far beyond the reach of daylight, each carrying its own miniature version of the country above: hummocks, channels, grazing things, a slow movement of mist.

Sometimes rain falls in these submerged landscapes. You see it passing between the shelves, silver and vertical, although the water around it remains still.

The rain overhead comes sideways. It beads on every hairlike stem and gathers into drops too heavy for the plants that hold them. Across the plateau, stems bow and spring upright in waves. The light is the low, pewter-colored light of an afternoon that never quite begins. Clouds drag their undersides through the taller growth, leaving frost. A clearing in the sky reveals no blue, only a thinner gray and, occasionally, the pale underside of something moving much higher.

For long stretches there is nothing upright at all. The vegetation fills the land without filling the air. It lies close, presses flat, makes a continuous skin. You can see across miles of it.

Then, alone in that openness, stands a growth as large as a town.

From a distance it looks like a grove, but nearer it resolves into one organism: a wide disk of black roots supporting a cluster of ribbed columns. The columns are hollow stems, each thicker than a house, tapering to blunt mouths. No two are quite vertical. Their surfaces are furred with tiny white leaves that lie flat in the wind and lift when the air goes still.

These growths are widely spaced. There may be three visible from a ridge, each with its own weather around it. Between them stretches the low wet country, apparently empty.

They are loud.

A deep rotating thump carries over the ground before it can be heard in the air. Water trembles in the little pools. Then comes a metallic chatter, a rising whine, the heavy regular concussion of something compressing and releasing. The sound is so convincingly mechanical that the first impulse is to look for a wall, a shed, a place where a door might open.

There are no walls. Nothing has been built here.

Within the columns, curtains of tough leaves run around loops of woody tissue. They catch falling water and draw it downward, turning knotted axles. Other chambers contain stacks of thin membranes that flex under pressure. Sap is lifted from deep beneath the plateau, separated, concentrated, driven through narrow vessels until it hisses. Wet pollen drifts from the mouths in yellow ropes. The ropes break in the wind and settle over the surrounding acres.

The nearest column sometimes stops. In the abrupt quiet you hear dripping from its inner chambers, followed by a series of clicks as something contracts. Then a shudder travels up its side. Its small leaves stand on end. With a low, strained note, it begins again.

These organisms have been running longer than there have been valleys here. Their oldest roots pass through seams of polished gravel, through the last crushed remnants of mountain rock. Some have died and become reservoirs for others. Some are so deeply incorporated into the plateau that only their exhausts remain above ground: low round openings breathing white vapor across the moss.

Nothing makes use of their output neatly.

The yellow pollen settles on pools where it develops into a skin. The skin thickens, buckles, and becomes pasture. Herds gather on it within hours. They are low, six-legged animals covered in translucent wool, their faces hidden beneath fringes of feeding tendrils. Hundreds can occupy one patch without looking crowded because most of their bodies are below the surface. Only their arched backs and the occasional raised limb show above the plants.

They graze by drawing long strips of living ground through their mouths. Behind them, the exposed tissue puckers and closes.

Small birds accompany them, if birds is the right word for creatures that fold their wings into the shape of leaves and root themselves when tired. In flight they are black flecks against the cloud. At rest they are indistinguishable from the dark foliage around a pool. Disturb a quiet stretch of turf and a thousand may rise at once, leaving behind thin threads that retract into their feet.

There are larger animals farther out. You see their tracks before you see them: broad lanes in the felt, pools emptied, banks turned inside out. At dusk one may surface in a distant hollow and stand for a moment with water pouring from its sides. Its body is long and indistinct, supported by many slender legs. The legs arrive on the ground at slightly different times, some before they have finished emerging from the body. As it walks, its rear portions fade into rain while its forward portions become more solid.

The herds watch these animals pass. Then they lower their heads and feed again.

Change comes violently, but seldom leaves a sharp edge.

A bank swells over the course of a minute. Water drains toward it from every direction. The felt stretches until its seams show, and then the entire rise rolls over, exposing its underside: white roots, glassy sacs, blind crawling things caught in the weave. For a few seconds there is a hill where a hollow used to be. It subsides with a sound like a ship settling heavily into water.

Elsewhere, acres of turf detach and begin to travel.

They slide over the buried water at walking speed, bearing their pools and grazing animals with them. Two sheets meet and climb over one another. A pool is folded shut. Its fish spill into the grass, where they work their fins against the stems until another pool opens nearby. The great columnar growths rock on their root disks. Some continue pumping while tilted almost horizontal, their discharge blowing across the ground in a dense, warm-smelling mist.

By morning the land has changed its arrangement. Nothing looks newly made. The fresh folds are smooth, the ruptures already mossed over, the overturned roots dark with age. A rise formed in the night may contain a dry, crumbling core and layers of ancient pollen. You can dig into it and find the flattened imprint of an animal that crossed there before the rise existed.

On cold evenings, the underground water becomes audible.

It is not a trickle or a subterranean river. It is a vast intermittent rush, followed by pauses during which the entire country seems to listen. With each rush, water rises in the shafts. The felt lifts. Pale bulbs push briefly through the surface, then withdraw. The columns increase their speed, and their overlapping rhythms fill the open air: hammering, rattling, a bass vibration felt in the jaw.

The animals become restless. Flocks uproot themselves. The submerged herds surface in their hundreds, crowding the patches that remain still.

Then, somewhere beyond sight, a whole tract of country drops.

The sound takes a long time to arrive. It begins as a dull pressure, becomes a roar, and ends in many small impacts. Water leaps from the pools. For a moment you can see bare ground beneath your feet, a gray substance scored with fine branching lines. The living felt closes over it before you can decide whether it was rock.

Afterward there is rain, and the work of the columns, and the animals returning to graze.

The place is ancient enough to have lost most of its mountains, but it has not become quiet. Even in the smallest hollow, where the wind is screened out and a few pale leaves lie flat against the water, something is arriving. A root advances along the bottom of the pool. A transparent larva follows it, opening and closing its jaws. Beneath both, the reflected clouds move in a direction the clouds overhead are not taking.

You can stay there until the cold stiffens your hands. The root will reach the other bank. The larva will disappear into the felt. The pool will narrow almost imperceptibly around them, and somewhere across the plateau a column will begin to turn faster.
