The uplands are still rising.

At their outer margins, the cliffs are so new that their broken faces have not yet darkened. A pale mineral skin stretches across them, splits, and curls away in flakes the size of valleys. Beneath it is black stone, warm enough to make the air tremble. There are no foothills. The land goes straight up from the lower darkness, shelf upon shelf, until distance obscures the individual edges and the whole horizon resembles a single enormous fracture.

Above that fracture lies a country wider than its sky can illuminate.

The light comes from a low, diffuse glow behind the clouds. It has no clear direction. Leaves cast shadows underneath themselves but rarely to either side, and the shadows are green at their edges, as though the plants have strained something out of the light. At intervals, an opening in the cloud reveals another high plateau overhead. Its underside is furred with roots. Whether it is a floating mass or a distant continuation of the ground is difficult to determine; both explanations work for a while, until you walk far enough.

The air is hot. Even on the highest ridges, where the atmosphere ought to be thin and cold, heat rises through the soles of your feet. Small cavities in the rock exhale a dry, metallic breath. The clouds travel slowly, but everything beneath them is changing too quickly.

A ravine visible at noon may be a narrow seam by evening. Farther away, new ravines open without any corresponding noise.

There is very little water to spare. Rain arrives in brief, localized falls, sometimes wetting only the upper branches of a single grove. A dark patch passes over the canopy; leaves turn their hollow undersides toward it; for a minute there is a sound like fingers brushing paper. Then the rain is finished. Nothing reaches the ground.

Most moisture condenses inside the vegetation. The larger plants are built around cool chambers, and their trunks sweat inward. If one splits, it reveals a wet, pale interior surrounded by layers of dry fiber and glossy black tissue. Animals gather at such wounds. They lick the exposed surfaces delicately, then retreat before the trunk closes.

The forests are lush but economical. Their leaves are thick, folded, overlapping, arranged to shade one another. Green occupies every protected crease in the terrain. On exposed rock it survives as tight mats; in the clefts it rises into immense layered canopies. Some valleys are packed so densely that you cannot see the ground even when standing on it. You advance through a succession of yielding surfaces: root felt, fallen fronds, warm bark, the backs of things that move away beneath your weight.

The vegetation does not all grow upward.

Along certain escarpments, forests grow horizontally into the air, their crowns extending miles from the cliff. Small creatures walk along their branches with the drop beside them rather than beneath them. There is a boundary near each of these forests where falling changes direction. Loose stones describe a shallow curve, settle against the cliff, and become part of its soil. The oldest horizontal groves have their own cloud banks. Their rain falls toward the rock face.

None of these boundaries is permanent.

When a ridge changes its inclination, the inhabitants begin moving before there is any visible sign. Long-bodied grazers detach themselves from the leaf beds. Wingless animals unfold membranes between their limbs. Columns of pale, jointed creatures emerge from holes in the stone and stream toward the next stable slope. The plants tighten their grips. Some pull their roots free and move a short distance, slowly enough that the movement looks like wilting until you notice the emptied socket behind them.

Then the direction of weight shifts.

A whole forest may settle against a different side of its valley. Branches bend together. Soil pours sideways in brown curtains. The sky acquires a new position between the trunks. Usually this happens in near silence, with only a few soft cracks from wood that could not adjust in time.

There are animals everywhere, though at first the place seems uninhabited.

Much of the fauna is broad and flat, fitted to the dense layers of vegetation: leaf-colored bodies sliding between leaves, black bodies clinging to black rock. Their eyes are often distributed along their margins. Some are small enough to shelter in a curled frond; others cover an entire grove and are mistaken for its canopy until they lift one edge to feed.

The most conspicuous herds move across the open mineral shelves. Each animal carries a cluster of dark, polished growths above its shoulders. They graze on the fresh seams where the ground has opened, breaking off brittle crystals with soft mouths. At rest, the clusters lean toward one another. Fine luminous threads appear between them, and the herd remains motionless for hours, transferring something from body to body.

In the surrounding forest, flowers open.

This is one of the landscape’s regularities. The herds gather; the air acquires a faint electrical pressure; distant buds unfold. It does not matter whether the flowers can be seen from the feeding grounds. They respond across enormous distances, and sometimes across a ridge that has not yet risen.

The place has the scale and coordination of industry, but there is nothing constructed here.

Inside the mountains are naturally occurring cylinders of dense magnetic stone. They turn within softer beds, driven by the unequal heat of the rising crust. You can feel their rotation on some ridges: a steady vibration through your knees, too low to hear. Nearby seams brighten and dim. Conductive roots run through the warm rock, dividing, reconnecting, carrying current into the forests.

Growth follows these currents. Long belts of vegetation ripen at once. Mineral nodules form by the million in deep-root chambers, each nearly identical, each wrapped in a thin edible membrane. Burrowing animals harvest them and carry them elsewhere. Their galleries ventilate the hot interior; their waste feeds the cooler groves. Whole slopes shed old tissue in synchronized sheets.

There are pauses, too. A region may go dim for several days. Leaves close. Animals flatten themselves into sheltered cracks. Deep inside the ground, the turning masses slow, and the heat begins to accumulate.

When the region wakes, it changes shape.

New ridges thrust through the forest. The vegetation rises with them, green strips stretched over sharp black ribs. Fields of nodules swell under the soil. Herds converge on freshly opened seams, and clouds thicken over places where no water was visible before. The renewal is immense, crowded, and almost soundless. From a distant shelf you can watch a province rearrange itself while hearing only the faint rasp of something feeding beside your boot.

There are a few pockets of standing water. They occur high up, in hollows whose edges are still sharp enough to cut skin. The pools are warm and very dark, with floating rafts of tiny plants. Animals approach them cautiously. Reflections are unreliable here. A pool may show the surrounding cliffs correctly but add a passage between them that does not exist. Sometimes it reflects a mature forest where there is only bare rock. Sometimes the bare rock begins to sprout while you are looking.

The animals do not drink from a pool while its reflection is changing.

Elsewhere, distance is the unreliable thing. A grove on the opposite side of a valley can take weeks to reach, although individual leaves are plainly visible. A black escarpment far beyond it may be only a short walk away. Routes are learned through temperature, smell, and the direction of the underground vibration. Migrating animals travel with their heads close to the ground. They will cross an apparently bottomless opening without hesitation, yet turn aside from a level stretch of fern.

Following them is possible, provided you follow immediately. Their passage seems to help keep the route intact.

The youngest country lies farther inland. There, the rock retains folds from its emergence. It is smooth between the fractures, dark and warm, already seeded with green. Small animals wait along the edges of fresh fissures. As the stone cools, they enter carrying spores in the folds of their bodies.

Beyond them, through the dimness, whole mountain systems are coming up.

Their sharp summits rise slowly through the low clouds. Moisture beads on the new surfaces and vanishes into the first roots. The magnetic stone begins to turn. A trembling passes along the mineral shelves, and animals you had not noticed lift their heads all around you.

For a moment the immense country is poised: warm, crowded, green in its depths, pale at every newly broken edge.

Then, somewhere too far away to hear, another slope changes direction. The creatures nearest you begin to move.
