The plain begins at ankle height.

There is no shore to step down from, no higher country behind it. A broad metal sill gives way to a wet surface, and beyond that the country continues level enough that distance is measured by changes in visibility rather than by the rise and fall of land. Near you, every seam is clear. Farther out, the seams merge into bands. At the horizon the bands disappear in a pale, warm haze, though the plain itself continues well beyond what its atmosphere allows you to see.

The ground is made of fitted plates, each about the size of a room. Some are pale ceramic, some a dull nickel alloy, some a translucent material through which you can see the dim ribs of the supporting frame. None lies quite flush with its neighbors. Their edges form shallow terraces, narrow troughs, long raised lips. Water occupies these differences: an inch over one plate, a dark channel between two others, a bead suspended along the underside of an overhang.

It has the appearance of a country just after rain, except that nothing is drying.

The air is warm against the face. Moisture gathers on the arms without becoming sweat. There is a mild smell of heated stone, electrical insulation, and clean shell. If you crouch and put a hand against the ground, the ceramic is almost exactly the temperature of your skin.

Every few hundred paces, something rises from the plain. A bank of exchanger fins. A low pumping house with a roof like folded sheet metal. Six enormous pipes lying side by side, disappearing into a ridge of bolted panels. These are the hills. Their slopes are access ramps; their outcrops are housings around machinery. Even the small grit caught in the wet seams consists of manufactured pellets, each with a minute hole through its center.

There is no buried soil. Under the plates are more plates, then lattices, then chambers, then structural decks. Inspection shafts descend past levels too deep to illuminate from above. You can look down one of them and see successive squares of warm light diminish into a depth that feels less like a foundation than another direction in which the landscape extends.

Nothing has settled. Corners remain sharp. The holes in the grit are unobstructed. The long edges of the ceramic terraces have the clean, slightly translucent finish of a fresh fracture, although they were never broken. They were cast that way.

And there are animals everywhere.

At first they resemble fittings. The little gray caps distributed along a conduit are snails, hundreds of them, their shells low and broad and ridged like thumbwheels. The pale fringe beneath a platform consists of folded legs. What looks like a patch of tarnish on a shallow pool resolves into fish lying close together, each one the color of the plate beneath it.

Once you begin to recognize them, the empty spaces disappear.

Small six-legged creatures occupy the grooves around bolt heads. Flat crabs fit into drainage slots with almost no clearance on either side. Transparent worms fill the clear tubes beneath the walking surface, making the tubes seem full of trembling glass fibers. Along the exchanger banks, broad-winged animals hang in overlapping rows. Their wings are not feathers or membranes but soft, finely perforated sheets. A little moisture rests in each perforation.

Larger creatures stand in the pools.

They are waist-high, heavy through the body, with narrow legs and blunt, downward-facing heads. Their backs carry paired ridges, and their skin is the warm ivory of old enamel. They stand so densely in some basins that the water is visible only in the triangular spaces between their ankles. Each has a small patch of dark bristles beneath the jaw. Occasionally one draws water through these bristles, slowly enough that the surface scarcely dimples.

There are no leaves for them to eat, no grass beneath their feet. Their feeding takes place at the seams. There, dissolved compounds arrive through porous strips too fine to see. Some animals press their mouths against the strips. Others graze a film on the metal, a faint pearly coating that has no stems, roots, or recognizable cells. It is renewed at precisely the rate it is removed.

The animals are warm. They have eyes, and the eyes follow you.

They do not flee when you pass. Neither do they approach. A crab withdraws far enough to let your foot settle beside its slot, then returns to the lip. One of the ivory animals shifts its weight to make room in the basin. The motion reveals another beneath its belly: a smaller creature, black and polished, standing in the shade with its forehead against the larger animal’s leg.

The abundance is intimate rather than loud. You hear the soft release of a foot from wet ceramic, a jaw closing, the occasional dry tap of shell against a pipe. Beneath these sounds is a low industrial note, more readily felt through the soles than heard. The pumps and engines are buried in paired mounts that cancel their vibration. Fan outlets are lined with thick, porous baffles. Even the water travels quietly, pressed along open channels with no fall steep enough to make it chatter.

There are gates larger than buildings whose edges remain within a finger’s width of their frames. Inside them, pressure changes. Outside, nothing clangs.

Overhead is an illuminated ceiling so high that its panel joints vanish in the haze. It gives a plain, generous light, bright enough to see color and fine detail. Shadows gather under the animals and the pipe racks. Far away, where moisture is denser, the ceiling becomes a white sky.

Clouds have formed beneath it.

They are broad, low banks of vapor suspended over districts of cooling equipment, their undersides level and their edges softly rounded. They do not drift. Water condenses along their margins and descends as an exceedingly fine rain. Each rainy district has a boundary you can walk through: one step in dry warmth, the next in a curtain of droplets, then out again several minutes later. On the ground, the wet outline matches the cloud overhead.

The scale becomes clearer at an observation platform.

Its stair has only twelve steps, but from the top you can see over the backs of the standing animals and across several neighboring basins. Repeated structures extend outward until repetition itself becomes a texture. A drainage channel wide enough to mistake for a river passes between two districts. Beyond it are ranks of cylindrical tanks, each carrying a whole shallow wetland on its roof. Beyond those, a low, continuous ridge encloses a machinery province whose ventilation clouds cover half the visible distance.

Lighted signs show route numbers. Some have six digits. Their arrows point along walkways disappearing through animal-filled colonnades.

Everything is accessible in principle. There are ladders on the largest shells of machinery, service doors beneath the pools, handrails leading into the haze. Yet the provision of access makes the size harder to comprehend. This is not an expanse left over after construction. It has been fitted throughout, down to the last place where a tool might need to turn.

Beside the platform is a gauge protected by a clear hood. Its needle holds steady. A row of lamps beneath it stays amber. Through a nearby inspection window, a massive piston moves almost imperceptibly within its cylinder.

The place is active, but its activity changes nothing.

Water is taken up and released. Heat passes through bodies and exchangers. Animals feed, breathe, sleep, and wake. Each process returns exactly what another requires. There is no season of surplus, no thinning of the herds, no advancing crust on the pipes. Wear is replaced at the point where it occurs. A scratch in a handrail closes from its bottom outward while you watch, taking several minutes to regain the rail’s satin finish.

The animals appear to have participated in this arrangement for a very long time. Their feet fit the ground. Their resting bodies leave just enough space for the conduits to remain exposed. Small swimmers lie across warm outlets, yielding when the flow strengthens, settling back when it eases. Nothing seems imprisoned; there are passages everywhere. But each passage leads to another equally occupied place.

You can remain beside one pool for hours and see no lasting alteration. The same fish are distributed over the pale floor. The same ivory backs stand above the water. A hanging animal unfolds its perforated wings, holds them open, then closes them along familiar creases. Droplets collect again in the little holes.

Eventually you notice a single drop beneath the railing.

It swells, gathers light, and falls.

Before it strikes the pool, a fish turns upward. The drop touches its mouth with a sound too slight to separate from breathing. Beneath the rail, the next bead begins to form. The fish returns to its place among the others.

Across the immense warm plain, such exchanges are happening everywhere: beneath each bridge, inside every shallow basin, behind the quiet housings of the engines. Enough life to cover a continent, sustained in a condition as exact as the fit between two plates. And room, still, for you to walk through it—for your shadow to cross the water, for a thousand eyes to register your passing, and for the surface behind you to settle back into the light.
