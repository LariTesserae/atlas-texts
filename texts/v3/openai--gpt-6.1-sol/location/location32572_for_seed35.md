The chamber lies just beneath the desert, close enough to the surface that warmth passes through its ceiling but too deep for daylight to find a direct way in. It is not a cavern worn open by water. Nothing here has been rounded, dissolved, or washed clean. The chamber opened when a sheet of buried mineral contracted and pulled away from itself, leaving a narrow, vaulted separation: a room made by a single enormous crack.

It is about the size of a small railway station. From its entrance, a person could see the far wall, if there were fewer things between.

Blades of pale mineral rise from the floor and hang from the ceiling. Some are translucent and as thin as panes of glass; others are thick, opaque wedges with black interiors showing at their broken tips. They stand nearly against one another, dividing the chamber into slots, alcoves, and crooked aisles. Their edges are so fresh that even dust has difficulty settling on them. A dropped scrap of cloth will sometimes catch on an edge and part under its own weight.

The rock looks newly fractured. It has looked that way for longer than anyone can account for.

There is no drip, no damp seam, no dark patch where a hand might find moisture. The floor is a compacted bed of dry granules, faintly greasy to the touch but leaving no stain. They are crystals of a substance that softens under pressure and hardens again when released. Footprints appear beneath a boot and vanish as the boot lifts, without any visible movement of the grains. Kneeling is unpleasant. The ground takes the shape of the knee too exactly.

The air is warm, almost comfortable until one begins to breathe deeply. Then its dryness becomes apparent: a hot mineral taste at the back of the mouth, the slight rasp of dust against the teeth. There is no wind. Heat enters through the ceiling and leaves through the floor, so evenly that no current develops between them.

Light comes from a scattering of industrial fittings mounted among the mineral blades. Perhaps twenty remain: thick ceramic collars, cages of black wire, small lamps burning an exhausted-looking orange. Their cables disappear into holes drilled in the stone. Some cables were severed long ago, and the lamps at their ends continue to shine.

The fittings illuminate nearby surfaces without doing much for the room as a whole. A blade may glow along its entire edge while the narrow passage behind it remains black. Looking across the chamber, one sees a crowded arrangement of luminous margins, like doors left slightly ajar in a building with no rooms.

The sound is considerable.

At first it seems to be machinery working beyond a wall: a dense, metallic roar, with a low pounding beneath it and a finer rattling overhead. But there is no beyond to most of the walls. The rock continues for hundreds of metres. The sound is inside the chamber, coming from nearly everything in it.

Small animals occupy the mineral faces in such numbers that the stone has an apparent second texture. They resemble jointed washers, each with a pale central plate and six black feet fitted beneath its rim. Most are no larger than a thumbnail. They brace themselves against the crystal and strike it with a hard organ on their undersides. Each strike makes a minute click. Together they produce the rattling.

They do not strike at random. On one blade, a colony sounds like a handful of bolts shaken in a tin. On another, the intervals are so close that the clicks become a continuous whine. Their feet feel for vibrations, and when an animal changes position, its neighbours adjust their spacing to accommodate it.

Larger creatures live in the aisles. Their bodies are low, broad, and covered in overlapping grey plates. They have no visible eyes. A fringe of fine wires surrounds the front of each body, moving delicately over the floor. They gather the smallest washer-creatures, lift them between their plates, and carry them elsewhere.

Whether this is feeding, husbandry, or something closer to maintenance is difficult to tell. The small animals usually emerge again, cleaner and sounding a little higher.

Under the floor there are heavier bodies. Their outlines can occasionally be seen as shallow rises in the granular bed: a long back, a shoulder-like bend, several buried limbs. They do not surface. Their movements are too slow to watch, but their knocking travels through the soles of the feet. It is this that gives the chamber its deep, regular percussion.

Only a few things here could be called plants. They grow in the lee of the ceramic lamp collars: stiff tufts of copper-coloured filament, each rooted in a crust of black mineral. Their stems fork at precise angles. Their leaves are little opaque scales, folded tightly together.

They take nourishment from electrical leakage. Where a cable enters the wall, a tuft may extend a single root along the insulation, following it into the stone. The animals avoid these roots, though they will shelter beneath the leaves. Nothing flowers. At the end of each stem is a bright bead that remains the same size indefinitely.

Near the centre of the chamber stands the only substantial construction: a squat engine on four bolted feet, enclosed by a waist-high railing. Its casing is ribbed iron, its flywheel scarcely wider than a person’s outstretched arms. A hand crank hangs from one side. On the other is a pressure gauge with its needle held just short of the red.

The engine runs.

Its flywheel turns at an unwavering speed. A belt passes from it through a slot in the floor, too taut to pluck. No fuel line enters the casing. No exhaust leaves it. The inspection hatch is warm, but not warmer than the surrounding rock.

Someone has marked the railing with short cuts in groups of five. There are not enough marks to suggest a long stay. Beside the engine sits a metal lunch tin, closed and furred with washer-creatures. They leave the latch untouched.

Farther back are two narrow shelters made from corrugated panels, fitted into gaps between the blades. Each contains a bench and a hook. One hook holds a glove, stiff with mineral dust. There are no beds, reservoirs, drains, or arrangements for waste. Whoever built here expected to work a shift and leave.

The engine’s noise is almost lost among the animals. When its casing is touched, however, the whole sound of the chamber seems to arrive through the fingers. The rattling plates, the buried knocking, the humming blades: all of them share something with its rotation. It is not obvious whether the machine powers the room or has merely found a way to turn by listening to it.

Nothing accumulates. Dust is plentiful but never deepens. The plants do not spread beyond the same collars. The mineral edges neither blunt nor lengthen. The animal colonies change position, but a blade vacated at one end acquires an equal crowd at the other. Even the broken cable ends retain their exposed copper shine.

There is activity everywhere, yet no visible consequence of activity. A visitor can stand beside the engine for an hour and see hundreds of small adjustments without seeing anything become different.

At the far wall, the blades close together so tightly that passage ends. Between them is a dark gap no wider than a finger. The sound there is loud enough to feel against the face. A few animals brace along its edges, their little feet spread, their bodies trembling with effort.

Beyond the gap, nothing can be seen.

They have been holding it open.
