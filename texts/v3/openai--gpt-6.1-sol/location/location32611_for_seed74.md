The town occupies a shallow fold in the land, low enough that cold air collects in its streets after sunset. From the ridge above it, you can see almost the whole place: a few long roofs, two factory chimneys, an empty square, and the pale line of a canal that ends before it reaches the farthest houses. Beyond them, the ground rises gently into a continuous surface of dull green.

There is very little exposed earth.

The streets are made of rootwood, laid down in broad, interlocking plates. Their grain follows the direction of travel. At intersections it becomes confused, curling around old drain openings and the feet of lamp columns. The plates have been worn smooth in the middle and remain softly ridged along the edges, where fewer people walk. They give a little underfoot, especially in the morning. By afternoon they stiffen.

Everything living here has learned to hold a shape.

The houses are grown from a species with a thick, pale bark and an interior dense enough to take screws. Their walls rise from low common rootstocks beneath the streets. Each house has been trained around a frame, though most of the frames have long since been absorbed. Occasionally an iron angle shows through a scar beside a window. The lintels have the rounded, patient look of branches that have supported the same weight for decades.

There are gutters, doorbells, street numbers. Copper wiring runs in grooves that the walls keep trying to close. Around every junction box, someone has cut a narrow border through the bark to prevent it from being swallowed. The oldest boxes sit deep in the walls, at the ends of short wooden tunnels.

Despite the amount of vegetation, the town feels open. There are no thickets, no crowded crowns. Leaves grow in thin fans along roof edges, or lie flat against the south-facing walls. Most of the plant’s body is structural: floors, beams, paving, the long hollow conduits beneath the road. Wide intervals separate the buildings. The square is almost bare, its greenish surface interrupted by four benches and a municipal weighing scale.

A few small weeds inhabit the seams around the scale. Here, where everything else has a use and a permitted outline, they look unusually wild.

The canal is narrow enough to jump in several places. Usually it contains only a dark ribbon of water below a ledge of exposed fibrous banks. Its bed is lined with an aquatic growth that resembles overlapping leather straps. When water arrives, the straps lift and loosen, allowing it through. As the level falls, they settle together again, covering the wet mud and slowing evaporation.

Rain is infrequent and seldom heavy. More often the town receives a night of mist, followed by a morning in which every railing is cold and wet. Droplets collect beneath the eaves. Fine channels in the walls carry them toward the foundations. At the end of each street, a broad-leaved collector tree holds its leaves nearly horizontal until enough water has gathered; then the leaf stems relax in sequence, tipping the moisture inward. For a minute or two, you hear irregular tapping inside the trunk.

By noon the roofs are dry. Dampness remains in doorways, under bridges, and inside the empty freight sheds. These sheltered places smell of cut stems, old iron, and the mineral sharpness of stored water.

The factories stand at opposite ends of the square. Neither is large. One produces cordage and packing fiber; the other presses boards from the annual growth trimmed off the town itself.

The cordage works has a tall roof supported by six living columns. Thin branches enter through high slots in the walls and are drawn slowly across a combing bed. Rollers separate the long fibers, twist them, and wind them onto spools. The machinery runs through much of the day, but at a restrained speed. A low vibration reaches the square through the paving. Close to the doors, it resolves into soft clicks and the hiss of material passing between oiled wheels.

There is no loud factory bell. At the end of a shift, the roof vents open.

You can tell which machines are working by the movement of the leaves above them. Warm air from the drying room keeps one patch fluttering even on still days. Elsewhere the foliage barely moves.

The pressing works uses a little electricity and a great deal of time. Fresh growth is sorted, compressed, heated, and left under weight. Finished boards emerge pale and slightly fragrant. They must be kept away from damp walls until their remaining life has gone out of them. A board installed too soon will attempt to join the building: first a bead of sap at the edge, then a small swelling, eventually a leaf where a nail should be.

The town has regulations about this. Timber intended to remain timber is stamped twice, once when it is pressed and again after its resting period. The second stamp is a small square without a center.

The older buildings carry hundreds of such marks, many almost polished away.

Weathering here has happened slowly. Corners have softened. Doorsteps have sunk toward the weight most often placed on them. The canal parapets are scalloped where generations rested their forearms, and the shallow hollows now collect enough moisture to support a fine, velvety growth. Old damage remains visible as changes in grain rather than gaps. A broken wall heals unevenly, leaving a knot or a bulge that builders must work around.

Behind the pressing works is a room whose outer wall collapsed many years ago. The surviving walls gradually leaned toward each other and joined overhead. It is used as a bicycle store now. Its ceiling is low, ribbed, and unexpectedly strong.

Nothing grows quickly enough to surprise you while you watch. Nevertheless, the town is busy.

Below the square, roots redistribute the night’s collected moisture. A roof thickens above a repaired beam. The cordage branches extend through their guide slots by fractions of an inch. Along the canal, the lining opens around a new trickle arriving from upstream. In the freight shed, loose fibers turn slowly beneath a ventilation fan.

At the edge of town, workers are lowering a roof.

Its rooms have been empty for several seasons, and maintaining them consumes more water than the town can spare. Broad straps have been fixed around the roof’s living ribs. Each week the workers tighten the straps a little, prune the outer leaves, and remove another length of internal bracing. Eventually the house will become a raised bed beside the road. Its doorway will remain as a low arch, and the street number will be transferred to a stake.

There are few animals. A gray bird sometimes visits the canal, standing motionless on one leg until something stirs beneath the straps. Once or twice a year, a small burrowing creature gets into the warm waste heaps behind the factories. Its passages are discovered by touch: a soft place in material that ought to be firm.

Most days, the only moving figures are people crossing the square, and even they are widely spaced. One carries an empty reel. Another stops to scrape a strip of bark from a cable groove. A cyclist passes slowly because the street has swelled near a drain.

The daylight is ordinary and sufficient. On clear afternoons, shadows lie sharply beneath the roof leaves, and the upper walls look almost yellow. A gray day makes the town seem flatter and brings out the dark seams in its paving. The street lamps come on early in winter. Their posts have rooted, although this was not intended, and each bears a small crown of leaves just below the light.

Near dusk, the cold returns to the fold in the land. Factory vents close. The canal darkens before the streets do. Somewhere a cart wheel catches in a pavement joint, then rolls free.

After the workers leave, the machinery continues at its slower night setting. There is a faint vibration under the benches. Condensation begins to gather on the copper guttering, and the broad collector leaves, lowered all afternoon, gradually lift to receive it.
