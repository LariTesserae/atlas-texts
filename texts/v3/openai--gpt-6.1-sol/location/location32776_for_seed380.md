The district lies one storey beneath the town, in a hollow made by the settling of its foundations. It is small enough that, standing on the central landing, you can see nearly all of it: six workshop fronts, a loading court, the blunt end of a canal, and three stairways ascending into grey daylight. The stairs are broad at the bottom and narrow at the top. Generations of repairs have thickened the walls around them.

Rain reaches the place long after it has stopped falling above. It arrives through mortar, down the backs of iron shutters, along cables in their glazed ceramic brackets. Sometimes a dry afternoon overhead produces the wettest hour here. The brickwork stores weather and releases it according to an older schedule.

There is no dry route across the court. There are merely routes with different depths.

Near the stairs, a film of water covers the paving without quite concealing it. Farther in, the paving gives way to broad stone slabs, some tilted, some hollowed into shallow basins. Their corners have been rounded by years of carts passing through water. A pair of iron rails runs between the workshops and disappears beneath the canal’s surface. Where the rails remain visible, they carry a soft orange bloom of rust interrupted by polished stretches: something still travels along them, though seldom while anyone is watching.

The air is cool enough to make the handrails unpleasant to hold. It smells of soaked brick, machine oil, and the clean mineral scent released when a stone is lifted from a stream. At ankle height there is another smell, faintly sweet, from the silt accumulated beneath the walkways.

Overhead, the town forms a low, uneven ceiling. You see the undersides of vaults, patched drains, the bellies of water tanks. A few shafts go all the way up, but daylight enters them reluctantly. It reaches the court as separate pale patches, revealing one stair tread or a square of water, then fades into the surrounding greenish dusk. The workshop lamps burn behind thick glass. Each has a small dark crescent of sediment at the bottom, as though it has once been flooded and will be flooded again.

Most of the workshops are closed. Their doors stand a little above the highest waterline, reached by metal steps that fold down into the court. Faded lettering survives on the lintels: valve repair, municipal winding, pressure instruments. In one window, brass fittings are arranged on a shelf lined with felt. The felt has gone white with fungus, but the fittings remain carefully spaced.

The occupied shop is at the canal end. Its machinery turns so slowly that movement first appears as a disagreement between one glance and the next. A wheel taller than a person rotates behind a wire guard. Broad leather belts descend from it into a pit. Every few minutes a piston advances, pauses, then withdraws with the gentle click of a latch being tested.

This is the station that keeps the district at its present depth.

The mechanism has been running for longer than the current town records extend. It draws water from beneath the foundations and feeds it into a header tank somewhere overhead. There should be a strong current through the canal, but there is none. The pump takes water in long, patient measures. Between them, the whole district seems to wait with it.

When the piston completes a stroke, every shallow pool rises by the width of a fingernail.

The water in the canal rises too. So does the water caught in a broken bottle on a windowsill. Even the drops hanging beneath the ceiling swell, hold for a moment, and settle back. Maintenance workers account for this in their measurements. The painted depth marks beside the canal have two scales, one for the ordinary level and one for the stroke.

Plant life keeps to narrow opportunities. A wiry grass grows beneath the largest light shaft, its blades bent permanently toward the opening. Ferns occupy three joints in the northern stair. Moss fills the lettering on an old cast-iron inspection plate, making the words legible in green. Elsewhere the surfaces are bare, or coated with such thin growth that their colour seems to belong to the stone.

There is considerably more animal life.

The first thing you notice is a snail crossing a bolt head. Then, once your eye adjusts, almost every sheltered edge is inhabited. Pale shrimp hang beneath the loading platform, facing into currents too weak for you to feel. Small fish rest in the channels between paving stones. Their bodies are translucent except for the eyes and a dark seam along the spine. When disturbed, they slip beneath the slabs rather than swim toward open water.

The brick cavities hold colonies of soft-bodied creatures whose feelers spread like fine roots. Beetles shelter inside abandoned cable sleeves. Long-legged birds, no larger than pigeons, stand singly on the canal rail, their toes curled around the wet iron. They lower their beaks into the water with extraordinary care and come up carrying something almost invisible.

None of these animals makes much noise. Their abundance becomes apparent through the number of things that move when you sit still.

On the workshop walls, several old flood marks wander away from the horizontal. One passes over a doorframe in a shallow arch. Another descends beside a drain, crosses the paving, and continues up the opposite wall. Small shelled animals graze along these lines, leaving clean tracks through the brown deposit. They will follow a flood mark over dry masonry rather than step into water beside it.

The district’s caretakers leave the marks intact. A fresh coat of paint can alter the water level in a building for weeks.

There are still rules posted beside the central landing. Test the depth before lowering a step. Keep the cable channels clear. Replace inspection covers in the direction marked. Beneath these, in smaller letters: do not carry a full vessel upstairs.

Empty vessels may be taken up. Water must go by the pipes.

You can hear ordinary life above, but only in fragments: a cartwheel crossing a loose stone, a door shut against the wind, the short scraping passage of a chair. Here those sounds become dull and difficult to place. The clearer sounds are close by—the tick of a cooling lamp, a shrimp tapping the underside of sheet metal, the pump’s latch engaging.

Nothing suggests abandonment. The lamps are tended. The wire guard has a recent patch. Someone has laid a fresh plank across a gap in the landing and weighted both ends with bricks. Yet human activity occupies very little of the space. A caretaker makes one circuit at dusk, carrying a hooded light and a narrow measuring rod. Occasionally a mechanic spends an afternoon inside the pump room. Their visits leave a few damp footprints above the waterline.

The rest of the time belongs to the slow work of the place: mortar softening, sediment finding a hollow, animals moving between one sheltered surface and another. The foundations have finished their great descent. What remains is a smaller adjustment, continuing everywhere.

Late in the day, the light withdraws from the northern stair. The grass beneath the shaft loses its colour. A bird shifts one foot on the rail.

Behind the workshop glass, the great wheel has turned far enough for a white chalk mark to become visible near its rim. The piston begins another stroke. Water gathers against the stone, enters the old grooves, lifts gently around the feet of the stairs.

For a little while, every pool is deeper together.
