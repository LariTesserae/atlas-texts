The high country begins long before the land visibly rises. First the trees become smaller, then fewer, then disappear from the slopes while continuing for another hundred miles in sheltered clefts. The rivers lose their banks. They pass between slabs of dark stone, vanish under gravel, and emerge farther on with a skin of ice already forming over them. Eventually there is only the plateau: a breadth of worn upland under a low ceiling of cloud, so extensive that storms develop over its interior and expire there without ever finding a coast.

Nothing here is sharply peaked. The mountains have been standing too long for that. Their summits are broad, their flanks scalloped by vanished glaciers, their ridgelines interrupted by saddles wide enough to contain entire watersheds. From one height you can see several others, faint and blue-gray, but never the edge of the country.

Most of the ground is bare. Shale lies in overlapping scales. Pale gravel collects in shallow fans below the slopes. There are fields of rounded stones with dark undersides, each resting in a depression it has occupied for centuries. Frost lifts them a little in winter; thaw lets them settle again. Over time, this small movement has sorted the earth into rings and stripes. Seen from above, whole valleys have the patient grain of timber.

The light seldom becomes bright enough to cast a firm shadow. Even at midday it seems to arrive through a great depth of water. A break in the clouds may illuminate a far ridge without warming it. For several minutes that ridge looks near, showing every drainage seam and patch of snow; then the opening closes, and the distance returns.

Moisture is present chiefly as evidence. A blackened underside to a stone. A damp stripe beneath a snowbank. Frost on the sheltered side of a boulder, although the air around it feels dry. The rain, when it comes, is fine and brief, drifting almost sideways. Much of it freezes before it can gather into drops. In hollows, however, water survives: narrow pools with ice at their margins, bogs no wider than an animal’s body, streams that travel for miles beneath rubble.

These wet places support the vegetation. Low sedges lie combed in one direction. Cushions of small, tough leaves grow in cracks, brown at a distance and dark green when touched. Some have been widening for longer than any of the surrounding animals have been alive. Their roots follow the warmth of buried water, and a single plant may show itself at three separate fissures, with a hundred feet of bare rock between them.

There is very little to look at until you learn how much of it is animal.

What appears to be a shelf of pale scree will lift its heads.

The great grazing herds lie down for most of the dim day. Their coats are the color of quartz dust, their bodies low and deep, their legs folded entirely beneath them. Frost accumulates along their backs. At rest, several thousand of them can resemble a patch of geological weathering, with darker stones scattered among the pale. The darker stones are their calves.

They do not eat much of the visible growth. They nose aside gravel and draw out the mats of roots below, or lick mineral-rich films from rock. Long tongues work through cracks too narrow for a muzzle. Their passage leaves the sedge mostly intact and the stone newly clean.

There are immense numbers of them, but immense distances too. One herd may occupy a basin the size of a small country. From its upper rim, you see only scattered figures and, farther away, a slight unevenness in the color of the ground. Days of walking might take you through the spaces between its gatherings. Only where a valley narrows do the numbers become unmistakable: backs filling the floor from wall to wall, the procession extending beyond both ends of sight.

The sound remains small. A scrape of hoof. Breathing close enough to feel. An occasional fracture of ice. The animals place their feet carefully, and the fine gravel does not ring beneath them. Even the wind has little to work on. There are no tall stems, no branches, no loose sheets of anything. It passes over the worn land with a low friction that is sometimes audible only when it stops.

At certain cold springs, the animals gather more densely. The rock there contains metals in broad, folded seams. Water rising from below carries iron and manganese to the surface, where dark crusts form around the outlets. Beneath those crusts live colonies of organisms that exchange charge across networks of fine filaments.

Their activity is slow, but the networks are enormous.

A seam may continue beneath several valleys, carrying a weak current along wet mineral surfaces. In a warmer country the living films would grow rapidly and exhaust their food. Here they work at the pace allowed by near-frozen water, maintaining themselves through long seasons of darkness. Their heat keeps some springs open all winter.

The grazers come to these springs to warm their mouths and take salts. Small flat animals cling beneath their jaws, feeding on material carried away from the mineral crusts. Others shelter in the warm footprints until the water freezes. Under the herds, below the stones, life is crowded into every available degree of warmth.

No one has built anything here. Nevertheless, the spring districts have the character of an industrial landscape. Black deposits spread from the outlets. Dense vapor rises in cold weather and settles as frost downwind. Water passes through deep strata, comes up carrying dissolved material, sheds it, and descends again through another fracture. The ground conducts. The living films refine what reaches them. Animals carry the products outward across the plateau.

Everything operates, and almost nothing looks busy.

There are smaller creatures everywhere: pale mites beneath flakes of shale, burrowing things in the dry banks, soft-bodied grazers inside the ice where algae grow. On mild afternoons, wingless insects emerge around pools and darken the shorelines. Birds arrive from far beyond the plateau, landing among them without a call. The birds remain widely spaced, each feeding in its own small circle, and then leave overnight.

Predators move through the herds with such patience that their presence can be mistaken for indecision. Long-bodied, dark-coated animals lie along ridges for hours, watching the routes below. They hunt by waiting near the few places where water and passable ground coincide. After a kill, scavengers may take weeks to find the body. Cold preserves it. A single carcass becomes a local abundance, supporting successive visitors and then, for years, a greener patch among the stones.

The seasons are distinguished less by warmth than by what can move. In winter the plateau holds nearly everything still. In summer, water opens routes through it. Herds cross divides that have become free of ice. Root mats drink. The conductive seams strengthen briefly as meltwater joins their separate wet chambers.

These changes travel slowly. A thaw can take months to climb from the southern basins into the central uplands. By the time it reaches them, the first basins are freezing again.

From a high ridge, none of this movement is immediately visible. You see the subdued land, its old curves, snow lodged in the same hollows, a few dark spring stains. Then, after watching for a long while, you notice that a pale strip on a distant slope has shifted. A second strip is crossing a saddle. Farther still, beyond a valley almost lost in cloud, the ground is moving too.
