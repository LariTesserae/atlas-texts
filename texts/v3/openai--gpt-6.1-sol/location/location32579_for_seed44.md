The district occupies a shallow pocket between a canal embankment and the backs of three old factory buildings. You could cross it in a few minutes if the paths were straight. They are not. They turn around settling tanks, pass beneath pipe racks, narrow beside sheds, and disappear into vegetation before emerging at another door. From above, it would look like a single green block in the town, with a few strips of corrugated roofing showing through.

At ground level there is water everywhere, though very little of it presents itself as open water. It shines between the bricks. It beads along the undersides of handrails. It stands in the folded leaves of plants beside the drains, and seeps from the mortar joints of a retaining wall whose lower courses have been dark for so many years that no one remembers their original color. The canal itself is visible only through a service gate: a broad, dull surface a little higher than the courtyard, held back by masonry.

This is the old pumping quarter. It still works.

The buildings are low and close together, with tall windows divided into small panes. Their brickwork has softened at the corners. Rust has spread beneath brackets in long brown fans; older repairs have become as weathered as the walls they patched. A roof gutter, replaced in bright galvanized steel, is the one conspicuously new thing. It empties into a barrel already overflowing into a narrow bed of sedges.

The pumps run inside the largest building. Their sound reaches every part of the place: a deep, continuous rotation, overlaid by a quicker metallic knocking. Near the engine-room wall, conversation requires a raised voice. Farther away, the noise becomes something you feel through the soles of your shoes on a steel walkway. Every few minutes a valve changes position with a hard clack, and water rushes through an overhead pipe. Then the established rhythm resumes.

The loudness does not make the place seem hurried. Nothing visible keeps pace with it.

A broad leaf takes several seconds to bow under the weight of accumulated droplets. Water advances down a window in an irregular line. The canal barely moves. Even the belts seen through the engine-room doorway appear steady enough to be mistaken, at first, for solid surfaces.

Vegetation fills almost everything that maintenance does not actively hold open. Ivy climbs the factory walls and crosses from building to building along obsolete cables. Ferns occupy the damp ledges beneath windows. Reed beds crowd the settling tanks, their stems so tightly packed that the concrete rims can be found only by following the handrails. Behind the smallest shed, a fig tree has spread against a warm wall; its roots have raised the paving into a broad, shallow swell.

There is no clear patch of soil. Wherever earth might be exposed, something covers it: creeping stems, moss, fallen leaves already softening into a new surface. The ground feels layered rather than flat. Under the steel mesh of the walkways, leaves press upward and flatten against the grid. Workers cut them back often enough to keep a passage, but the cut stems remain green, and new growth rises between them.

Some of the planting is deliberate. Rows of young willow and alder stand in rectangular propagation beds beside the pump house, grown for the town’s canal banks. Water drawn from the settling tanks feeds them through black irrigation tubes. Their labels are written on plastic stakes, half-hidden by leaves. Beyond the labeled rows, the same species grow wherever seeds have lodged. It is difficult to say exactly where the nursery ends.

The warmth collects in the enclosure. Brick gives back yesterday’s heat; the pumps add their own. The air smells of wet leaves, warm oil, and the mineral dampness of old concrete. A worker has left the engine-room doors open, but the breeze seldom reaches them. The outer trees and buildings break it up before it can cross the yard.

Daylight falls plainly from an overcast sky. On the open walkway it is bright enough to show each rust flake and each bead of water on a leaf. Beneath the pipe racks and vine-covered stairs, it becomes green and diffuse. There are electric lamps above the doors, switched off for now, with moisture filmed inside their glass covers.

The district has acquired its shape through repetition. Water escaping from the same flange has worn a pale groove into the concrete below. Feet have polished the raised centers of old bricks while moss has thickened in the joints. A channel along the courtyard edge follows the lowest ground, bending around a foundation laid before the pumps were installed. Its water is clear over dark sediment, moving so slowly that a fallen petal remains beside the same bolt head for most of an afternoon.

Plants have made the neglected structures useful again in small ways. A roofless storehouse shelters shade-loving ferns between its surviving walls. An old loading frame supports climbing stems. On a disused tank, the circular railing holds up a dense curtain of vine, enclosing a cool, damp space that maintenance workers use to keep spare hose. The hose smells faintly of rubber and algae.

There are few animals to notice. Once, a small bird moves along the canal wall, visible for only a moment between leaves. A snail clings beneath a valve wheel. Insects gather near the warm lamps after dark, but by day the movement belongs mostly to water and machinery.

People appear intermittently, carrying tools or inspecting gauges. Their routes are precise. A narrow strip beside each cabinet must remain clear; no growth is allowed across the intake screens; the drain beneath the main stair is cleaned every week. Elsewhere, the plants are left alone. The quarter is neither abandoned nor kept tidy. It is maintained just enough to continue doing its work.

By late afternoon, condensation has gathered on the cooler pipes. Drops fall onto the leaves beneath them, each landing with a small, sharp sound almost lost in the pumps. Somewhere beyond the walls a truck changes gear, then fades into the town. Here, a gauge needle trembles, a reed rubs against a railing, and warm water continues along the old channel toward the canal.
