The hollow lies just below the mountain’s highest ridge, where a seam of softer stone has worn away beneath a harder cap. From outside, it is almost nothing: a black notch between two pale shoulders of rock, narrow enough to step across if there were anywhere safe to put your feet. Inside, it opens into a chamber the size of a small grove. The ceiling rises, folds, and disappears. Most of the floor is occupied by fallen stone.

There is no view of the country below. You know the height by the thinness of the air at the entrance, by the long time a dislodged pebble takes to stop making noise, and by the clouds that occasionally pass through the notch rather than above it. Somewhere beyond these walls the mountain falls away for a very great distance. Here, everything is close.

The stone is crowded with shapes. Broad slabs lean against rounded pillars; pillars swell into buttresses; buttresses are undercut into shallow alcoves. Nothing has a fresh edge. Even the great blocks on the floor have settled into the hollow’s weather, their fractures softened, their upper faces pitted like old teeth. Small stones collect in the spaces beneath them, and smaller ones beneath those. To cross the chamber is to choose among overlapping surfaces rather than walk on any continuous ground.

Warmth comes up through the floor.

It is modest at the entrance, a little surprising in the cold mountain air. Farther in, it gathers around the ankles and under the ribs. Certain cracks breathe steadily against the skin. Others are cool, their dark interiors drawing air downward. The whole hollow circulates: warmth rises through the rubble, slides along the ceiling, and leaves through the upper slit, while the mountain’s colder breath comes crawling in below.

On still days, this circulation is almost the only movement that belongs to the place itself.

Everything else belongs to the animals.

The ceiling is occupied so densely that its texture is difficult to distinguish from the bodies hanging on it. Small bats gather in the deeper folds, packed flank to flank, their fur damp at the tips. In the higher crevices, dark birds cling upright to the rock. Their nests are little more than hardened cups attached beneath ledges, made from saliva and fibers carried in from far below. There are separate territories here, although no empty space marks them. One kind occupies the warm vault; another takes the narrow chimneys; another lives along the cooler lip where a little outside air reaches the stone.

At first the sound seems like running water. Then individual pieces emerge: dry clicking, the thin scraping of claws, wings opening and closing, a rising chatter answered from the opposite wall. A bird gives a sharp call that strikes the ceiling and comes back several times. Beneath it is the restless, papery noise of hundreds of bodies adjusting their grip.

The chamber is loud even when almost nothing is happening.

A falling drop adds a deep, clean note.

Water is present in separate places, each with its own small geography. A black patch on the ceiling beads slowly until one drop becomes heavy enough to fall. A shallow basin behind a slab holds water so still that it looks solid. Along one wall, a wet line winds downward through a succession of dents, overflowing from one to the next. It disappears into the floor before it can become a stream.

Most of this moisture comes from the warm air touching cold stone. Rain reaches the chamber only when wind drives it through the opening. Then the outer rocks darken, the dust settles, and a brief trickle threads between the slabs. Hours later, there may be no sign of it except a few swollen scraps of moss and the damp smell rising from the lower pockets.

Vegetation survives near the entrance, where daylight arrives as a narrow gray wash. There are low cushions of moss, a fern with only three leathery fronds, and several pale stems rooted in a crack too small to see into. They do not spread across the hollow. They remain in their particular inches of shelter, enduring long dry intervals, then opening or softening when the clouds come through.

Beyond that, the living cover is animal.

Crickets stand in the seams with antennae extended into the warm air. Their bodies are the color of wet stone, but their legs catch what little light there is. Beetles move beneath the surface of the fallen droppings. Tiny mites gather around feathers and shed skins. In the wettest hollows, translucent larvae flex through a dark paste that smells sharply of mineral water and decay.

The floor’s apparently inert material is being handled constantly. A flake shifts. A grain rolls uphill on the back of something scarcely larger than itself. Under a dead bird, the ground rises and collapses in minute places.

This abundance is fed from outside. Every evening, the winged inhabitants leave through the notch and the thin shafts above it, descending toward valleys whose warmth comes from the sun. They return carrying that lower world within them. Seeds appear in crevices. Fragments of insect shell accumulate beneath favorite perches. A dry wing from some large moth may lie here among the stones, far above any flower it could have visited.

For a short interval after the departures, the hollow becomes spacious. The ceiling resolves into rock. The wing noise dwindles, leaving the crickets and the patient drip. Warm vapor lifts from the deepest cracks and becomes visible where it crosses the last light.

Then darkness finishes filling the chamber.

A few residents never leave. A squat, soft-footed predator waits beneath an overhang, its eyes briefly reflecting the entrance when it turns its head. It hunts by listening. Sometimes it climbs high enough to disturb a roost, and the resulting outcry fills every recess at once. More often it stays still for so long that dust settles on its back.

The hollow changes slowly. Water enlarges one basin while sealing another with a faint mineral crust. A nesting ledge thickens by fractions. Rubble settles into the warmth below. The same routes remain open through the crowded stone, polished where generations of small feet have passed.

Near dawn, the returning wings interrupt this patience. They enter suddenly, fold, vanish upward. For a while the darkness seems full of crossings. Then each body finds its place, claws tighten, calls subside into murmuring, and the warm chamber is occupied again.

Outside, the summit is exposed to wind and empty sky. Inside, a single drop gathers beneath the stone, above more mouths than it could ever satisfy.
