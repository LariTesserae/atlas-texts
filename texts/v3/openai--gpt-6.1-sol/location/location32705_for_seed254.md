The place lies one storey below the street, behind a narrow door between two warehouses. From outside, there is only a greenish pane above the lintel, a warm draught, and the sound of machinery somewhere underfoot. The stairs descend through damp brick. Halfway down, leaves begin to reach across the handrail.

At the bottom is an old municipal growing house: a sunken court, perhaps twenty metres across, roofed in glass and enclosed by the foundations of the surrounding buildings. It once supplied plants for public halls, station platforms, the sheltered corners of markets. It still does, though the room has grown much denser than its original plan allowed.

There are plants on the floor, on the walls, beneath the benches, along the overhead pipes. Plants occupy the gutters where roof water collects. Their roots have entered the mortar and run along it in pale cords, disappearing behind slabs of moss. At first the whole court seems to be one continuous body of foliage. Only after standing still for a while do the divisions emerge: concrete aisles, tiers of growing shelves, a little pump room, the black mouths of drains.

The floor is lower in the middle. Years of washing and settlement have given it the shallow shape of a basin, and the aisles are raised just enough to remain walkable. Even these are wet. Water lies in the worn hollows of the concrete, trembling with the vibration of the pumps. A shoe placed carelessly will send a thin sheet of it over the edge and into the ferns.

Nothing here is dry enough to become dust.

The largest leaves belong to old specimens that have long since ceased to be portable. Their trunks rise from brick-sided beds and bend beneath the roof trusses, broad blades gathered near the glass. Lower down are the plants that tolerate their shade: ferns, mosses, small glossy shrubs, vines with leaves pressed flat against the walls. Some beds have become so thoroughly rooted that their soil cannot be seen. Fallen leaves soften where they land, slipping into the dark mass beneath the living ones.

The air is warm, but not with the clean warmth of sunlight. Heat comes from the pipes and the circulating water, from the adjoining boiler cellar, from brick that never has time to cool. On entering, the warmth settles against the face. A few minutes later a collar feels damp. The smell is of wet leaf stems, humus, old iron, and the faint mineral sharpness of warm water running over concrete.

Above, the glass roof is at pavement level. Light reaches it between the warehouse walls, through protective wire grilles and a film of soot that rain only partly removes. The highest foliage has a dull silver-green edge. Below it, everything is darker: deep greens, nearly black undersides, brown water between roots. Old electric lamps supplement the daylight over the propagation benches. Their light falls in narrow strips, sufficient for trays of cuttings but not enough to brighten the room.

Occasionally a shadow passes over the roof. Someone crossing the court at street level briefly interrupts the pale squares overhead. The growing house is too far below, and too full of its own noise, for footsteps to be heard.

The pumps are loud.

One produces a heavy, even drone behind a partition almost concealed by climbing stems. Another has a worn bearing that adds a higher note. Water rattles through a loose pipe bracket, strikes the lid of a settling tank, and pours over a low overflow behind the central beds. Each sound is steady enough to disappear from attention, but together they fill the enclosure. Conversation requires raised voices. A metal tool dropped on the floor makes a brief sharp intrusion, then the machinery closes around it again.

There is little else to hear. No birds use the roof. No insects circle the lamps. The growing house keeps no animals, and its screened vents and treated incoming stock have left it unusually empty of them. A damaged leaf remains still until water or a worker moves it.

The construction is old and heavily adapted. Galvanized ducts pass beneath brick arches. New plastic irrigation tubes are fastened to rusting steel rails. A cast-iron column has been boxed around with planting benches, leaving only its swollen base visible among pots. The original floor tiles survive beside the stair, their pattern abraded almost away. Where a drainage channel turns a corner, decades of flowing water have polished the concrete smooth enough to reflect a lamp.

There are labels everywhere, though many have disappeared behind growth. White tags tied to stems. Faded numbers on bench ends. A painted arrow directing workers toward an aisle now narrowed to the width of a shoulder. The room remains organized, but its organization has to be maintained by cutting back what wants to cross it.

Along one wall, a service passage gives access to valves, switches, and the water tanks. Here the vegetation is clipped into an abrupt vertical face. Fresh cut ends shine among older stems. Beyond that plane, the foliage is almost impenetrable. A worker can open a valve without touching a leaf, but cannot turn around without brushing against them.

Change comes slowly. The same pumps run through the morning and the afternoon. Water returns to the settling tank, is warmed, and passes through the beds again. A leaf uncurls over several days. A root thickens against a drain cover over several years. Mineral deposits gather below a leaking joint until they make a small pale ledge, green at its lower edge.

The clearest signs of use are small clearings: a space where three pots have recently been lifted, a swept patch beside the stair, a bench carrying rows of new cuttings. These gaps do not last. Pots are shifted into them; leaves lean across them; water darkens the newly exposed surfaces.

From the lowest step, the room has almost no visible distance. It is full from the wet floor to the dim roof. Yet narrow routes persist through it, worn and pruned and carefully kept open. Warm water moves behind the leaves. The machinery keeps its volume. Above the glass, the city continues at street level, while below, in the crowded green court, another season is being grown.
