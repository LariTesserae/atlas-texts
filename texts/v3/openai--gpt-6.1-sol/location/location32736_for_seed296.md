At first the country appears level. Roads run across it without much rise or fall, and the roofs of the town lie close to the height of the surrounding ground. Only at the western edge, where a street ends at a railing, does the elevation become apparent. Beyond the railing there is nothing for a long distance downward: a pale interval of cloud, then another, and finally the faint green crease of a valley so far below that its river seems stationary.

The town stands on what remains of a mountain range.

There are no peaks left on this part of it. Weather has worked the summit country into a broad field of low stone humps, shallow basins, and rounded ridges. The rock is dark gray when dry and almost black under moisture. In places it has been polished by ages of windborne grit; elsewhere it has opened into thin, parallel leaves. A person can lift one of these leaves from the roadside and find the impression of another surface beneath it, equally old-looking, equally smooth. Quarrymen stopped digging here generations ago. Whatever depth they reached, they found stone that looked as though it had already been exposed to the sky.

The high country extends several miles around the settlement. It is wide enough to contain districts with different weather. The eastern gardens can be bright with a cold, diluted sun while condensation streams down windows in the western streets. Northward, the land breaks into shelves overlooking a cloud-filled gorge. Southward, low wooded folds converge around the machinery yards. Beyond these are more plateaus, separated by drops too deep to see from an ordinary window.

Most of the day is dim. Clouds form below the town and rise against its sides, thinning as they pass over the rim. Their light comes upward into rooms. People who live along the western edge keep their lower panes uncovered and draw curtains across the upper ones.

There is rain, but not often enough to explain all the green.

Moisture collects selectively. Certain walls bead with water in clear weather. Some stone hollows stay damp through weeks when the surrounding soil becomes friable and pale. On the undersides of iron walkways, droplets grow with great patience, then fall almost simultaneously along an entire span. The maintenance crews know which bridges do this in the morning and which do it after dusk. They carry folded oilcloth hoods.

Water is stored wherever a basin will hold it: in roof tanks, in old grinding bowls, in reservoirs constructed from abandoned courtyards. Narrow channels cross the streets under removable grates. Every household has a legal right to some portion of the passing trickle, and the doors of the water offices are surrounded by small, exact arguments. A disputed gutter may serve five apartments, two greenhouses, a public washhouse, and a bed of medicinal roots.

The vegetation presses into these arrangements.

There are low trees with broad, leathery leaves, their trunks bent more by years of frost than by wind. They grow close together in the sheltered folds, branching almost at ground level. Between them rise ferns as tall as a person, dark climbing shrubs, and thick mats of small round leaves that conceal the drainage cuts. The town’s gardens are not separated cleanly from the surrounding growth. A vegetable bed continues into a stairwell; a fern thicket occupies the roof of a disused tram shed; roots enter the warm gravel beneath a transformer and have to be lifted out each spring.

In the older quarters, houses are reached through vegetation rather than past it. Stone steps descend between banks of glossy leaves. Upper rooms open onto narrow bridges with planted troughs along both sides. Ground-floor windows are often nothing more than faint yellow rectangles behind overlapping stems.

The growth is lush but slow to become tall. Everything invests in thickness. Leaves have waxy skins. Moss forms pads dense enough to support a boot before releasing the water held inside. Tree roots spread into broad collars around stones, and the oldest trunks look less like columns than bundles of hardened rope. Dead wood seldom dries completely. It softens from within, nourishing pale fungi whose branching bodies emerge from cracks in the bark.

There is a continual rearrangement under way. A bank of moss swells after a night of fog and obstructs a channel. Workers clear it, sending water into a bed that has been dry for days. The bed darkens; folded leaves open; a film of green begins to form across the wet stone. By evening, another channel farther downhill has gone empty. Much of the town’s labor consists of watching such changes and responding before they spread.

Industry occupies the southern hollows, where buildings can be sunk into the ground and protected from the cold. Long sheds have roofs of ribbed glass, usually clouded from within. Beneath them, belt-driven presses manufacture tiles, valve housings, woven insulation, and the dark flexible sheets used to line reservoirs. Electric motors turn at low speed. Heavy flywheels continue moving after the power is cut, and a shift can finish several operations on their stored momentum.

The factories are surprisingly quiet from outside. Their foundations rest on deep layers of fibrous peat, which absorb vibration. Walls are packed with felted plant material. Open doors reveal movement before sound: belts passing overhead, a press lowering, workers crossing between benches with trays held level. Then comes the soft repetitive knock of machinery, and the smell of hot metal and wet cloth.

Steam is carefully recovered. Pipes lead from the works into public drying rooms, propagation houses, and heated seats at tram stops. In winter, the warm routes through town can be read in the vegetation. A line of unfrosted leaves climbs a wall, disappears behind a bakery, and emerges beside a stair rail.

The trams are short, with narrow bodies and tall windows. They travel slowly because the tracks cross so many water channels. At junctions they wait while a keeper raises a section of rail to let accumulated leaves pass underneath. Their bells are padded with leather. In the streets, the loudest regular noise is often the click of a cooling pipe.

Animal life is sparse enough to attract notice. Small gray birds arrive in pairs during clear spells, walking along the gutters and probing beneath the grates. There are pale moths in the heated houses, but few survive outside. Once or twice a year, a shaggy animal from the distant shelves appears among the northern gardens. It eats the lower bark from several trees, sleeps beside a warm wall, and leaves without anyone seeing how it crossed the gorge.

Children keep records of these visits. A bird on a particular roof can remain a subject of conversation for days.

Without many animals to disturb it, the vegetation carries out much of its business visibly. Spores rise in brief clouds from the woodland folds. Seedpods tighten in the cold and split when the factory warmth reaches them. Pollination brushes hang beside greenhouse doors, and workers use them on their way to other tasks. Some shrubs spread by dropping long branches into adjoining beds; those branches root, and within a few seasons a path has shifted several feet.

There are places where the stone contributes moisture of its own.

On certain cold afternoons, water gathers on bare rock that has been dry since morning. It forms in the shallow depressions first, then along pale seams. Instruments detect a slight rise in temperature beneath the surface. The town has built small collection gutters around the more reliable patches, though attempts to enclose them have failed: roofed stone stops producing water, even when the roof is glass.

No one agrees on the cause. The oldest engineering maps simply mark these areas as open sources and specify that they must be left exposed.

Near one such source stands a square of abandoned apartment buildings, roofless but still connected by their upper walkways. Their rooms are filled with shrubs. Window frames carry moss so thick that the openings have become round. Water gathers on the courtyard paving every seventeen days, though the interval occasionally shortens in prolonged cold. People come with buckets and wait. While they wait, they inspect the old walls for movement, because a new seam has been opening across the square for nearly forty years.

Very little here happens quickly enough to startle anyone, but almost nothing is still.

At dusk, crews go out to change the catchment screens along the rim. The factory roofs glow faintly. Trams bring the last shift home through streets crowded with wet leaves, suspended walkways, pipes, and the backs of adjoining houses. Above the roofs, the broad, worn upland remains visible in patches: a stone rise, a stand of low trees, the silver line of a reservoir.

Then cloud lifts over the western railing and moves through the town at walking speed. It wets the exposed stone and passes between the buildings. People close their upper windows. In the gutters, after a while, the water begins to run.
