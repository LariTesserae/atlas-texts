The first impression is of a country lifted too high.

Not mountains rising from a plain, but the remnants of a plain standing far above everything else: immense tables of pale stone, their tops almost level, their sides falling into distances that the clear air cannot quite resolve. From some edges you can see other edges below, and beneath those a brown brightness where suspended dust obscures the lower world. No one here measures height from the bottom. Heights are measured from the railway.

The sun is white and ordinary. It casts definite shadows beneath bolts, beneath animals, beneath the lips of worn steps. By noon the stone is warm enough to feel through a boot sole. The wind, when it comes, is also warm, having crossed hundreds of miles of exposed rock. It carries no suggestion of rain.

Across the uplands, the surface has been worked into shallow ribs and broad hollows. There are places that look like dried riverbeds, but the channels were cut by grains moving along the ground, grain against grain, for longer than the cities have existed. Elsewhere, sheets of harder material have survived as low shelves, their edges rounded and polished. The whole landscape has the settled look of something that has already lost everything easy to remove.

There is almost no loose sand. Sand is a traveling material here. It moves in thin, intermittent streams along grooves in the rock and disappears over the precipices. What remains underfoot is dense, warm, finely pitted stone, interrupted by seams of dark glass and patches of a white mineral that cracks into small hexagons.

The cities occupy the intersections of those seams.

From a distance, a city can be difficult to distinguish from a more complicated patch of geology. Its buildings are low relative to the plateau, but some are large enough to contain their own horizons of girders. Long, flat roofs extend over factory halls. Chimneys stand unused for weeks, then give off a little ocher smoke. Cylindrical storage towers lean inward against the wind. There are residential blocks with deeply recessed windows, freight yards, switching stations, and streets laid out with a confidence that suggests crowds.

The crowds never came, or have thinned, or were never necessary.

A street may run for an hour’s walk between buildings without passing a shop. Between districts lie miles of bare plateau. These spaces are included in the city maps, named and numbered, supplied with power at their margins. A solitary streetlamp stands where two surveyed avenues cross on unaltered rock. It lights every evening. Farther away, a pedestrian bridge spans a shallow depression that could easily be walked around. Its handrails have been polished by generations of contact.

The inhabitants prefer construction that makes few demands. Doors are weighted rather than sprung. Shutters close under their own mass. Walls are thick, often faced with slabs cut from the foundations. Every occupied room has a settling shelf below its windows, where dust can collect without falling onto the floor. A housekeeper sweeps that shelf into a tin perhaps once a month. The dust is sold back to the works.

Water exists, but indoors and under supervision. It arrives in sealed cylinders, passes through bodies and machinery, and is recovered. Public drinking rooms have padded floors beneath the dispensers so that a dropped cup will not break. There are no fountains, baths, roof gutters, or ornamental basins. Children know the sound of running water from the narrow pipes behind washroom walls; they can reach adolescence without seeing it fall freely through air.

Out on the plateau, the rare stain left by a leak becomes an event. Animals gather at it. The pale stone darkens, gives off a sharp mineral smell, and then returns very slowly to its former color.

There are animals everywhere.

At first they are mistaken for fittings, scraps, or irregularities in the rock. A row of dark clamps along a parapet unfolds and moves off. A discarded length of insulated cable lifts its front end and feels beneath a door. The small, flat things lying in the street are resting animals, not flakes of metal, and will slide aside if a shadow remains over them too long.

The commonest have broad bodies, six short legs, and shells the color of iron left in dry heat. They graze on weathering surfaces. Their mouths are circular pads which they press against the ground, lifting microscopic scales of mineral, along with whatever finer organisms inhabit the stone. At dusk the plateau is covered with the tiny rasping marks they have made. Most of these disappear within a season. Some, repeated along the same route for centuries, become paths.

Larger creatures inhabit the cliffs. They are narrow enough to lie inside a fracture, with numerous hooked limbs and a dark membrane folded along each side. Occasionally one opens those membranes and lets go.

It does not quite fly. It sinks through the warm air, steering toward another wall, crossing a gulf that would take a train two days to go around. Watching it, you understand something of the true dimensions of the place. For a long time it remains a recognizable animal. Then it becomes a moving fleck. Then you continue watching a section of cliff where it may or may not have arrived.

Under the loading platforms live colonies of soft gray scavengers. They eat grease, insulation, shed skin, and the dead of other species. Factory workers leave perforated boxes for them near the bearings of stationary machines. Their presence is useful, provided their numbers are watched. A motor left dormant through an entire hot season may be cleaned so thoroughly that its windings have to be replaced.

There are few calls and no songs. Most of the animals communicate through contact with the ground. Set a cup on a stone windowsill and, on certain evenings, its surface will tremble with messages passing through the city foundations. People who work alone sometimes keep a listening rod beside their chairs: a length of metal, one end placed against the floor, the other held to the jaw. Through it comes a subdued clicking, a scrape, a pause, then the answering scrape from surprisingly far away.

Nothing green grows. There is no season of leaves and no smell of cut stems. The finer life occupies pores, shaded undersides, the interiors of cracks. Some of it forms a faint bronze film on old masonry. Some makes the stone smell dusty even before it has been disturbed. The animals feed on it, spread it, and carry its dormant forms in the joints of their shells.

At the factories, that same life is cultivated on mineral beds beneath heated rollers. The harvest is scraped away as a dull powder, separated into food, lubricant, and a conductive paste used in electrical contacts. Processing halls are warm and nearly silent. Inside, enormous belts move at a pace slow enough that their motion is apparent only beside a fixed mark. Workers cross above them on grated walkways. A shift can end with the same ridge of material still approaching the same roller.

The heaviest machinery is set below ground, not for concealment but because a plateau is a resonant structure. Early engines could be heard through the stone fifty miles away. They unsettled the cliff animals, confused migrations, and brought swarms of small grazing creatures into the streets. Now the engine rooms hang in excavated chambers on layered suspension frames. Their exhaust travels through ducts to vents beyond the inhabited districts.

You may walk above a works producing enough power for several cities and hear only your own steps, a loose sign tapping against its bracket, and the faint dry friction of an animal feeding at the curb.

The railways carry most of the visible movement. They cross the uplands in long straight runs and curve inland before reaching an edge. Where two plateaus stand sufficiently close, the line passes over a bridge. These bridges are among the few structures that announce themselves against the landscape: dark lattices suspended in bright emptiness, with maintenance cabins lodged among their members.

Trains are infrequent and very long. A locomotive may arrive in the morning and the last wagon not pass the station until the light has begun to change. They carry mineral slabs, sealed water cylinders, replacement machinery, powdered food, and shells gathered from the molting grounds. Passengers travel in a few windowed cars near the center, where the movement is gentlest.

Nobody hurries onto a train. A departure is posted days in advance. Once the couplings have taken the load, there is still time to walk beside it, speak to someone through an open window, and turn back before the platform ends.

Weather develops across the empty districts. Heat slowly gathers over the dark seams; cooler air settles above the pale expanses. By late afternoon, these differences may draw a broad circulation across an entire plateau. Dust rises in columns several miles wide, transparent except where they cross the sun. They migrate at walking speed.

A city in their path closes its outer shutters. Freight wagons are secured. Street sweepers retreat to recesses built for the purpose. Then the dust arrives, softening the distances, putting a little grit in every unsealed joint. It may last an hour or three days. Behind closed windows the daylight turns beige, but there is little noise. When the air clears, the nearest buildings seem oddly sharp, and familiar things have acquired fresh pale edges.

Sometimes a dust column goes over a cliff and continues turning out in open air. It keeps the shape it learned on the plateau until it has dispersed too far to see.

There are rules for living beside the edges. Nothing is thrown over. Waste goes to the recovery yards, including broken masonry. Doors facing a precipice open inward. Every stair descending into a cliff has a shelter at each landing, because the weather below can be different from the weather above. A clear, calm morning at a station does not mean its lower maintenance galleries are safe.

The cities also keep buildings back from certain fractures. Surveyors revisit these with measuring bars and notebooks, often returning to measurements begun by their grandparents. A crack widening by the thickness of paper in ten years is watched. One widening faster is given a street name, fenced, and eventually treated as a new boundary.

Much of the local history concerns such adjustments. A district was moved uphill. A rail spur was lengthened around an unstable shelf. A factory floor, once continuous, was divided by a joint in the bedrock; the two halves still operate, connected by flexible ducts and a footbridge. There are maps showing houses that now stand alone on a detached pillar, their contents removed, their windows shuttered against weather that will enter anyway.

Near sunset, the cliff faces turn ocher while the uplands remain pale. Shadow fills the deep intervals first. The animals leave their daytime shelters, and small movements become apparent all across what looked, at noon, like empty ground.

In the cities, windows light one by one. A crane advances a suspended load a few feet and stops. Someone comes out onto a roof to check the heat vanes. On a distant railway, the leading lamps of a freight train appear; for a while they seem stationary.

The warmth stays in the paving long after dark. You can sit on a doorstep with your palm against it and feel the minute vibrations of the feeding animals. Far below, beyond the last visible ledges, a warm wind moves dust through the lower country. Up here a loose washer rolls a short distance across a roof, rests against a seam, and is still.
