The district occupies a shallow hollow, perhaps two hundred paces across. From its rim you can see almost all of it: a pumping house, six narrow buildings, a paved square, and a canal that enters through one wall and leaves through another. Beyond the walls lies pale gravel, level enough that distance does not make much difference to the view. The sky is the color of unpolished tin. There is enough light to see a screw lying in the road.

The first impression is of space. The buildings stand well apart. Nothing has been erected in the middle of the square, and the streets are much wider than they need to be. Between the pumping house and the nearest row of doors, forty yards of empty paving slope gently toward a drain. A few iron bollards interrupt it. Each casts a short, soft shadow.

Then your eyes adjust to the surfaces.

What looked like a dark seam beneath a doorstep is a hundred folded legs. The roughness on the canal wall consists of small, overlapping backs. The bollards have animals packed around their bases, each animal turned inward, gripping the metal with a mouth too small to distinguish. There are creatures in the drain, creatures between the paving stones, creatures beneath the narrow lips of every windowsill. Almost all the available shelter is occupied.

The open ground remains empty. They respect exposure with a precision that makes the square look swept.

There is no grass, no weed emerging from a crack, no stain that resolves into moss. The stone is bare even where it stays wet. The dampness has other colors: clear beads along a pipe, white mineral crust under a valve, a broad gray patch where condensation soaks into a wall. In sheltered places the water smells faintly of iron and cold teeth.

The canal is only a yard wide. Its banks are masonry worn into long, shallow curves, with the joints nearly erased. Water passes through it at an unvarying speed. A lost button could travel from one end of the district to the other in four minutes, if anything were ever lost here.

At the inlet, the surface is smooth. Beneath the first footbridge it becomes granular. Thousands of tiny animals hold themselves just below the water, showing only their blunt heads. Their bodies trail downstream in pale threads. They neither advance nor fall behind. The current pulls; their mouths grip the submerged stone; the two forces remain equal.

The bridge trembles underfoot.

It is not the water that makes it tremble.

The pumping house has a low roof and four immense doors, all closed. Its brickwork has been rounded by weather until the building resembles something taken from a river and carefully set upright. Iron straps hold the corners. Pipes pass through the walls at knee height, shoulder height, above the lintels. Some are thick enough for a person to crawl through. Others are hardly wider than a finger.

Every pipe is working.

You can hear pressure moving through them, a hard, crowded hiss. Inside the building, a flywheel turns with a knocking sound so large that it seems to belong to a much bigger district. Smaller noises fit into the intervals: valve clicks, a belt slapping its housing, the sustained whine of a bearing. The square is loud enough that speech becomes an effort.

But the machinery supplies only the lower part of the sound.

From the eaves comes a grinding chorus. Flat animals cling upside down beneath the roof, their bellies facing the brick. Along both sides of each body are rows of little white plates. They rub these together continuously. In the gutters, heavier creatures answer with a clatter like crockery being sorted. Something inside each bollard whistles. The animals packed around its base tighten and loosen their mouths, and the pitch rises through them.

At first you try to separate the calls. Soon the whole district is audible as one structure: deep impacts beneath the floor, shrilling overhead, a dry rasp from the walls, water speaking through densely occupied channels.

No call goes unanswered. No answer seems urgent.

The six buildings have windows, stairways, loading doors, and the remains of signs whose lettering has softened beyond reading. Their lower rooms are visible through wired glass. There are workbenches inside, racks of cylindrical parts, presses with polished beds. No people stand at the machines. Broad-backed animals occupy the stations instead.

One lies under a press with its head fitted into a circular socket. As the ram descends, a ridge along its spine compresses. A bead of dark fluid appears at its mouth and falls into a metal cup. The ram rises; the ridge returns to its previous height. Beside it, another animal turns a shaft by walking inside a cage wheel. Its six feet strike the bars in an exact sequence. Its skin is loose, gray, and entirely without hair.

The products travel along narrow belts into the pumping house. Some are cups of fluid. Some are little porous blocks. Some look like eggs until you notice the threaded holes.

There is no warehouse and no loading road out of the hollow. Everything produced here is used here.

Behind the buildings, the passages narrow. This is where the abundance becomes difficult to bear. A handrail is warm with crowded bodies, though the air remains cool. An open inspection hatch reveals a living mass extending down into darkness. Legs move through legs. Small transparent creatures pass between larger ones, carrying bright particles in their stomachs. The larger animals do not make room; there is already exactly enough room.

You can feel their collective weight through the soles of your shoes.

They consume the residue left by the machines. The machines process what the animals excrete. Water carries heat out of the pumping house and returns beneath the paving. In the return channels, pale larvae sift the mineral suspension into their jointed shells. When a shell becomes too heavy, it separates into plates. The plates move along the bottom until a screened intake catches them.

From there, apparently, they enter the presses.

The place has been doing this for a very long time. The evidence is in the wear: stair treads hollowed in the middle, pipe brackets bedded deep into brick, the canal’s rounded corners. Yet there is remarkably little damage. A fallen plate is taken up by something. A leaking joint supports a ring of thirsty mouths. The district has learned to incorporate the small failures of its own operation.

Nothing seems to be waiting for a different condition.

The air stays cool. The canal does not rise. Moisture gathers on the same lengths of pipe and falls onto the same dark circles in the paving. The flywheel turns at the same speed. Even the animals’ movements have the settled quality of water passing over a permanent obstruction: immense activity, no alteration of the view.

At the edge of the square stands a single public bench. Its seat is dry. Its legs disappear into clusters of small, ivory-colored bodies.

You can sit there, if you can endure the noise.

From that position, the district looks almost deserted again. Wide paving. Shut doors. Low roofs under an ordinary sky. A little water moving between worn banks. Only when you lean back does the bench transmit the life beneath you—a fine, continuous vibration, too regular to be shivering, too strong to ignore.
