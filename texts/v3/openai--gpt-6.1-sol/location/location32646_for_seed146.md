The first thing you notice is the warmth coming through your soles.

The air is warm too, but not close or stifling. It has the dry, almost weightless feel of air that has never passed over water. There is no smell of damp stone, no coolness waiting in a crevice. Set a metal object down and it slowly takes on the temperature of the floor. Sleep against a wall and the side of your body touching it will be warmer than the side facing the dark.

Above you is rock. How far above, no one agrees.

The inhabited passages lie beneath a continent, perhaps beneath several. They are not tunnels in the ordinary sense. The underground has opened into enormous, low-roofed expanses divided by sheets of black mineral: some upright, some leaning, some folded back on themselves like the pages of a book forced shut. Between them are narrow ways that suddenly widen into spaces large enough to contain their own horizons. A traveler can walk for days with a wall on the left and never find its end.

The roof is usually invisible. You know it is there because of what hangs from it.

Not stalactites. Nothing here has been deposited by dripping water. These are blunt ribs of stone, thin blades, branching crystal spars, formations that grew directly out of the surrounding rock when this underworld first opened. Many still have edges sharp enough to shave a strip from leather. Broken surfaces show no rounding. A chip lying under a ledge looks as fresh as the scar from which it fell.

The place formed young and then, somehow, ceased to age.

There is dust, but very little of it is stone worn down. Most is shed by animals.

In lamplight, the floor resolves into several kinds of material. Black glass lies in great level rafts, interrupted by seams of pale salt. Elsewhere, the ground is covered in closely fitted mineral tiles, each naturally six-sided but seldom regular, with raised edges that catch the foot. In the broad depressions there are beds of dull red grains, heavier than sand. Walking across them leaves crisp tracks. A track can remain recognizable for generations unless something walks over it.

There are no rivers, no buried pools. The pale veins that suggest old watercourses are solid from bank to bank. They bend, divide, and gather into basins, but what once moved through them—if anything ever did—was not water. Their surfaces have the fine wrinkles of cooling wax.

Occasionally, one of these veins gives off a dim green light.

It is not enough to illuminate a room. It is enough to show that the darkness continues beyond it.

Life is everywhere, though at first you may take most of it for terrain.

The low mounds along the walls are animals with layered backs. They rest against the warm rock so closely that the seam between body and stone is hard to find. Their shells are made of overlapping gray plates, thin at the edges and thick in the middle. Beneath each mound, dozens of blunt feet hold position. They feed through a soft surface pressed against the wall, drawing some minute nourishment from the mineral seam beneath them.

A colony may extend for miles. Its members do not compete by pushing or fighting. They fit themselves around one another until the wall is occupied almost completely: large bodies beneath, small ones tucked into the junctions, young ones filling the last narrow spaces.

The plates rise and settle as they breathe. You can feel this if you put your palm against one. You cannot hear it.

There are long-bodied creatures in the floor seams, dark and smooth, with joints arranged down only one side. They move by easing the entire length of themselves forward, then drawing the rear half into place. Their eyes, if they have eyes, do not respond to light. They turn toward a footstep while the foot is still in the air, sensing the slight change in pressure before it lands.

Other animals hang overhead on cords finer than hair. Their bodies are flattened discs, pale underneath, black above. A passing lamp reveals a few, then dozens, then a ceiling crowded with them at different heights. They remain absolutely still until a warm body passes below. Then they tilt—not falling, not striking, simply adjusting their angle to take some benefit from the warmth.

Travelers learn not to carry lamps above shoulder height. Heat can gather the hanging animals into a slow, silent curtain.

Nothing sings here. There are no wings beating through the air, no insects rattling, no distant calls answering one another. Most of the creatures communicate through contact, warmth, or vibrations too low to become sound.

What little sound exists is unusually distinct.

A buckle taps a stone edge. A boot grinds one red grain beneath its heel. Somewhere, a shell separates from the wall with a noise like a fingernail lifted from a tabletop.

In the narrower passages, such sounds die quickly. In the great chambers, they can return much later, softened and stripped of direction. People pause after dropping a tool. They wait for the last echo before moving again.

The larger spaces have air currents. These are steady, not gusting: a broad warm flow beneath the roof and a cooler return close to the floor. They have occupied the same courses for as long as anyone remembers. Settlements are placed where the lower current passes through a gap, taking away the heat of bodies and lamps. In the still chambers, even a small group can make the air uncomfortable.

There is weather of a kind. Fine scales shed by the wall animals lift into the upper current and travel enormous distances. Where the roof descends, they settle in soft drifts, gray on gray. Sometimes a chamber has a permanent fall of them, so sparse that a person must hold out a dark sleeve for several minutes to prove anything is falling at all.

These drifts are the richest feeding grounds. Small, jointed mouths live beneath their surface. Larger creatures come to lie at their margins, letting the fine material collect in channels along their backs. Their shells support still smaller animals. The inhabitants seldom agree on where one creature ends and another begins.

Human structures are scarce.

You find them where a passage narrows or where three mineral veins meet: a low enclosure of fitted slabs, a sheltered lamp shelf, a sleeping platform raised just enough to keep the seam-dwellers from passing over a sleeper’s face. Nothing has a peaked roof. The ceiling of the world makes such a thing unnecessary.

Walls are built without mortar. Thin black stone is split with wedges, tested by tapping, then set edge to edge. The best builders can make a room that admits the floor current while keeping out the falling scales. They leave small gaps near the base for animals whose feeding keeps the slabs clean.

Tools are few and carefully maintained: bone scrapers, stone wedges, heavy needles made from crystal splinters, small knives hammered from a soft native metal. Lamps burn a dark wax gathered from the undersides of certain shell animals. The flame is short and reddish. It reveals hands, faces, and perhaps the next few steps, while leaving the chamber around them almost intact.

A dwelling seen from a distance is therefore not a building. It is a thumb-sized ember, apparently suspended in a darkness without floor or walls.

There are older marks between settlements. Shallow cups cut into ledges. Rows of notches at knee height. Small piles of selected stones placed beside otherwise indistinguishable turns. Some mark distances. Some mark feeding colonies that must not be disturbed. Others warn of glass floors too thin to bear weight.

Below such floors are further chambers.

A dropped pebble may fall for a long time before making a sound. Sometimes it makes none.

The stillness is not an absence of activity. Animals feed. Children grow. Lamps are replenished, tools passed from hand to hand. But almost everything happens within a narrow range that the place seems able to accommodate indefinitely. There are no seasons to prepare for, no floods, no sudden cold. The air takes its appointed route. The mineral seams remain warm. Colonies renew themselves without changing their outlines.

A traveler returning after forty years can recognize a wall by the arrangement of its living mounds.

In some places, a familiar animal has been replaced by its descendants, occupying precisely the same hollow.

Far from the settlements, the dark becomes so complete that the body begins inventing distances. A wall within arm’s reach feels miles away. An open chamber feels like a low ceiling pressing on the forehead. You learn to extend a staff before you, to trust the pressure of the floor against your feet, to count the gentle changes in the air.

Then a green vein appears ahead.

Beside it, hundreds of pale animals are arranged along a fracture, each with its mouth touching the stone. Their bodies are almost transparent. Within them are branching black structures, remarkably like the mineral pattern on which they feed.

They have gathered there without noise. They give off no light of their own. The vein shines through them just enough to reveal their outlines.

Beyond them, the fracture runs onward into the dark, and the warm floor continues.
