The first impression is of a valley on a bright, unsettled afternoon. Only after a while do you notice that the pale expanse above it is stone.

The roof is high enough for weather to gather beneath it. It rises beyond the haze on both sides, crossed by seams that shine white and yellow. Some seams are narrow as threads; others spread into broad, luminous patches, giving the valley the uneven brightness of sunlight through cloud. Nothing casts a steady shadow. A dark shape beside your foot may stretch, divide, and disappear as a fresh fracture opens overhead.

The air is warm. Moisture gathers under the chin, inside the elbows, against any cool piece of metal. It smells of wet chalk, hot clay, and animals.

There is no grass.

For several miles, the floor consists of pale shelves, low ridges, shallow basins, and a crowded assortment of things still becoming rock. Cream-colored mineral paste wells from cracks and sets in folds. Blunt columns rise where warm water presses upward through its own accumulating deposits. Along the edges of the valley, new crust hangs in sheets like cloth laid over furniture, except that the furniture is moving. A mound may swell during the morning, split open before midday, and pour out enough slurry to bury the hollow below it.

The land has very few worn surfaces. Its edges are young and brittle. Footprints remain sharply stamped until something grows over them.

Across this unfinished ground move the animals.

At a distance, they seem to be the texture of the valley itself: gray patches traveling across white slopes, dark ripples circling pools, a fine brown trembling along every ledge. Closer, the patches resolve into bodies. There are broad-backed grazers with six short legs and mouths like masonry scrapers. They work across newly hardened crust, shaving off the outermost layer while it is still rich with salts and the pale, soft organisms that flourish in it. Their passage leaves parallel grooves, damp and faintly pink.

Smaller animals follow directly behind them, picking material from those grooves. Smaller ones again cling to their ankles.

Nothing here grows leaves or lifts a stem toward the light. Life stays close to its food. It makes films inside porous stone, rubbery bladders in warm puddles, thin living skins under mineral flakes. Much of it is translucent. A stone turned over may reveal a crowded underside, all the apparent grit unfolding legs.

The valley is not uniformly wet. Between the channels lie broad expanses of dry, powdery crust, warm underfoot. Water comes in pulses. A vent opens somewhere beneath the basin, and for an hour or a day a stream runs where there was none. It is usually shallow, quick, and cloudy enough to hide the bottom. Along its banks, animals assemble almost before the first water arrives.

They hear it through the ground.

A quiet slope suddenly fills with activity: scraping grazers turn downhill, jointed creatures emerge from fissures, and long pale hunters begin testing the air with the wet ends of their tails. Then the stone gives a hollow knock. The new spring bursts out sideways, carrying fragments of its own sealed mouth.

Every animal seems to know what to do, but they do not all agree on the same direction.

There are stampedes that barely travel a hundred paces before becoming feeding grounds. A predator runs into a mass of shelled animals and has to climb over them. Burrowers surface beneath sleeping herds. Winged creatures, too heavy for graceful flight, launch from the high ledges and skim the fresh streams, striking one another in the air. Their wings make a dry rattling sound, like handfuls of thin plates shaken together.

Underneath that comes the low grinding of thousands of feeding mouths.

The valley is loud without being continuously deafening. Sound arrives in layers. Nearby, claws tick on crust and wet bodies slip against one another. Farther away, herds give out hoarse contact calls. From the ceiling comes a slow succession of cracks, some so deep they are felt in the ribs before they are heard. Occasionally a whole district falls silent for a few seconds. Then a mineral shelf breaks, or a steam pocket blows open, and the noise resumes around the disturbance.

The steam is rarely scalding. It rolls low across the ground, warm and dense, hiding the legs of animals while their backs remain visible above it. Droplets collect on shell ridges and run down into channels worn by years of growth. The oldest grazers carry enough mineral deposit to resemble moving outcrops. Young ones are almost smooth, the color of damp leather.

There are people here, though their works are easy to mistake for the valley’s other accumulations.

Their houses stand on three or four thick stone legs, with gaps beneath them for floods and migrating animals. The walls are made from slabs cut before the crust fully hardens. Roofs overlap like shell plates and can be lifted away when a vent changes direction. No settlement is very large. A dozen houses on one stable shelf; five more across a dry channel; a single watch shelter on a ridge that has not yet begun to swell.

Between them lie cairns, drying racks, abandoned platforms, and stone steps that now end above or below the paths they once served.

The people use bone wedges, flint blades, hide ropes, and heavy wooden-looking handles made from bundled animal spines. They harvest thin sheets of fresh stone, scrape food from the mineral films, and trap the smaller grazers in pits with polished sides. Water is carried in stitched skins and cooled in shaded hollows. Good drinking pools are marked with circles of dark pebbles. Those marks have to be renewed constantly: a pool can turn bitter overnight, or disappear beneath a flawless white lid.

They do not build across active channels, however narrow. They do not sleep against a warm wall. They leave wide lanes through every settlement, because a herd that has sensed a new spring will not stop for a house.

Above one village hangs a large bronze bell, green at its lower rim. It is among the few metal objects in the valley. The bell is rung when the floor begins to lift.

From a high shelf, you can see how much is happening at once.

On the far side, a fresh ridge is pushing up through a feeding ground. Animals collect along its crest as it rises, eating the soft deposits before they stiffen. Below it, a white flood has entered an old basin and is driving a black tide of small bodies toward the banks. Nearer by, people dismantle a house and carry its roof panels uphill, pausing to let a procession of broad-backed grazers pass between them. Overhead, a bright seam widens with a faint tearing sound.

For several breaths, the whole valley becomes clearer. Colors emerge from what seemed almost monochrome: rust-red joints, blue-gray shells, the amber of shallow water, yellow powder on the mouths of vents. Far across the basin, little airborne animals flash silver as they turn.

Then dust rises from a collapsing shelf and softens the distance again.

The landscape never quite settles into a finished arrangement. There is too much pressure below it, too much appetite upon it. Water lays down stone; bodies open paths through it; the next pulse fills those paths. A channel formed yesterday may already have banks crowded with nests. A ledge that looks ancient may still be warm from its making.

Toward the valley’s center is a plain broad enough that its opposite edge disappears into the humid air. It is almost level, almost dry, and covered with animals so closely gathered that from above they resemble cobbles. Beneath them, the ground is slowly rising.

You can watch the motion by choosing one exposed white point and keeping your eyes on it. After a while it stands higher than the shells around it. A hairline fracture reaches outward. The nearest animals shift their feet.

A little water beads in the crack.

Before it has formed a stream, there are already mouths waiting.
