At first there seems to be no ground.

There is wind, cold enough to hurt the teeth, and a darkness in which distances cannot be judged. Something pale crosses far below, then disappears. You wait for the sound of it landing. None comes.

Under your feet, however, the surface is firm. It has a grain. Kneeling, you can feel long fibers through your gloves, all running toward the same edge. The edge is only a few paces away: a clean green lip, rimmed with frost, beyond which the wind rises out of a depth you cannot see.

You are standing on a leaf.

Not a fallen leaf, and not quite a leaf of any familiar kind. Its upper skin is thick enough to take a stone axe without yielding more than a shallow dent. Its ribs are separated by valleys wide enough to shelter villages. Along the margin, younger tissue curls inward, translucent when a lamp is held behind it. The whole surface slopes gently toward its stalk, somewhere beyond several days’ walking.

There are other leaves. On clear nights they show as darker obstructions against the stars: shelves, folded planes, narrow blades standing almost upright. Some are above you. Most are below. Between them lie enormous volumes of empty air.

This is how the country is arranged. Almost every surface is alive, and almost all the space is vacant.

***

The plant rises out of a lower atmosphere that nobody here has reached.

From the highest ridges, when the darkness thins enough, you can make out a few of its supporting stems. They are broad, hollow columns, separated by distances that produce different weather. Their sides bear the scars of shed growth: long oval wounds sealed with hard resin, socket-like depressions, pale seams where new branches have forced their way out. They vanish downward into cloud and upward among the leaf platforms.

Whether it is one organism or many grafted together is disputed in the settlements. It changes little about daily life. A cut made near one settlement may bleed sap at another, but only after a delay, and not reliably enough to settle an argument.

The inhabited levels are high and cold. The sun, when it reaches them, is usually hidden behind the upper crown. You may see a bright slit slide across a distant stem while your own ground remains in darkness. For a few minutes every needle of frost on that stem shines separately. Then a leaf turns, very slowly, and the slit closes.

Growth continues in the shade. Much of it occurs underneath, where broad surfaces catch the light rising from the cloud deck. People crossing a valley sometimes notice that the path is warm beneath their boots, though the air is freezing. Elsewhere it is bitterly cold right down to the root hairs. The living ground distributes heat unevenly, abandoning an old shelf while feeding a new one.

The land is young because it is still unfolding.

A ridge that looks permanent may be the stiffened pleat of a leaf not yet fully open. A wall may split along a fresh seam. Beyond the oldest inhabited country, pale blades emerge with their margins still pressed together. They separate over weeks, pushing outward into empty air, and the first frost reaches them before any animal does.

There is little soil. There has not been time for it to accumulate. What looks like earth in the sheltered hollows is mostly powdered bark, frass, and the dry husks of small plants that live on the larger one. Sharp ribs divide these hollows. Sap still glistens in cracks that no rain has rounded.

***

Water arrives briefly and leaves traces.

Clouds pass through the lower galleries, wetting everything they touch. Higher up, moisture beads on the undersides of leaves and freezes there. The icicles point down into the void, thousands of them along a single rim. When the wind changes, they strike one another with a light, busy ringing.

A warm current can loosen a whole fringe at once.

You hear the first few pieces fall, then a gathering crash below, where the ice hits another leaf. Fragments scatter over its surface. Some slide to the next edge. Long afterward, from much farther down, another impact comes back through the cold.

Pools collect where veins intersect. Most are shallow, dark, and sealed beneath thin ice. Their margins are crowded with low black mosses and tightly furled fronds. For a few hours after a thaw, insects emerge there in such numbers that the pool appears to steam.

The water is often bitter. People learn which hollows receive condensation and which leak from the living tissue. A drinking pool has a little frame of split cane around it, more warning than fence. The ice is lifted off whole if possible, so it can be laid back at night.

Rain is rarer. When it comes, it may miss an entire district while flooding another. On a broad leaf, the drops sound like stones thrown onto taut hide. Streams form quickly in the grooves between ribs, racing toward the central vein. Footpaths become channels. Animals climb to the ridges. At the stalk, the runoff pours into a throat of overlapping tissue and disappears inside the plant.

By the following evening, a traveler may find only frost and a few stranded fish.

***

The emptiness is loud.

Wind moves freely through the great spaces, but it catches on every edge. A curled margin whistles. A torn sheet booms. Hollow stems carry tones so low they are felt through the soles before they are heard. The sound changes as the plant grows: a familiar passage acquires a new note, or falls silent after a split opens elsewhere.

There are days when conversation is possible only in the lee of a rib. Across an exposed shelf, people signal with their hands and keep their mouths covered.

Then the wind drops, and other sounds emerge.

Claws tick on frozen skin. Something chews within a stalk. From beneath the platform comes the rasping call of a large animal moving upside down along the veins. A distant flock passes through the dark, crying continuously to keep together.

The animals are abundant, though they occupy very little of the available space.

Pale grazers travel in bands along the warmest ridges. They are low-bodied, broad-footed creatures with coarse coats and hard pads beneath their claws. They scrape young tissue from the ground, leaving white crescents that close slowly behind them. In a feeding hollow there may be hundreds. A mile away, nothing moves.

Long-winged hunters circle the columns. They launch from bark ledges and let the rising air carry them until a flock, a grazer, or a climbing animal crosses beneath. Their shadows are seldom visible. People recognize them by a sudden change in the smaller birds’ calls.

There are climbing things with tails longer than their bodies, and blind burrowers that follow the warmth inside living wood. Moths shelter in frost cracks. Beetles bore chambers in dead margins. On the underside of a leaf, where no person can walk, entire colonies hang from the ribs: nests, sleeping bodies, dangling young.

Occasionally one of the grazers falls.

The noise of the herd stops. Heads turn toward the edge. Far below, a hunter leaves its perch.

***

The settlements are small enough to overlook.

They sit in the sheltered angle between a main rib and a branching vein: a handful of low shelters made from shed bark, sewn together with fiber cord. No one builds near a margin, however convenient the water might be. No one cuts deeply into a supporting rib.

Lamps burn resin in stone cups. Their light is yellow and smoky, barely enough to find a doorway. A traveler can pass within shouting distance of a settlement and never see it, particularly when the wind is high.

There are gardens, but they look unlike clearings. People encourage edible growth into mats, peg young shoots into reachable shapes, and scrape away competing moss. Hooks and blades are made from horn, chipped stone, or the hardened tips of old thorns. A few households possess soft metal tools traded from lower levels. These are used carefully and never left where the ground might grow over them.

Paths are marked with knots of pale fiber. The marks have to be renewed. Bark thickens, ribs twist, and a post that stood upright in spring may lean over a new crease by winter.

Bridges are rarer than paths. Most are short rope crossings over splits in a single leaf. The great intervals between platforms are crossed only where growth has brought them close: at an overlapping edge, a branch junction, a hanging tangle of roots. Routes open and close without regard for the villages that depend on them.

There are abandoned shelters on inaccessible shelves. Sometimes you can see their lamp niches from the opposite side of a gap. Nothing appears wrong with the place. The gardens are still green. The gap has simply widened.

***

Late in the long night, the temperature falls sharply.

Frost creeps out of the grooves and feathers across the walking surface. The grazing bands retreat toward the stalks. Moths fold themselves deeper into cracks. In the settlements, people take down anything hanging outside; a bucket or a loose panel can keep a household awake all night in the rising wind.

Far away, a new leaf is opening.

At first the sound resembles ice breaking. Then it becomes a sustained tearing, interrupted by heavy reports as the folded tissue releases. Birds lift from platforms on either side. The air shifts. For several breaths, the wind blows toward the sound rather than away from it.

A pale surface advances into a gap that was empty yesterday.

It is too distant to watch closely, but by morning it has hidden a familiar star. Frost is already forming on its edge. Below it, animals begin altering their routes. One flock finds a new sheltered current; another turns back. Somewhere beneath the settlements, water that once dripped freely now strikes the growing blade.

The people will wait before going out to inspect it. New tissue can carry weight, or tear under it, and there is no reliable way to tell from a distance.

For now they listen from their bark houses as the leaf unfolds. The ground beneath them trembles faintly. In the dark beyond their lamps, a fresh ridge rises, a passage narrows, and the wind begins learning another way through.
