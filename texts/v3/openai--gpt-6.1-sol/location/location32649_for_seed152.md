The place lies between two buttress roots, in a hollow scarcely larger than a pair of cupped hands. From above, it is almost invisible: a dark opening beneath a fern, a little loose earth against the foot of a tree. Inside, the roots rise like the walls of a courtyard. Their upper edges admit a narrow, greenish light.

It is warm here even before the sun reaches the canopy. The wood holds the heat of yesterday, and the air moves too slowly to carry it away. There is a smell of leaf skin, mushroom flesh, and fresh earth—a sweetness with something mineral underneath.

The ground is still being made.

A broad fallen leaf forms most of its foundation. Its veins remain intact beneath a thin covering of dark crumbs, though the spaces between them have softened and begun to disappear. At one end, pale fragments of wood are becoming soil. At the other, an old seed coat has split into three curved plates. None of this material has traveled far. It has dropped, settled, been eaten, and been laid down again. The slopes are new enough that each grain seems to have a reason for being where it is.

Across the center is an open patch the color of weak tea. Almost nothing grows there. A few green threads stand well apart, each casting a shadow too faint to notice until something moves through it. The lushness gathers at the edges: a tuft of moss beside the left root, two fern seedlings under the lip of bark, a small plant with glossy leaves that overlap like wet scales. Between these clusters there is room—bare ground, loose particles, low ridges, the clear interval around a stone.

Water does not stay for long. After rain, it enters by three routes: down the bark, along a root, and from the pointed tip of the fern overhead. Each route has its own landing place. The fern’s drops make a round, shallow basin. Water from the root spreads beneath the fallen leaf, where it remains hidden, darkening the foundation from below. The bark gives only a slow seep, enough to keep a narrow strip of moss tender.

Now the basin holds a bead of water about the width of a seed. Its surface is level with the soil. A tiny fly stands beside it, touching the rim with its forefeet. Farther away, where the ground looks dry, the undersides of wood fragments are still damp.

Most of the settlement is underground.

There are entrances along the base of the right-hand root: four neat holes, each surrounded by a low collar of freshly placed grains. Ants come through them carrying pale chips, dark crumbs, an occasional piece of leaf larger than their heads. They lay the loads down, turn, and return. The heaps do not simply rise. Their edges shift as workers take material from one side and place it on another. An open route is preserved between two entrances. A shallow trench is roofed with bits of bark. Beneath that roof, traffic continues out of sight.

Aboveground, the courtyard looks sparsely occupied. Below it are chambers, passages, places for young, places where food is held. The small openings are the visible part of a much busier space.

Nothing about this activity is loud. There is no rustling mass, no audible industry. Occasionally a grain falls from the side of a mound and ticks against the dry seed coat. A drop gathers overhead for several minutes before falling with a soft tap. Most movements happen inside the silence.

Along the moss, springtails appear and vanish between the green stems. A translucent mite crosses a patch of pale wood, stops, then slips into a fissure. Beneath the fern, a spider waits against the root, so closely matched to the bark that its legs become visible before its body does. Its web spans only the gap between a splinter and a curled leaf edge. One strand carries a droplet; the rest can be seen only from a particular angle.

An ant approaches the web and turns away. The spider does not move.

The light changes without ever becoming bright. A breeze high in the canopy shifts the fern, allowing a thin bar of yellow to reach the central clearing. For a moment the damp soil shows its separate particles: amber fibers, black flakes, a grain of sand with a cloudy interior. Then the fern settles and everything returns to green shadow.

Here, a slight rise in the ground can redirect the next rain. A dropped husk can shelter several animals. When a leaf curls as it dries, it opens a passage beneath itself; when it becomes wet again, that passage closes. The inhabitants keep adjusting to these changes. Ants move an entrance uphill. A springtail finds a newly moist edge. The moss extends into a patch of soil that was bare a few days before.

By afternoon the visible water is gone. The open ground lightens, while the sheltered margins remain dark. Warmth draws a faint smell from the decaying leaf below. Under one seed plate, something has eaten a new hollow. Beside an entrance, a worker sets down another grain.

The courtyard is a little higher than it was in the morning.
