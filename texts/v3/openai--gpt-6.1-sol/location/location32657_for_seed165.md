The first thing you find is shelter, though there is almost nothing to shelter behind.

A low fold of stone rises out of the plateau, scarcely higher than your shoulders. Its underside has been hollowed by a wind that no longer changes direction. You stand in the hollow and discover that the noise outside has not diminished. It has merely become more distant, as though the nearest sound were now coming from the far end of a valley.

There is no valley yet. There is gray ground, a little moisture in the seams, and the pale reach of the plateau disappearing into cloud.

Then your eyes adjust.

The ground is divided into broad shelves, each several miles across, separated by narrow trenches. Beyond them stand three ridges, their summits dark and rounded. Everything has been worn for a very long time. Loose stones have settled into sockets precisely their size. The lips of the trenches are blunt. Even the smallest fractures have softened edges, and the frost inside them is dull rather than glittering. Nothing here looks recently broken.

You are very high up. That much is apparent from the thinness of a breath and the effort required to lift a foot. But the height is difficult to locate. There are no lower slopes visible, no distant plains. The trenches go down a little way, then reveal further plateau. At the edges of the outer shelves, where you expect a precipice, you see the same country from beneath: the undersides of ridges, the bellies of stones, the dark roots of the few plants.

Cloud rests against these undersides. It does not travel.

The light comes through it unevenly, giving the landscape the illumination of a room with its curtains drawn. There is enough to distinguish textures and almost no color. A wet surface is black. A dry surface is gray. The oldest frost has a faint green cast, which at first you mistake for vegetation.

Actual vegetation is harder to find.

There are cushions of coarse, brownish moss tucked into the sheltered sides of cracks. Each is no larger than a closed hand. Their centers are wet, their edges brittle. Here and there a stem grows horizontally from a fissure, carrying three or four thick leaves pressed together like shut eyelids. These plants never reach upward. They keep to the stone, following its contours, and their roots run through hairline channels where water has been present long enough to polish the walls.

The water is scarce, but it is everywhere in small amounts. A dark line beneath an overhang. A bead held on the tip of a root. A shallow pool in a depression that would fit a foot.

Across one shelf, rain falls in a strip perhaps twenty paces wide. The drops are widely spaced and large. They strike with a hard ticking sound. You can walk around the strip, or through it, but the edges remain exact. Outside it the ground is dry; inside, a thin sheet of water lies over the rock without spreading.

Farther on, a narrow stream occupies a trench. It makes the loud, crowded noise of a much larger river. Its water is clear and only ankle-deep, running over dark stone toward a bend beneath one of the ridges. Looking closely, you notice a bubble beside a white pebble. Minutes later it is still there, trembling in the same place. The water passes through it.

There are several such things you can use to measure your stay: the bubble, a drop hanging from a stone lip, a bent grass blade that never quite touches the water. They remain where they are. Your breath comes and goes. Your hands grow cold. The country keeps its arrangements.

The sound is the largest thing in it.

Even where the surface appears dry, you hear water laboring underneath: a deep rushing, full of separate impacts, as if countless small falls were dropping into chambers below the plateau. Over this runs a continuous wind-note. It is low enough to feel in your teeth. Sometimes you can distinguish higher tones in it, thin and steady, coming from the narrow spaces between stacked rocks.

There are no gusts. Nothing rattles intermittently. A loose flake resting on a ledge vibrates against the stone with such regularity that its clicking seems to have become part of the ledge itself.

You can speak, but the place does not give you an echo. Your words are absorbed into the surrounding noise. A shout travels no farther than ordinary speech.

The scattered shelters become visible only when you are close to them. They resemble the first hollow, though some are more complete: a roof slab resting on three upright stones, a round recess under an enormous tilted block, a narrow chamber with a level threshold. None has a door. None contains a hearth, a tool, or anything that can be identified as a possession.

A few are too regular to accept easily as natural. One has five steps descending to its entrance. The steps are shallow and evenly spaced, worn smooth along their centers. Inside, the roof is higher than it looked from outside. You can stand upright, then stretch an arm above your head without reaching it.

At the rear is a ledge. On it lies a small patch of dried moss, gathered into an oval.

You leave it alone.

Other chambers are barely begun, or have nearly disappeared. A rectangular shadow on a cliff. Two uprights with no roof. A row of hollows that might once have held posts, though no wood grows anywhere in sight. Their surfaces have the same weathering as the surrounding rock. Whatever made them has been absent long enough for the distinction between shelter and outcrop to become uncertain.

Animals are present, but seldom in the open.

Under a shelf beside the stream, something pale lies curled with its feet tucked beneath its body. It is about the size of a hare. The fur along its spine is gray with mineral dust, and its ears are so short that you see them only when it turns its head. It watches you without rising. A fringe of fine whiskers around its muzzle rests against the wet stone.

You hear it drinking, though its mouth does not move.

Much later, you find another beneath a different shelf. Or the same one. There is no track between the two places, and no continuous route under cover. Both animals have a dark mark beside the left eye.

On the highest ridge stands a bird, alone. It faces into the wind with one foot lifted. Its feathers are compressed against its body; only a few pale filaments at its throat quiver. Against the dim sky it looks very small. You watch for a long time before realizing that its lifted foot casts a shadow on the ground several yards away.

The shadows here are patient, and not always attached.

A boulder may have a shadow beneath it, as expected, and another on a distant shelf. In the second shadow you can see the impression of a cleft that the boulder itself does not possess. Some trenches are darker along the upper edge than at the bottom. Inside one shelter, the light grows stronger as you go deeper, until the rear wall can be seen with a clarity unavailable outdoors.

This is how you eventually find the far ridge: by entering that shelter and looking at the rear wall.

The wall shows no image. It is simply far away. What appeared from the entrance to be a small chamber opens into several miles of stone, with the ridge at its end. You can walk into it. Behind you, the doorway stays near enough to touch.

There is no abrupt transition, no sensation of falling or crossing. Your feet continue to find firm ground. The cold remains the same.

Up close, the far ridge is covered with shallow basins. Most are empty. A few contain water, and in those the reflected sky is darker than the sky overhead. Along their rims grow the small, closed-leaf plants. Between the basins lie flat stones with thin seams of frost underneath, as though the cold were rising from the ground rather than descending through the air.

Here the noise is immense. You feel the river-sound through the soles of your feet. Yet the pools are smooth, and the plants do not shake.

You sit in one of the empty basins. It fits your body comfortably. The stone is dry and has the faint warmth of something protected from wind, although there is no protection you can see.

From this height the plateau resolves into a limited country: three ridges, perhaps a dozen broad shelves, a few dark watercourses, and the small shelters dispersed among them. Beyond it there is only pale depth. No neighboring mountain appears. No horizon gathers the land into a larger world.

You can see the strip of rain below. You can see the bird standing on the ridge opposite. Near your first shelter, the pale animal has its head lowered to the stone.

Nothing arrives while you watch. Nothing departs.

Still, the place is not waiting. Water runs, roots draw moisture, fur is kept dry beneath the ledges. The shelters hold their open entrances toward the same enduring weather. Everywhere there is enough activity to sustain what is here, and no visible remainder.

At last you rise. Your legs are stiff from the cold. As you walk back, a few damp grains adhere to your palm where you touched the basin. You rub them loose.

They fall behind you with a small sound you can hear quite clearly through the roar.
