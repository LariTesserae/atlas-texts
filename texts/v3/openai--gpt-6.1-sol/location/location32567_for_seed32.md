The place is behind a wall, but it is not the space between walls. There is no loose mortar here, no forgotten cavity opening into darkness. It is a shallow chamber made deliberately, small enough that a person could reach from its entrance to its farthest corner without stepping inside.

Almost all of it is occupied.

Pale ceramic forms fit against one another from floor to ceiling: folded plates, blunt columns, thick ribs with rounded ends. Some resemble stairways reduced to three steps; others are curved like the inside of a bowl. None is quite large enough to be architecture, though together they have the authority of buildings. Between them run narrow, crooked passages, broad enough for a fingertip in some places, too thin for a sheet of paper in others.

The floor can be seen only in fragments. It is flat, low, and made from the same cool white material as the enclosing walls. There is no earth beneath it. Where a small piece has chipped away, another fitted layer shows underneath.

Everything was placed here. Even the irregularities belong to the work.

The ceramic has weathered for a long time without becoming fragile. Its corners are softly blunted. Along the most exposed edges, the original glaze survives as a thin yellowish line, while the broad surfaces have the matte finish of a stone handled for centuries. There are scratches that begin decisively and then fade into the material, their deepest portions worn almost level. Tiny flakes lie in recesses, packed so firmly that they have become another kind of surface.

Nothing looks recent. Nothing looks on the verge of collapse.

The light is surprisingly strong.

It enters through a narrow band of translucent tile near the ceiling and spreads through the crowded chamber by reflection. Each pale surface gives it to the next. Even beneath an overhang, the shadow is a clear gray rather than black. Fine details stand out: a hairline join, a grain caught in a groove, the faint brown rim around a patch of glaze.

There is no visible lamp, no wire, no polished apparatus. The bright tile is simply part of the wall. Its light has the fixed, colorless quality of an overcast noon, though there is no sky in the chamber and no sign of a day passing elsewhere.

The air is cool and completely dry. A hand laid against the ceramic would find no trace of dampness, only a chill that persists beneath the skin. There are no water marks in the low places, no swollen seams, no dark pockets where moisture might have collected. The dust is fine and mineral, but it does not lift. It has settled into all the places available to it.

Vegetation survives in a few of those places.

Along three seams grows a short, rigid growth the color of old straw. It consists of tiny overlapping scales, pressed close to the wall, with occasional blunt tips extending into the light. Elsewhere, a dark green crust occupies the underside of a ledge. Its margin follows the edge exactly and stops at a fitted joint.

These growths are sparse, but they do not look starved. They hold their small allotments with the same permanence as the ceramic. No leaves have fallen beneath them. No shoots reach out in search of a better position. Whatever nourishment the seams provide appears sufficient.

There are no animals. No webs span the passages, no husks collect in the corners, and no small movements interrupt the bright stillness.

Yet the chamber is loud.

A deep, close sound fills it continuously. It is difficult to locate because every ceramic piece carries it. Beside a broad plate it becomes a low roar; near the narrow passages, a higher note presses through, sharp enough to be felt behind the teeth. Putting an ear near the wall does not bring the source any closer. The wall sounds much like the air.

Nothing rattles. The straw-colored growth does not tremble. A loose grain resting on a curved ledge remains where it is.

The sound seems to have settled here as thoroughly as the dust. It has no audible beginning, no pulses, no moment when one can listen for its return. After a while, attention shifts from its loudness to the differences between one recess and another. A shallow notch holds a thin whistle. A rounded hollow holds a broad, muffled note. Each opening has its own voice, and none changes.

From outside, the entrance could easily pass for a poorly finished joint in the wall. Looking through it, one first sees only whiteness and close-set shapes. Then the chamber resolves into its many small distances: the narrow route beneath a plate, the green ledge beyond it, the far corner bright behind two intersecting ribs.

There is more space here than the entrance promises, but not more room. Every surface has a neighbor. Every gap ends against something made.

The place remains cool, bright, and densely inhabited by its own materials, with its few plants fixed among them and its great, steady noise confined to a chamber barely large enough to hold a hand.
