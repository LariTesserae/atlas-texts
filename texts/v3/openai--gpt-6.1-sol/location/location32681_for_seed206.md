The place lies under a fold of vegetation at the edge of a warm marsh. From outside, there is little to distinguish it from the rest of the bank: a swelling of roots, three broad leaves laid over one another, a dark seam where water goes in. You could pass within arm’s reach and never notice that the seam opens into a hollow large enough to sit inside.

There is no earth in the hollow. What looks like ground is the exposed back of a root, worn nearly flat and covered with a short, firm nap of living fibers. The walls are roots too, joined so completely that their individual outlines have softened into one continuous surface. Above, the leaves make a low ceiling. Ordinary daylight comes through their thinner sections, greenish but quite bright, showing veins, small scars, and the silhouettes of insects walking on the other side.

Despite all this growth, the room feels empty.

A great deal of its small volume is clear air. No vines hang across it. No thicket crowds the entrance. Two pale stalks rise near the far wall, well apart, each no thicker than a finger. Between them is a shallow basin of water. The rest is a broad, bare sweep of root, comfortable to the eye in the way a clean stretch of shore is comfortable. Vegetation has become the surface of the place rather than something occupying it.

The root-floor is warm. Press a palm against it and the warmth arrives slowly, evenly, without a hot spot. Its fibers bend under pressure and return when the hand is lifted. In a few places, long use by small feet has polished the nap away, exposing a dark material like soaked wood, though it is supple and alive. These paths are narrow, rounded at their edges, and old enough to have become part of the root’s growth.

Water is always close. It shines in the wall seams and beads along the ceiling veins. The basin is only a few inches deep, with a bottom so smooth that reflected leaves seem to lie beneath the water rather than above it. At its near edge, a damp strip broadens across the floor. At the far edge, water slips into a row of small openings, each shaded by a fleshy lip.

The smell is of wet stalks split open: sweet at first, then mineral, with a faint oily bitterness. The air is humid enough that your skin does not quite dry. Yet there is no rot smell, no sour pocket in a corner. Even the few fallen leaf fragments have been reduced to translucent scraps, their tougher veins resting cleanly against the floor.

The sound is much larger than the hollow.

A continuous heavy humming comes through the roots. Under it runs a regular knocking, fast enough to merge into a rattle until you listen closely. Every few seconds there is a soft, broad release of pressure, like steam venting through cloth. The basin trembles at these releases. Tiny rings spread across it, meet the edges, and disappear.

At first the noise seems to come from somewhere beyond the marsh. Then you put a hand on the wall.

It is all happening here.

Behind the smooth inner rind, chambers contract in sequence. Water enters through one set of valves and leaves through another. Bundles of pale tissue tighten, pulling on flexible ribs; the ribs spring back; the next chamber takes the load. Along the ceiling, wet veins carry an electrical rhythm that can sometimes be felt as a faint prickling through a damp fingertip. Nothing has been installed. There are no pipes fitted into the roots, no metal hidden under the leaves. The living mass has grown its own engines.

Their work is modest and incessant. They draw marsh water, separate what can be used, send moisture toward the exposed leaves, and extrude food into the hollow.

One of the pale stalks bears a line of soft white pellets. They emerge from a slit near its top, identical in size, and descend by tiny steps as the stalk’s skin contracts beneath them. At the base, a beetle takes one away. Another beetle waits behind it, antennae resting lightly against the first beetle’s shell.

The second stalk produces a clear syrup so slowly that its tip always appears to hold the same bead. Small flies stand around it without touching one another, their feet braced on a ring of hairs. As they feed, the bead is replenished from within.

There are animals everywhere once your eyes learn their size.

Thin shrimp graze the basin floor. A translucent frog rests in a wall recess, its throat pulsing in time with neither the pumps nor the knocks. Mites travel the ceiling veins in widely spaced files. Under the polished margins of the paths, pale worms extend their heads into the damp, retreat, and extend them again. Occasionally a marsh insect enters through the seam, folds its wings, and stays.

The larger creatures remain few and well separated. Each has a recess or a stretch of floor. Even the frogs do not gather together. Much of the abundance is tucked beneath surfaces, visible only in a moving shadow or the brief appearance of a jointed leg. The open space remains open.

The hollow has been this shape for a very long time. Water has rounded every projecting lip. The entrance is polished where bodies brush past it. Old injuries in the walls have healed into shallow depressions, and the root’s later growth has preserved those depressions instead of filling them. No edge looks newly broken. Nothing seems about to collapse or push through.

Its activity does not lead to an outcome. The stalks do not become taller. The basin does not deepen. Feeding, pumping, hatching, grazing: all continue, while the place keeps its proportions. Whatever the animals remove, the living walls replace. Whatever water enters, the same amount leaves. There is little waste and nowhere for it to accumulate.

Outside, leaves may turn in the wind, and rain may strike the marsh hard enough to flatten it. Inside, those disturbances arrive as slight changes in the daylight or a brief extra tremor in the water. The hum holds. The warm floor holds. A beetle finishes carrying its pellet across the open root, and another begins.

You can stay until the loudness becomes familiar enough to hear its separate parts: the wet click of a valve, the scrape of feet, the low working note of the wall beside your shoulder. The hollow offers no view beyond its entrance. Just the thin brightness of marsh water there, and, closer, this small clear space maintained within an immense living body.
