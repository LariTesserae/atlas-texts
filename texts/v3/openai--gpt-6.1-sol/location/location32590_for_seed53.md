The hollow is just below the summit, though from inside it there is no summit to see. A wall of pale stone rises on three sides, its upper edges so brightly lit that they seem thinner than the rock beneath them. On the fourth side, the ground falls away through a narrow notch. Beyond it lies blue distance, almost without detail. Clouds occasionally move across that opening from below.

The whole place could be crossed in a few minutes, if crossing were simple. It is crowded with upright slabs, fallen ribs of stone, shallow basins, and heaps of thin fragments that shift underfoot. The slabs stand close enough to make passages between them. Some lean against their neighbors. Others have settled into sockets worn by their own slow movement, and their lower edges disappear beneath gravel as fine and white as crushed teeth.

Everything has been here a long time. Corners are rounded, but not softened. The stone retains a severe, worked appearance, as if weathering has been finishing it rather than wearing it away. Vertical grooves descend the walls. Where several grooves meet, they have made narrow flutes with polished bottoms; elsewhere the surface peels in hand-sized scales. A few fallen plates are so thin that daylight passes through their edges.

There is no soil. In the cracks lies powdered rock, sometimes packed hard, sometimes loose enough to take an impression. Nothing roots there. No green stains occupy the wet places, and no insects collect beside the water. The hollow’s smallest details are grains, crystals, bubbles caught in ice.

The sunlight is abundant and gives little warmth.

By midmorning it reaches over the eastern wall and spreads among the slabs. Every white face becomes brilliant. Shadows remain blue and exact, with cold pooled at their bases. A hand placed in the sun feels the warmth on its back while the fingertips, touching stone, begin to ache. The air is thin enough that a short climb leaves a person breathing carefully. There is a faint metallic dryness in the mouth, even beside water.

Most of the moisture arrives invisibly. Air rising from the lower valleys passes through the notch and cools against the rock. A sheen develops on the shaded faces: first a darkening, then beads, then tiny threads that join and descend. For an hour or two the hollow seems to sweat. Afterward the air changes, and the exposed surfaces dry almost at once.

The water persists in sheltered places. Beneath an overhanging slab is a basin no larger than a table, filled to the depth of a palm. Its floor is tiled with flat pebbles, each visible through water of extraordinary clarity. A narrow lip lets the excess escape into a crack. That trickle travels out of sight, reappears farther down between two stones, and vanishes again. There is no stream broad enough to follow with the eye. Its course is learned from dark seams and the occasional shine at the bottom of a fissure.

At night the basin freezes from the edges inward. In daylight the ice lifts slightly from the rock as water forms beneath it. Sometimes a bubble moves under the clear sheet, slowly enlarging as it travels. When the sheet finally breaks, it makes a small, surprisingly musical sound.

Sound rarely carries far within the hollow. The close-set slabs interrupt it. A boot scrape is crisp nearby and absent a few passages away. A dropped pebble may click once, then several times below the visible ground. Wind crossing the notch produces a low breath, but inside, the air is often still.

There are stretches when the loudest thing is water detaching from stone.

Near the basin stands a small enclosure made by hand. It has three walls of stacked flakes and a roof formed from two broad slabs, pitched toward one another. The roof is scarcely high enough to sit beneath. Its builders fitted the stones with care: narrow chips fill the larger gaps, and the inward faces are smoother than the outward ones. No mortar was used. On the floor is a shallow depression where a person could have curled up with knees drawn close.

A blackened patch survives beneath the roof. It is not large enough to suggest much fuel. Whatever burned here was carried up.

Outside lie two stone wedges, a broken wooden handle bleached almost white, and a vessel carved from soft rock. The vessel has a heavy rim and an uneven interior. It was placed beneath a reliable drip. Water has since cut a clean channel through one side, so that it now fills only halfway before overflowing.

There are other marks of use: footholds chipped into a steep step near the notch; a flattened ledge; three little stacks of stone set where the safest route crosses loose ground. These are difficult to distinguish from natural arrangements until viewed from the direction of arrival. Then they become legible. Someone climbing through the opening would see the first stack against a dark slab, then the second above it, and know where to put a hand.

No one appears to have lived here comfortably. It seems more like a stopping place, perhaps visited in a particular season, perhaps reached for the water.

Toward afternoon the hollow becomes active in small increments. Warmed surfaces shed their remaining moisture. Grains loosen and slide down the walls. Now and then a flake falls, leaving behind a fresh pale patch that will take years to match its surroundings. The upright slabs expand in the sun, almost imperceptibly, and press against one another. A tight seam may open enough to release dust. A stone that was secure in the morning rocks underfoot by evening.

In the coldest passage, the mineral crusts do something stranger. They bend toward the damp air. The movement is too slow to watch, but a branching deposit photographed at dawn would have changed its angle by noon. Thin translucent scales swell along one side, curling the whole cluster outward. When they dry, most return to their former position. A few remain displaced. Over many seasons, these clusters have grown into fragile shelves and hooked combs, all oriented toward the notch.

They look almost attentive. They are only salt and water.

Late in the day, light enters horizontally through the opening. It reaches places untouched by the overhead sun: the underside of the shelter roof, the inner walls of cracks, the cloudy centers of ice fragments. For a few minutes the hollow is bright from within as well as above. Every passage offers another pale surface beyond the last.

Then the notch goes blue. The stone begins releasing its heat into the clear sky. Damp seams stiffen; the basin acquires a skin; a little water trapped behind a flake freezes and pushes.

Long after the last direct light has left, something small breaks loose high on the wall. It strikes two ledges and comes to rest among the fragments below. The hollow settles around its new shape.
