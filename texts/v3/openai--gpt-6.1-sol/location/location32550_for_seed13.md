The city occupies a shelf of pale rock high above the tree line of the lower valleys. It is not crowded onto the shelf. There is room between its districts for whole meadows, for outcrops with no path over them, for dark patches of woodland that conceal one building and leave the next entirely exposed. From a distance, the city looks almost unfinished: roofs, terraces, gardens, and long retaining walls distributed across a broad, tilted basin. But the spaces belong to its design. Every open tract has an edge, every district a clear relation to the others.

Beyond the buildings rise mountains with unusually clean outlines. Their ridges are narrow, their faces divided into large planes. There is little scree beneath them. A cliff may end in a sharp basal corner instead of a slope of rubble, and small fractures in the stone have lips as crisp as broken pottery. The whole high country has the appearance of something recently uncovered, though nothing here is visibly in the process of becoming.

The rock is a cool gray with a slight green cast. Where it has been cut for building, it reveals thin white bands. Houses are made of broad slabs laid without mortar, their joints fitted closely enough to exclude roots. Many have only one story facing uphill and three facing down. Their roofs are low and heavy, supported by peeled timber beams, covered with overlapping stone plates. Under the eaves, wooden pegs hold baskets, bundles of fiber, simple hoes, and narrow-bladed knives with dull iron edges.

There are no wheels in the streets. Goods belong to sledges, carrying frames, shoulder poles. The streets themselves are more often stairs: shallow, wide flights interrupted by level courts where a burden can be put down. Larger routes follow the contours of the basin, linking storehouses, meeting halls, workshops, and the cistern districts. They are paved only along their centers. At either side, grass grows in a strip between stonework and wall.

The light is clear enough to distinguish all of this from the opposite ridge. It is ordinary daylight, neither golden nor blue, resting on the city from a high angle. Roofs cast short, definite shadows. In the shade, the air has the coldness of an unheated room; in the open, it is comfortable only with a wool cloak. Small remnants of snow lie in deep clefts above the highest houses, white and granular, sharply bounded against the rock.

Water is present chiefly in sheltered places.

A shallow river occupies the lowest crease of the basin. Much of its bed is exposed: pale slabs, little shelves, dark hollows holding transparent water. The wetted channel divides around islands of sedge and joins again below them. Higher up, each neighborhood has a stone-lined basin collecting seepage from the mountain. Some are scarcely larger than a trough. Others are broad enough to have steps descending into them and a low wall around their rim.

Dampness gathers beneath lintels and on the north sides of boulders. There are beads of water on the underside of a conduit, on a spider’s web strung across a drain, along the tips of broad leaves. Nothing looks soaked through. The paths are dry, the walls dry, the grasses silver-green rather than dark with rain. Moisture has exact boundaries here: a velvet patch of moss ends at a fracture; a wet stair is followed by seven dry ones.

Around these small reserves, vegetation is luxuriant. Ferns fill the gaps between cistern walls. Thick mats of tiny round leaves lie over the soil beside the channels. Gardens contain close-grown beans, cabbages with blue outer leaves, and tall herbs supported by lattices of split wood. Fruit trees have been trained flat against south-facing stone, their branches tied with braided grass. The wild trees are low and broad, with trunks bent close to the ground and dense crowns of leathery leaves.

The richness is local rather than continuous. Between two green courts may lie a hundred paces of bare pavement and exposed rock. Between two wooded hollows, a dry meadow spreads almost empty, its grass short enough to reveal every stone. There is always somewhere for the eye to rest.

Animals occupy nearly every kind of place, though they rarely gather in numbers. A pair of long-eared mountain goats stands on a roof reached from the slope behind it. Small tawny deer are visible among the orchard trunks. Along the river, dark birds with pale throats perch on stones at intervals, each with its own stretch of shallow water. Beetles sit on the warm faces of walls. In the cisterns, slender fish hang above the bottom, their fins open. There are moths beneath the eaves, lizards in the joints of the stairs, and a fox in a narrow passage beside a granary, its brush extended almost level behind it.

All of them are alive.

None of them moves.

This becomes apparent in the smallest details before it is apparent in the whole place. A bird above the river has its wings partly folded, its feet a finger’s breadth from a stone. The water beneath it has a depression where a drop has struck, but the rim of the depression does not flatten. At one cistern, a thread of overflow projects from the lip and breaks into six distinct beads before reaching the basin. The beads remain where they are.

Grass is bent in places as if under a light wind. A wool hanging curves away from a balcony rail. Smoke extends from a chimney in a loose gray shape, thin enough to show the roof behind it. Neither wind nor smoke advances. The sunlight has no perceptible journey across the paving.

The people are as undisturbed as the animals. A woman sits on a low stool beside a loom, one hand between the threads. Two children occupy opposite ends of a narrow bridge. A porter leans into the straps of a loaded carrying frame. Their faces show attention, fatigue, mild amusement: the small expressions of an ordinary day. There is no common direction to their gaze, no sign that they witnessed anything together.

The city has no evident boundary beyond which this condition ceases. The distant valleys are equally clear. A pale cloud occupies the notch between two summits with an edge as definite as the ridges themselves.

It is not entirely silent. In the covered channel beneath the main stair, there is a low, sustained watery sound. Near the largest hall, a thin wooden resonance hangs in the air, like the quiet note left by a struck bowl. Farther away is a faint vibration whose source is difficult to place. These sounds neither swell nor fade. They give the broad spaces a little depth without filling them.

Even the exposed tools are free of rust. A cut branch beside a garden gate has moist pale wood at its center. The mountain’s sharp edges, the woven cord on a water carrier’s shoulder, the soft growth on a shaded wall—all retain their own condition without wearing into one another.

From the upper terraces, the arrangement of the city is especially plain. The roads make a loose network around the water, and the buildings turn their open sides toward gardens or views. Stone benches stand where a person carrying a load would need to rest. Low walls shelter the orchards without hiding the mountains. In one open court, there is a large table with places set well apart: wooden bowls, a jug, a folded cloth beside each bowl.

The jug is tilted over one of them. A clear curve of water joins its mouth to the cup below, bright along one edge, transparent enough to show the grain of the wood behind it.
