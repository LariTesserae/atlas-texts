The country begins above the weather.

Far below, clouds move through the ordinary mountain valleys, catching rain against the slopes and carrying it away toward places where rivers can remain rivers. Up here, water arrives mostly as a trace: a pale deposit on stone, a wet seam beneath a root, beads that gather on the underside of leaves while their upper surfaces are crusted with frost. The air is too thin to hold much moisture, and too cold to relinquish it easily.

There is no summit. After the last steep ascent, the mountains open into a broad, elevated country of shallow basins, rounded ridges, and long shelves overlooking depths that cannot be seen. The land continues for days in every direction. Its few prominent rocks stand far apart, each surrounded by enough empty space to seem alone.

Almost everything low to the ground is alive.

Vegetation covers the basin floors in thick, intricate layers: black-green cushions, overlapping scales, soft fronds with pale undersides, succulent stems no taller than a thumb. What looks from a distance like bare, dark soil resolves underfoot into a forest lying flat. There are tiny clearings inside it, tunnels roofed with leaves, sheltered chambers where the temperature stays just above freezing. Some plants spread broad, ribbed fans directly over the stone. Others grow beneath those fans, feeding their roots into the damp collected there.

The richness is horizontal. Nothing rises without paying for it.

A few plants attempt height along the sheltered sides of ridges. Their trunks creep over the ground for twenty or thirty paces before turning upward into a short tuft of needles. Old ones have several such tufts, widely separated, connected by wood buried in the living carpet. A whole grove may be one tree.

The darkness is not complete, but it is persistent. Somewhere beyond the enclosing heights, sunlight reaches the world. Here there is a faint silver dilution along one edge of the sky, enough to reveal the outlines of distant slopes, never enough to cast a shadow. At long intervals the cloud sea below brightens, and the underside of a projecting cliff appears for a moment: smooth, gray, astonishingly deep. Then it disappears again.

Closer things have their own ways of becoming visible. Damp leaf margins shine green. Cracks in certain stones give off a dim red light. Under the broadest plants, moving specks of blue mark the passage of small animals.

There are many animals, though a person could stand here for an hour and see none.

They live in the thickness of the ground cover or travel through it at a level beneath the wind. Narrow-bodied grazers part the moss without disturbing its surface. White, many-jointed creatures hang under leaves, scraping the frost into their mouths. Predators wait beside the places where exposed stone interrupts the vegetation. When a grazer crosses, a pale limb unfolds; afterward the limb withdraws, and the stone is bare again.

Across the open basins, larger animals migrate in loose lines. They are low, broad-backed things with fur so dark that their bodies disappear against the plants. Only the frost on their shoulders gives them away. They take hours to cross a single stretch of visible land, stopping often to lower their faces into the greenery. Their feet spread before touching down and gather again when lifted. Even over brittle ice, they make almost no sound.

Silence here is less a lack of movement than a property of the air. Wind passes with little voice. Leaves bend, but their soft, thick edges do not rustle. A stone dislodged from a cliff may vanish into the dark without any report from below. The sounds that do arrive are intimate: a drop loosening from a root, a faint scrape beneath the moss, the sudden fine ticking of frost spreading across a leaf.

There is one other sound, most easily heard with an ear against the ground.

It is a steady, uneven turning.

Beneath the oldest vegetation are beds of metallic stone, laid down before the plateau reached this height. The plants have entered them. Roots split along iron-rich seams and grow into dark conductive cords. In certain basins, those cords converge beneath thick circular colonies whose leaves turn around a central axis.

The motion is slow enough to miss while watching, fast enough to notice when returning. One fan that faced a ridge in the evening faces the open basin by morning. Beneath it, a ring of smaller leaves travels the opposite way. Frost builds along one margin and melts along another. The roots warm. Minute currents pass into the surrounding ground.

There are no buildings, no tools, no evidence of anyone having assembled these mechanisms. Their parts are grown, shed, and grown again. Yet they have the physical character of machinery: repeated tolerances, nested moving surfaces, a vibration transmitted through their foundations. Some colonies occupy whole acres. Others fit beneath a loose stone.

Their warmth supports the lushest patches. Around a mature turning colony, the ground cover deepens into a tangled belt of glossy leaves, fungal threads, and soft translucent stalks. Water remains liquid in small cavities below the surface. Animals crowd into these warm margins, feeding, mating, hunting, departing before the heat shifts elsewhere.

For the colonies do not run continuously. A basin can remain still for months, its plants dark and tightly folded. Then something changes in the stone below. The turning begins at one edge and spreads. Leaves lift. Frost releases its hold. Creatures emerge from seams that appeared empty. Over several nights, the basin becomes busy without becoming loud.

From the ridge above, this looks like a slow adjustment of darkness.

The land itself has settled into old shapes. Its gullies are broad and blunt; its cliffs have shed their unstable faces. Wind has polished the exposed stone until some surfaces feel almost greasy. Where a little meltwater travels each season, it follows channels scarcely deeper than a finger. These do not join into streams. The water enters the plants, passes underground, or freezes in a shaded hollow.

Occasionally, a hollow fills enough to become a pool. The water is black and perfectly still. Its edges are crowded with vegetation, but its center remains clear. Small animals approach it cautiously. Their reflections arrive before they do: a pale shape appears in the water, then the creature itself emerges from the moss and lowers its mouth.

Nothing seems troubled by this.

Above the pools, above the turning leaves and the widely separated backs of grazing animals, the air remains immense and nearly vacant. No wings cross it. No tall canopy divides it into rooms. Most of the life of this place occupies the first few inches above the stone, under a sky that gives it almost nothing.

Yet along the underside of every ridge, in every shallow shelter, the plants continue unfolding. A fresh fringe of leaves presses into the frost. A root finds a wet mineral seam. Somewhere beneath the basin floor, something begins to turn, and the animals change their paths.
