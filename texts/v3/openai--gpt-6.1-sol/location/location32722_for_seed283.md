The heat has not gone out of the ground, though it has been dark for hours. It rises from the bare patches between roots and hangs beneath the leaves, held there by the canopy. In the few places where you can see the sky, there are stars, but no moon. Most of the landscape is known by what it obstructs: a black mass of palms against a slightly less black sky, the creek interrupting the earth beneath your feet, a low branch encountered first against your shoulder.

This is low country, almost flat. Small differences in height decide everything. A rise of half a meter supports trees with broad, leathery leaves; a shallow depression holds reeds and a little standing water. Farther out, where the ground sinks almost imperceptibly, palms give way to mangroves. Their roots make a dense, repeated pattern that continues beyond sight. The creek passes among them in bends so gradual that, in daylight, it can be difficult to tell which way the water is moving.

There has been no substantial rain for weeks. The greenery survives on water stored out of sight: moisture deep in the clay, seepage along old channels, the small daily return of the tide. The uppermost leaves are dusty. Some have curled their edges inward. Yet beneath them are fresh shoots, vines thick as wrists, ferns growing where a fallen trunk has kept one patch of earth shaded for years. At the bottom of that trunk, the wood is soft enough to press a finger into. At the top, it is dry, pale, and hard.

Nothing here is arranged for passage. Roots meet roots. Shrubs fill the openings left by fallen trees. Where a vine has died, another has climbed its length, keeping its shape occupied. Even the creek banks offer little clearance: sedges lean over the water, and behind them lie mats of old palm fronds, each rib caught beneath the next. You can go through this country, but seldom in a straight line.

The terrain opens beyond the first belt of trees into a broad, shallow basin. There are dark islands of woodland, reed beds, exposed stretches of cracked silt, and pools remaining in the deepest hollows. The whole basin extends for kilometers, with no single feature large enough to command it. Its creeks have worn their routes into the clay over a long time. Their banks are rounded, undercut in familiar places, reinforced elsewhere by roots. When a little soil falls away, it joins a slope already made of such falls.

The animals occupy all of this.

On the dry ridges, ants move beneath the litter in such numbers that a disturbed leaf reveals several intersecting streams. Termites work inside dead branches whose outer surfaces still look intact. Beetles sit under peeling bark. Centipedes fit themselves into cracks beside the roots. Above them, moths rest against the undersides of leaves, and geckos wait on the stems, almost indistinguishable from knots.

The pools are shallow but crowded. Small fish gather where a thread of water enters. Their movements briefly roughen the surface, then cease. Crabs stand at the mouths of holes in the banks. A frog occupies nearly every suitable pocket of shade, though few are calling now. Along one reach of creek, sleeping birds cover the branches over the water: folded herons, small waders close together, a heavier bird shifting its weight and settling again. Their droppings whiten the leaves below.

There are bats in the hollow trees, rats in the palms, snakes in the tangled margins of the pools. Something substantial moves through the reeds without hurrying. It presses stalks aside, pauses, and continues. The reeds close behind it.

For so much life, there is surprisingly little sound. The dry weather has thinned the frog chorus. Many insects remain still in the heat or make sounds too fine to carry far. A leaf scrapes another leaf. A fish takes something from the surface with a soft click. Occasionally a bird wakes and gives one brief, irritated note.

Beyond these close sounds is a low mechanical hum.

It comes from across the basin, from an industrial district outside the wetland. No building can be seen from here. Trees conceal its lights, and the slight rise of the far bank conceals the rest. But on still nights the sound reaches this far: cooling fans, pumps, machinery running through hours when people are asleep. Sometimes the hum deepens for a minute. Sometimes it stops, and the sudden absence makes the creek seem farther away.

There are no roads here, no walls, no discarded objects caught in the roots. The industrial presence arrives by air and water. On certain winds, a dry mineral smell mixes with the scent of warm mud. A trace of light touches the lowest clouds, if there are clouds. At the outermost creeks, the water can be a little warmer than it is inland. These differences are small enough that they might escape a single visit. They persist long enough to affect where algae thicken, where fish linger, where the first leaves yellow during the dry season.

Closer to the ground, the smells are stronger and more specific. Crushed foliage. Sour fruit fermenting beneath a palm. Mud exposed by the falling tide. In one hollow, the sharp smell of a crowded animal roost; in another, the clean, faintly metallic smell of seep water emerging through sand.

Toward morning, moisture collects on the cooler surfaces. It beads on smooth leaves and along the backs of sleeping beetles. A few drops fall into the litter. They darken small spots, soften a curled leaf, and are absorbed. There is no widespread wetness, only these scattered returns.

The night proceeds by such slight changes. Water lowers against a root. A crab emerges far enough to feed. A flowering vine loses several petals. Under the earth, animals continue enlarging passages that will collapse and be opened again.

Near dawn, the canopy becomes visible from below, leaf against leaf. The birds over the creek begin to stir, although the ground remains dark. Heat is still coming from it. In the shallow pools, fish move toward the last bands of shade, and on the dry ridges the ants keep their paths beneath the litter.
