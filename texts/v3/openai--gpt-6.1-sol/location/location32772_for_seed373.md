The garden occupies a notch in the summit, no wider than a village square. Above it is blue sky. Below it, beyond the broken rim, the mountains fall away through several layers of pale air. From the approach ridge, the place looks empty: a shallow stone basin, three leaning frames, a strip of green along one edge.

Then the wind turns, and the green rises.

It lifts from the rock in sheets, unfolds into fronds, and streams across the basin without quite touching anything. What looked like a narrow border becomes a canopy. Leaves thinner than onion skin spread over the open space, some emerald, some the brown-red of light passing through a closed eyelid. Between them are long clear intervals. You can see the opposite rim through almost every part of the garden, but everywhere you look, something is growing.

The plants live on almost no substance. Their stems are hollow, their leaves mostly fine ribs surrounding air. A mature specimen can shade a person and weigh less than the dust on that person’s boots. Their roots enter seams in the summit rock, where they draw up a cold, oily mineral secretion. Water is scarce enough here that a spilled mouthful would change the ecology of a patch of stone for years. There is no dew in the morning, no frost in the shade, no stream under the roots. The white deposits around them are salts.

The garden is cool even at noon. Wind comes up from the northern escarpment with the dry, penetrating chill of an opened vault. Sunlight is otherwise ordinary: bright enough to make you narrow your eyes, warm against the back of your hand. Shadows travel across the basin in recognizable hours. It is only what happens inside those hours that refuses to settle.

Nothing stops moving.

The rooted plants whirl around their attachments. Fronds turn inside out and recover. A climbing species releases twenty or thirty filaments into the air, hooks a distant lip of rock, and pulls itself across the basin until its old stem parts at a joint. What appears to be a loose leaf may be an animal. What appears to be an animal may be a seed casing with six temporary legs. Some things change which of these they are during the crossing.

There are more creatures than you can count, but very little creature by weight.

They come in the shapes suited to an almost empty place: loops, hairs, fans, inflated bladders, cages of articulated bone. Most have broad surfaces and minute bodies. Their organs lie along the edges of their wings. A flock passing overhead is visible chiefly as a disturbance in the sunlight, a hundred moving outlines briefly caught against the blue.

At the rim live little stone-colored grazers that flatten into cracks when the wind rises. In calm moments they emerge in such numbers that the rock seems to loosen and crawl. They scrape mineral growth from the roots, then climb onto the leaves and lick the bitter crystals caught there. Their feet make a delicate ticking you will never hear.

The basin is deafening.

Wind entering the summit’s cavities produces a continuous, many-throated roar. Some passages are broad enough to give out a bass note felt through the soles of your feet. Others whistle above the range where the sound is comfortable, and the thin plants vibrate in sympathy. The whole canopy becomes a set of reeds. Every alteration in its shape changes the chord.

The animals add to it. A loose spiral of flying creatures contracts and cracks like a flag in a gale. Bladder-bodied scavengers discharge pressure in explosive coughs. The grazers communicate by striking their shells against the rock; with your palm against the ground, you can feel their messages running beneath the larger thunder.

There is no useful speaking here. Two people standing shoulder to shoulder must touch to attract each other’s attention. Even your own voice seems to have occurred elsewhere.

The stone has borne this for a very long time. Its weathering is patient beneath the violence. Dust carried through the notch has polished the exposed surfaces into overlapping hollows, each smooth on the windward side and edged with a small, hard lip behind. The rim is rounded rather than jagged. Ancient fractures have filled with compressed root fiber and shed shells. Where a slab broke away long ago, generations of growth have embroidered its outline without concealing it.

Three structures stand among these settled forms.

One is a doorway, or resembles one: two dark uprights and a lintel, tilted toward the sky. There is no wall attached. Windblown grit passes through it and appears on the other side moving more slowly, then drops to the stone in a narrow, immaculate line.

Another is a shallow cabinet embedded in the rim. Its front has become as weathered as the surrounding rock, except for six circular depressions that remain perfectly sharp. Small animals shelter in them. Each depression is warmer than the next, although no visible mechanism supplies the heat. Green roots gather around the coldest one and avoid the warmest.

The third structure hangs over the middle of the basin.

It is a ladder of seven square rungs, unsupported, each rung turned slightly from the one below. You notice it late because it is the same pale color as the sky. There are no rails and no cables. Sometimes the lower squares lie flat, like stepping stones across the air. Sometimes all seven rotate edge-on.

The garden moves differently around it.

A torn leaf entering the space beneath the lowest rung slows, opens, and rises. A seed can linger there for weeks. Winged animals use the sheltered column to lay eggs, each egg suspended separately, with the careful spacing of berries on an invisible branch. When the ladder turns, everything beneath it is released at once.

That release is the garden’s storm.

It happens without a visible schedule. The rungs begin rotating. The open basin, which a moment earlier held only a few drifting outlines, fills with fluttering membranes. Eggs hatch in midair. Seeds split. Animals that have waited almost motionless suddenly accelerate into the wind, and the plants throw up their curled new leaves to intercept them.

Within seconds, the basin becomes a struggle over light and attachment.

Fresh tendrils lash between stones. Predators skim the canopy, folding their limbs against their bodies to pass through gaps. Grazers swarm up bending stems. Broad leaves are stripped to transparent lattices, while new ones swell and open beside them. A frond may grow across half the garden, capture a mouthful of airborne mineral dust, and be eaten before its tip finds a place to fasten.

The sound gains a sharp, splintering register. Standing at the rim, you feel the gusts tug at your clothing in several directions at once. A leaf wraps around your wrist. Its edge is cool and faintly sticky. Before you can free it, three tiny animals have run along it, taking bites, and it tears away in their wake.

Then the upper rung stops.

The remaining squares follow, one after another. A clear column forms beneath them. Loose matter begins to collect there again: seeds, eggs, fragments too small to distinguish. The canopy subsides toward the rock, though the wind continues and nothing is truly still.

These bursts have left surprisingly little wreckage. Nearly everything made here can be used again. Shells become root channels. Wings are caught and woven into new leaves. The cold mineral oils return along the cracks. The doorway catches the heavier grit that would otherwise bury the basin and lays it where the grazers can reach it.

Near the cabinet is a sheltered ledge large enough to sit on. Someone once cut footholds leading down to it. They are shallow now, worn by feet and dust, and one has been occupied by a dense tuft of crimson filaments. There are two finger-sized holes in the wall, perhaps where a handrail was fixed.

From that ledge, your eyes gradually learn the place. The emptiness resolves into distances between living surfaces. A grazer makes its slow crossing of a slab while immense leaves race overhead. A cluster of eggs hangs beneath the ladder, each one turning in its own small current. On the far rim, the green canopy folds down into the same narrow strip you saw from the ridge.

Beyond it, the mountains stand in clear afternoon light. They seem utterly quiet.

Here, in this little notch, the stone hums against your spine, dry leaves brush your face, and another generation begins to gather in the air.
