The land is so low that water does not seem to arrive from anywhere. It appears in the shallow places, gathers along the edges of stone, and darkens the earth from underneath. After rain, there is little difference between a pool and the ground beside it except the way each receives the light.

For miles, the surface rises and falls by scarcely more than the height of an animal’s back. Long shelves of weathered rock lie almost flush with the mud. Between them are broad pans of brown water, pale clay flats, and channels whose banks are too soft to hold a sharp edge. Nothing here suggests a recent break. Even the exposed stone has lost the look of fracture. It has become rounded, shallow-backed, worn into forms that let water pass without much disturbance.

A few ridges remain, but they are low enough to disappear when seen from the wrong angle. On their crests, the rock is warm and dry by midday. Below, moisture beads in old seams and seeps out through the grain. Small trickles descend a hand’s breadth, enter a hollow, and stop.

The sky occupies most of the view.

In clear weather, sunlight lies plainly on everything: gray stone, dull green reed stems, the copper-colored skin of a pool. There is no permanent haze, though the air is humid enough to soften the farthest shore. Clouds travel across the flats with large, slowly moving shadows. Under one of these, the water loses its brown tint and seems suddenly deep. When the sun returns, submerged stones become visible again, along with the little tracks crossing the bottom.

The pools are usually shallow. Their surfaces hold the day’s warmth, and their bottoms hold the warmth of several days before. Bare mud can be almost hot beneath the thin film left by receding water. Where a channel widens, fish gather in the slightly cooler current, scarcely moving except to correct their position.

Vegetation occurs in patches rather than cover. There are stands of stiff, salt-tolerant grass on the higher margins, widely spaced sedges, and low succulent plants with thick leaves dusty at the tips. A few shrubs grow where a tongue of firmer ground reaches into the wet flats. Their roots spread sideways, following the narrow layer in which there is both air and moisture.

Much of the ground remains bare.

Some pans are too salty for roots. Others stay flooded too long, or dry to a hard crust before seedlings can establish themselves. Along their rims, white mineral deposits brighten as the water withdraws. A heavy rain dissolves these rims and spreads them again. The plants occupy the places where such changes are mild enough to survive: a slightly raised lip, a gravel seam, the sheltered side of a stone.

The sparse growth makes the animals easier to see.

Small wading birds move in loose groups across the shallows, leaving a great deal of space between one another. They pause, probe, take three or four steps, and pause again. Farther out, larger birds stand in water up to their knees. At a distance they could be mistaken for narrow pieces of driftwood until one unfolds its wings.

Crabs work along the wet margins. Their holes stipple the exposed mud, each with a small fan of excavated pellets beside it. Tiny fish enter pools through channels no wider than a body. Snails gather beneath the shaded overhang of a rock. Insects skate on water where it is fresh enough, while clouds of smaller flies hover over the warmer, stiller basins.

Life is plentiful without filling the view. There are stretches where nothing seems to move, then a strip of mud crowded with feeding birds, or one pool so full of small fish that its surface trembles.

The sound is much larger than the visible activity.

Bird calls carry over the unobstructed ground: sharp whistles, coarse rattles, a repeated nasal cry from a distant nesting patch. Frogs call from the few dense stands of sedge, their voices overlapping until the plants seem to contain one continuous vibration. In the warmest hours, insects add a high, steady rasp. Near the mouth of a channel, water makes a low sucking noise as it passes beneath a mat of roots.

There is little to absorb these sounds. No forest muffles them, no steep bank blocks them. A bird calling well beyond sight can seem close, while a bird standing nearby may remain silent for an hour.

Toward the edge of the region, the separate pools join into a broad, brackish reach. Beyond that lies open water, its horizon almost indistinguishable from the low horizon of the land. The tide arrives without surf. Over several hours, channels fill, pools meet, and dark wetness advances across the flats. Stones that were separate become islands. Crab holes vanish. The birds move upslope by a few paces.

When the water withdraws, it leaves fine bands of sediment around every slight obstruction. These marks are delicate, but the pattern of the place is durable. The same hollows fill first. The same shelves emerge last. Year after year, the water lays another thin layer into depressions already worn almost level.

By late afternoon, the exposed rock releases its heat into the damp air. Shadows lengthen, though there is little tall enough to cast them. Bird calls grow more frequent around the shallow feeding grounds. The pools settle into reflected sky, interrupted by a fin, an insect, the measured descent of a beak.

Beyond them, more flats extend out of sight: another low ridge, another basin, another faint line of reeds. The evening tide is still coming in. Along the nearest stone, water has reached a dry stain left earlier in the day and is slowly covering it.
