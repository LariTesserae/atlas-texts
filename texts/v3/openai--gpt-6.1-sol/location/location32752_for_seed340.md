The descent ends not on stone but on a leaf still unfolding.

It is broad enough to hold a village. Its central rib rises beside the path like a pale embankment, branching into smaller ribs that make low ridges across the ground. Between them, the surface is springy, densely furred, and warm through the soles of your feet. The outer edge remains rolled into a tight scroll. Every few minutes it loosens with a crack like a tree splitting, and another strip of country becomes available.

There is no water below.

No seepage darkens the ceiling. No drops collect on the roots. The hollows contain dust, husks, or animals; never puddles. What looks wet at a distance is polished tissue, and what shines in a wound is a thick amber substance that draws itself back into the flesh before it can fall. The plants move nutrients through threads of molten wax. Their leaves take salts from the air and heat from the buried mineral seams. A cut stalk smells of scorched almonds and gives off a little smoke.

Here, dryness is not a season. Everything has grown for it.

The cavern extends for several miles, though few places allow you to see that far. Its floor is almost entirely living growth: leaf terraces, braided stems, swollen tubers the size of hills. Curtains of roots divide the distance into chambers. There are low forests beneath high forests, hanging forests whose crowns point toward the ground, and fields of stiff, jointed shoots that grow sideways across gaps.

Above them all is the old rock roof, visible only in fragments. Pale crystals embedded in it give a steady light when heated by the air rising from below. They are bright enough to cast shadows. The country has the ordinary illumination of a warm afternoon, with no sun to locate and no evening coming. Where the plants crowd close to the roof, they shade themselves. Elsewhere, crystal light falls through openings and picks out yellow leaf edges, clouds of pollen, the backs of moving animals.

The air is hot, dusty, and in constant motion.

So is the ground.

A slope that was gentle yesterday may have steepened as the stem beneath it thickened. A passage can narrow over an hour until the creatures using it must turn sideways. Far off, a cluster of pale shoots pushes upward against an older canopy, lifting it in stages. Each lift sends a tremor through the connected tissues. You feel it underfoot before you hear the great tearing noise.

Almost nothing here has had time to wear away. There are no rounded stones, no old channels, no smooth gullies cut by years of traffic. The exposed surfaces are new: satin skins, raw fibers, fresh splinters, bright membranes stretched between supports. Even the dust is mostly recent, shaken from pollen chambers or ground from husks by teeth.

The plants are continually making terrain. Animals follow close behind, finishing it badly.

There are too many to count, or even to separate cleanly from one another.

Long-bodied grazers move in columns along the leaf ribs, their dozens of feet ticking in perfect sequence. They shave the soft fuzz from the ground and leave pale tracks that regrow behind them. Small armored animals pack themselves into crevices, clicking their shells against one another as the walls tighten. Moths with stiff, translucent wings crowd the warmer openings overhead. They descend in such numbers that a clearing may become dim for several minutes.

Among the high branches, heavy climbing creatures haul themselves from stem to stem on hooked forelimbs. Their bodies are covered in loose scales that rattle when they move. They eat the swollen joints where plants are preparing to branch. A whole grove may change its direction because one of them fed there.

Predators live in the gaps this activity creates. Some are no larger than a hand and hunt by racing through the fur on a leaf. Others remain motionless inside curled fronds, indistinguishable from the dry inner surface until the frond fills with prey and closes.

The great burrowers are seldom visible. They travel inside the thickest stems, eating a little and widening the rest by force. Their progress can be followed by a moving bulge beneath the skin. Sometimes a bulge passes directly under a settlement, and everyone must stop what they are doing and loosen the lashings that hold their floors in place.

The sound is appalling.

At first it seems like one sustained roar. After a while, separate noises become apparent, though none becomes quieter. There is the deep rasp of grazers feeding, the hard chatter of shells, the whir of wings, the knocking calls of climbers, and the shrill vibration of insects perched on taut fibers. Seeds discharge from their cases with explosive reports. Thick stems groan under loads. New leaves open with long, ripping crashes that travel through the cavern.

The ground carries much of it. Put a hand against a rib and you can feel several events happening at once: feeding nearby, something digging below, a stand of shoots forcing its way upward far across the basin.

No one who lives here speaks across an open space. Voices cannot survive it. Messages are tapped against stems with hardwood mallets, or passed hand to hand as small bundles of knotted fiber. Different knots name routes, trades, hazards. A knot pulled tight means the way is still passable. A loose one means it must be checked again.

The settlements are scattered and slight.

From a distance, they look like dry material caught in the growth: a few woven platforms, a pale awning, ladders running up the sheltered side of a stalk. They occupy places where the plants have paused, usually around a mature joint or beneath the overhang of a thick leaf. Houses are made from shed bark and the stiff cases left after seeds burst. Nothing is fastened deeply. Pegs are short, ropes easy to cut, and the floors come apart into pieces that two people can carry.

People work with chipped mineral blades, thorn needles, bone wedges, and wooden levers hardened over vents. They prize tools that are light enough to abandon. Their finest craft is the brace: a frame fitted around a growing shoot that persuades it to bend without breaking. With braces, they make stairs, support awnings, and turn young stems away from doorways.

A large brace takes many hands to move. You may come upon a work party hauling one along a ridge while animals stream past their ankles and pollen drifts through the frame. The workers keep their mouths covered. They touch one another’s shoulders to signal when to pull.

There are abandoned frames everywhere. Some hang high in the canopy, carried upward by the growth they once controlled. Others have become buried in living wood. Occasionally a new shoot splits open and reveals a neatly woven wall inside it, still square at one corner.

Food is abundant but difficult to claim. The tender growth attracts grazing animals as soon as it emerges. Seed bodies are nutritious once their explosive outer layers have been removed, but gathering them requires careful timing. Root tissue can be shaved thin and heated until the wax softens. It has a rich, dry taste and leaves the teeth coated.

The inhabitants also eat the small grazers, which are caught in sleeve-shaped traps. These traps are never left unattended for long. A good catch brings predators; a dead animal brings an entire succession of smaller eaters. Within an hour, a forgotten meal may have become a busy patch of ground with no recognizable meal left in it.

The landscape can change fastest where the animal crowds are thickest.

A migrating herd presses through a low thicket, breaking stems and opening a broad route. Warm air enters. Dormant seeds respond to the heat. Shoots spring up before the herd has fully passed, brushing the bellies of the last animals. In the disturbance, hunters arrive, seed-eaters burrow in, climbers descend from above. Broken plant matter is chewed into flakes and packed into crevices. By the time the noise recedes, there may be a new ridge where the path was.

Not everything succeeds. Whole stands exhaust themselves in a burst of growth and go rigid. Their leaves bleach and hold their shape. For a little while these dead groves are comparatively stable, and people hurry to use them: drying food, setting out tools, taking measurements for structures they may never finish.

Then the underside begins to stir. Animals have been eating the supports.

Near the center of the cavern is a wide basin where several enormous plants are opening together. From a high platform you can see it between root curtains: overlapping pale leaves lifting and sliding across one another, dark swarms flowing into every exposed seam. The light catches each new surface before anything has walked on it.

There is a settlement at the basin’s edge, six houses and a work shelter. Its residents have been watching the leaves all day. Beside each doorway is a packed carrying frame.

A deep crack sounds from the basin. One leaf lifts higher than the rest, shedding animals in a rattling cascade. A gap opens beneath it, bright with reflected crystal light. Already, winged things are entering. Already, shoots are reaching across from the opposite side.

The people take down one awning. They wait with their hands on the ropes.

Under their feet, the broad leaf gives another small, deliberate stretch.
