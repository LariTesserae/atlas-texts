The town occupies a shallow fold in the top of the mountain. From the ridge above, it looks like a patch of exposed bone: pale walls, open courts, a few dark passages, all gathered into an area small enough to cross before the weather changes. Beyond it, the upland continues almost level to the horizon. There are no sharp peaks nearby. The mountain is very old, and has worn down to a broad back lifted into the cold.

Clouds catch along its western edge. On clear days, lower mountains are visible beneath that edge, blue and apparently unfinished, their steep valleys still full of shadow.

Here, the sky takes up nearly everything.

The walls stand only a little higher than a person. Some have been reduced to waist-high curves; others rise around empty chambers with rounded corners and floors of green-black growth. There are streets, though none is quite straight. They converge on a long central court, divide around small enclosures, and open into passages too narrow for two bodies to pass together. It is a town’s arrangement, with shared walls and repeated thresholds, but there is no masonry. No joint, no tool mark, no difference between the substance of a wall and the substance of the ground beneath it.

The pale material is porous. Where it has broken, its interior consists of fine, branching chambers.

Only along the sheltered bases is it still alive.

There, a thin gray lip advances over the soil, incorporating grit, dead leaves, and the white fragments shed by the older walls. Over many seasons it thickens into a low rim. Rims meet and fuse. An enclosed patch of ground becomes a room. The growth rarely rises high enough now to make a roof, but there are a few old vaulted places, smooth inside and blackened by damp, where the air is several degrees warmer than outside.

The settlement grows slowly. Most of what can be seen was made under a climate that no longer exists.

Wind comes over the plateau almost without interruption. It slides across the low walls, then drops into the streets and becomes loud.

Each passage has a different voice. A narrow slit whistles. The open court produces a low, continuous roar. In the roofed chambers, the sound gathers into wavering notes that can be felt against the ribs. Gusts passing over the porous material find cavities below the surface, and occasionally a whole wall answers with a deep, rough bellow. It can sound close enough to make a visitor turn around.

Then the wind shifts, and the bellow comes from somewhere else.

Loose seeds rattle along the streets. Water taps in a chamber that appears dry. Underfoot, a brittle patch of pale ground gives a little crack, disproportionately sharp in the enclosed air. There is very little moving here large enough to see, yet the place is almost never quiet.

The cold is not absolute. In sunlight, the dark soil warms enough to release a smell of wet leaves. But anything in shadow retains the night. Frost survives until afternoon inside certain doorways. Along the north sides of the walls, moss holds small white crystals between its stems, while a hand’s breadth away, exposed to the sun, the same moss is bright green and dripping.

Plants fill the lower part of the town without filling its space.

They stay close to the ground. Cushions of tiny leaves swell over thresholds. Ferns no taller than a finger occupy the protected corners, their fronds densely overlapping. A creeping shrub sends burgundy stems through cracks in the pale walls; its leaves are thick, glossy, and curled under at the edges. In the warmer courts, it forms continuous mats beneath which the old street surface has disappeared.

Nothing rises above the walls except a few flower stalks, already bent sideways.

The upper air remains empty. From within any room, one can see a large piece of sky, and beyond the nearest wall there is often nothing else to interrupt it. Even where the vegetation is richest, the town feels sparsely occupied, as though all its life has agreed to use the lowest few inches.

Water arrives in episodes.

A cloud settles over the fold and stays for an hour. Droplets collect on the leaves, on the cold interiors of arches, and especially on the fine pores of the walls. The pale material darkens unevenly. Wetness spreads in branching patches before any rain has fallen.

Then the walls begin to drain.

Tiny streams emerge from holes halfway up their sides. They run down grooves made by earlier streams, gather at thresholds, and disappear into the green floors. A dry room becomes a shallow pool. In another, the water travels beneath the moss, visible only where it lifts a loose edge and makes it tremble.

There is no permanent river. There are three permanent pools.

The smallest lies in a bowl beside the central court, dark and round, with a rim polished by windblown grit. The largest occupies the floor of a roofless chamber. Its water is clear enough to show every drowned stem, but the bottom remains hard to judge: the pale floor is riddled with openings, and some of them descend farther than the light.

The third pool is usually heard before it is found. It lies under a low arch, behind a curtain of trailing growth. Water drops into it from the ceiling at irregular intervals. Each drop makes a heavy, hollow note.

During a long dry spell, all three shrink. The vegetation around them contracts without turning brown. Leaves fold together; moss cushions become hard and gray; the creeping stems loosen their hold on the walls. A little rain reverses this within hours. The place greens first in sheltered seams, then along the drainage lines, then across whole courts. The return is quick enough to watch.

Animals come less reliably.

Sometimes a small, pale bird walks along a wall, probing its cavities. It has a dark bill and long toes that grip the rough surface. When it finds something, it pulls backward with its whole body. Usually it flies away carrying nothing visible.

On damp mornings, translucent grazers emerge from beneath the moss. They are flat, thumb-sized creatures, almost indistinguishable from wet leaves until they move. Their backs hold grains of black soil. They feed along the living gray rims and withdraw before the direct sun reaches them. The pale growth closes behind their feeding marks over the next few days.

There are tracks in the soft ground near the largest pool, but rarely anything making them. The prints are narrow and deep, in pairs. They enter by one street and leave by another. Whatever drinks here prefers the hours when the town is filled with cloud.

Much of the movement belongs to the walls themselves.

A thaw loosens their outer layers. Flakes fall into the streets, sometimes in such numbers that a floor becomes white overnight. At the same time, the living bases take up water and swell. A passage narrows. A threshold rises just enough to divert the next runoff into a neighboring room. Water redirected in this way brings soil and seeds; a bare chamber becomes planted, while another begins to dry.

These changes are small, but there are so few competing movements that each is noticeable.

Near the eastern end is a room almost entirely worn away. Its walls survive as four low, separated humps. The wind crosses it freely. Grass has grown outside its former boundary and scarcely at all within it, leaving a pale rectangular clearing. In the middle lies a single curved piece of roof, too heavy for the wind to turn.

Its underside is finely ridged. Meltwater gathers between the ridges, and in summer a narrow line of green follows each one.

Farther west, where cloud touches down most often, new enclosures are forming. They have no entrances yet. Their walls are low, soft, and faintly warm to the touch. If a foot presses against one, the surface yields and slowly returns. Beneath the skin, dark strands draw together around the pressure.

By evening, the sun comes underneath the clouds and shines horizontally through the passages. Wet walls glow cream and gold. The pools become bright pieces of sky at ground level. Every moss stem throws a long, delicate shadow.

For a few minutes the wind may drop. The whole town can then be heard draining: trickles behind walls, droplets in the vaulted rooms, the faint settling of saturated growth. A bird calls once from the plateau and receives no answer.

As the light leaves, frost begins in the oldest chambers. It forms around the pores, spreading inward from each opening until the walls look dusted with salt. The newer rims stay wet longer. Their warmth is slight but persistent.

Then cloud closes over the fold again. The far edge of the town disappears, although it is only a short walk away. Moisture gathers on the walls. Somewhere in the white air, a narrow passage starts to whistle, and the deeper rooms slowly join it.
