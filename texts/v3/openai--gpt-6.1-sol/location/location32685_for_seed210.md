The hollow lies high enough that clouds arrive from below.

They press against the outer lip of the rock, pale and densely folded, but seldom cross it. Inside, the air is dark, cool, and wet. The place is not large: a sloping floor, three steep walls, and an opening through which a narrow stretch of distant mountain can be seen. You could walk from one end to the other in a few minutes, if walking here worked quite as it does elsewhere.

The stone looks newly broken. Its edges are so sharp that water gathers along them without rounding them, forming clear seams on the black mineral. There is no rubble at the foot of the walls. Each face rises cleanly out of the ground, as though the hollow has opened by separation rather than collapse. On one wall, the two halves of a split crystal remain an arm’s length apart. The gap between them is full of fern roots.

Little soil is visible. Vegetation grows from joints in the rock, from shallow pools, from places where moisture has thickened into something that roots can hold. There are broad leaves almost the color of the stone, pale branching mosses, and stands of a low, glossy plant whose leaves overlap like scales. Everything is vigorous. A fern frond can be as wide as your chest. The moss rises in deep cushions, full of narrow passages.

Yet there is room between these growths. Most of the floor is open: flat black shelves, still water, and gaps through which you can see down into the mountain. The lushness gathers in distinct patches. Around each patch is a margin of bare rock, clean enough to show the slightest reflection. Nothing sprawls across those margins.

The light comes principally from the wet surfaces. A little gray daylight enters above, but it seems to lose strength on the way down. Water preserves what reaches it. Under a leaf, in a seam, on the belly of a resting animal, there is often a faint brightness with no corresponding brightness overhead.

At first the sound resembles machinery working somewhere beyond the hollow.

A deep rotating note runs under everything. Above it are smaller sounds: a hard ticking, an occasional hiss, the clack of something engaging under load. The noise is loud enough that you would need to raise your voice to someone standing a few paces away. It does not echo much. The plants absorb the upper frequencies, and the lower ones are felt through the soles of your feet.

There is nothing built here.

No road enters the hollow. No cable crosses it. Nothing has been fastened to the rock, and there are no cut marks, foundations, or discarded parts. Nevertheless, the mountain is operating.

You can see this most clearly at a shallow cleft in the floor. Down inside it, columns of dark mineral turn past one another. They have grown in interlocking forms, their ridges meeting with exact clearance. Water passes between them under pressure. It flashes white in the narrowest spaces, then returns to clear water as it spreads through a bed of roots.

The columns do not wear. Their faces have the sharpness of crystals just exposed to air.

Elsewhere, stone plates tremble without shifting position. A branching vein glows faintly beneath the surface, carrying a charge that makes the hair on your arms lift. Beneath one pool, something turns quickly enough to be invisible, though its outline can be felt as a steady pull on the water. The whole pool is flat except for a small, permanent depression in its center.

There is no evident product. Nothing is being extracted or assembled. The noise, pressure, and electrical activity belong to the place as completely as the damp belongs to the moss.

Most of the animals are difficult to see until you stop looking for them.

The fern beds are full of small pale bodies. Some cling to the undersides of fronds; some hang head-down from roots over the clefts. They have soft fur, six narrow limbs, and eyes with a dull green reflection. Their breathing is rapid and audible at close range. Several are feeding from the same leaf, which remains whole beneath their mouths.

In the moss live jointed creatures with transparent backs. You can see a bright speck travel through each one, returning by another route. They crowd the little passages so densely that the moss appears to have a second, slower texture moving beneath its surface.

The pools contain long fish with square snouts. They hold their positions against currents you cannot otherwise detect. Their fins work continuously. Occasionally a fish opens its mouth and a metallic click sounds from deep in the stone.

Higher up, dark birds occupy widely spaced ledges. Each bird has a great deal of empty cliff around it. Some sit with their heads tucked; others remain in flight, wings moving, directly in front of a particular patch of rock. Their cries are short and harsh. They fit into the underlying mechanical noise so well that it takes time to distinguish them from it.

There is abundant life, but very little passage from one part of the hollow to another. A root basin can contain hundreds of creatures. The bare shelf beside it contains none. Across that shelf, another basin supports an entirely different population. No tracks connect them.

The longer you remain, the more difficult it becomes to say what has happened.

Water runs down the wall. You can follow a bright strand with your eyes and hear it striking the pool below. But a droplet caught halfway along a projecting edge never falls. A fish swims, and stays precisely beside the same submerged crystal. A bird beats its wings without advancing or losing height.

These are not brief suspensions. Nothing is waiting to resume.

At the base of the largest fern, a frond is unfurling. Its tip is soft, rolled inward, edged with fine white hairs. It has the tender appearance of growth seen at exactly the right hour. Look at it again after a long while, and it is still at that hour. The oldest leaves are not older. The broken tip of one root remains moist and fresh. There is no litter beneath the plants.

Even the apparent irregularities endure. A thread of water misses its pool and strikes bare stone. One bird gives a broken, two-part call. A small animal holds one forefoot clear of the moss. These details feel accidental, but they are as fixed as the walls.

You may try to disturb something.

Press a finger into the moss and it yields. Take your finger away and there is no interval in which it rises back. It is simply unpressed. Lift a loose-looking flake of stone and feel its weight; look beneath it and the flake is still there, occupying the place you have uncovered. The one in your hand grows difficult to focus on. When you loosen your grip, you cannot determine whether it has fallen.

The hollow permits contact more readily than consequence.

This is why its surfaces are so young. They are not recent in the ordinary sense. The split crystal has never had a weathered face. The first wetness still lies on the exposed rock. Plants fill the fissures without having needed time to establish themselves. Animals feed without diminishing what feeds them, and the enormous work inside the mountain never progresses toward exhaustion.

You can feel the cold, though. That part is immediate and convincing. Moisture collects on your clothes. The rock is hard beneath your hand. Near the clefts, the vibration enters your jaw.

Beyond the opening, the distant mountains seem almost unreal by comparison. Their slopes have landslides, snowfields, long valleys worn by rivers. A cloud moves across them and alters their color.

Here, a broad leaf holds a bead of water at its lowest point. Behind it, in the dimness, a small animal drinks. Its tongue touches the bead again and again. The bead trembles. Through the roots below comes the heavy, uninterrupted sound of the mountain at work.
