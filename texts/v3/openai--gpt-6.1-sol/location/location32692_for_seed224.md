The entrance is a flaw in a piece of black ceramic, halfway up the north face of a mountain. From outside, it looks like a chip no wider than a fingernail. Dust has collected along its lower edge. There is no light behind it, and no reason to distinguish it from the thousands of other chips in the weathered casing.

Inside is a high, dry basin.

Its far wall is close enough to touch, although reaching it takes most of a day. The basin has no reliable diameter. Seen through the entrance, it fits comfortably beneath a thumb; from the floor, its rim stands above you like the lip of a crater. Neither view is mistaken. The discrepancy belongs to the place.

The ground consists of pale mineral grit over a dark, continuous substrate. In a few patches the grit has been swept aside, revealing a surface like graphite, worn to a dull polish. It is cold enough that bare skin would adhere to it, but there is no frost. No vapor clouds the entrance. Nothing here has enough water to freeze.

Along the eastern wall, the powder rises into a succession of low terraces. Their edges are rounded. Small fans of debris descend from them and stop well short of the basin floor, as though falling stones eventually lose interest in downward motion. A few grains remain suspended at ankle height. They have hung there long enough to gather finer dust.

There is almost no color. Ash-white, iron-black, the faint violet of a metal viewed at the wrong angle. Once every several hours, a seam in the upper wall becomes blue. It is too dim to illuminate anything, but during that interval the shadows acquire edges, and the basin seems briefly more solid.

The light has no visible source. Most of it reaches the ground after passing through something that is no longer present. Beneath an overhang, a narrow band of brightness persists where a reflecting surface must once have stood. There is nothing there now, only the light continuing to make the turn.

No wind enters. The dust moves anyway.

It travels slowly uphill in thin, separated lines, leaving broad areas untouched. Each line advances perhaps the width of a grain during the longest period one can comfortably watch. Where two lines meet, their particles arrange themselves into a little ridge and remain that way for years. These ridges account for much of the terrain. The basin has weathered through tiny differences in attraction, with no need for rain, roots, or moving air.

There are structures, though they are easily mistaken for exposed geology.

Near the center stands a short pillar with a hollow square cut through it. It is made of the same black material as the entrance casing, but its surface carries an irregular pattern of shallow pits. The pits have softened with age. Several have merged. Whatever distinctions they once encoded have become difficult to recover.

Beyond it, three silver pins emerge from the grit. They are widely spaced and lean at different angles. Their shadows meet at a point that is not on the ground.

Closer to the far wall lies a shallow dish, cracked cleanly in two. The halves have separated by less than the thickness of a hair. Across that gap, the basin has acquired an extra direction.

You notice this only when you try to step over the dish. Your foot comes down beside where you intended, on a narrow shelf that was previously invisible. Turning around does not bring the dish back into view. You must lean slightly toward a direction your body cannot ordinarily lean; then the shelf disappears, and the cracked silver surface is below you again.

The mechanism still operates. It has simply been working for a very long time without anyone adjusting it.

Higher up, almost concealed beneath an overhang, is a doorway too small for a hand. A lintel, two uprights, a recessed threshold: its construction is unmistakable. The interior is empty. Dust ends neatly at the threshold, and the dim ambient light penetrates no farther than the first joint in the floor.

Beside the doorway, the wall bears marks resembling scratches made by a fine tool. Most run vertically. A few bend into hooks. They are not writing, or not writing meant to be read in succession. Looking at one alters the apparent depth of the others. From the correct angle, the whole group becomes a staircase.

There are only seven steps. The top one is higher than the mountain containing the basin.

That height can be felt throughout the cavity. Near the rim, breathing becomes effortful, although the air is as dense there as it is at the entrance. Metal placed on an upper terrace grows colder than metal on the floor. A loose fragment dropped from the seventh step does not fall into the basin. It remains visible above it, becoming smaller and smaller.

Several such fragments hang overhead. You might first take them for stars.

The air is still, thinly lit, and carries the dry smell of ground stone and a faint electrical sharpness. No insects worry the dust. No threads, spores, shells, or tracks interrupt its surface. The white deposits in the wall’s creases are fractured ceramic, not growth. Here and there a filament extends from one of them, straight as a whisker, but it is extruded material: the casing relieving stresses too slowly to be seen.

The quiet is almost complete.

Occasionally, the pillar makes a soft click. It can be felt through the substrate before it is heard. Following the click, one suspended grain drops, or one of the silver pins changes its inclination by an amount too small to remember accurately. The intervals are uneven. Long observation suggests that the clicks occur when something outside the basin changes, though perhaps not when it changes nearby.

A voice does not echo here. It becomes shallow and loses its lower tones. The sound seems to lie against the walls for a moment before disappearing. Footsteps are similarly brief. Even broken grit gives little crunch beneath a boot; pieces part cleanly and settle without scattering.

The oldest disturbance is a footprint beside the pillar.

Its edges have worn smooth, and pale dust has filled the heel. The front remains sharp. Whoever stood there faced the small doorway, but the print is oriented as though the ground then sloped in another direction. There is no second print. Around it, the mineral terraces continue undisturbed.

The basin is still changing. The hollow in the pillar narrows imperceptibly. Dust gathers in the crack across the dish. One day that crack may close, and the hidden shelf will be inaccessible. The seven steps may eventually become six.

For now, the place holds its few objects far apart, with long, dim stretches of grit between them. Above the empty doorway, a ceramic filament presses slowly outward. Near the entrance, a grain rests on the black lip, poised between the ordinary drop down the mountain face and the much greater height within.

It has not yet fallen either way.
