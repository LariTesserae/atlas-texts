The ground is so new that it has not yet learned to crumble.

Across the low country, dark stone rises through the vegetation in broad, rounded backs. There are no cliffs, no broken faces, almost no loose gravel. Where a root lifts clear of the soil, the surface beneath it is smooth and faintly greasy, the color of a seed just taken from its fruit. Some of the stone still wears a translucent skin. In shallow depressions this skin gathers into folds, thick enough to hold the print of a passing animal, though the print closes slowly after the foot has gone.

Nothing here looks ruined. Nothing looks finished.

The country extends for miles: low swells of ground, dark stands of vegetation, openings where water shines beneath a lid of leaves. From any one place, very little of it can be seen. The distances are revealed in sounds—in a call answered far beyond the nearest thicket, in a low collective roaring that comes from another basin, in the brief silence that spreads through animals hidden across a much larger area than the eye can reach.

Above it all hangs a continuous roof of cloud. Its underside is brown-gray, luminous without brightness. The sun is never visible, but its heat is everywhere. It presses through the cloud and remains beneath the canopy. Even in the deepest shade, where the air has the green taste of crushed stems, there is no coolness. Water in the hollows is warm. The undersides of leaves are warm. A pale root exposed above a pool feels as though something living has just withdrawn from it.

The plants grow in layers so closely fitted that the gaps between them seem occupied too.

At the bottom are fleshy ground leaves, each broad enough to hide several small animals. Water beads along their raised veins and collects at their tips, falling at long intervals into cups formed by older leaves below. Above them stand thickets of jointed stalks, soft-spined bushes, and ferns whose fronds open almost horizontally. Higher still, thick trunks divide early into many limbs. Their crowns meet, but do not make a simple ceiling. Leaves turn edgewise, hang downward, fold around branches. There are little chambers of darkness inside every tree.

Vines fill the remaining space. Some are as thin as hairs and carry droplets larger than their stems. Others lie in heavy loops between trunks, covered with small rooted plants and pale, motionless flowers. No flower faces upward. They open toward bark, toward water, toward the warm shelter beneath another leaf. Their scents are dense and separate: pepper, overripe sweetness, bitter sap, something like the inside of a freshly opened shell.

Water is near rather than abundant. It does not command the landscape. There are no great rivers, no broad lake, no waterfall to gather everything into a single noise. Instead there are pools under root fans, wet seams in the stone, narrow channels that disappear beneath vegetation and emerge a few paces away. You can cross a long stretch on apparently dry ground and then feel it yield under your foot, revealing a thin sheet of water just below the surface.

Along the edges of the pools, pale mineral matter rises in soft rims. The rims have no chips or stains of age. They look newly exuded. Tiny plants hold to them with transparent roots, and small shelled creatures graze there, scraping the surface with movements too slight to see. Yet the rims never grow higher. The stone remains at this first stage of exposure, the soil at its first depth. Rain falls often enough to keep the leaves washed, but has never cut a gully.

There are animals wherever there is room for an animal, and in many places where there seems to be none.

A fold in a leaf opens one wet eye. Bark shifts, and a dozen flattened bodies separate from it. Something narrow passes through a vine’s hollow stem, visible for a moment as a swelling that travels upward. The water is crowded with translucent swimmers, broad-headed larvae, small dark fish, and creatures that walk upside down on its underside. Their shadows overlap on the pool bottoms until the sediment seems patterned, though it is bare.

Larger animals move with surprising care. Heavy, blunt-faced browsers ease between the stalks, placing their feet in the same soft hollows again and again. Their hides carry a faint green dust. They strip leaves slowly, chewing while insects settle around their mouths. Long-bodied climbers lie across several branches at once, their limbs dangling into different levels of the canopy. Here and there a dark back breaks the surface of a pool. The animal beneath it exhales, making a low bubbling sound, and sinks without leaving a wake that travels far.

The air is thick with wings. Most of the fliers stay close to leaves, making short journeys between feeding places. Their passage produces a dry ticking, oddly crisp in all the moisture. Larger wings beat above them with a sound like wet cloth shaken once. Occasionally a pale creature drops almost vertically from the canopy, catches itself a hand’s breadth above the ground, and climbs back through the same opening.

There is little empty silence. The sound is layered: scraping, clicking, throat pulses, wingbeats, the hollow knock of animals calling from inside stems. Beneath these runs a deep, uneven breathing from the pools. Individual calls are easy to hear nearby; farther away they join into a coarse, steady murmur. A sudden cry does not stop the whole landscape. Only the creatures closest to it pause. Beyond them, feeding and calling continue.

The strangeness becomes apparent slowly. You might first mistake it for the repetition natural to a crowded place. The same browser passes the same root. A drop gathers again at the tip of a leaf. A low call returns from the distant basin.

But a fallen branch never appears. There is no accumulating bed of dead wood. The soft stone does not harden, and the translucent skin on it does not dry. Where a mouth takes a leaf, another occupies the opening before the absence has become part of the view. The animals feed, hunt, sleep, and wake; nothing is motionless. Still, no clearing widens, no pool silts up, no tree rises above its neighbors.

Even the heat has no arrival or departure. It is not the heat of noon, nor heat left over from a vanished day. It belongs to the place as the smooth ground does.

At the edge of one shallow basin, roots enter the water without rippling it. Between them, a young shoot stands tightly furled. Its tip is pale and tender. Nearby, a small animal waits with its mouth against the stem, breathing through nostrils just above the surface.

The shoot is always about to open.

The animal is always well fed.

Beyond them, under the dim cloud, the low country continues: wet at its seams, crowded to the last available surface, still coming into being, with no need to become anything else.
