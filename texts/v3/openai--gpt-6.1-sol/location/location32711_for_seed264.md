The basin is shallow enough that you can see its far rim through the warm haze. It lies several miles away: a low, blunt rise, scarcely a hill, with long slopes worn into the color and texture of old bone. There are no sharp summits. Even the larger outcrops have settled into broad backs and rounded shoulders, separated by expanses of pale gravel.

From a distance, the floor looks dry. Close to the ground, it is not.

Moisture holds underneath the stones, in the shaded sides of cracks, along the lower edges of mineral shelves. Lift a flat piece of shale and its underside is dark and slick. The exposed surface is warm enough to dry your palm. Between the two is the particular smell of this place: heated stone, wet iron, and a faint saline bitterness.

There are no plants. No roots pry at the seams; no grass softens the margins of the water. Where the basin ought to be green, it is simply darker. Damp gravel meets dry gravel without an intervening fringe of life.

A few channels cross the open floor. They are wide and very shallow, their banks rounded until it is difficult to say where the channel begins. Water occupies only narrow portions of them, lying in long silver strips between ribs of exposed limestone. Some strips are no wider than a foot. Others broaden into pools, clear at their edges and smoky blue where the bottom drops below a mineral lip.

The water is warm. It leaves a faint metallic taste in the air above it.

You hear the basin before you understand anything about it.

There is a deep, continuous roar, louder than wind but without wind’s irregularity. Over it runs a thinner grinding note, and above that a high electrical whine which seems to come from the bare spaces between the rocks. None of these sounds rises or falls. Walking changes their proportions. Near a watercourse, the low note presses against your ribs. Beside certain outcrops, the high note becomes almost painful. There are places where two sounds cancel one another and you can hear the small scrape of your own shoes.

Then you step away, and the roar returns.

Nothing has been built here. There are no walls, foundations, cables, abandoned machines. The industrial quality comes from the ground itself.

Under the basin, warm brine moves through a deep, continuous system of mineral passages. Heat, pressure, and the conductivity of the dissolved salts maintain its circulation. The passages have been worn to steady dimensions over an immense period. Where fluid passes through a constriction, the rock vibrates. Where mineral layers hold an electrical difference across a thin wet seam, they sing.

It is a landscape operating at capacity.

The exposed stones bear signs of that operation. Some have fine parallel grooves that resemble tool marks until you follow them around an entire boulder and see how the grain of the rock determines every turn. Others have shallow circular hollows, their interiors polished by water and suspended grit. Thin sheets of black, iron-rich stone lie between pale layers. At broken edges they look like laminated machine parts, but they continue into the ground, following folds much older than anything that might have been assembled.

Everything capable of wearing away has already found the rate at which it can remain.

There is very little loose dust. The gravel fits closely together, each piece resting in a depression just sufficient to hold it. The channels have no fresh banks. No fallen rock waits beneath the outcrops. Even the small mineral crusts around the pools are smooth and durable, their edges thickened rather than brittle.

The light is ordinary enough to make the strangeness difficult to dismiss. It comes from a pale sun in a lightly hazed sky. Shadows fall cleanly beneath ledges. Wet surfaces shine. Beyond the basin, the low rim is softened by distance, exactly as a distant ridge should be.

But the shadows stay where they are.

The warmth does not accumulate. Water does not recede from the shallows. Moisture on the shaded stone remains a film of the same thickness, although the dry faces continue to give off heat. The basin has reached a condition in which its losses and returns meet so precisely that there is no visible drift toward anything else.

Its immense noise is not the noise of an event. It is a condition, like its elevation or its color.

At first, the few animals are easy to overlook. They are low, grey creatures with broad feet and short bodies, resting in the narrow damp spaces under mineral shelves. Their backs have the mottled appearance of the gravel. No more than two or three occupy any one stretch of channel.

A pale crust grows beneath them: perhaps microbial, perhaps something simpler, sustained by the chemical difference between the wet seam and the heated stone. It has no stems, leaves, or softness. At its thickest it resembles a dusting of porcelain.

The animals’ mouths rest against it. Their feeding is too slight to distinguish from stillness. You can watch one for a long time without seeing it change position. Only the damp impression of its feet identifies it as something other than another stone.

Farther into the basin, the outcrops become less frequent. There is an open reach where the channels spread into thin, intersecting lines, and the sound becomes extraordinarily even. No one rock seems to produce it. The air is warm around your ankles and slightly cooler at your face. You can see a mile in every direction without seeing anything tall enough to hide a person.

Here, a hand-sized pool lies in a hollow of black stone. Its surface is perfectly level despite the force of the sound. A small patch of sky sits in it.

You crouch beside it. The stone transmits a strong vibration through your fingertips, steady and finely textured. There is enough power in it to suggest pumps, turbines, whole halls of machinery concealed below your hand.

Across the basin, the rounded rocks hold their places. The narrow waters remain bright. Nothing approaches, shuts down, begins to fail, or finishes its work.

You are the only thing here that has arrived.
