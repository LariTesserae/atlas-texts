Beneath the lowest stair of the old baths, behind a limestone slab too heavy to lift and too small to interest a mason, there is a city.

It occupies the space left by a dissolved knot of softer rock. From wall to wall, the chamber is no wider than a person’s reach. Its ceiling almost touches the tallest roofs. A crack at the back admits water; another, beneath the threshold, lets exactly as much escape. Between them, the city has settled into a wetness that neither rises nor falls.

The light comes through the stair joints. It is gray, without a visible source, and sufficient to distinguish a ledge from the darkness underneath it. There are places where it catches on droplets and makes them look like holes pierced through the stone into a brighter room. Elsewhere it fails completely. Entire districts are known by their sounds.

The first impression is of crowding. Little buildings climb over one another toward the ceiling, their walls assembled from flakes of limestone and squared chips of broken tile. Some have been mortared with a pale paste; others depend on careful stacking. A house may be the size of a thumbnail, with three rooms, a cistern, and a passage beneath the floor through which its neighbor’s wastewater travels. Doorways are low even for the inhabitants. No one has space for a straight street.

The streets turn sideways, become stairs, squeeze through holes in load-bearing walls. They cross the water on slabs so thin that the water darkens them from below. In several places the paving has been worn into shallow channels, and the channels have acquired their own names and rights of passage. A pedestrian must wait while a trickle passes, though it never finishes passing. The proper crossing is a projecting pebble polished by generations of feet.

Nothing grows here.

There is no moss on the steps, no green at the mouth of the crack, no roots probing down from above. Even the black films along the drains are mineral: manganese laid down in dull, soft-edged bands. The chamber smells of wet chalk, cold clay, and the metallic tang of water that has traveled through a long depth of stone. Its white deposits resemble folded cloth. Its brown deposits resemble old wounds. Neither is alive.

Nevertheless, the place is densely occupied.

Most of its inhabitants remain indoors. Through an open doorway one might see a pale hand drawing a thread through a drilled bead, or two figures lifting a stone basin between them. They have bodies suited to narrowness: small shoulders, flat ribs, fingers that can hook into a mortar seam. Their clothing is made of thin scraped membrane. It hangs stiffly in the damp. They do not seem troubled by the cold.

Their tools are simple and well worn. Chipped flint for cutting. Bone wedges. Hammers made from pebbles bound into forked handles. A mason working in an upper lane braces both feet against the opposite house and taps a wall with a hammer no larger than a grain of rice. The tapping is almost lost in the noise.

For the city is loud.

Water strikes a succession of hollow stones beneath the central stairway. Each stone gives a different note, and the notes overlap into a continuous clattering roar. Narrow drains hiss. A sluice under the eastern quarter makes a deep, breathy sound far out of proportion to its size. Roofs tremble in it. Loose crockery has long since been packed with clay or discarded.

There is nowhere quiet enough to hear a whisper. The inhabitants speak close to one another’s ears, or communicate by touching the wrist. Public notices are carved into the soft walls. At crossings, a person announces their presence by striking the pavement with a heel; the vibration travels through the little bridge more reliably than a call travels through the air.

The loudest place is also the civic center.

Here a single drop, continuously forming and continuously falling, lands in a cup cut into the floor. Its impact fills the square with a hard, bright crack. Around the cup stand the council benches, the measuring stones, and a low building whose lintel has sagged but never fallen. The overflow leaves by seven notches. No notch may be deepened. No object may be placed inside the cup. These are not ceremonial prohibitions. A grain caught in the wrong notch can send water down a street whose houses have no upper doors.

Much of the city’s law concerns such small obstructions.

A person may own a wall but not the seepage through it. A roof must leave clearance for the ceiling droplets. A stair cannot be built across a mineral seam until its dampness has been observed and agreed upon. The oldest buildings bear shallow grooves where earlier owners tried to divert water and were required to undo their work.

There are few animals. In a cavity behind the council house lives a translucent creature with six legs and a broad, blunt head. It grazes on something in the water too fine to see. Occasionally it crosses the square, slowly, its feet slipping on the polished stone. Everyone makes room. It is larger than a cart, though still small enough to disappear beneath a human fingernail.

Farther in, under a bridge, a single shell is visible in a pool. The shell has not moved within anyone’s memory. A feeler extends from it into the current. Whether this is an animal resting or the last functioning part of an animal no longer alive is a matter on which the city has no settled opinion.

The pools are shallow and very clear. Looking into them, one sees inverted streets between the submerged gravel: another crowded arrangement of walls and arches, assembled by reflections. At some angles it is difficult to tell which passage is dry. Children are taught to touch a surface before putting their weight on it.

There is no weather here, although the whole place looks as if rain has just stopped.

Every threshold is dark with moisture. Every roof bears a bead of water. The air is cool enough that an outsider’s breath would briefly cloud the square. Yet the deposits on the walls no longer thicken. The worn steps no longer wear lower. Water brings in a little dissolved stone and takes away the same amount. Repairs use fragments already present in the chamber; nothing is added, nothing carried off.

Even the damage has become dependable. The cracked arch over the western channel is held in place by the pressure of the houses built against it. A tilted chimney drains better than a straight one would. Behind a fallen lintel lies a dry recess used for storing flint, and no one would consider clearing it.

From above, the city is invisible. The bath stair has a damp joint and one chipped corner. A person descending it might hear a muffled rush and suppose there was a pipe behind the wall.

Under the slab, the rush fills every room. Water passes under the benches, a mason keeps tapping, and a bead hangs from the ceiling just above the highest roof. It falls. Another takes its place.
