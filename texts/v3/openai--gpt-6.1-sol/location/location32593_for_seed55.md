The first thing visible is not the bottom but a pale seam in the water: the rounded edge of a wall, appearing out of darkness and continuing beyond sight in both directions. Silt has gathered along its crown. Where the current has brushed the silt away, the stone is smooth enough to reflect the little light that reaches it.

There is water above it, water beside it, water in every opening beneath it. The place has been flooded so long that it no longer looks like a place into which water came. Its passages seem built for this stillness. Its stairs descend through the cold without offering any promise of dry ground.

The bottom, when it becomes visible, is paved.

Broad slabs lie under a thin, undisturbed deposit. Their joints are almost lost, but occasionally a root follows one, revealing a line straighter than anything else nearby. Beyond the pavement is a terrace; beyond that, another wall. The terraces descend by shallow increments, so that the basin is very wide without becoming abruptly deep. A traveler might cross many miles of floor before finding water appreciably darker than where they began.

Nothing rises to a peak. The high places are low embankments, the crests of old retaining walls, the upper edges of courts. All have been worn down to soft profiles. The steps are hollowed in the middle, their corners gone. Blocks that once fitted closely now lean against one another through cushions of sediment and roots. Some walls have subsided until only their uppermost courses remain exposed, resembling long strips of stone laid flat across the floor.

Even here, beneath the settled mud, there is no unworked ground. A broken pavement exposes rubble packing. Beneath the rubble is clay, pressed in layers. A collapsed channel reveals a deeper channel, its sides faced with smaller stones. The basin was made by stacking, filling, cutting, and carrying. Its smallest pieces are things a person could have lifted. Its largest structures are those pieces repeated over distances too great to see.

The marks of that labor survive in sheltered places. There are shallow chisel grooves beneath overhangs, thumb-sized hollows in fired clay, uneven rims on drainage pipes shaped by hand. A few blocks bear wedge holes. No surface suggests a machine. The work varies slightly from course to course: one section laid with narrow joints, another with thick beds of mortar, a third made from salvaged pieces whose old sockets face uselessly into the water.

What gives the place its scale is the persistence of it. A corridor opens into a court, the court into an avenue, the avenue into another system of terraces. There are storage chambers in the embankments and broad spillways between basins. Low bridges cross channels whose beds have almost filled. Seen from above, through water clear enough for only the nearest features to register, the construction disappears outward in bands. It has the extent of a landscape, but everywhere within reach is evidence of hands.

The water is cold throughout. In the upper reaches it has a faint gray illumination, less a view of the sky than an easing of the darkness. Far above, floating growth and the suspended remains of generations of plants absorb most of the daylight. Under that cover there are no sharp shadows. Edges emerge gradually, and distance is easily mistaken for opacity.

Vegetation occupies almost every surface that receives this weak light.

Dark water moss lies over the parapets in thick, sodden folds. Long ribbons of submerged leaves rise from the sediment, extending toward the brightness and then bending back across neighboring plants. The shallower terraces hold dense stands of narrow stems. Their lower portions are brown and crowded with older growth; only the tips carry the muted green of active leaves.

It is lush growth, but cold and slow. A leaf may remain in place through several seasons. Old stems take a long time to soften. Living shoots grow through them, and the beds become layered tangles in which it is difficult to distinguish this year’s growth from the remnants beneath it. Some chambers are entirely filled with plants, from the floor to the underside of the vaulted ceiling. At their entrances, leaves press close enough together to conceal the opening.

On the upper walls, algae form velvety sheets. Where a little more light enters through a collapsed roof, they spread over every broken tile and mortar fragment. Those patches are the brightest things here, though their color would look nearly black in daylight.

Deeper down, the green dwindles. Plants trail over the last ledges into water too dark to sustain new leaves. Below them, pale films grow on decaying wood and the slow accumulation of fallen stems. The floors are covered by the remains of the gardens overhead. Material descends a little at a time, a leaf fragment turning as it sinks, a thread of root settling across an old threshold. Nothing quickly clears it away.

There are no fish moving between the stems. No small bodies flicker over the silt. The green corridors remain empty even where they seem to offer perfect shelter. A leaf may tremble, but the cause is a current passing through a distant breach. When the mud lifts in a soft cloud, it is because a weakened joint has yielded somewhere nearby.

The absence of animals makes the growth unusually continuous. Leaves keep their edges. Root mats spread without being burrowed through. Fallen matter stays where it lands until water, decay, or the pressure of more matter alters it. In places, the sediment has preserved the fine arrangement of a whole collapsed plant, its branches laid across the paving like a drawing.

Sound travels through the basin, but there is little to carry.

Occasionally a small stone shifts inside a wall. The click arrives with surprising clarity, followed by a faint scrape and then nothing. Water moving through a narrow clay pipe makes a low, uneven murmur. It may continue for hours. In a chamber with a trapped pocket of air beneath the ceiling, a bubble sometimes collects enough volume to rise, touch the stone, and break with a dull tap.

Most of the time there is only the pressure of the water and the silence of objects resting in it.

The broadest currents are almost imperceptible. They follow the old channels, passing through gates whose leaves have long since rotted away, crossing courts, and returning beneath low bridges. Slight differences in temperature draw water from one basin into another. Those movements carry the finest silt and make slow distinctions across the plain: clean stone on one side of an avenue, deep powdery sediment on the other; upright stems in one enclosure, trailing growth in the next.

Over long periods, the basin develops regions of its own. Some courts remain relatively clear because their water is always moving. Others accumulate dark, leaf-rich mud until the lowest steps disappear. A narrow opening may keep a garden alive behind an otherwise intact wall. When that opening closes with roots or fallen masonry, the leaves gradually thin, and the room becomes another store of pale stems in darkness.

The construction still determines almost everything, though none of it works as it once did. Embankments shelter quiet water. Drainage trenches guide roots. Storage vessels, half buried and broken, hold pockets of rich sediment. The shallowest roofs support dense meadows of submerged growth; under those roofs are empty rooms, colder and darker, with the occasional root entering through a crack.

At the edge of a large court, a stairway survives nearly whole. Its lowest steps have vanished into mud. The upper ones are covered by a thick mat of fine leaves, each moving slightly, each attached somewhere out of sight. Beside it stands a wall with a broad opening worn smooth around the sides. Through the opening is another court, then a low colonnade, then the faint line of a further embankment.

The view ends there, but the place does not.

Beyond sight are more pavements under sediment, more channels carrying cold water, more gardens growing in the small amount of light left to them. The walls settle slowly. Mortar loosens grain by grain. Roots enter joints without forcing them open very far. Where a roof has fallen, its stones already lie comfortably among the leaves.

Nothing in the basin is untouched, and almost nothing is still being made. It remains vast and inhabited by growth, its hand-laid surfaces wearing down beneath water that seldom has reason to move.
