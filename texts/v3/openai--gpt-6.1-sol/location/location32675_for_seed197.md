At first the place appears to be a courtyard: a pale floor, a ring of high walls, open sky. Warm daylight reaches almost every surface. There is no furniture, no rubble, no object left in the middle to give the eye a measure of distance. A person could cross it in a minute.

Then the floor takes a breath.

It rises only slightly, enough to lift the loose cuffs of your trousers, and settles again. Along the walls, a few vertical seams open and close. What looked like plaster is the underside of bark. What looked like paving is a single broad leaf, worn smooth by centuries of feet.

The whole district is alive.

It occupies a shallow hollow scarcely larger than a hamlet. Around its edge, the roofs bend down into the surrounding ground, so that there is no clear outer wall. Beyond them lies a low, sunlit plain of tightly folded leaves. Here and there a stalk rises, leans toward the town, and sinks into it. No hills interrupt the horizon. The air is warm without being stifling, and smells of peeled stems, old pollen, and something sweet that has begun to ferment.

Inside, there is almost nothing.

The streets are wide. The chambers are wider. Long galleries run between inhabited walls, their centers entirely vacant. The living material has withdrawn to the margins, becoming floor, ceiling, lintel, stair. It leaves an extraordinary amount of air between itself and itself. From one doorway you can see through seven others, each framing the same empty brightness, though the last doorway should be outside the district.

These passages were grown to a plan, or learned a plan through use. There are drainage channels, shaded arcades, raised thresholds, broad public basins, small recesses placed at intervals where someone might sit. Roots are braided into arches. Tough fibers make handrails along steps that climb to rooms only a body’s height above the ground. The oldest routes have deep, comfortable curves: generations of traffic have worn them, and the plant has thickened around the wear rather than repairing it.

Some doors retain the shape of shoulders that no longer pass through them.

Nothing here has been cut. A stair is a row of swollen joints. A window is a patch where the wall stopped growing. The covered market consists of six branching stems supporting a roof of fused leaves, with shallow bowls along the sides in which fruit ripens. Most of the bowls are empty. One holds a single brown seed as large as a sleeping dog.

There are no tools, signs, lamps, or fastenings. Even the few loose things—the seed, a fallen husk, a curled shaving of bark—seem temporary. By evening they may have rooted, or dissolved, or become a part of the pavement.

The town is deafening.

You notice this before you understand what you are seeing, but the sound has no single source. It fills the courtyards with the force of heavy surf. A harsh, continuous rasp runs through it, joined by whistles, wet snapping noises, deep booms from below, and thousands of small impacts like hard rain striking a roof.

There is no rain.

The animals are mostly inside the walls.

Insects crowd the thin spaces between layers of bark. Pale, many-jointed creatures move through translucent veins overhead. Small frogs cling upside down in ventilation chambers, swelling their throats until whole sections of ceiling tremble. Beetles with round, polished backs drum against hollow stems. Something much larger travels underneath the floor; its passage pushes a slow ridge across the courtyard and makes the leaf-paving tighten beneath your feet.

At intervals, a wall opens a hand’s breadth and spills out a stream of animals. They cross a threshold, climb another wall, and vanish. For a few seconds the empty street is full of legs, wings, and slick bodies. Then it is vacant again.

The abundance is folded into the substance of the place. Every apparently solid pillar contains chambers. Every chamber has ledges, nests, burrows, and narrow routes that never touch the public streets. Predators wait behind little doors made of their own backs. Worms graze the inner surfaces of leaves. Blind lizards follow the warm ducts carrying sap.

The noise serves them. Some creatures navigate by the echoes of their calls; others hunt by the momentary gaps when prey stops calling. A sudden silence would be dangerous. When a patch of wall goes quiet, the neighboring animals begin to hammer it, and usually, after a moment, something inside answers.

Water is scarce enough to be gathered carefully, but not scarce enough to make the place brittle. The plant stores it deep in its lower tissues. At dawn, condensation beads beneath the roofs and moves along fine grooves toward the public basins. There are no open streams. A little water appears on a ledge, disappears through a pore, and emerges hours later in another room.

The basins are usually only damp. Their bottoms are dark green, soft underfoot, and patterned with the tracks of drinking animals. In one shaded chamber a pool no larger than a table holds several floating leaves and an astonishing number of eyes.

Occasional rain changes everything. The roof loosens, opening narrow slits to admit it. Gutters unroll along the arcades. The floor slopes toward the channels, and the district drinks with a loud, sustained sucking sound. Animals emerge to wash or feed; tiny predators take stations beside the fresh rivulets. Before the clouds have passed, most of the water is gone from sight.

Afterward, the streets smell clean and bruised.

This has been happening for a very long time. The lower walls are rounded by weather and traffic. Old scars have become smooth cavities. Some roots are worn nearly through, yet remain alive because newer roots have grown around them and taken their weight. In the driest court, the paving bears the faint circular marks of raindrops that fell before the surrounding rooms existed.

The district remembers damage in its shape. A broken arch does not grow back into an arch. It becomes two curved benches, or a doorway into a smaller chamber, or a place where animals gather because sunlight reaches the floor. Decay is slow and busy. A dead section of roof may remain overhead for decades while moss and small biting creatures hollow it into lace.

Space is less reliable.

A passage may be short when you enter and long when you look back. Rooms share corners without sharing walls. You can hear animals moving in a chamber visible through a window, yet putting your head through the window reveals only the outer plain. The chamber remains in view when you withdraw.

These irregularities are well used. Frogs call from one courtyard and catch insects in another without moving. A vine passes through a wall and flowers on both sides at different times of day. Some burrows open into yesterday’s rain. Their occupants come out wet even during a dry week, trailing mud that slowly fades before it reaches the main street.

You learn to follow worn paths. The animals have worn the dependable ones.

Near the center stands a little square with four entrances and no roof. It is the most ordinary-looking place in the district. Light lies plainly on its green floor. The walls are low enough to see over. Nothing crosses it.

Here the noise is so intense that you feel it in your teeth. Beneath the square, something enormous is chewing steadily. The walls shed pollen at each bite. Birds gather along their upper edges, wings held out, letting the vibration shake parasites from their feathers.

At midday the four entrances contract. The square grows smaller by the width of a foot. Somewhere beyond it, an empty room gains that much space.

The birds do not stir. The chewing continues.

Later, as the light softens, the floors cool and the roofs begin collecting moisture. A clear drop forms beneath each leaf-tip. The animals shift toward the warmer inner walls, and their calls deepen. Doorways swell slightly around their edges. In the market, the large brown seed splits with a crack that carries through every gallery.

A pale root slips out and touches the floor.

By morning there will be a new obstruction to walk around. Within a season, perhaps, a pillar. In time the street will bend to accommodate it, and the rooms on either side will grow a little farther apart. The district has space for it. It has always been making space.
