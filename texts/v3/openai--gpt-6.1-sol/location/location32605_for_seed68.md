The place occupies one city block, though from inside it seems less like a block than a shallow basin left behind when the buildings withdrew.

Three sides are brick. The fourth is a railway embankment, low enough that the roofs of passing freight cars remain visible above it. There are gates, but none stands directly opposite another. To cross the enclosure you must change direction twice, walking around a square engine house and then along the edge of a planted trench. Someone planned these detours. The paving is too regular, the corners too carefully reinforced, for the arrangement to be accidental.

Most of the ground is open. Pale concrete extends between the buildings in broad, unoccupied reaches, with a few rails laid flush into it. Here and there the surface has lifted into ridges. These are recent: their edges are sharp, their exposed interiors clean, the little stones inside them still pale enough to look freshly broken. Along one ridge, an iron drainage cover has tilted upright without coming loose. Grass grows behind it as if behind a small windbreak.

The block is called the settling yard.

It is not abandoned. At seven each morning a worker unlocks the engine house, and at seven each evening another worker locks it. Between those hours, and often through the night, a machine inside turns with an immense, regular knocking. Its purpose is to keep the ground still.

The ground does not quite oblige.

***

There is more green here than the first view suggests.

From the gate, you see expanses of concrete, dark windows, the rectangular engine house, and perhaps six trees. The trees are widely spaced. None is especially tall. Their crowns do not meet, and there is room to walk around each trunk without entering another tree’s shade.

Then you notice the lower growth.

Every building has a broad bed at its foot, raised in places by the pressure of roots. Ferns crowd beneath loading platforms. Thick-leaved plants occupy the shadows of staircases. A vine has filled the space between two drainpipes so completely that it resembles a third pipe, much wider and less straight. On the northern wall, vegetation climbs only as high as the old water stain, making a dark green strip around the yard.

The planted trench is the richest part. It follows a buried water line from the engine house to the railway embankment, bending around foundations rather than taking the shortest route. Its banks are steep and new. Someone cut through layers of paving, rubble, and black industrial soil, leaving the strata visible. The topmost concrete still has paint on it: a yellow stripe, interrupted midway down the trench wall.

At the bottom there is usually no visible water.

There are, instead, enormous leaves.

They overlap in cool, matte layers, holding their stems low over the ground. Small ferns grow beneath them, and beneath the ferns is a soft tangle of moss, pale roots, and the discarded shells of snails. A person leaning over the trench gets a smell of wet iron and crushed stalks. The rest of the yard may smell only of stone.

Water comes intermittently. A short rain beads on the pavement and disappears down narrow cuts. On some mornings, condensation gathers beneath the steel walkways and falls for an hour after the air elsewhere has dried. The trench keeps these small arrivals. Even late in a dry week, its deepest leaves remain cool to the touch.

***

Daylight is ordinary here, and plentiful.

There is no permanent mist and no roof over the yard. The sky opens generously between the buildings. On clear days, the light reveals everything: rust scales along the rails, individual feathers caught against a grille, the fine dust on an unused winch. White clouds pass over the square opening with no apparent interest in what lies below.

Yet it is usually cool. The walls hold the previous night into the afternoon. Under the loading platforms, the air feels several hours behind the weather.

Sun-loving plants occupy the middle of the yard in separate beds, each surrounded by a considerable emptiness. There are tall grasses, flowering stalks, small fruiting shrubs. Their abundance is local and concentrated. A bed can be so thick with leaves that nothing beneath them is visible, while ten paces away a dropped washer lies alone on an expanse of clean pavement.

People have kept these intervals clear. The maintenance carts need space to turn, and the workers need to see where the surface is lifting. Around each tree is a painted circle. When roots push through it, someone paints a larger one.

***

The yard is loud.

The engine’s knocking passes through the soles of your shoes. An extractor mounted high on the eastern wall adds a continuous, rough-edged roar. Pipes click as they warm. Several times a day, a train crosses the embankment, and the wheels strike the joints with such force that the engine briefly seems quiet.

Animals live inside all of this.

Sparrows nest in the ventilator housings. Starlings work the open ground, walking in quick, purposeful lines before gathering at the trench. They know which areas remain shaded longest. In summer, bees make the flowering beds hum, though you hear them only when you put your face near the blossoms.

There are beetles under the loose edges of paving, woodlice beneath the steps, and snails in numbers that seem extravagant for a place with so little open water. Their trails show best in the morning, crossing the rails and curving around bolts. A small brown frog lives beneath the crooked drainage cover. Workers have seen it there for years, or have seen successive frogs and chosen not to distinguish them.

Near dusk, bats emerge from a gap behind the engine house’s clock. The clock has no hands. Its face is still regularly washed.

The animals do not wait for quiet. Birds feed within a few feet of the knocking wall. Young sparrows learn the engine’s rhythm before they learn the sound of rain. When a train passes, they crouch slightly, then resume.

***

Everything moves slowly except the things built to move.

Belts turn fast inside the engine house. Freight cars cross in a rush. The extractor’s blades are invisible behind their grille. But the important changes in the yard happen by fractions.

A ridge rises the thickness of a coin over winter. A tree leans enough for a new support to be installed. Water darkens a patch of soil that was dry last month. The workers measure these changes with rulers, string, and a brass level kept in a padded box. Their instruments are small beside the machinery.

The present surface is only eleven years old. Before that, the block was almost entirely roofed: workshops, coal stores, sheds built against sheds. When they were removed, the buried ground began to swell. Some blame the old fill taking up moisture. Some blame roots cut off for decades from light and space. In the engine house there are diagrams supporting both explanations, pinned to opposite walls.

The machine was installed after the first major uplift. It drives heavy plates downward through shafts beneath the floor. Each stroke presses a different part of the block. You can follow its progress by the water trembling in the few shallow puddles.

It has reduced the movement. The railway line remains level. No wall has fallen.

Still, the trench gets deeper without anyone digging it.

***

There is one peculiarity the workers discuss only with newcomers.

After the engine stops, its knocking continues in certain parts of the yard.

Not loudly. Beneath the northern staircase you can hear perhaps six or seven more strokes. Beside the largest tree, the sound lasts longer, softened and irregular, as though heard through several closed doors. The foreman says it is vibration passing through the foundations. The gardener says the roots carry it.

You can test it by staying after the evening shift.

The extractor winds down first. Its roar thins to a whine, then a metallic rubbing, then nothing. The engine delivers its last blows. Birds call across the cleared space. Somewhere on the embankment, ballast slips beneath a small animal’s feet.

Under the tree, the delayed knocking continues.

With the machinery silent, other details become audible: drops falling from the underside of a walkway, leaves scraping the brick, a snail shell displaced by a foraging bird. The yard has not become still. Its quieter inhabitants have merely become possible to hear.

The worker closes the engine-house door and checks the trench before leaving. Usually there is nothing to report. The leaves stand thickly in the cooling air. The new ridges retain the day’s light along their pale edges. Between them, the pavement lies open and almost empty.

Then the gate is locked, and the block is left overnight to rise a little.
