The high basin is bright enough to make you narrow your eyes, even under cloud. Light comes from the sky and from the ground: from water held in moss, from wet leaves turned upward, from the pale undersides of grasses bending in the wind. The clouds are thin here. Behind them the sun remains a white, unmistakable presence, and when a gap opens, the whole basin flashes.

There is almost nowhere to stand that is not alive.

The slopes wear a continuous skin of low vegetation, thick enough to conceal their stones: moss over peat, sedge rooted through moss, small heaths spreading between the sedges. In the wetter hollows, leaves overlap until the water beneath them disappears. On the rises, tight cushions of alpine plants hold droplets in their seams. The few exposed boulders have green caps and green sides; only their overhanging bellies remain bare. From a distance the land looks smooth. Up close it is a difficult surface of hummocks, soft pits, springy ridges, and stems that catch at the ankles.

Trees are scarce. A few mountain birches stand far apart on sheltered knolls, bent in the same direction, with ample daylight between them. Each has a low skirt of old branches and a narrow crown of new leaves. Around them the basin opens for miles, climbing gently toward a broken rim of higher country. Its vegetation is nearly complete, but its occupants are few. You can look across a whole slope without finding a bird, a roof, or anything taller than your knee.

The ground is young.

Until recently, in the long reckoning of a mountain, ice occupied much of this basin. It left mounds of rubble, shallow depressions, and ridges too irregular to be called hills. The plants have covered these before weather has had time to smooth them. Beneath a lush-looking bank there may be loose gravel that has never settled properly. A round green knoll may contain one enormous stone lodged on smaller stones, with cold air moving through the gaps.

The streams are still finding their beds. One slips sideways through a sedge meadow, vanishes under a floating mat of roots, then returns thirty yards downhill through several separate mouths. Another has begun to cut a narrow channel, exposing clean sand and angular fragments beneath the vegetation. Where the water undercuts a bank, the turf hangs intact for a while, a little green roof over the new hollow.

Water is usually close to the surface. A boot pressed into firm-looking moss darkens the ground around it. A footprint fills slowly, clear at first, then amber as it draws water from the peat. Even on the steeper slopes there are wet seams. They shine in the sun and continue across the hillside without any obvious spring at their head.

The rain has stopped, but the basin has not finished shedding it. Every grass tip carries a drop. The birches drip steadily. Mist lies in the channels and lifts in loose scraps when the wind passes. The air is cool enough that bare fingers soon become stiff, especially after touching the saturated ground. In sunshine, the smell is unexpectedly strong: wet leaves, bruised heath, peat, and the faint mineral smell of water running over fresh stone.

The noise reaches everywhere.

At the lower lip of the basin, a broad spillway discharges toward a valley hidden below. You hear it long before you can identify its shape. The sound is deep and continuous, with a harsher rushing layer above it. Across the open ground it seems to have no single source. Close to the lip, it becomes difficult to speak to someone standing beside you.

Only a little of the machinery is visible. A low concrete intake building sits beside a dark pool, its flat roof bright with accumulated water. Heavy grilles descend into the pool. Weed strands gather against them, quivering in the current. Beyond the building, three large pipes run down the slope on short supports before disappearing over the edge. Their paint is pale and weathered, their joints darker with moisture.

There are no houses around the installation. A narrow service road reaches it along the firmer ground, two gravel tracks divided by sedge. In places the tracks have sunk below the surrounding turf and become channels themselves. A few poles carry power across the basin. They are widely spaced, and their wires are almost lost against the bright sky.

Beside the intake is a steel shed with one window, a locked door, and a ventilation fan turning behind a square mesh. The fan makes a sharp mechanical note that can be heard only when you stand close enough to put a hand on the wall. Farther away, the spillway swallows it.

The plant cover climbs right up to these structures. Moss fills the seams around the concrete apron. Small ferns grow beneath the pipe supports, sheltered from the wind but constantly wetted by spray. The shed’s drain empties into a patch of unusually tall grass. A maintenance worker has cut it back along the door, leaving pale stalk ends that are already putting out new blades.

For all the force of the sound, there is little rapid movement to watch. Water passes through the intake in a dark, even glide. The hanging weed trembles without going anywhere. On the broad slopes, a gust travels through the sedges and subsides. Clouds change shape so gradually that the light seems steady until you notice that a distant ridge has become sharper.

Once, a small bird rises from the heath. It flies low for a short distance and drops out of sight again. Its call does not carry through the water’s roar.

Near the spillway, spray has made a permanent wet strip along the bank. Here the moss is especially deep and vivid, interrupted by little pools so clear that each stem beneath them can be counted. The pool surfaces shake with the vibration of falling water. Sunlight catches the moving ripples, throwing fine bright lines onto the undersides of leaves.

Beyond this strip, the basin remains broad and nearly empty. The birches stand on their separate knolls. The pipes descend out of view. New channels work quietly beneath the rooted surface, moving grains of sand a few inches at a time. Overhead, the cloud opens again, and for several minutes every wet part of the country shines.
