The first thing you notice is how much room there is beneath the ground.

Through a crack in the pale stone, you can see another ledge far below, and below that a dim arrangement of terraces, and below those nothing that your eyes can settle on. The air between them has a faint gray color. Distances remain visible long after details disappear. A shelf that looks close enough to reach by climbing may be several days away; a small dark mark on its edge may be a doorway or an entire district.

There is no bottom in view. Even the oldest fallen stones are still falling somewhere.

Up here, the cold is mild but absolute. It has no wetness in it, no promise of frost. It enters through cloth and stays against the skin. The stone is cooler than the air, except along certain narrow seams where it gives off a little warmth. These seams cross the terraces without regard for their edges, continuing on the far side of gaps as though the missing ground were still there.

The place resembles a city most strongly when you stop trying to find its inhabitants.

There are broad streets, courts, long ranks of chambers. There are stairs worn into shallow slopes, balconies overlooking enormous vacancies, passages that turn politely around spaces where nothing stands. All of it is made from the same fine-grained mineral: gray at a distance, close up the color of old bone with a trace of violet. No block rests on another. There are no joints, fittings, tool marks, or separate materials. Walls emerge from floors as smoothly as the sides of a hollow in a rock.

Yet the arrangement is unmistakably civic. Doorways face one another. Narrow lanes open into public squares. Along a high ridge, a hundred empty recesses are spaced with the regularity of market stalls.

You can follow a street for an hour without passing anything you would call a building. Its curb alone persists, a low lip bordering an immeasurable drop. Then a whole neighborhood appears: five walls, three roofless rooms, a stair climbing into empty air. Beyond it the street continues, perfectly level, on another ridge too far away to hear a shout.

Whatever makes these forms has no need to occupy the space between them.

The stone has been weathered for a very long time. Sharp corners survive only in sheltered interiors. Exposed surfaces are gently cupped, their hollows overlapping like thumbprints. Thin pillars have been worn into waists. Some walls are now translucent along the top, letting the dim sky shine through their mineral grains.

There is almost no loose rubble. The terraces are swept clean by winds too slight to move a sleeve but persistent enough, over centuries, to carry away powder. Dust streams from the edges in pale horizontal lines. It does not always descend. Some of it rises, gathering into slow processions above the streets, then spreads into high, nearly invisible veils.

Those veils account for much of the light.

The sky has no obvious source of illumination. Its brightness is distributed unevenly, with broad areas of pearl gray fading into deep violet. At intervals, a lighter region travels overhead. Shadows soften beneath it, then recover. It brings no warmth. There are places where the shadow of a wall remains on the ground after the wall itself has worn away, and others where a shadow arrives a few moments before whatever casts it.

The living things are easiest to find at the warm seams.

A dark, brittle film occupies a few sheltered cracks. It is so thin that it looks like a change in the stone’s color. Seen closely, its edge is fringed with little folded pockets. These open while the seam is warm and close as it cools. Over the course of several hours, the film advances by less than the width of a fingernail. It leaves behind a clean white surface, having taken something from the mineral that neither wind nor age could remove.

Nothing green grows here. Nothing reaches toward the sky.

Once in a great while, an animal passes.

It has a narrow body and six long limbs, with joints that fold in several directions. Its hide is dusty gray. It keeps close to the ground, testing the surface ahead with two limbs before moving the others. At an edge it pauses, lowers itself, and steps onto the underside of the terrace.

You can watch it through a gap as it continues upside down, unhurried. A moment later it steps onto another wall at right angles to the first. Its body does not swing or strain. It seems to carry a small, private agreement about which direction is down.

These creatures gather rarely, perhaps twice during a long passage of the brighter sky. Three or four meet in a sheltered court and press the flat sides of their heads against the floor. They remain there while warmth passes beneath them. Then each takes a different street. No call announces the meeting, and nothing visible is exchanged.

There are marks along the lower walls that may be their work: narrow pale bands at the same height above every floor, including floors on ceilings.

Much is happening, though you can stand still for an hour and hear almost none of it.

The terraces shift. Their movement is usually too slow to see, but a distant ridge can be in a different place when you look back. Entire districts pass one another across the gulf, sometimes drawing close enough for their streets to join. During these brief alignments, dust travels from one to the other. The small animals cross. Warm seams connect, and the dark films along them open all at once.

Then the districts separate.

Some connections last for days. Others close while you are walking toward them. A stair may end against a blank wall in the morning and overlook a descending avenue later on. The old wear on its steps suggests that this has happened many times.

At certain edges, the stone grows.

A faint line appears beyond a broken curb, holding its shape in the air. Grains of dust collect along it. By degrees it becomes solid enough to catch light, then thick enough to bear the weight of one of the animals. The new surface is already smooth and weathered. Sometimes it has hollows where many feet seem to have passed.

Elsewhere, a room thins until the sky is visible through all its walls. Its doorway remains opaque longest. Finally that too fades, leaving a rectangular patch of deeper dimness which may persist for years.

The few sounds come from inside the stone. A small click beneath a heel. A long, soft settling noise that seems to move from chamber to chamber. Very occasionally there is a low note from somewhere below, so faint you feel it in your teeth before you hear it. When it passes, dust lifts from every ledge in sight.

There are no vessels, lamps, signs, or abandoned possessions. The chambers have niches, but nothing has been stored in them. The squares have central depressions, but they contain neither water nor its residue. If the city was made for anyone, its accommodations are difficult to recognize. Some rooms are too shallow to enter. Others have a ceiling so high that it disappears into the same violet dimness as the open sky.

Still, in the lee of a wall, the wind falls away. A broad step gives you somewhere to sit. The warmed stone makes a small refuge from the cold.

From there you can watch a district moving past at the far side of the gulf. Its empty windows catch the light one after another. A delicate bridge begins to form between two terraces. Below it, an animal hangs beneath an arch, waiting for the distance to close.

For a while the streets almost meet.

Then the light shifts, and you see how much space remains.
