The garden occupies the hollow of a broken insulator near the top of the last transmission tower.

From below, if there were anyone below, it would be invisible: a dark nick in a ceramic collar, smaller than a cupped hand. The tower rises above the old mountain summits into a region where the air has thinned almost to nothing. Its lower sections disappear into darkness. Here, at its highest crossarm, the sky is charcoal, the stars faint and crowded, and the nearest land is so far down that it looks less like ground than a stain on the underside of the world.

Inside the nick, there is barely room to put a fingertip.

There is also a forest.

It begins at the broken lip, where the ceramic has weathered into overlapping ledges. Their edges are blunt, worn by ages of dry abrasion. Fine mineral dust is packed into every joint. There are no fresh fractures. Even the smallest chips have settled into the shapes they will keep.

The plants grow directly from this material. Their roots enter pores too narrow to see, branch through the insulator, and reach the metal spindle at its center. Nothing here has ever needed water. Their tissues contain a dense, waxy electrolyte that remains supple in the cold; they draw carbon from the thin air and charge from the spindle. A cut leaf would show no sap, only a soft metallic sheen between its layers.

But no leaf is cut.

The growth is extravagantly crowded. Broad, blue-black fronds lie over shelves of pale, curled foliage. Small trees with transparent trunks occupy the deeper cavities, their branches flattened against one another by the simple fact that all the available space is taken. Between them, mats of jointed stems fill openings no wider than a grain of salt. Where those stems cannot fit, a velvet crust of minute leaves covers the mineral.

Some leaves are thick as folded leather. Others are thin enough to reveal the veins of leaves beneath them. Their edges have hooks, combs, little translucent teeth. One species has grown in tight pleats along the roof of the hollow, each pleat nested inside the next, making a ceiling like the gills of an enormous mushroom. Beneath it, a grove of white stalks supports square, dark blades.

There are no flowers. There is nowhere for a visitor to land.

The absence of animals is complete. No eggs beneath the leaves, no mites in the dust, no patient creature occupying the deepest shade. Nothing eats anything else. No tunnels have been bitten through the roots. Every opening belongs to the vegetation, the old ceramic, or the few pieces of machinery embedded among them.

Those pieces are easy to mistake for part of the terrain.

A ceramic bridge spans a narrow cleft, scarcely longer than a thumbnail, carrying three wires in parallel grooves. Its maker pressed identification marks into the wet clay before firing it. Roots now fill the marks. Farther in stands a little metal housing with a roof of flaking enamel. It was once a regulator. The cover is still attached by one screw, and a transparent stem has grown through the empty screw hole beside it.

Under the lowest shelf, half buried in black foliage, is the head of a bolt. Its six faces make a tiny plaza. Nothing grows on the upper surface; the alloy refuses attachment. Around it, the leaves crowd so closely that their tips overhang the bare metal without touching it.

This is the only open ground.

The cold has no bite here. There is no wind inside the hollow, no alternation between shelter and exposure. The ceramic, leaves, wires, and dark air have reached the same cool temperature. A hand entering would bring a catastrophe of warmth.

The light comes mostly from within. Along the veins of certain fronds, a dull green glow makes the surrounding leaves visible. Small points of violet show where roots contact exposed metal. Neither light is bright enough to reach the entrance. Looking into the garden from outside, one would first see only the broken edge; then, after a long adjustment, the layered interior would begin to separate from the darkness.

A leaf near the center is lit from below and appears almost black. Behind it is another leaf, copper-colored. Behind that, the pale wall of the insulator curves away. What seems at first to be a deep valley is a groove in the ceramic. What seems to be a distant canopy is less than a centimeter from the opening.

The sound is much larger than the place.

The tower’s conductor passes immediately behind the hollow. It carries a continuous electrical load whose source and destination are both beyond sight. The metal spindle vibrates with it, and the insulator gives that vibration a voice: a low, enormous hum with a hard ringing note suspended inside it.

Outside, in the thin air, the sound would be weak. Inside, it comes through every surface. A root transmits it. A leaf transmits it. The bolt head sings at a slightly different pitch from the bridge. There is no quiet corner; even the softest-looking growth is coupled to the ceramic beneath it.

The foliage has shapes suited to this permanent pressure. Some blades are braced edge-on to the vibration. Some have thick ribs that carry it harmlessly into their stems. The pleated ceiling holds its folds at exact distances from one another. Long ago, less suitable shapes must have failed. What remains is fitted to the note.

Nothing perceptible changes now.

The conductor does not surge. The glow does not pulse. No dust enters, no fragment falls. The plants maintain their tissues without extending them, renewing the same minute structures in place. They have reached the limits of the hollow and of one another. A new shoot has nowhere to go, and no old shoot yields room.

This crowded stillness has lasted long enough for the distinction between growth and architecture to become uncertain. Roots brace the little bridge. The bridge divides two masses of foliage. The foliage shields the exposed contacts. Each presence supports the conditions that keep the others intact.

Near the entrance, one pale leaf lies flat against a ceramic ledge. Its outer edge stops exactly at the break. Beyond it is the immense drop, the tower, the mountains far below. Within it, packed into the thickness of a thumb, are trunks, clearings, roofs, precipices, and the unceasing industrial note.

The leaf does not tremble.
