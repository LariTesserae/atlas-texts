The chamber lies well below the lowest roots, but it is bright enough to make you narrow your eyes.

Light gathers in the wet floor. It rises through the shallow water in broad, wavering sheets, illuminating the undersides of ledges and leaving the tops of rocks comparatively dim. A person standing here casts a faint shadow on the ceiling. Where spray hangs in the air, the light becomes almost solid: pale columns that bend and break as the currents shift beneath them.

The chamber is perhaps seventy paces across. You can see most of its boundaries from the entrance, although getting from one side to the other is another matter. Water divides the floor into low islands of stone. Some channels are narrow enough to step over; others disappear beneath overhangs, their depth impossible to judge in the upward glare. The entrance is a dry slit halfway up one wall, reached by a stair whose lower steps have worn into shallow bowls. Warm air pours out through it. Coming down, you feel moisture settle first on your wrists, then along your hairline.

The stone is cream-colored, heavily veined, and worn smooth wherever the water reaches it. Its shapes are old and unhurried: shelves with rounded lips, basins joined by narrow throats, pillars thick at their bases and undercut around the middle. There are no sharp fallen blocks. Even the fragments at the foot of the walls have been rubbed into blunt, elongated forms, like tools whose purpose has been forgotten.

Across these settled shapes, water moves violently.

A channel may run quietly for several minutes, clear enough to show every grain on its bed. Then its surface lifts. A ridge travels along it against the current, swells under a stone bridge, and bursts out on the other side as white water. Pools spill into one another. Hollow pockets beneath the floor fill with a deep knocking sound. On the far wall, a wet stain abruptly becomes a waterfall, pouring from fissures that were only dripping a moment before.

The noise occupies the whole room. There is the close hiss of spray, the hard clatter of water striking thin stone, and underneath both a rolling concussion that you feel through the soles of your feet. Speech works only at arm’s length. Farther away, a raised voice loses its words and becomes another brief disturbance in the air.

Yet nothing here looks newly damaged. The violent routes have been used for a very long time. At each bend, the stone has the exact hollow needed to receive the surge. Overflow finds grooves waiting for it. Water thrown high against the wall drains back along polished seams. A stranger might spend an hour expecting a ledge to collapse, then notice the thin mineral crust along its rim and understand that it has survived countless such hours.

Near the center stands the only substantial structure: a narrow arch of dark ceramic, low enough that a tall person would have to stoop beneath it. Its feet are buried in separate islands, with water passing through the space between. It has no visible joints. Along its inner edge are small rectangular recesses, most empty, a few holding translucent plates cloudy with age.

The arch is still working.

You notice this first in a thread of water suspended below its crown. The thread hangs without falling, trembling in place. Droplets strike it from below and travel upward into the ceramic. Occasionally the entire arch gives a sharp, dry click, audible even through the torrents, and the light in the floor shifts several paces sideways.

Someone once placed three shallow dishes beside one of its feet. Two are now full of mineral sediment. The third remains clean, and contains a small lens of water that neither spills nor evaporates. Dust and grit accumulate around it. Nothing lands inside.

There are other traces of work, but they are easy to miss: a hand-sized panel flush with the entrance wall, a line of metal staples crossing a place too smooth to climb, a square patch on one pillar where a surface layer has been removed. No cables run between them. No screen offers an explanation. Whatever supplies the arch is buried deeper than the chamber, and whatever regulates it responds to things a visitor cannot see.

The warmth seems to come from below as well. Kneeling on a dry island, you can feel it through the stone. Some pools are pleasantly warm; others sting the fingertips. These temperatures migrate. A basin that was tolerable when you entered may be steaming when you return. Fine bubbles emerge from the floor in loose constellations, gather beneath a skin of mineral film, then rupture all at once.

Very little grows here. A few leathery green fronds cling to ledges beyond the usual reach of the surges. They are short, widely spaced, and glossy with condensation. Their roots enter hairline cracks rather than soil. Around them, a dark coating follows the stone’s grain, thin enough to be mistaken for staining until you see its softer edges.

In a protected pool near the entrance, two translucent animals move over the bottom. Each is no longer than a finger, with a flattened body and delicate legs nearly invisible against the pale rock. They remain motionless while the water is calm. When a pressure wave approaches, they slip into narrow holes before the surface begins to rise.

Their departure is a useful warning.

There are places where you can stand safely, though none has been marked. The broadest island receives little spray, but its surface is so smooth that wet feet slide on it. A smaller shelf beside the stairs is rougher and stays dry even during the strongest surges. From there, the chamber gradually becomes readable. You learn which deep note precedes an overflow, which flicker in the water means a channel is about to reverse. You begin to hear intervals in what first seemed continuous noise.

During one of those intervals, a drip from the ceiling strikes the dry shelf beside you. Then another. For a few seconds, these small impacts are distinct.

Across the room, the suspended thread under the arch thickens. The clean dish trembles. Light spreads beneath a distant basin, revealing a submerged passage that was invisible a moment ago.

The two small animals withdraw into the stone.

Water rises over the rounded lip of the basin and comes racing toward the center, bright from within, carrying no mud, no leaves, almost nothing that can be swept away.
