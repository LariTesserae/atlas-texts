The place is a shallow hollow on the crown of a very old mountain. You could cross it in a few minutes if you knew where to put your feet. You could spend a whole night crossing it if you did not.

There is no light, including overhead. The sky is as dark as the underside of a stone.

The mountain has worn down so far that its summit no longer resembles a summit. It is a broad, warm shoulder of rock, rounded before the first structures were built here. Rain has removed the brittle minerals and left a dark, fine-grained substance that feels almost waxy when wet. Nothing breaks underfoot. Loose stones are rare. The few that remain have settled into hollows made precisely for them by ages of movement.

The basin sits within a low rim, no higher than a person in most places. Beyond it, the mountain falls away into distances that can be heard but never seen: a faint, continuous rush of air, occasionally deepened by weather passing below. Here, high above that weather, the air is warm enough for bare arms. Moisture gathers on the hair and in the folds of clothing without becoming quite a mist.

Most of the hollow is empty.

That emptiness is easiest to understand by sound. A footstep has room to travel. A cough comes back from the far side, softened but distinct. There are no trunks in the middle, no boulders, no dividing walls. The floor slopes gently inward, with a few shallow channels too wide to step over and too shallow to deserve the name of streams. They carry water continuously, though the flow is so slow that a floating seed may remain beside the same foot for several breaths.

Along the edges, the growth is extravagant.

Roots lie braided against the stone. Fernlike plants hang from the rim in sheets, their fronds large enough to wrap around a sleeping body. There are thick, jointed stems with water held in their swollen sections, and broad leaves whose undersides have the soft nap of an animal’s ear. Everything grows outward from the boundary, extending into the hollow without quite occupying it. A leaf encountered at face height can be followed by hand along a stalk for several paces before the stalk reaches its root.

The plants do not seek light. Their older ancestors did; some retain the broad surfaces and branching habits of that inheritance. Here their leaves collect moisture, trap airborne salts, and host dense films of organisms that feed on what rises through the mountain.

The rock sweats a little more than water.

There is warmth in it, and dissolved matter, and a small, persistent imbalance that living things have learned to spend. At the roots, threads thinner than spider silk penetrate seams that cannot be found by touch. The threads belong partly to the plants, partly to their accompanying fungi, and partly to something installed so long ago that the distinction has ceased to be useful. Together they draw nourishment from depths where ordinary chemistry would not provide it.

This is why the place is lush. It is also why the middle remains open. The buried supply follows the rim.

At certain points, you can feel that supply working. Put a palm flat against the rock and there is a slow tightening beneath it, then a release. No vibration, no hum: simply the brief impression that the stone has acquired another fraction of weight. The interval is long enough that most visitors lift their hands before it happens again.

There are three structures in the basin.

One is a squat shelter fitted into the western bank. Its doorway is low, and its roof has become a root bed. Inside are two dry ledges and a floor smoother than the surrounding rock. The air smells faintly of clean metal. Water will bead on every surface outside while refusing to enter this room, even when blown directly toward the opening. A wet hand pressed against the threshold comes away dry.

The second structure is a narrow upright near the center. It rises only to the waist. The edges are perfectly rounded; the top holds a depression shaped to receive two hands. There is no lettering. If both hands are placed there, the next few drops falling anywhere in the basin arrive together.

No one has found a use for this. The animals dislike it. For a moment after the drops strike, the whole hollow falls quiet.

The third structure can be recognized only by walking around it. It is a ring of small sockets in the floor, each no wider than a fingertip. Some are filled with roots. Others contain water that feels cold despite the warmth of everything around it. A straight stick lowered into one socket comes out through another, a few strides away, without bending. The depth between them is less than the length of a finger.

Whatever once stood in this ring is gone. The floor inside it is bare and slightly raised. Small creatures cross it freely, but the larger ones go around.

There are many creatures.

They live primarily in the fringe, where the leaves and roots make layer upon layer of habitable space. Tiny grazing animals rasp the films from wet stems. Flat-bodied hunters wait underneath leaves, fastening themselves by the entire surface of their bellies. Soft-winged insects pass through the open center in such numbers that an outstretched arm will feel several brush against it within a minute.

Something about the size of a cat moves slowly through the hanging growth, using six gripping feet. It has a loose skin that collects condensation. Every so often it folds itself nearly in half and drinks from its own flank.

Farther down among the roots, hard-shelled animals knock against one another. Their shells have hollows worn into them, and the knocks are surprisingly resonant. One begins; another answers; soon the bank is sounding with wooden clacks and low, bell-shaped notes. The exchanges can continue for hours. A particular sequence may take most of the night to pass around the rim.

Over this comes the louder breathing of the leaf beds. Their stems contain air chambers connected through the roots. As moisture accumulates, valves close; as it drains away, they open. A long exhalation moves along one side of the hollow, stops, and resumes on the other. Up close it is a wet, papery sound. From the center, it resembles surf.

None of this is quick. The hunters wait longer than they pursue. The six-footed climbers may take an hour to change branches. Even the winged things drift, settling frequently, their wings built for suspension rather than speed. Abundance here has had time to find a pace that does not exhaust its supply.

The water gathers at the lowest point in a pool barely deep enough to cover an ankle. Its bed is smooth. There is no mud in the center; sediment settles into narrow grooves near the bank, where roots take it apart and hold it. Kneeling beside the pool, you hear drops striking around you and feel their ripples reach your knees. Sometimes a larger ripple arrives without a corresponding sound. A creature has entered from beneath a shelf, or left by one.

The darkness remains complete.

There are no luminous fungi, no lit instruments on the old structures, no brief flashes from the wings. The animals navigate by pressure, scent, contact, and the patient repetition of calls. A visitor gradually learns to do the same. The shelter is where the echoes shorten. The central upright interrupts the sound from the eastern bank. The pool makes the air cooler at shin height.

After a while, the hollow acquires dimensions. The lush fringe is close enough to smell, far enough away to leave room around the body. The roofless space above seems immense, but the low rim gives the feet a boundary. Under one hand is a leaf, under the other the old smooth stone.

Occasionally the wind changes beyond the mountain. The distant rushing grows louder, and the plants lean inward a little. Water shaken from their leaves spatters the empty floor. The animals pause until the fronds settle, then take up their calls at almost the same places in the sequence.

Near the shelter doorway, a groove has been worn by people sitting with their heels against the threshold. It is shallow, polished, and usually full of warm water. Someone once waited here often enough to mark stone that rain had scarcely altered for thousands of years.

There is room to sit beside that mark. From there, you can listen to the basin working through its small changes, while beneath the roots the buried apparatus continues to supply it, slowly and without light.
