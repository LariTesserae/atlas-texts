At first the country looks bare.

From the high rim, under a white and almost shadowless sky, you see miles of pale ground folded into shallow ridges. Beyond them are wider folds, then a horizon lifted so far above the lower world that clouds strike its edge and come apart. There are a few dark uprights in the distance. Nothing suggests a forest. The spaces between those uprights could hold cities.

Then the ground moves beneath your hand.

Only a little: a tightening of the surface, a slow contraction passing through the pale material. What looked like stone is a thick, weathered skin. Its cracks have rounded lips. Its loose flakes are dead scales. Beneath them lies a compact grain of fibers, packed in alternating directions, and farther down a living tissue that carries its nourishment without any visible liquid.

There is no soil here. Everything you can stand on grew.

The plateau is the back of a plant that has forgotten the distinction between itself and a continent.

***

Water is scarce enough that no animal has ever seen its own reflection in a pool. There are no streams, no wet hollows under the roots, no evening dew. The cold air burns moisture away from anything brought into it. A cup of water, if there were a cup, would become a precious little weather system: a dim cloud spreading across miles of hungry surface.

The native growth keeps what it needs locked into waxes and dense chemical gels. Its sap does not run. Cut a living strand and it beads with something clear and stiff, which hardens before it can fall.

The surface smells of warm resin even in the cold.

It is not soft country. The broad living crust has the toughness of horn, the low sheen of polished bone. Wind has worked it for ages. Some ridges are worn down almost to their conducting layers, exposing fine bronze-colored cords. Elsewhere the plant has grown over old wounds in tremendous scalloped seams. You can follow one of these seams all day and find that it marks the healed edge of a storm injury older than any animal now alive.

New growth is everywhere, but it seldom looks young. It comes out armored.

***

The scattered uprights are not trees in any familiar sense.

Most stand alone, their nearest neighbors several miles away. They rise from the continuous substrate in thick, tapering shafts, dark at the base and silver above. Their crowns are narrow. Instead of leaves they carry long fans of rigid vanes, set around a central spindle.

At a distance, the vanes seem motionless.

Closer, you see that they are turning.

Each crown rotates in the high wind, sometimes slowly enough to count its divisions, sometimes until its silver edges disappear into a translucent disk. The shaft below twists against the rooted ground. Deep inside, nested rings of tissue rub and release, converting that motion into heat, charge, and the persistent movement of gases through the plateau.

There are hundreds of thousands of them beyond the horizon.

Nothing built them. No foundation was poured, no metal forged. Yet the country has the noise and scale of a machine district. Its towers pump. Its buried fibers conduct. Its great breathing chambers compress the dry atmosphere and send it into places where daylight never reaches.

The industrial smell comes from the plants themselves: hot resin, scorched dust, the sharp bite of electrically split air.

***

The sound is enormous.

On the rim it arrives as one continuous roar, so complete that you first mistake it for silence. There is no quiet background against which to measure it. Your clothes tremble. The bones around your eyes ache.

Once you descend, differences emerge.

A low hammering comes up through the soles of your feet. There is a broad rushing sound overhead, interrupted by the shriek of a crown running too fast. Somewhere to the east, a pressure chamber vents in short bursts, each one big enough to send pale dust leaping from the ridges. The long hollows between them catch these noises and carry them outward. A sound made a hundred miles away may arrive clear and immediate, while something nearby is hidden inside the plateau’s bass vibration.

The animals do not call.

They signal by stamping, flashing, changing color, touching antennae to the living ground. Their bodies have evolved around the fact that the air cannot be heard through.

Even the small ones have heavily braced heads.

***

There are more animals than the emptiness initially admits.

Thin herds graze the pale crust with downward-facing mouthparts, moving in lines along the warm conducting bands. They are low to the ground and the same color as old fiber. When a shadow crosses them, they flatten into the shallow corrugations and become almost invisible.

Bright mites gather around fresh cracks. Long-legged scavengers hurry after storms, looking for stunned grazers before the substrate grows over them. In the lee of the rotating towers, winged creatures hang in the air with hardly a wingbeat, feeding on spores shaken loose from the vanes.

The largest animals are visible chiefly by the disturbances they leave.

A traveling bulge passes beneath a field of weathered scales. A ridge lifts and settles. These burrowers eat their way through dead layers, making tunnels broad enough to alter the circulation of the living body around them. The plant seals some passages, keeps others open, and grows dense cushions where the animals repeatedly sleep.

Neither side seems wholly in command.

When a burrower dies, the plateau closes around it over several seasons. For a while there is an unusually green island above the body, a patch of delicate fronds in a landscape that otherwise allows very little delicacy.

Such islands are far apart. Birds—or something near enough to birds—find them from astonishing distances.

***

By midday the light becomes hard to bear.

The pale surface throws it back upward. The silver crowns break it into moving flashes. Beneath your feet, translucent bands admit it into the substrate, where it travels a little way through packed fibers and reappears at the mouths of cracks.

But the air remains cool. You can stand in a blaze of reflected light and feel cold working through your sleeves.

Warmth exists in strips and pockets. A root conduit may be hot enough to make the air above it shimmer. Two steps away, a depression lies in permanent chill. Animals move along these thermal paths with great precision. A grazer pauses with its forefeet on a warm band and its hindquarters in cold shade, lowering its body by degrees until it reaches the balance it wants.

At this elevation, weather comes quickly and with little warning.

The first sign is often a change in the towers’ rotation. Several crowns slow together. Others turn out of alignment. Their vanes feather against the wind, and the steady roar develops gaps.

Then the ground begins to breathe faster.

***

A storm here need not contain water.

Cold air pours over the rim and meets the warmer exhaust rising from the plant’s chambers. The collision lifts dust, scales, spores, and long strips of shed skin. Electrical charge accumulates along the exposed conducting fibers. Blue sparks travel across miles of ground, stopping at old scars, branching around grazing herds.

The towers swing into new positions. Some lock their crowns. Others spin faster.

All at once the plateau seems full, although its vast open spaces have not diminished. Things are happening at every scale: mites fleeing a charged crack, a whole herd turning, a crown shedding a vane thirty yards long, a wall of suspended scales advancing across the horizon.

The shed vane falls edge-first. It strikes, bends, and lies trembling on the surface. Within minutes, scavengers are on it. Within hours, thin roots will emerge from its broken end.

The storm is destructive, but the country is old enough to have uses for most kinds of damage.

Its ridges follow prevailing winds. Its deepest scars are pressure outlets. Its loose outer skin is meant to leave.

Still, individual lives end abruptly. A gust takes the airborne feeders out of their sheltered column. A chamber ruptures beneath a herd. The surviving animals gather at the edge of the new hollow, stamping messages into ground that is already beginning to close.

***

Far inland, where the plateau broadens beyond sight, there are regions no upright growth occupies.

The crust there is smooth and slightly convex. You could walk for days without reaching a landmark. Only the shallow seams would tell you that you had moved at all.

Beneath that emptiness are the largest chambers.

Once in a long while, one opens.

A line appears across the ground, extending from one horizon to the other. Its edges lift. Air begins to flow toward it, carrying loose scales and animals too small to brace themselves. Then the line widens into a dark gulf, and a sustained note comes out of the continent, low enough to be felt through the chest and strong enough to raise dust from surfaces miles away.

For a few minutes, the underside of the landscape is visible: ribbed living walls, taut cables of root, pale membranes flexing in the dark.

When the chamber closes, the seam lies unevenly for a time. Grazers avoid it. Small scavengers search the places where the edges failed to meet cleanly.

Above them, the crowns keep turning in the cold bright wind. The repaired surface settles by fractions. Far off, another storm is gathering, visible as a faint brown lift along the rim.
