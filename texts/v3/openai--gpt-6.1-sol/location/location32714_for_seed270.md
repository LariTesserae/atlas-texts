The place is under the last three steps of an iron staircase, where the engine-house wall stops short of the bedrock.

From outside, it looks like a black seam. A dropped washer might roll into it and be considered lost. But beyond the seam the stone opens into a shallow chamber, no higher than a person’s knee and perhaps four paces across. Daylight comes through a cracked inspection grille at the back. Enough light enters to show colors: cream, rust, graphite, the dull amber of old oil. At noon, the grille lays six bright bars across the floor.

There is almost no water here. None stands, none runs, and the air passing through the chamber has already been dried by the warm machinery upstairs. Yet the floor stays cool. The rock extends deep beneath the works, into a thickness of earth that has never learned the factory’s daily schedule. Put your palm against it and the cold arrives slowly, then persists after you have taken your hand away.

The chamber was not dug. Its shape belongs to a much older landscape.

An ancient bed of pale limestone has weathered down around harder mineral knots, leaving a low, uneven pavement. Everything that could once have been sharp has become blunt. Ridges are thumb-high; depressions are no deeper than a saucer. Some of the hollows are nested inside larger hollows, the surviving traces of several different surfaces, each worn almost away before the next was exposed. A pebble embedded near the entrance has been polished flush with the stone. Another, softer, is gone, leaving only its smooth socket.

In places the floor resembles a coast seen from a great height, though its bays could be covered with a fingernail.

Nothing green grows. There are no roots in the cracks, no pale shoots reaching toward the grille. Even the dust is mostly mineral: powdered lime, flakes of iron oxide, fine black material rubbed from belts. The softest ground is a drift of this powder against the rear wall. Its surface never remains smooth for long.

The animals see to that.

At first the chamber seems to be trembling. Then the movement separates into bodies.

Flat, pale mites travel in streams along the depressions. Small beetles, black as cooled solder, shoulder through them without haste. Under every loose flake of stone there are more animals pressed together, resting, shedding their skins, feeding. Silver bristles protrude from a narrow crack like frost until the creatures carrying them begin to move. A centipede comes out sideways, its legs finding purchase on a wall so crowded with lesser life that it seems to be climbing over an inhabited carpet.

There are more bodies than bare places.

Some animals are familiar enough to name. Others would need a lens and considerable patience. The smallest are nearly transparent, visible chiefly when they cross a dark grain of dust. A few have amber bellies and carry bright specks inside them. They stop beneath the shafts of daylight, turning until the specks catch the light, then go on.

The food arrives from above.

The engine house leaks a very fine mist of lubricant through imperfect joints. Most settles upstairs, but a little reaches this chamber: a tacky film on the grille, a yellow stain beneath a bolt, a bead forming on the underside of the staircase. The bead grows for days. When it falls, the animals closest to it retreat from the impact. Then they return.

Different mouths take different portions. Some feed on the softer compounds; others scrape the residue left behind. Predators wait at the edges of the gathering. Dead bodies are opened, emptied, dismantled. Wings collect in sheltered hollows because almost nothing wants them.

Here, oil is not merely a spill. It is weather.

There are scattered constructions among the stone forms. A short length of copper tube disappears into the rear wall. Two brick fragments retain a trace of whitewash. A bent retaining clip makes a little bridge across one depression, and generations of animals have used it instead of descending to the floor. Beneath the brightest part of the grille stands a threaded stud, broken off at about the height of a beetle. Its valleys are packed with dust. Small hunters patrol around it, circling the same iron rise.

Someone once tried to seal the chamber. A ribbon of hardened compound remains along the entrance, cracked and lifted at its edges. The animals have excavated beneath it. In one place they have worn a passage through it, a smooth tunnel barely wider than a pin.

The machinery above is loud.

A shaft knocks once every revolution. Gears produce a steady, heavy hiss. At intervals a press strikes, and the whole chamber jumps. Dust loosens from the ceiling; an iron scale falls; hundreds of animals flatten themselves at once. For an instant, the floor becomes still.

Then activity resumes, first along the edges, then everywhere.

Inside the pocket, smaller sounds become audible between the industrial ones. Beetle feet tick on thin metal. Dry husks scrape over stone. Something beneath a brick makes a rapid rasp, pauses, answers a second rasp from the wall. The inspection grille resonates with the machinery and occasionally sings a clear, sustained note. When it does, the mites abandon its shadow. They return before the note has quite faded.

The chamber has schedules that do not agree with those upstairs. Light travels slowly across the shallow basins while oil arrives unpredictably. The coldest cracks remain crowded through the working day. When the engines stop, the air changes direction and the inhabitants spread onto surfaces they avoided while the shaft was turning. Pale bodies gather on the copper tube. Larger beetles emerge from under the staircase. Their passage opens temporary lanes through the smaller creatures, which close behind them almost immediately.

Predation is constant, but rarely dramatic. A small hunter waits beside a grain of quartz. A pale animal passes too near. There is a brief adjustment of legs, and the hunter is holding something that still moves. Nearby, two beetles pull at the same dried carcass until it breaks. One carries its portion into the foot of the wall; the other stays where it is, feeding in daylight.

The terrain changes a little each day. Dust migrates. A husk blocks a channel. Bodies wear a greasy path across a mineral rise. Beneath all this, the old limestone barely changes at all.

To see the place properly, you have to kneel beside the stairs and let your eyes adjust. Your sleeve brushes the entrance. The exposed animals retreat, but only a short distance; there is nowhere uncrowded to go. Soon they begin crossing the bright bars again. Behind them the cool pavement extends to the wall, low and densely occupied, its ancient hollows filling and emptying with life.
