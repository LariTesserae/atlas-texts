The town stands so low that its streets are almost level with the water in its wells.

There are no hills within it, only raised foundations, thresholds, and the shallow backs of embankments. From a roof, the whole settlement appears to rest on one broad shelf: pale masonry crowded between a dark cliff and a gray lagoon. Beyond the last houses, that shelf continues in bare tongues of clay and lime, separated by narrow channels. The distance is open, but never empty. There are walls out there, fish pens, squat storehouses, rows of stakes, birds standing so close together that their bodies seem to make another shore.

Everything looks recently made. The banks have sharp edges. Blocks retain the little ridges left by their molds. Where a foundation meets the ground, there is no rounded shoulder of accumulated soil, no weathered slope softening the join. A fallen fragment of plaster lies with its corners intact in the wet clay.

Yet nothing is being hurried toward completion. The town has the settled habits of a much older place. Its paths occupy their shallow grooves; its water finds the same openings. A wall may be patched, a basket replaced, but the pattern persists.

The ground beneath it is not quite rock. Springs emerge along the foot of the cliff, bringing warm water through limestone. They have laid down a low terrace of mineral crust over mud, shells, and gravel. In some places the crust is thick enough to support a house. In others, a person can break through it with a heel.

There, beneath a skin of white stone, lies soft gray material with the consistency of soaked flour.

The builders know these places. Houses stand on broad footings rather than deep ones, and the largest walls are low. A room added above another room is exceptional; most of the town spreads by fitting smaller rooms into whatever gap remains. Courtyards shrink to shafts of air. Passageways narrow around the bulges of ovens and cisterns. Two houses may share a wall, a drain, and the stone slab on which their occupants clean fish.

From above, the roofs nearly touch. At ground level, the town is full of elbows.

The materials are few. Pale blocks cut from the spring terrace. Dark clay shaped by hand. Shell lime for mortar. Coarse pottery. Bone pins, shell scoops, stone blades, lengths of cord twisted from hide. Wood is valuable enough that a good beam may outlast several houses; it is rubbed smooth by handling and fitted into new sockets when a wall is rebuilt. Most roofs are made instead from short slabs spanning close-set masonry ribs. Their undersides hang low over rooms that smell of warm stone, damp wool, fish oil, and people.

There is almost no green.

Nothing trails from the roofs or takes root in the seams. The empty ground beyond the town has no scrub to give it scale. Even the cliff is bare except for darker patches where water seeps through. Salt gathers along the margins of those patches. Close up, the wet stone carries thin brown and rust-colored films, slippery under a finger, but from a distance these look like stains.

Without foliage, all the small differences in the mineral surface become conspicuous: cream-colored crust beside blue-gray mud, a black pebble pressed into white mortar, the pink inside of a broken shell. Dampness makes the colors stronger. Dry surfaces turn almost uniformly pale.

The air is warm enough that wet clothing remains uncomfortable rather than chilling. It smells faintly of sulfur near the cliff and strongly of brine along the outer channels. Water beads on pottery even when no rain has fallen. In enclosed passages, droplets collect beneath lintels and fall onto the same dark spots in the paving.

The light is dim but not cold. The cliff cuts off much of the sky, and a low marine haze hides the rest. Daylight arrives as a broad, diluted brightness, with few distinct shadows. White walls ought to glare; here they seem to hold the light inside themselves. Faces remain shadowed beneath brows. The bottoms of stairways are difficult to see.

There are polished shell fragments set beside some doorways. They catch what little light is available and mark the edges of steps.

Water is frequent rather than vast. You cross it repeatedly without seeing much of it at once: a gutter behind a workbench, a square settling basin, a hand-wide stream beneath a slab, a well sunk into the floor of a room. The largest channels are only a few paces across. Their banks are reinforced with stone and broken pots, and their surfaces carry slow rafts of foam.

The lagoon is broader, but much of it is shallow. Fish backs disturb the surface beyond the pens. Farther out, the water loses its visible motion and merges with the haze.

Near the cliff, however, it is loud.

The springs do not all emerge gently. Several spill through narrow cracks into covered collection chambers, and inside those chambers falling water strikes stone with a deep, continuous thunder. The chambers keep grit out of the channels, but their openings face the streets. Sound pours from them along with damp air.

That roar is the town’s background. Everything else has to rise above it.

Seabirds crowd the outer walls and the stakes of the fish pens. They cry from roofs, quarrel over scraps, and beat their wings in the narrow spaces between buildings. Their claws scrape on ceramic tiles. Below them, pigs occupy low pens along the refuse channels, shoving one another against partitions and squealing whenever a carrying basket appears. Dogs lie under benches until something worth fighting over drops. Cats move along the coping stones, avoiding the wet paving.

In the darker storehouses, bats hang between the roof ribs. Their smell is strong enough to identify a building before its doorway is visible.

The smaller animals are more numerous still. Crabs inhabit holes beneath the embankments. Snails cluster under channel lips. Shellfish encrust the submerged faces of the pen walls. Insects gather over the warmer pools and settle on every uncovered bowl. At the lagoon’s edge, the mud trembles with burrowing bodies.

Much of this life feeds in water that looks nearly barren. The springs bring minerals; the sea brings suspended food. Thin microbial films coat the warm shallows, and shellfish strain the clouded channels. Fish enter from the lagoon and remain among the pens, where small creatures gather on the stonework. The town adds its own waste to this crowded economy, though the channels carrying drinking water are kept separate and fiercely guarded.

Food comes from the sea, from the pens, and in dry loads carried down the road from beyond the cliff. Grain is stored in narrow-necked jars sealed with clay. Nothing resembling a field lies within sight.

The sound of work joins the animals and the water. Stone pestles strike basins. Pottery knocks against pottery. A blade being sharpened makes a harsh, repeated rasp. Doors are slabs dragged in grooves, and their movement sets the teeth on edge. People call across courtyards because ordinary speech cannot reach. At a crowded landing, a quarrel may be visible for some time before its words can be distinguished.

Only inside the thickest rooms does the noise subside, and even there the springs are audible through the floor.

There are rules to building here that no one needs written down. A drain must remain reachable by hand. A channel cannot be roofed over for more than the length of a pole. No doorway opens below the high-water mark cut into its nearest wall. Heavy jars stay against the footings. Anyone adding a roof must leave room for the birds to pass above the shared gutter, or the neighboring households will remove the obstruction.

These rules give the town its shape more than any grand plan does.

Its permanence is practical. The water level hardly varies. The lagoon is sheltered from large waves, the springs flow steadily, and the damp air keeps exposed clay from drying too quickly. There is little frost, little windblown sand, little violent rain. Fresh mineral surfaces remain fresh-looking. A chip in a step stays a chip rather than becoming the beginning of a worn hollow.

Daily labor maintains the rest. Silt is lifted from the settling basins. Cracked mortar is pressed back into joints. The sharp edges of the banks are faced again before they slump. From a distance, all this tending disappears, leaving a place that seems to have reached its present form without acquiring age.

At the outermost houses, the dense passageways finally open onto the bare shelf. The roar remains behind you, mixed now with the uninterrupted cries of birds. Warm water moves through channels at your feet. Across the lagoon are more pale walls, more stakes, another compact cluster of roofs half lost in the haze.

There is enough space to see how far the settlement extends, but nowhere that looks untouched. Even the blank mud between its outlying structures bears footings, markers, and carefully maintained cuts for water.

Under the gray light, the whole shore has the blunt, clean-edged look of work just finished: a dense town resting on a shallow crust, surrounded by animals, wet to its foundations, with every necessary sound already filling the air.
