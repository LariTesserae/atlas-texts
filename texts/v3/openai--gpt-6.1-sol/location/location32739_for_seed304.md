The town fills a shallow basin hardly wider than a long stone’s throw. From its rim you can see almost every roof, but no whole street. The houses stand too close for that: backs pressed to backs, walls sharing corners, low annexes tucked beneath the overhangs of taller rooms. Between them, passages turn and narrow around stone tanks, drying racks, steps, and the mouths of drains. There is scarcely a piece of ground that does not serve some purpose.

Water enters everywhere.

It beads on the underside of lintels. It wells through the flagstones in the kitchens. It slips from a crack in one courtyard wall at the steady rate of a thread being drawn through cloth. Most of it arrives without rain, rising from beneath the basin through pale, recently fractured stone. The townspeople say the ground is still settling into its shape. They remember where the first spring appeared, though no one now agrees which house was built around it.

The basin lies only a little above the surrounding mudflats. Its rim is a low shelf of gray-white rock, sharp-edged where it has broken, almost creamy where water has begun to work it. There are no rounded boulders here. Loose fragments retain their corners; a person can cut a palm by lifting one carelessly. Some exposed faces look freshly opened even where they have stood for years, because each cold season another thin plate loosens and drops away.

The houses use this stone. Their lower walls are heavy blocks, poorly matched but skillfully wedged, with black clay packed into the seams. Above shoulder height the construction becomes lighter: split boards, woven reeds plastered on both sides, roof beams with their bark still on. Roofs overlap. In several places the eave of one dwelling is also the covered walk of another, and rain from three buildings is caught by a single wooden trough.

Everything has swollen slightly with damp. Doors drag. Pegs grip too tightly to be removed. The handles of tools are polished dark by hands and water, while unused wood develops a soft nap that must be scraped away.

There is daylight enough to see these textures plainly. The clouds often thin in the middle of the day, leaving a broad, pale illumination that reaches the roof ridges and upper walls. Down among the passages, light comes in small shapes: a bright wedge across a doorstep, a trembling rectangle reflected from a cistern, the white underside of a wet plank. When the sun appears, the entire settlement begins to drip faster.

The water is cold, but not all of it behaves alike. Water caught from the roofs pours and splashes normally. Water drawn from the deeper fissures clings to surfaces longer than it should. It follows the underside of a beam, gathers at a raised nail, and hangs there in a heavy clear bulb. On certain mornings it creeps a finger’s breadth up the outside of a cup.

This is troublesome rather than marvelous. The town has learned to give every channel a clean break: a projecting lip, a notch, a short fall through open air. A gutter without these interruptions may send water back along its own underside and into the wall it was meant to keep dry. The best gutter-makers use bone chisels and small copper blades, and their work is judged by whether the wall beneath it stays pale.

There are rules for walking here. Keep to the rough strips scored into the paving. Do not place a bucket over an unfamiliar seep. Do not block a passage with anything that cannot be lifted by one person. A channel the width of two fingers may carry very little for weeks, then fill between breakfast and dusk.

The settlement has no central square. Its nearest equivalent is a roofed washing court, crowded with shallow stone basins, where people can meet without standing in someone else’s doorway. Flat washing paddles hang from the posts. A strip of open sky runs down the middle of the roof, admitting rain to the deepest basin. Even when nobody is there, the court sounds occupied: a drop strikes stone, another lands in a wooden bowl, a third makes the low hollow note of water falling into water.

Those are the dominant sounds. There is no broad rush of river or surf. The flow is divided among hundreds of small routes, each nearly silent. You hear a scrape as someone clears grit from a drain, the creak of a loaded basket rubbing against a wall, a mallet tapping a new wedge into masonry. Voices travel oddly through the covered alleys. A conversation may be distinct two houses away and inaudible from the next doorway.

Plants occupy what the buildings cannot use. A few stiff grasses grow along the basin’s outer shelf, their blades bent by moisture rather than wind. In sheltered cracks are small black-green cushions that look charred until touched. Pale shoots emerge beneath stairways and remain there, living on reflected light. People pull them from drains but leave them around cistern rims, where their roots hold the soft sealing clay together.

There are no gardens on the ground. A handful of households keep shallow boxes on their roofs, filled with grit, scraps of reed, and soil brought in baskets from farther inland. The plants in them are low and leathery. Their leaves are eaten sparingly; their stems are dried for cord. A box that produces a full hand’s breadth of new growth in a season is considered well tended.

Animals are seen less often than their traces. Tiny water insects leave delicate scratches across the surface of sheltered pools. Occasionally a gray bird lands on the outer wall and waits, motionless, for something to rise through a drain mouth. It never walks into the town. Once or twice a week, a narrow pale fish appears in a household basin, carried up through a fissure too tight to admit a finger. It lies quietly against the stone until someone lifts it out and takes it to the lower catchment pool.

People there disagree about whether these are young fish or very old ones. No one has found eggs.

The catchment pool occupies the lowest chamber of the settlement. It is an open-topped space hemmed in by blank house walls, with a ledge around three sides and a dark outlet beneath the fourth. Most of the town’s water passes through it eventually. Fibers, grit, and chips of pale stone collect against a removable screen of woven willow. The screen must be lifted and cleaned each day, and after rain each hour.

Below the screen, the water becomes still enough to reflect the walls. These reflections have a slight delay. A hanging cloth may stop moving while its reflected hem continues to sway. The delay is brief, usually no more than a breath, and easier to notice on cold mornings. Children watch it from the ledge until someone sends them away to work.

Nothing else in the town moves slowly enough to be neglected. A channel crusts over with mineral deposit and must be reopened. A wall drinks water through its footing; its clay seam softens; a mason packs in fresh clay and braces it while it dries. Roof reeds rot from the underside. The rock itself gives an occasional quiet click, followed by a fresh line of moisture.

Near the upper entrance, one house has been dismantled almost to its foundations. Its beams are stacked under a neighboring roof, its door is already in use elsewhere. In the emptied room, a new ridge of stone has risen through the floor. It stands no higher than a knee, narrow and jagged, with water shining along its crest. The builders have left space on either side of it. When they rebuild, the ridge will divide the room, and a trough will catch its runoff.

By late afternoon, smoke lies low above the roofs. The hearths burn short lengths of imported wood and bundles of dry reed, so fires are kept small and used in turn. The smell of smoke enters the passages alongside wet clay, stone dust, and the faint sourness of soaked baskets. Doors are propped open long enough to exchange the warm damp inside for the cold damp outside.

At evening, the people lift their floor mats onto hooks. They set bowls beneath dependable drips and empty the bowls beneath uncertain ones. At a doorway near the washing court, someone kneels to deepen a notch in the threshold. The blade makes a fine scratching sound. A little water passes through, hesitates at the new edge, and drops cleanly into the channel below.

The work stops. For a moment there is only that small, successful falling sound, and then the other sounds of the town become audible again.
