The sea lies over the mountain range like a second sky.

Its surface is nearly a kilometer above the highest peaks, sealed under pale ice, and the light that comes through is ordinary daylight: white, sufficient, without the blue darkness one expects at such a depth. There are places where you can see for thirty kilometers. A ridge appears in the distance with every tooth intact, every fracture visible, and only its lack of shadow tells you how far away it is. Between you and that ridge there is almost nothing. No silt, no weed, no drifting snow of organic matter. The water is so clear that it takes the passage of an animal, or a bubble escaping from a machine, to make you remember what fills the space.

Below the ridge, the mountains continue downward.

Their summits stand eight kilometers above the buried plains. You are submerged, but you are also very high. Valleys open beneath the water in enormous clean cuts, their floors beyond sight. Occasionally the daylight catches a lower terrace, a small gray ledge suspended halfway down the dark, and the ledge is large enough to hold a city.

There are no cities.

The rock is young basalt, black where it has split recently and silver where a thin glaze of mineral has settled on it. Some faces are as smooth as cast metal. Others show the clustered columns of cooling lava, snapped across at different heights so that a mountainside resembles a broken bundle of pencils. The peaks have not acquired the bluntness of old mountains. Edges remain edges. A narrow fin can rise two hundred meters from a shoulder without crumbling, tapering to a blade that would cut a cable drawn across it.

The sea arrived before weather could work here. Then the cold arrested almost everything else.

There are no streams carrying grit down the slopes, no roots prying at the joints, no alternation of thaw and frost. A rockfall lies exactly where it landed: great angular blocks leaning against one another, their dust still caught in the hollows beneath them. The dust has compacted, but it has not traveled. Even on the steepest slopes the mineral coating grows too slowly to soften a corner.

The cold is present in the materials more than in the view. Steel contracts. Rubber stiffens. A hand laid against an observation window aches through its glove. The water remains liquid because of pressure and dissolved salts, but it has the temperature of something that ought to be solid.

And the entire ocean roars.

At first you would assume the sound came from somewhere nearby. A turbine behind the next ridge; a colossal waterfall entering a concealed shaft. It has that density, that complete occupation of the ear. There is a bass note felt in the teeth, a higher metallic vibration in the jaw, and above both a rushing sound so broad that it has no apparent direction.

Moving does not bring you closer to it.

You can descend a thousand meters, cross a basin, take shelter under a rock overhang, and the sound stays almost the same. Inside a sealed vehicle it enters through the hull. Inside a pressure station it makes the cutlery creep toward the rims of plates. People sleep in suspended bunks, with nothing rigid touching the frame.

The source is scattered across the range: old pumping houses, few enough that weeks of travel may pass between them. Each stands on a summit or a broad saddle, anchored directly into the basalt. They are practical buildings, squat and ribbed, with thick windows and loading doors too small for the machinery inside. Their pipes plunge into bored holes in the mountain. Their intake towers rise into clear water.

They were built to circulate the deep sea through the high sea, maintaining a balance whose original necessity is no longer obvious. They still do it.

The pumps turn at constant speed. Water enters, water leaves. No plume follows the outlets, because the discharged water has the same temperature and clarity as the water around it. Only a loose object reveals the flow: a dropped wrench sliding away in a straight line; the fringe of a passing animal pulled briefly taut.

The machinery would be loud in any sea. Here, the cold water, the stone basins, and the thick ceiling of ice carry and return its vibration. A sound crosses the range, comes back, meets another sound, and goes out again. The pumping houses have filled a volume much larger than themselves. Several stations no longer operate, their rotors locked and their housings dark, but they stand within the same roar as the working ones.

Beside one station, a maintenance gantry extends over a vertical drop. Its crane hook hangs at the level of the roof. There is no sway in it. On the deck are six crates, a length of hose, and an empty wheeled cart with one wheel turned sideways. Lamps burn over the service doors, though daylight reaches the building easily. The lamps illuminate scratches in the paint and the white shells of animals living around the hinges.

Life gathers at these small interruptions.

Across most of the water there is almost nothing visible. Then a seam in the rock turns out to be packed with pale bodies. A pump intake carries a collar of shellfish three meters thick. Under a ledge, hundreds of translucent animals hang with their limbs spread, all facing the same gentle current.

There are no leaves, no branching beds of kelp, no green film on the stones. The smaller life feeds on dissolved minerals and the work of organisms too slight to see. The larger life feeds on that. A mountain can look sterile from a distance and prove, close up, to have every sheltered crack occupied.

The most common swimmers are long and nearly transparent, with a silver strip down each side and blunt heads full of small teeth. They travel in groups so loosely arranged that one animal may be hundreds of meters from its nearest companion. You see one crossing a white rock face, then another far above it, then several more against the black mouth of a valley. Their abundance becomes apparent only after you have watched for a long time. There are always more arriving from the empty distance.

Near the machines, heavier creatures graze on the shell beds. They have low armored backs and six paddle-like limbs. Their mouths work against the intake grilles with a slow rasp that cannot be heard over the pumps. Engineers once fitted spikes to discourage them. The animals learned to brace between the spikes.

Elsewhere, broad, soft-bodied hunters rest against the cliffs. At a glance they resemble patches of mineral bloom. When they release their hold, a whole section of apparent stone peels away and folds itself into the water. They descend without haste, passing from daylight into the dark beneath the high ledges.

Nothing here needs to move quickly. The cold favors thrift, and the persistent sound makes sudden movement difficult to conceal. Much of the fauna senses through contact and vibration. Animals pause with a limb against rock, waiting to feel another creature disturb the steady tremor. Around the stations, their bodies rise and settle to the working rhythm of the pumps.

The place has very little that could be called weather. There are broad currents, but their courses do not wander. The light remains diffuse beneath the ice. No storm comes down into the water; no fresh cloud of sediment dims a valley. Instruments left on a ridge record such small differences that a year of readings can resemble a faulty sensor repeating itself.

Yet there is movement everywhere you look long enough: a transparent swimmer turning, a jaw working on a shell, a turbine shaft revolving behind thick glass. Along the cliffs, animals leave faint polished tracks through the mineral glaze. The same routes have been used for generations.

Far from the stations, you can settle on a narrow summit and look across the flooded range. Peaks stand in ranks, sharp and fully lit, with immense empty channels between them. Beneath your feet, the mountain falls away into darkness. Above you, the pale ice roof is too distant to show its texture.

A single swimmer passes across that brightness. It is large, though there is nothing near it by which to judge its size. Its shadow moves over the summit and is gone.

The water remains clear. The crane hook, somewhere beyond the ridges, remains still. Through the rock under your boots comes the unbroken force of the pumps.
