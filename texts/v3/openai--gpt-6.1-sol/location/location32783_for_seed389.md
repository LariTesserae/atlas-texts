At this height, water boils before it is hot enough to scald, but the stone can burn through the sole of a boot.

The city occupies the uppermost shelves of a mountain that is still being made. Its streets cross pale, newly hardened rock, and the edges of its foundations disappear into material with the consistency of cooling wax. Far below, older ranges lie in blue layers beneath the haze. They have slopes, scree, drainage channels—the familiar consequences of having stood somewhere for a long time. This mountain has mostly corners. Some are natural; some were cut; others formed against the undersides of buildings and retained the shape of their floors.

From a distance, the settlement resembles a dense assembly of kilns left on an impossibly high workbench. Square towers crowd around wider cylinders. Bridges pass through buildings rather than between them. Reservoirs hang in cradles of riveted metal, shaded by roofs barely a handspan above their surfaces. Everything seems to have another thing fitted beneath it: a workshop under a stair, a condenser under a bridge, a row of dwellings in the gap between a retaining wall and the rock it retains.

Yet the country remains visible everywhere. A doorway opens onto thirty miles of empty air. Through the grating of a market floor, you can see the lower terraces, then the unfinished flank of the mountain, then clouds. At the end of a narrow lane, a gap between two foundries frames an entire distant range.

The heat comes upward.

There is no spectacular crater, no fountain of lava. Instead, the mountain releases warmth through an immense number of small surfaces. A crack beneath a threshold. The exposed face of a quarry. A patch of paving that has gone slightly translucent. In some streets, the handrails are wrapped in several layers of ceramic cloth; in others, people know which side of a railing can safely be touched. Public seats have hollow backs through which cool air is drawn from shaded shafts. The difference between a tolerable place and an unbearable one can be the width of a wall.

Daylight is ordinary and clear. Under the blue sky, washing hangs between houses, work lamps are unnecessary, and shadows lie sharply beneath carts. The strangeness is easier to notice in the shade, where the ground gives off enough heat to lift a loose sleeve.

Water arrives in brief weather.

A cloud climbs from the low country and catches on the eastern towers. For twenty minutes the city is damp. Droplets collect on cables, run down glazed brick, darken the dust along the seams of the pavement. There may be rain, though much of it vanishes before reaching the streets. People bring out shallow trays. Roof shutters turn with deliberate slowness, presenting their cooler undersides to the mist.

Then the cloud moves on, and the wet surfaces begin to pale.

The city keeps what it can. Condensation gutters thread its walls. Each bridge carries a narrow water line, insulated with white sleeves and interrupted by bulbous pressure chambers. Public taps dispense into closed vessels; an open bucket loses too much on the walk home. The few uncovered pools occupy deep pockets in the rock, where the mineral is porous and evaporation chills the surface. Their rims are black, their centers a cloudy green. Nothing grows around them. The green comes from dissolved metal.

There is one channel that people call the river. It crosses six districts in a trough scarcely wider than a person’s shoulders. Most days it contains only a damp stripe and a scattering of salt crystals. After the eastern fogs, a clear stream runs through it for several hours, clicking quietly over the teeth of measuring wheels. Children follow it from bridge to bridge. Near the northern works, it disappears into a sealed tank.

No trees interrupt the city’s hard outlines. No grass softens its unused corners. Even the oldest walls remain bare, except for crusts deposited by water. Where a roof has collapsed, the exposed rooms collect dust, loose screws, and sometimes a delicate white bloom of salt. Years later they will look much the same, provided the mountain has not risen through them.

Food comes up in lifts from the lower valleys. So do timber, cloth, and most living things. Fresh produce is sold in a roofed hall where the stalls stand on cooling pipes. A crate of leafy vegetables attracts attention for its smell as much as its value. The city’s own characteristic smells are hot ceramic, machine oil, mineral steam, and the dry metallic odor released when a new fissure opens.

Its factories make the mountain.

Beneath the inhabited terraces, pressure houses receive a thick, pale slurry from shafts sunk into the interior. The material is pumped into galleries, between retaining plates, beneath suspended streets. It settles, darkens, and hardens. Over months, a cavity becomes a buttress. Over years, a buttress becomes a ridge. The waste heat travels upward into the city; the water driven out of the setting stone is caught in condensers and returned to the works.

Nobody quite agrees how much of the process is controlled. The pumps can decide where material enters. They cannot always decide where it ends up. A wall may thicken beyond its intended dimensions. A service tunnel may acquire a low, rounded ceiling although no one has poured anything above it. Surveyors take measurements every morning, marking departures in chalk.

The buildings accommodate this uncertainty. Their lower floors have telescoping columns, replaceable stair sections, hinges where another city would use fixed joints. Doorframes bear graduated scales. A red line beside a lintel shows how far the floor has risen since the house was built.

In older districts, ground-floor windows have become vents close to the pavement. The former rooms below them are filled with rock. Residents move upstairs gradually, carrying cupboards and beds through enlarged stairwells. Eventually another story is added to the roof. The address remains the same.

For all this machinery, the city is quiet.

The air is thin, and most engines turn slowly. Great compression wheels make one revolution in several minutes. Pumps work through long strokes separated by pauses. Their loads are enormous, but their motion is so patient that you can stand beside one and mistake it for a fixed structure until its shadow begins to move.

There are local sounds: a wrench set down, the chatter of a loose valve, someone calling across a courtyard. Beyond the next wall, they diminish. From an upper terrace the factories appear almost silent, though their pressure gauges tremble and their cables are taut.

Occasionally a deep knock passes through the mountain. Cups shift on shelves. A suspended bridge swings a fraction of an inch. People stop long enough to feel whether a second knock follows. Usually it does not, and conversation resumes.

Animals are uncommon, but some have found a place here.

Small pale creatures live beneath condenser housings, coming out when the metal cools. They have flat bodies, many jointed legs, and dark plates along their backs. They gather around droplets, then retreat before sunrise. Workers call them tank lice, although the name accounts for neither their size nor their occasional flights: once mature, a few unfold thin, stiff wings and drift down through the warmer shafts.

On the highest roofs, narrow-bodied birds wait beside steam outlets. Their feathers are dusty gray; their feet are conspicuously thick. They feed on the condenser creatures and on scraps near the food lifts. There may be only a dozen in a district. When one passes a window, its shadow is brief and startling.

The most open part of the city is the unfinished western shelf.

Here the streets are laid out, but only half the foundations have received buildings. Rows of anchored bolts stand in the sun. Pipes end in blank flanges. The fresh ground is smooth enough to preserve footprints in places and too hard to scratch a few yards farther on. Survey flags hardly move in the thin air.

From this shelf, the city’s density becomes visible: all its stacked balconies, lift towers, tanks, vents, occupied roofs. Beyond them rise the new ridges, lighter in color than the inhabited stone. One carries a straight horizontal impression left by a casting plate. Another has grown around a crane abandoned during a pressure surge; its upper arm still projects from the rock.

Nothing changes quickly enough to watch. A worker may return from a month in the valleys and find a passage narrower, a distant ridge higher, a familiar stair requiring one additional step.

At evening, the roofs cool first. A little moisture gathers on the shaded metal, and the air between the buildings becomes briefly comfortable. People come out onto balconies. Lamps switch on behind shutters. Far below, clouds turn gray over the older mountains.

Under the city, the pumps continue their slow strokes. Warm stone presses against the foundations, and in a locked survey room a pen draws an almost level line across a moving strip of paper. By morning, it will have risen slightly.
