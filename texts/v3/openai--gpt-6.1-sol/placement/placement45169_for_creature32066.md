At first I would not know whether I was in a room or beneath one.

There would be stone close behind me, water passing in front, and a warmer current arriving from somewhere below. Nothing would give me the shape of the whole enclosure. I would know a nearby opening because food came through it. I would know the roof by the interruptions it made in that arrival.

For a long time, this would be enough to locate me.

I might seem to belong to the wall. My chamber would be one of several pale tubes rising from a dark, uneven surface. When the water strengthened, I would open a small crown into it. When something brushed the crown, I would withdraw. The closing would happen before anything resembling recognition: touch, contraction, the sheltered water of the tube.

Outside, a shrimp would pick at the rim.

I would wait until its feet had moved away.

There would be other chambers beside mine, joined at their bases. Some occupied, some empty, some so thoroughly sealed beneath later growth that they no longer held water. Our newest edges would be thin. Nourishment would pass between our bodies through narrow connections in the living tissue. A damaged member could remain supplied while it repaired itself; a crowded one could help sustain growth at a freer edge.

I would be one of these small bodies, but being one would not quite end at my own tube.

The surface beneath us would occasionally shift.

Not often enough to prevent attachment. Not sharply enough, at first, to break anything. A pressure would travel through the foundation, and the mouths of several tubes would tilt together. Then the current would change. Water that had arrived from the left would come from above. The warm source would diminish, return briefly, and disappear.

We would close.

Later, when we opened, the food would be different.

There would be more settled matter, loosened particles, traces of torn bacterial film. Something broad would be passing close overhead. The water between it and us would accelerate, then slow. A few of our tallest rims would scrape against a hard surface.

I would find myself on the back of the long animal.

Not near its head. Farther behind, where the plates were older and the substances that discouraged settlement had weakened. Our colony would occupy a shallow depression in one plate and extend toward its edge. We would have begun there when the animal remained beneath a terrace for many days. A young body drifting out of the water would have fastened itself to what offered shelter.

There would have been no reason to distinguish this foundation from the terrace.

For us, its differences would emerge slowly.

The feeding place could leave. A roof could arrive without growing. The surface beneath our chambers could become warmer while the surrounding water cooled. Sometimes a long shiver would pass under us, lifting one end of the colony and settling it again.

We would build through these changes.

Where a lip protected our crowns, we would add to it. Where the flow delivered more food, new tubes would gather. At an exposed corner, growth would remain low. Some of us would fail there, leaving small cups that caught silt and made the next attempt easier.

Eventually our outer edge would reach the neighboring plate.

The gap would be narrow when the animal lay flat. We would extend across it in that position, laying down a little material at a time. There would be good water there. The gap drew a thread of current through our newest chambers.

When the animal bent, the thread widened.

Our connections stretched. A few tore and closed over. Others held. Growth continued along them until the two sides were joined by a low, uneven ridge.

I would be somewhere inside that ridge.

My crown would open between taller crowns. I would receive less food than those at the leading edge, but I would be better sheltered when the animal scraped beneath a ceiling. A worm would have its hole nearby. Its withdrawal would shake my tube, and I would close each time, even after hundreds of such disturbances.

The animal would carry us through passages we could not enter independently.

For several feeding pulses it might rest beside a spring. We would grow quickly there. The soft tissue between our chambers would thicken; the rims would become clean and pale. Then it would move into the old quarter and press itself beneath an arch where little reached us.

Our crowns would remain out longer.

The animal’s rear would lie partly across the floor. Shrimp would climb over us. Silt would settle in the gap we had bridged.

By then, bending would pull against our foundation.

The disturbance would reach us from below: first a slight strain, then a stronger one. The animal would gather its foot and turn. One plate would slide beneath the next, except where our ridge held them together.

For a moment the whole colony would be under pressure.

Then something would give.

Several tubes would break near their bases. Water would enter places that had been enclosed since we first grew across the gap. The animal would continue turning, and the ridge would part unevenly, leaving a rough edge on each plate.

I would survive on one side.

For a while, nourishment would leak from the broken connections beside me. My crown would stay withdrawn. The exposed tissue would contract and seal, and the surviving members would begin covering the damage.

Across the gap, the rest of the colony would do the same.

When the plates lay flat again, we would be close enough for the broken edges to touch. But they would no longer fit. A fragment had fallen away. A shrimp would work through the new opening, finding material our crowns could not take.

Water would reach my chamber more freely.

I would open.

That is where I think I would find myself: in a small tube near a broken edge, carried low through the city, alive among a handful of other surviving bodies.

The animal would settle beneath the growing wall. Its foot would spread against the stone, and the current would steady around us. For a long interval our foundation would scarcely move.

We would begin adding to it again.
