I would be in the upper half of a doorway, where the water changes temperature.

Not passing through it. Staying there.

One shoulder would rest against the frame, and my feet would find two shallow places in the opposite wall. Below my chest, the water would be warm enough to give my body a little color. Above it, I would be difficult to distinguish from the room beyond. Someone looking in might see an arm and a narrow part of a face, then decide they had seen a root crossing behind the plaster.

I would have chosen the doorway because two currents meet there without quite mixing. One comes up from the market cellars. The other descends from a broken window, carrying the coolness of the lagoon. Between them is a wavering seam. It moves over my body so slowly that I can follow it with a fingertip.

There would be a room behind me, but it would not yet be my room.

Its cupboard doors would stand open. A basin would lie on its side beneath the table, with a fern growing through the drain. On the wall, a rectangular patch would remain lighter than the surrounding plaster. Whatever hung there would be gone.

I would return to this place often enough that someone would eventually leave an object for me.

A short piece of glazed pipe, perhaps. Blue on the outside, white within. It would fit into the doorway’s lowest hinge socket and give my heel a more comfortable purchase.

I would know who had brought it by the disturbance left in the fine growth around the socket. I would not go to thank them immediately. They would find me using it the next time they passed.

***

One of my hands would be darker than the other.

As a child, I would have held the same warm ring for too long. The person carrying me would have worked beneath the pumping house, and I would have learned the machinery first through that hand: the gathering pressure before a rod withdrew, the uneven warmth of a bearing, the brief firmness of a pipe when water entered it from below.

Later, I would have preferred the upper passages. The rest of my body would have lengthened and cleared. But the hand would remain thick, with pale deposits at the knuckles.

It would be useful. It could hold a loose edge of metal without tearing. It could feel a change through several layers of growth.

It would also catch in places my other hand could pass through easily.

I would dislike this.

There would be an opening behind the northern exchange through which most people of my shape could slide without turning. I would have to lead with the dark hand, bend the wrist, then draw the elbow after it. Every time, I would think of widening the opening. Every time, once through, I would find the idea less important.

Someone younger would eventually show me a better way.

I would use it when alone before using it in front of them.

***

My work would begin with a mistake.

A thin root would have grown across a clearance near the former market cellars. I would ease it aside, believing I was helping the water reach a room where the thread on a banister had gone slack.

The thread would lift.

For a while, everything would appear improved.

Then someone from the house above would come down and lay their fingers against the wall beside mine. They would stay until I felt what they felt: the new current entering their ceiling, lifting the warm water from a recess where a young child rested.

I would have opened one room by making another uncomfortable.

We would spend a long time there together. Neither of us would pull the root back into place. It would already have begun adjusting to its new position. Instead, we would find a fallen shutter in the adjoining passage and move it by small degrees until part of the water turned downward again.

Afterward, I would visit both rooms.

This would become my work, though not immediately, and not because anyone gave it a name. I would learn which openings could be widened, which needed to remain narrow, which obstructions had become useful. People would begin waiting for me beside passages where the water had changed.

I would carry a little wedge of green glass with one blunted edge. For a long time it would be my only tool.

Sometimes there would be nothing to do. A room might be cooling because the rooms beneath it were cooling. A thread might hang still because a household had stopped using the passage whose small disturbances once moved it.

I would have to learn to leave such threads alone.

***

The room behind my doorway would become mine gradually.

First I would turn the basin upright.

Much later, I would remove the fern from its drain, dividing the roots carefully and finding another crack where it could hold. I would leave the basin empty. Its curved interior would offer a place to rest my dark hand without pressing the swollen knuckles against stone.

I would use it more than I expected.

Someone would begin visiting me there: a person who worked along the upper windows, long-bodied and nearly clear, with a cloudy band across one shoulder. They would dislike the warm lower current. I would make a place for them beside the cupboard.

They would never arrive by the same route twice.

At first I would assume they were checking the passages. Later I would understand that they simply liked arriving from different directions. Once they would come through the ceiling, dislodging enough silt to keep us both motionless until it settled.

I would find this irritating.

They would return through the doorway after that, for several visits. Then, one pale afternoon, I would look up and find their face appearing slowly between the ceiling roots.

By then I would have cleared a small space there.

***

I would sometimes go to the boundary.

Not far beyond it. Just to the last house in which a hanging thread still leaned toward the engines.

There would be objects in the sand outside: a bottle neck, a length of chain, the edge of something enameled. Most would stay where they were. Occasionally the circulation would loosen one and bring it within reach.

I would retrieve things without being sure why.

A porcelain knob could be fitted to a tool. A piece of wire could secure a panel. But there would also be a small lid with no vessel, and a brass letter detached from a sign, and a spoon so worn that its bowl admitted light.

I would keep these in the open cupboard.

One day the person from the upper windows would take the lid out and turn it over. On its underside would be three shallow marks.

They would carry it away.

Much later, when I passed their household, I would see it fixed beside a window latch. I would not know whether they had recognized it or merely found a use for it. For some time I would wait for them to tell me.

Eventually I would touch the three marks myself when I visited.

***

There would be one clearance I kept open although hardly anyone used it.

It would run behind the southern exchange, below the handrail traveled by the white eel. Too narrow for an adult to turn around in, it would end at a little window facing the buried houses.

As a child, I would have waited there for the eel.

The window would have been clearer then. Beyond it, the sand would have shown a roof ridge and part of a chimney. I would have watched those things while keeping one hand on the rail, hoping to feel the eel before seeing it.

I would never have succeeded.

Now a root would repeatedly enter the passage from above. I would guide it toward the window jamb. Mineral growth would narrow the lowest part of the opening, and I would rub it away with my dark thumb.

There would be no practical reason to preserve the route. The water could reach the window by other ways.

Once, while I was working, a child would arrive behind me. I would have to withdraw carefully to let them pass. They would settle where I had settled long before, one curled hand on the rail.

I would remain outside until they had learned the way back.

I might then neglect the passage for a while, feeling that it had become somebody else’s place. When I returned, the root would be across it again.

***

I would not usually sit on the courtyard bench. I would like knowing it was occupied.

From my doorway I could feel, very faintly, the person there making room for the unfolding leaf. Their movement would reach me through a rail, a wall, and the loose hinge beneath my heel. By then it would be too slight to identify unless I had been waiting for it.

Some afternoons I would wait.

On others I would be busy with a stubborn shutter, or visiting the upper windows, or holding my dark hand inside the basin while the cooler water passed over it.

And sometimes, after the person at the bench had moved, I would shift a little in my doorway too.

The glazed pipe would turn beneath my heel. A leaf below the sill would incline. Somewhere in the adjoining house, someone would feel that I was home.
