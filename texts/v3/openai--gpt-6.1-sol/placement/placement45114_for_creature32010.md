I think I would first be found under the observation stair.

Not beneath the whole platform, where the pipes make a considerable shelter, but under the fourth tread, in the narrow space between its folded edge and the bracket that supports it. There is water there even when nobody has recently climbed the stairs. It gathers on the underside of the metal and moves toward a screw, where it becomes too heavy to remain.

From that place I could see a strip of the nearest basin. The ivory animals would pass through it as legs, then as a brief enclosure of warm skin, then as legs again. Beyond them, occasionally, there would be the bright horizontal line of the water.

I would know the stair differently when someone stood on it.

The bracket would change its pressure against the tread. Not enough to move visibly. Enough to make one part of my shelter narrower and another part easier to enter. A person pausing above me would open a small space beside the screw.

For a while, that is all I can tell about myself: I am somewhere warm, noticing that a space has opened.

Then there is the matter of how I notice.

I don’t think I have eyes at first. Or rather, I have them, but they are folded against something, and light is less immediate than the contact along my back. My back is spread over the metal. My underside is gathered into several small, firm places that can hold or release independently.

When the pressure changes, I do not receive it as a single sensation. One end of me knows before the other. Between them the change travels through a soft interior, arriving with enough delay that I can tell which way it came.

I am longer than I seemed.

What looked like a dark wet line under the stair has a head at one end, tucked around the bracket, and another part extending almost to the next support. I can shorten myself. When I do, pale transverse folds appear along my sides. When I stretch, they close, leaving a smooth gray surface with a faint shine.

There are limbs, but not the sort that would let me stand above the ground. They come in closely spaced pairs. Each ends in a blunt divided pad, and each can turn far enough to hold the underside of a lip while the next reaches around it. On a flat surface I would be slow. At an edge I can move readily.

My head is small. It is not immediately clear where it begins.

When I turn it out from behind the bracket, two dark eyes separate from the gray of my body. They face a little sideways. Between them is a short, soft projection that touches the screw without opening. Below it, my mouth unfolds against the metal.

There is food there. A little, and then a little more as I remove it.

But I would not stay under that tread merely because it feeds me.

I would stay because footsteps pass overhead.

Each step reaches the stair in a different way. A light walker loads the front edge and leaves it quickly. Someone tired puts weight down slowly, sometimes through both feet at once. Someone looking out across the plain becomes still on the landing, and the whole stair holds a new tension for several minutes.

I do not know what they are looking at. I know when they turn.

My feet adjust before their next step.

***

I would come out when the platform had been empty for some time.

First my head, then a few inches of body, then more than a person watching would have expected. I would descend along the inside edge of the stringer, close enough to the metal that my shadow scarcely separates from me.

At the bottom I would hesitate.

There is a distance of perhaps two finger-widths between the stair and the raised lip of a ceramic plate. Water fills it. Fish pass through the shallow opening, and one lies there now, its color divided between the dull metal above and the pale floor beneath.

I extend my head. The fish turns one of its upper eyes toward me.

Neither of us moves for a little while.

Then I lower the front of my body into the water, feel for the ceramic with two pairs of feet, and cross. The rest follows in a low arch. The fish stays where it is.

I have crossed there before. There is a sheltered route along the plate, under a pipe, and through a passage between two broad mounting blocks. I can take it without exposing more than my head.

Today I would go the other way.

A person has been sitting at the basin’s edge. The water is still returning to the place their heel occupied. One hand rests on a low rail.

I approach along the underside.

Their hand warms a short length of metal. The warmth spreads unevenly: more at the palm, less where a finger scarcely touches. I stop below the smallest finger and flatten my head against the rail.

They may feel nothing.

They may feel a faint movement, as though water has slipped beneath their hand.

If they lift it, I hold still. My eyes, turned outward now, can see the shape above me, though not all at once. An arm. A patch of clothing. The dark underside of a face against the ceiling.

If the hand returns, I move farther along the rail.

I would not climb onto it. Skin offers warmth, but no reliable edge. It yields beneath my pads. The slight movements continue even when the person is trying to be still.

Instead I remain under the little finger until they leave.

For a while afterward, the rail is warmer there.

I stay until I can no longer distinguish that place by touch.

***

I think I would have a pale line on one side of my head.

Not an injury the machinery has failed to repair. Something in the way my skin grew. From one angle it would resemble a scratch on the rail behind me, except that it would move when I moved.

There would be others of my kind, though I would seldom meet them face to face.

On the underside of a bridge, I might find a length of metal recently crossed by another body. The water there would cling in closely spaced bands, one for each fold. I would follow it to a joint and find the other animal resting inside, with only the last few pairs of feet visible.

I could fit beside it.

For some time we would lie facing opposite directions. Changes in the bridge would reach us both, but from different ends. When it began to move, the release of its pads would travel along my flank.

I might remain after it had gone.

There would be smaller ones, too, so short that the whole animal could rest within the curve of a washer. They would have disproportionately large heads and only a few folds. A small one could follow me along a rail and then stop where I continued, having found everything it needed in the shadow of a fastening.

I do not think I would guide it onward.

***

Eventually I would descend.

Under the observation platform is a service door, and around the door is a seam wide enough for my head. Beyond it, a cable turns down into a shaft. The cable’s covering has a fine raised braid beneath the surface, something my pads can hold.

Here I can move without flattening myself.

My body hangs slightly away from the cable. Several pairs of feet bear my weight while the others reach down. The lights are far apart. Between them my eyes fold back, and I travel by the small ridges beneath my soles.

I pass a warm outlet where transparent worms turn within a tube.

Lower down, the cable approaches a broad passage. Something pale moves across it. I stop with every foot fastened.

The animal’s flank fills the opening. Its skin is deeply folded near the bases of its limbs, smoother between them. It moves slowly, making contact with the passage wall in a succession of places. Air comes up around it, carrying warmth and a smell I have not met on the plain.

There is room for me on the cable. There is room for it in the passage.

I wait until it has passed.

Then I go a little farther, to where the cable enters a collar and the collar meets a structural beam. Water has collected in the upper curve. I drink there, placing my mouth at the edge without disturbing the whole bead.

I could remain. The light is dim, the surfaces warm. Nothing drives me upward.

But after resting, I turn.

It takes time to bring my long body around. For a while I am folded beside myself, feet exchanging their holds. Then my head points toward the distant squares of light.

By the time I reach the stair, someone is standing on the platform.

I climb into the space beneath the fourth tread. My back meets the metal. The pressure is unfamiliar: a little more weight on one side, a slight repeated movement on the other.

Above me, a shoe shifts.

I loosen two feet and set them down again farther along the bracket. My head rests beside the screw, where another bead is forming.
