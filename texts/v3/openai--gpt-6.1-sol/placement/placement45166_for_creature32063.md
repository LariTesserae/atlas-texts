At first I would be the one whose hand keeps lifting.

The flat stone is colder than my palm. After a while the cold becomes an ache, and I draw my hand into my sleeve. Outside, the inhabitant resumes placing grains in the seam.

I put my hand back.

Nothing follows that I can distinguish. The stone does not warm. There is no answering pressure. But now I notice that I have been resting only the heel of my hand on it, keeping my fingers slightly raised, as though touching something that might move.

I let the fingers settle.

One does not reach the surface. Beneath it there is a shallow groove, too broad to be a crack. I move my hand until the finger lies in it.

That is the first adjustment I make which is not solely for comfort.

***

I would not become like them quickly.

For a long time I would carry my body badly through the place. I would strike my knee against a threshold that had looked farther away. I would take a step toward a shadow and find no stone beneath it. I would put my whole weight on my hands when rising, disturbing something small under the edge of a slab.

The inhabitants would sometimes watch.

Once, after I had crossed an open shelf, one would touch the ground where my foot had been. It would hold its fingers there, then place them beneath its own jaw.

I would look back at the shelf. There would be no footprint.

I would begin to walk more slowly, without knowing whether that was what it had meant.

The pale animal would be easier company. It would come beneath my overhang and lie just beyond the reach of my knees. Its eye would remain open until I stopped looking at it.

I would learn to sit without making the small noises with which I usually announce that I am uncomfortable.

The discomfort would remain.

***

My shelter would be the first hollow, but I would spend much of my time beside the two uprights where the roof was missing.

There is more room there for my shoulders. Between the stones, the wind-note has an uneven edge. It does not change pitch, exactly. It seems to come nearer on one side than on the other.

The inhabitant who tended the moss would sometimes be there. Sometimes the space would contain only its dark crescent against the ground.

I would sit outside that crescent.

There would be a loose stone beneath my thigh, rounded on top, with a narrow underside that pressed painfully through my clothes. Each time I sat down I would shift away from it. Each time, after an hour or more, I would discover that I had shifted back.

Eventually I would lift it.

Its socket would be full of dry grains.

I would clear out a few with my little finger and lower the stone again. It would sit a little deeper, almost level with the ground.

When I next came, the inhabitant would be resting one wrist on it.

It would not move the wrist to make room for me. I would sit on the other side.

***

The first thing I could do well here would be to hear things falling.

A grain released from a palm. A fragment of dry moss breaking beneath a finger. The last drop leaving the end of a root.

The great noise would not hide these sounds from me. They would stand close to my body, startlingly plain.

I would look up at each one.

The inhabitants would notice this before I understood that it was unusual.

One would hold a small flake above the ground and release it while I was facing away. I would turn. It would recover the flake, wait, and release it again.

The second time I would point to where it had fallen.

It would pick it up and leave.

For several days I would think I had failed to understand an invitation.

Then, beneath another shelter, I would find the same inhabitant holding a plant stem away from the wall. Below the stem was a scatter of pale fragments. It would wait until I had settled beside it, then loosen its fingers.

Something would fall.

I would hear it land behind a stone and put my hand there. A small piece of root would adhere to my fingertip, white and almost weightless.

The inhabitant would take it from me. It would examine the broken end with its lips, then lay the stem into a different channel.

I would stay until it finished.

***

I would become useful in these small, uncertain ways.

Not everywhere. There would be chambers whose entrances I could never find twice, and tasks from which a hand would quietly exclude mine. I would still have to drink with my mouth. The plants would not be enough for me, or would be enough only after a hunger I had not learned to bear.

Some days I would do nothing but sit.

On one of those days, a young inhabitant would settle on the far side of the missing roof. It would have a thin length of root stretched between its hands. It would bend the root, release it, bend it again.

I would rest my palm on the upright beside me.

The young one would stop.

Under my hand there would be a tremor so faint that I would at first attribute it to my own pulse.

I would press harder. It would disappear.

I would ease my palm away until only the fingertips touched.

There it would be again.

The young one would be watching my wrist. After a while it would put down the root and touch the other upright.

We would remain like that until my arm tired.

When I lowered it, the young one would lower its own.

***

I think my body would change, though not into a copy of theirs.

The hair on the backs of my hands would collect pale dust. My nails would become blunt from working in the seams. The skin along one hip would darken where I leaned against the same stone.

I would still be too tall for most shelters.

There would always be a part of me outside: a knee beyond the overhang, a shoulder exposed to the rain, my head above a slab beneath which everyone else could sit upright. I would learn where to put these excesses. A dry notch for the knee. A place beside the rain where the shoulder could remain wet without wetting the moss.

Perhaps that would be what I was here: a being whose fit was made in several places rather than one.

I would sleep in the first hollow. I would drink near the stream. I would sit between the uprights to feel the fine movements passing through their bases. And when something fell into a place too narrow for an inhabitant’s broad fingers, I would lie flat and reach for it.

My arms would still be my own arms.

***

Much later, I would find a cushion of moss between the two uprights.

It would not occupy the dark crescent. It would be on my side, its center pressed into the slight depression left by my heel.

There would also be a narrow flake set upright in a seam.

I would recognize it as one I had recovered from beneath a ledge. I had given it to an inhabitant because I could not find where it belonged.

Now the wind would move against its edge. A thin tone would pass through the stone under my hand.

I would sit down carefully.

The tone would continue. Beneath it I would feel something less steady, interrupted by the shifting of my weight. I would try several positions, then return to the first.

Outside the uprights, the pale animal would lower itself onto the ground. Its whiskers would touch my exposed ankle.

Across from me, the inhabitant would lift a grain from its palm and hold it over a hairline opening.

I would wait for the sound.

It would wait too.

Then it would let go.
