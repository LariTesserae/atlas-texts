At first I would put myself beneath the loose brick, where the heat is gentler and the water arrives already strained through grit. There is room there for something thin. A body could press against the brick’s underside and leave the floor almost empty.

But the brick has been moved.

I would be beneath the place where it used to be, or approaching that place from the cabinet: the narrow end that comes out in spring and stops on the exposed concrete.

Not the whole animal. Not quite a separate one.

Behind me, through a crack too narrow to admit a matchstick, there would be more of me than I could bring forward. A broad part holding water beneath the cabinet. A passage flattened around a buried fixing. Farther away, folds opening under the plinth. What happens there reaches this end after a delay, diminished or altered by the route it takes.

I would not know the court as a room. There would be no far wall, no three arches arranged together, no stair seen through steam. There would be surfaces I could continue along and surfaces where continuation became costly. The underside of a pipe would be a long possibility. A dry joint would narrow the world abruptly.

Some distances would be measured by how much of myself I had to leave exposed to cross them.

The place beneath the old brick had once been inexpensive. Little water was lost there. The overhead surface stayed close, holding a thin layer of damp against my skin. Small animals entered along its edges. Their movements pressed briefly through me; sometimes one stopped moving, and afterward there was something to take in.

Now I would arrive and find warmth without shelter.

I would remain.

Not because I pictured the brick above me. I would have no such picture. But the route into that space would still be easy to begin. The part behind the cabinet would still fill and lengthen in the same direction. Water would move down the same narrow channels. My tip would reach the place at which, before, it had been possible to spread.

There I would make myself wider by a little.

The concrete would take moisture from me. Grit would adhere. Nothing would press back from above.

For several days, perhaps, this would be enough to keep me there.

***

I think I would have a poor boundary.

A larva caught in a fold would remain distinctly troublesome for a while, bending and striking against the soft edges. Then its movement would cease. Later there would be material passing inward, and later still a small hard piece worked out onto the floor.

A grain of sand could stay longer than the larva. A washer might become the shape of my skin without ever becoming part of what sustained it.

A mineral plate would accumulate upon me so slowly that there would be no first moment of carrying it. The burden would disclose itself when a fold failed to lift. I would pull against it, rest, pull again. Eventually the living surface would come free, leaving the plate in almost the same position.

For a time, anyone looking could still mistake it for me.

I would be less certain about the pieces the workers cut away.

The blade would meet an exposed length. Everything nearby would tighten at once. Water would be drawn back through the passage, and the cut edge would shrink into its own thickness.

But in the bucket, the removed piece would settle.

Its edge would find the curved meeting of wall and bottom. If water covered it, some folds would open. It would begin making use of a place I could no longer reach.

I would not receive this as news. Nothing would return from it. Yet the piece would continue doing what, under the plinth, I called continuing.

Perhaps I would be that piece instead.

For a few hours I would inhabit the bucket’s seam, taking in the weak residue of whatever had last been carried there. Every movement of the bucket would bring water against me from a different direction. The bottom would be smooth, with no gap to enter. I could spread, but there would be nowhere to get under.

Then the worker might tip me onto the excavated earth.

I would lie among the dried plates of clay, with my upper surface exposed and my folds pressed into dirt. The water inside me would become important in a new way. I would spend it moving downward.

Whether I reached the damp base would depend on very little: how I landed, which plate shifted under my weight, how long the tools remained upstairs.

I cannot quite place myself there for good. That feels like an interruption, though it might last years.

The end beside the cabinet seems closer.

***

The pumps would be the largest recurrent change in my body.

Their first stroke would arrive through the concrete before the water changed. The narrow connection behind me would flatten. Farther in, something broad would settle against a hollow. I would feel the altered pressure as a restriction, then as the usual condition.

In the pauses, I could open a little.

I would have no reason to count the strokes, but I would come to fit them. A fold would begin closing before the next pressure reached it. Water would be held where it could wait. Some parts of me would scarcely move at all.

When the pumps stopped for maintenance, this fit would become briefly wrong.

Passages ordinarily held narrow would swell. An unsupported length would lower into a gap. My end on the concrete might gain enough water to advance, just when footsteps were approaching.

The footsteps would not be a person to me. They would be a sequence of disturbances with long spaces between them, then a weight that stayed. Light would follow, irritating the exposed surface. A tool might touch the floor nearby.

I would withdraw before knowing which disturbance mattered.

Elsewhere, under the plinth, I might still be opening.

There would be no single place in me that decided what the whole court meant.

***

I would keep returning to the missing brick.

Other ends would turn away from dry surfaces. One would leave a newly cleaned band around an inspection cover. Another would abandon a seam after detergent passed through it. Much of me would be capable of changing.

This end would follow the cabinet crack, cross the short exposed distance, and stop.

Perhaps the first successful passage had thickened something along that route. Perhaps there was still a little more moisture there than on either side. Perhaps each return made the next easier to begin.

The cause need not be hidden deep in the body. It might be spread through the entire approach: the slope of the crack, the way water gathered behind it, the old branching of my channels.

I would wait until the exposed edge darkened.

Then I would draw back slowly enough that a rim of grit remained in the shape I had occupied. Water would retreat through me toward the cabinet. The last part to leave would be the blunt tip, briefly rounded as it entered the crack.

Beneath the plinth, folds would continue feeding. A rat would step across the darker lobe at the pipe elbow. The pump would start, and several narrow places would tighten.

By the next spring, the grit on the open concrete would have been disturbed by runoff, insects, a worker’s shoe.

I would come out along the same route, carrying enough water to reach it.
