I would be found below the house, where the descending road makes its first turn out of sight.

There is a retaining wall there, mostly intact. One stone has shifted forward. Behind it, a narrow space remains between the wall and the mountain, and water enters this space through a crack too small to support a grove. A few pale roots reach into it. They do not grow much, but they do not die.

For a long time, this is all that would be visible of me: roots entering a gap, a flat stone slightly out of place.

I would not yet be able to cross the road.

When the current strengthens, a little grit rises behind the stone. It hangs there after the other grit has settled. A strand catches on the stone’s upper edge, and sometimes its free end points into the current rather than away.

The house is above me. I cannot see its doorway from here.

I know when the curtain rises because a torn piece of leaf comes down the road. Not every time. Often enough that I begin to wait for it.

***

My first work is keeping a second opening clear.

The water enters through the mountain crack and leaves through the retaining wall. Between these openings lies a pocket of old leaves. If the leaves settle against the outlet, the roots become motionless. A pale film spreads over them.

I move one leaf.

It takes a long time. The leaf has softened until its veins are stronger than the material between them, and whenever I pull, some part comes away. I have no useful distinction between pulling with myself and pulling with the water. I gather a little more tightly behind its stem. The grit rises. The strand stiffens.

Eventually the leaf turns edge-on.

Water passes it.

For a while afterward, I can reach the front of the shifted stone.

There is something beyond the gap that belongs to me, although I do not know how long it has been there. A short piece of worked wood lies in the road vegetation. One end has been cut into a shallow notch. The other is dark and swollen.

I spend several changes of the roof trying to bring it closer.

Then I stop trying to bring it closer and move toward it instead.

This is how I leave the wall.

***

The wooden piece is not a tool I know how to use.

It becomes the lower part of me. The strand passes around the notch. A small wedge of stone rests above it, and the roots I have brought away from the gap spread along the wood.

I have taken only a few roots. Most remain behind the wall.

For some time I return there whenever I lose my shape. I settle into the pocket, hold the outlet clear, and wait until my pieces rise again. The gap receives me easily. Outside it, I must attend to everything at once.

A current across the road pulls the wood one way and the stone another. The strand is not long enough. I lower myself into the vegetation before it can break.

Once, while doing this, I become caught beneath a broad leaf.

I remain there through a long dim hour, held flat against the paving. The leaf claps slowly above me. Each time it lifts, I can see the next stretch of road.

There is another stone within reach.

I take it.

It is less smooth than my first stone and harder to carry. I lose it twice before I learn where it can rest.

With it, I reach the channel below the turn.

There the water draws my pieces apart without scattering them. A narrow hollow opens between the wood and the stones. Sediment enters it, and one grain rises almost to the top before descending again.

I stay beside the channel long after I could have left.

***

Others pass.

I do not know whether they recognize me at first. One pauses upstream, carrying a curved branch with several leaves still attached. Its lower stones are pale. Near its hollow hangs a length of cord folded back on itself so many times that it resembles a thick root.

It settles beside a loose piece of paving.

I settle too.

Nothing passes between us. The channel runs through both our frames, and for a while the branch’s leaves touch my wood.

When it leaves, my roots remain open farther down the road than they have ever done before.

Another comes with a rod laid along its side. This one puts the rod beneath a stone at the channel’s edge and lifts. The stone moves less than its own width. A thin run of water enters the road vegetation.

The inhabitant waits.

I move into the new run, and it withdraws its rod.

For several seasons I believe this is something it did for me.

Later I find the same narrow run choked with fallen growth. I clear it as well as I can. The water resumes its course toward the wall where I began.

I never determine whether the inhabitant knew I was there.

***

I would become a small traveler.

Not small enough to pass unnoticed in a channel, but too small to sustain anything like a standing body. My wood would usually lie obliquely, one end close to the ground. The stones would shift around it according to the slope. In still water, my upper piece would settle until I resembled something laid down and forgotten.

I would lose the rough stone on my first crossing of the basin.

There would be no sudden separation. It would fall behind by degrees. I would turn back once and draw it forward. The second time, the vegetation would be too thin to give me purchase.

I would wait beside it.

Then I would lower it into a depression where loose grit could collect around its base.

For years afterward, I would find it there.

I would not always take the same road. Beyond the basin there is a shelter with three plant beds, and a spring that leaves red stains on the stone. Warm water enters beneath the shelter’s rear wall. A crooked roof slab keeps most drifting leaves away from the beds, but some enter through a break at one corner.

These leaves are easier to lift than the one behind the retaining wall.

I learn to slide my notched wood under their stems.

I learn to carry two at once.

A pale shoot comes loose while I am working. Its roots trail across the opening in my middle. I hold it against myself, uncertain where to put it, until another inhabitant arrives and waits beside the lowest bed.

I place the shoot there.

The other presses a pebble against its roots.

Afterward, I leave the shelter with enough shape to cross a patch of bare rock. My stones remain separated from the ground. I cannot keep them there all the way to the road, but I travel farther than I expect.

***

Eventually I climb to the house.

At the threshold, the water draws my upper stone inward. I resist at first. The curtain is falling, and its leaves touch the wood I carry.

Inside, the inhabitant is spread against the rear wall.

The bowl rests in its niche.

I have seen shaped wood before, but none so thin. A piece of the inhabitant lies along the rim, fitted to its curve. The short cord disappears among pale fibers.

I remain just inside the doorway. The clear strip is wide enough for both of us, though not for us to pass without touching.

After some time, a stone leaves the wall.

It settles nearer the niche.

I move forward by the space this opens.

There is little to do in the house. The floor carries an even layer of silt. Water keeps its own passage clear. Nothing has fallen onto the bowl.

I wait until I can hold myself together without bracing against the threshold.

Then I leave.

On my next visit, I bring the crooked stem of a plant from the shelter. It has several fine roots and no leaves. I lay it beneath the rooted branch on the roof.

It does not take hold.

I find it later at the turn in the road and carry it back to the warm crack.

***

When the seam opens overhead, I am in the basin.

For the first time I see that the stone I left there is red.

I settle beside it. Light reaches its upper face and the shallow depression beneath. The grit around it is red too, though I had carried a white stone and a black one past this place many times without noticing any difference between them.

I lift it.

My body has changed enough to hold it now. The roots along my wood are thicker. I have acquired another strand, long enough to pass around the stone and return to the notch.

I carry it as far as the house.

The curtain is raised.

The inhabitant stands at the threshold with the bowl held level. Its frame is broader here than it is inside. The basin lies beyond it, newly visible down to the first dark shelf.

I stop beside the doorway.

There is no place to set the red stone without obstructing the clear strip, so I continue to carry it.

The bowl remains out over the threshold. Water passes across its worn rim. A faint brightness rests on the inner wood, changing whenever the leaves move.

My strand touches the bowl’s cord.

Neither of us draws away.

When the curtain begins to fall, the inhabitant turns inward. One of its lower pieces catches against the sill. I put my notched wood beneath it and raise it just enough.

The bowl passes into the house.

I follow.

***

That is where I would most often be found afterward.

Not always inside. I would still return to the retaining wall, and to the beds, and to the red spring. My shape would continue to change. Some winters I would be little more than the worked wood, two stones, and the long strand gathered close around them.

But I would learn the narrow place beside the niche where the water is sufficient for me to rest.

When the curtain rises, I would move out of that place.

The other would gather around the bowl.

I would keep one piece near the sill until it had crossed, then settle beside the doorway, low enough that the basin remained unobstructed.

Sometimes a leaf would come through and lodge against my wood. I would let it stay until the bowl turned inward.

Then I would lift it clear.
