At first I would put myself near the water.

Not in the basin, exactly. The basin is too exposed in ways that are difficult to notice: its surface receives whatever falls, and the narrow approach, which looks like protection, admits a mouth. I would be farther underneath, where its weight rests on another stone. There must be a seam there. Water would enter it without making a drop.

I imagine a dark line, then something within the line.

For a while I have no size. I am the coolness that persists beneath the basin while warmth passes around it. I know when the predator drinks because the water withdraws from the edge. I know when it leaves because the stone stops carrying its weight.

But coolness cannot move closer to a wet place when it begins to dry.

I would need a body.

Small enough that a feather could shelter it. Narrow enough to turn inside a fracture. At first I imagine it soft, almost transparent, with the warmth showing through. Then I find myself wanting a harder back. There are feet everywhere here, even in the places that look empty. A shifting grain can be a considerable event.

So: a small beetle, pale beneath a film of dark dust. No useful wings. Two close-fitting cases over a body that has never needed to enter the air.

I would live under the basin’s lower lip, where the stone stays wet after the surface above has gone dull.

My antennae would reach the world before I did.

One would follow the underside of the stone. The other would sweep through the gap beneath it. Sometimes their tips would find the same object from opposite sides, and I would pause with its size held between them: a seed, a curled scrap of skin, the end of another animal’s leg.

The chamber would be much larger than the part I could ever know. Even so, I would have routes.

There would be a ridge I crossed by putting three feet on either side. A patch of mineral crust that offered poor purchase. A passage where old droppings had fallen and dried into a low roof. Beneath that roof I could walk upright; beyond it, I would have to flatten myself and draw my legs closer.

I would eat at the edges of things.

A fallen feather would last a long time. First the material adhering to its shaft, then the softened tissue at its base, then whatever grew in the damp beneath it. I would return until the feather shifted or the place dried. When it was gone, I would still approach along the same route for a while, touching the bare stone where it had lain.

The predator would arrive through my feet.

Its passage would begin before any part of it came near me. Pressure would alter the contacts between the stones. A faint movement would travel along the underside of my shelter. I would stop with one foreleg lifted.

Then the ribs would scrape overhead.

Dust would loosen. A hair might enter the seam, bend, and withdraw. The basin stone would carry the animal’s weight differently as it reached the water.

I would wait through the lapping.

There would be no whole animal in my knowledge of it. There would be the disturbance, the warm smell, the immense soft obstruction briefly closing the way out. There would be intervals when it remained so still that I began moving again beneath it.

Once, I would emerge too soon.

A bare pad would be resting beside the seam. I would touch it with an antenna: a yielding surface, warmer than the floor, crossed by shallow lines. The antenna would continue its ordinary sweep. The surface would move.

I would turn sharply enough to strike my back against the roof.

Perhaps a claw would enter after me. Perhaps only the tip of a toe. Either would occupy nearly everything. I would press into a narrowing that I had used before, though never so quickly, and stay there while small grains settled against my legs.

Afterward I would take another route for several feedings.

The mountain would sometimes reach me as air moving the wrong way.

A cloud entering the hollow would leave moisture on surfaces too thin to hold a bead. My antennae would become heavier. The dust on my back would bind into patches. Paths that usually ended in dryness would open farther, and I would follow one beneath a tilted block.

There I would find fibers, feather fragments, and the warm underside of a nest.

The young predator’s squeak would pass through the materials around me. I would stop, then continue. There would be food here: softened scraps pressed into the bedding, a little skin, a dead mite. I would work beneath the outermost feather while the young animal moved above.

One of its large feet would push down.

The feather would bend until it touched my back. I would remain under it, legs folded as far as they could fold, with the pressure distributed along the closed cases of my wings.

Eventually the foot would lift.

I would finish feeding.

Somewhere in the seam below the basin I would leave eggs, each fixed against the sheltered side of a grain. I would not stay beside them. The wet edge would retreat, food would draw me elsewhere, and my routes would carry me past the place without always bringing me close enough to touch it.

For a time, what I had left there would be indistinguishable from what was already there.

Then one grain would acquire a pale moving edge.

By dawn, the large bodies would return to the ceiling. Their landings would reach the floor as scattered tremors. The predator would unfold its feet beneath the overhang. A feather would turn slowly down through the warm air and come to rest across two stones.

I would be below those stones, following a damp line.

When the feather landed, dust would fall into my passage. I would draw back, wait, and put one antenna forward again.
