I would begin beside a jar, in the cold part of the night, trying to decide whether the sound I had heard came from inside it or underneath.

The jar would be taller than my knee. Its rim would have a chip worn smooth enough that nobody remembered the breaking. A folded hide under one side would keep it upright. Above it, out of reach, water would collect along a seam and fall at irregular intervals.

There would be another vessel waiting beside me, empty, and a third that I had been told not to use.

At first I would not know why.

I would have been hired because I was awake when someone needed a watcher. Perhaps I had come down the western steps with a bag too light to set down confidently among other people’s bags. Perhaps I had lived in the city all my life and had recently lost the person who knew which work to send me toward. Either way, someone would have pointed out the seam, the vessels, the place where I could sit.

“When this one is full, put that one under.”

“And the third?”

“Leave it.”

I would spend much of the first night watching the forbidden jar.

By morning I would understand that it belonged to the household across the passage. They had a right to the water when the seam ran quickly enough to produce a second drip. Nobody had thought it necessary to explain this until I asked. The woman who came to collect the full vessel would seem surprised that I had imagined a mystery.

She would pay me with a portion of fat and a little salt. There had been an agreement about the fat. I would not know whether the salt was kindness, payment, or an error, and would be afraid to ask in case asking took it away.

For a while, that would be my place: underneath things other people owned.

***

I would not be especially good at reading the ground.

A bowl leaning on its peg would look to me like a poorly balanced bowl. I would hear a settling noise and turn toward the wrong court. When lifting a slab, I would anticipate the command and put my strength into it too soon. The person at the other end would have to stop and explain again.

I would remember the explanation. This would not make my hands understand it.

But I would become good with vessels.

Not all at once. I would overfill one, and move another too early, spilling a thin sheet of water into dust. Once I would leave a jar beneath a drip that had crept sideways, and return from fetching my coat to find the outside wet and the inside nearly empty.

After that I would stop staring only at the mouth of the jar. I would watch the streaks on its shoulders, the little dark mark appearing on the floor, the shape of a drop just before it let go of the ceiling.

A jar filling in darkness changes its voice. The first drops make a hard sound, almost a knock. Later they strike water. Later still the note grows small and close. I would learn which vessel was nearly full without lifting my head.

People would begin asking me to sit beside difficult drips. Not the plentiful ones with broad wet mouths, but those that divided, wandered, stopped for several nights and then returned somewhere inconvenient.

I would have a cushion made from three pieces of hide, badly matched. A bone scoop. A small lamp that I usually left unlit. My work bag would contain more spare padding than anyone thought reasonable.

“You’re moving jars,” someone would say. “Not putting them to bed.”

Then they would hand me a vessel with a cracked foot and ask whether I had anything soft.

***

The household I joined would not be the one whose water I watched.

It would belong to a woman who repaired hide fastenings, her sister, the sister’s son, and an elderly man whose connection to them nobody explained in the same way twice. I would first eat there because the boy brought me a bowl during a long watch and demanded its return before morning.

I would wash the bowl and bring it back.

The next time, I would bring back two.

Eventually there would be a place for my scoop among the things hanging above their cooking stone. I would continue carrying it in my bag for several days after they cleared the space. The woman who repaired fastenings would notice, but say nothing.

I would sleep in a narrow room across the court. When rain reached that room, I would sleep near the cooking place. When the boy grew too large for his corner, we would exchange.

There would be no particular evening on which I became one of them. But one afternoon someone would call from the other side of a wall, and I would leave my own bag to help carry theirs.

Afterward, when I returned for it, the elderly man would have it beside his feet.

“You left this,” he would say.

“I knew where it was.”

“So did I.”

***

The trouble would come over a seam that divided.

I would watch it happen slowly. For several nights the water would gather in a long bead before choosing where to fall. Then there would be two points. One would be weak, intermittent, barely worth placing a bowl beneath.

I would put a bowl beneath it anyway.

By the end of the week, that bowl would need emptying twice each night.

The owner of the larger drip would tell me to pour both into her vessels. The household across the passage would say the new water was theirs. Both would speak as if I had merely forgotten an arrangement.

I would know where the water fell. I would know when it began falling there. I would not know where a wall had stood before I was born.

At the hearing, someone would bring a tally bone. Someone else would bring an old piece of cord said to preserve a measurement. A man who had never stayed awake beneath the seam would explain its habits to me.

They would ask whether the two drips were really separate.

I would say that sometimes a thread of water ran between them along the ceiling.

They would ask whether this meant they were one drip.

I would say I did not know.

This answer would disappoint everyone.

For a few nights afterward, neither household would employ me. They would take turns watching their own jars and discover that certainty was easier to maintain in daylight.

Then the woman who owned the larger drip would send for me again. She would pay the ordinary amount, without salt.

I would go.

I would also tell the household across the passage whenever their bowl needed changing. This would be permitted under the eventual agreement, though neither side would like the manner in which I did it.

***

As I grew older, I would have a reputation for remembering small changes and forgetting important names.

I would know which jar rang strangely because its wall was thin, not because the ground beneath it was hollow. I would know that a certain grazer came to lick spilled water behind the washing court, and that its absence meant very little unless it stayed away for three nights. I would know where a wet line used to end because, years earlier, I had tried to sleep there and woken with one sleeve soaked.

Children would be sent to fetch me, and sometimes remain to listen.

I would teach them to tap a vessel lightly with a fingernail. Empty, partly full, almost full. They would prefer the loud empty note and strike too hard.

“Again,” I would say. “Less.”

One child would learn quickly and become intolerable about it. Another would hear no difference for weeks, then identify a full jar from the far side of a hide door.

Neither would necessarily become a watcher.

I might acquire a drip right of my own, a poor one beneath a ledge that required the vessel to be wedged close against a wall. I would argue its merits when buying it and its inconvenience whenever someone asked whether it made me prosperous.

On good nights it would give more than we needed. On bad nights I would sit beneath it long after my paid work was done.

***

Where would you find me?

Not always beside water.

Some afternoons I would sit in the market with a jar in my lap, smoothing a patch of hide around its base. I would be listening to an argument about the price of grease while pretending to attend only to my work. I would have dust on my coat and unusually clean fingertips. Someone would be waiting for me to finish, and I would be taking longer than necessary because the jar rested warmly against my legs.

At home, my things would occupy a shallow wall recess: scoop, lamp, spare cord, a little packet of salt gone hard. My sleeping mat would usually be rolled, though not always. I would have an opinion about where to keep the wedges that nobody followed.

And one dusk, carrying an empty vessel through a doorway, I would find that my shoulder brushed the lintel where it had never brushed before.

I would stop.

I would set the jar down somewhere it could not obstruct the passage. Then I would go back and put my hand against the stone.

From the cooking room, someone would ask what was taking me so long.

I would ask them to come and feel it too.
