I would be beneath the bench, but not one of the ivory animals around its feet. Those have places that fit them. I would live where the iron frame stops touching the wood.

There is a washer there, recessed into the underside of the seat. Once it lay flat. Repeated loading has tilted it slightly, and one edge has pressed into the grain. Beneath the other edge is a space no wider than a knife blade. The space opens toward the canal.

At first you might mistake me for material squeezed out of the joint: a gray lip, moist at its inner edge, with several dark points pressed against the iron. I would have very little visible back. Most of my body would lie along the bolt, inside the wood, in a passage enlarged by successive occupants. Whether I was one of those occupants or the continuation of them would be difficult to establish. A piece of old skin would still line the passage. I would sometimes eat it.

My feet would be arranged unevenly. The ones on the wood side would be broad and soft, fitted to the grain. Those against the iron would end in two hooks. I could loosen either side without falling, but not both.

The district would reach me differently through each surface.

Through the iron: the pump, the bench legs, the mouths at their bases.

Through the wood: weather, although there is so little of it; the faint work of joints swelling and drying; whatever touches the seat.

I would spend much of my life stretched between these accounts.

There would be food in the difference. When the frame flexed, a small quantity of dark liquid would rise along the bolt. I would take it into a slit behind my foremost feet. Transparent animals would gather there afterward, moving their sharp legs against my softer ones. Some would remove crystals before they could catch in the folds. Others would remain under my skin until the next flex brought them something to carry.

I would not direct them. Occasionally one would enter too deeply, and I would contract until it came out.

The bench would ordinarily carry only itself. That would be a narrow condition, familiar in every part of my body. During those hours I would lie almost flat, with my hooked feet holding the iron and the others resting lightly against the wood. Calls from the pumping house would pass through the bolt. I would answer by drawing a hard edge across its threads.

The answer would be faint. You could not hear it from above.

An animal at the nearest foot would tighten its mouth. Farther away, another would release a little moisture. There would be no need for anything larger to happen.

When someone sat down, the washer would turn.

The wood-side feet would meet the descending grain. My body would shorten; the folds along it would fill. For several moments I would become thicker than the place in which I lived.

This would hurt in a limited, familiar way. There would be a strip of tissue beside the bolt which had never quite recovered from the first time. Under pressure it would go white. I would pull that strip away from the iron before setting the rest of myself against it.

You might feel the resulting tremor through the seat.

I would learn a sitter by the pauses in their weight. Someone leaning forward would open my passage at one end while closing it at the other. Someone sitting very still would seem steady at first, but breathing would slowly become distinguishable. I would find the longer interval beneath the machinery and fit one small contraction into it.

I would not know that it was breathing.

I would know that certain loads returned without quite repeating.

Of all the ways the bench could be occupied, I would prefer weight a little to the left. There, the washer would lift enough to uncover a shallow groove in the wood. I could extend a soft part of my mouth into it. The groove would hold dampness longer than the bolt did, and its surface would be worn smooth.

A sitter who shifted to the right would close it again.

I would continue working. The preference would make no difference to the district.

Sometimes, while the bench was occupied, one of my young would leave.

They would form along my underside, pale knots with only their iron hooks developed. I would keep them there through many cycles of empty seat and loaded seat. A few would detach into the passage and remain beside me, taking up fluid I had failed to swallow. One would grow broad wood-feet early. It would push against me until I turned enough to let it reach the washer’s edge.

There would be nowhere prepared for it.

When weight came onto the bench, new spaces would briefly open in the frame. The young animal would enter one. It might return when the sitter moved. It might be caught somewhere from which returning was no longer possible. I would feel its movements for a while through the same piece of iron.

Then the movements would become too small to separate from everything else.

I would have left my own passage once.

A bench slat had split near the bolt. The split carried dampness into the wood and changed the pull on my feet. For several days I would have held farther inside, waiting for the familiar pressure to resume. Instead, something behind me would begin occupying the wet crack. Its back would bear against mine. It would grow without pushing quickly enough for me to push back.

Eventually I would loosen my iron feet.

Outside, the underside of the seat would seem immense. My broad feet would cling poorly to wood without the opposing surface of the bolt. I would move sideways, carrying my body in short folds. The canal’s light would reach around the edge of the bench. Every contraction would expose another wet surface.

Below me would be paving.

I would cross six inches before finding the washer at the next joint. That journey would leave one side of my body permanently darker. For a long time afterward, transparent animals would favor that side.

This is where I would be now.

If you sat above me, I would begin adjusting before you had settled. I would take in the fluid, brace the wood, and make my small reply along the bolt. After a while, your weight would become a condition I could almost rest within.

When you stood, the groove would close. The washer would ease down. My soft feet would follow the rising wood, expecting it to descend again.

For several contractions I would keep the interval you had brought.

Then I would flatten into the joint. One hooked foot would remain on a higher thread than before. There would be room to lower it, but I would leave it there.
