I would live above a passage that had been widened twice and was still too narrow for two loaded carts to pass.

My room would have a high window facing a wall. For perhaps twenty minutes each morning, reflected light from an upper roof would reach the ceiling. I would keep my table beneath it, although the light would miss the table entirely.

There would be a channel along the other wall, and a place where the plaster stayed dark. When I first moved in, I would put a bowl beneath the dark patch. The landlord would tell me that it had never dripped.

“It will,” I would say.

“Everything will.”

I would leave the bowl there.

At first I imagine myself employed to copy things: sluice records, inspection dates, the names of households displaced by a repair. There would be a dry office somewhere, a desk with a raised rim to stop pencils rolling off, work that could be finished before going home.

But I don’t think I would remain behind that desk. Too many of the entries would require someone to go downstairs and ask what they meant.

A wall reported as sound might have been sounded with a hammer, or merely judged unlikely to fall. A gate described as replaced might retain its old frame, its old lever, and the particular obstruction everyone assumed the new operator had been told about.

I would become the person sent to ask.

Not an engineer. I would know enough to keep out of the way of one. My work would concern the awkward interval between knowing something and getting it to the person who needed it.

The city would already have several arrangements for this. Shift books. Notices. Apprentices. Men and women who visited a pump house ostensibly to borrow a tool and stayed to explain something they had remembered in bed.

My position would have been created after an expensive mistake. Its official name would be long. People would call me the slate clerk.

***

I would carry a narrow metal case with paper inside, a piece of chalk, and three blunt pencils. There would also be a little folding stool. This would make me look more important than I was until I opened it.

Much of the work would consist of waiting for someone to finish.

A gallery keeper could not leave a failing bed to explain it to me. A mechanic would answer questions between strokes of a file. The woman responsible for a row of household overflows would talk readily, but only while walking, and would take me through passages so low that I would emerge with wet shoulders.

I would learn not to begin with “What should be recorded?”

People had already been asked that by too many visitors.

Instead I would ask, “Who do you tell?”

Or, “What does the next shift get wrong?”

Sometimes I would be given something useful. Sometimes I would be given a grievance thirty years old, involving a person who had since died. I would write down enough of both to distinguish them later.

I would not always succeed.

There would be a warning in the west galleries:

*Do not open after the second bell.*

Nobody would agree on the bell.

One worker would mean the end of a shift. Another would remember a signal once sounded during reservoir drawdown. A retired keeper would insist it referred to a crack in a brass casting: the first ringing sound was harmless; the second meant the thing was about to part.

For a while I would be pleased with these possibilities. I would set them down carefully, with names and dates.

Then someone would ask whether they could open the gate.

That would be the useful embarrassment of my profession.

***

I would have an unreasonable interest in warnings whose authors were dead.

Not because I believed old workers infallible. The oldest notices would include mistakes, obsolete instructions, and at least one prohibition maintained chiefly because everyone disliked the man who had wanted it removed.

But a warning worn faint by generations of thumbs would trouble me. Someone had found it worth saying. Someone else had found it worth leaving.

I would be inclined to preserve too much.

In my first year I would probably help fasten an old notice beside a rebuilt pump. It would advise operators never to let the return line run dry. A month later, an apprentice would keep water in the line during a procedure that required it to be emptied.

No one would be injured. The repair would take the rest of the day.

The foreman would bring me the notice.

“You checked that they used to need this,” she would say.

“Yes.”

“You didn’t check whether we do.”

After that, I would date everything.

I would ask what had been changed. I would ask to see it. There would still be occasions when I misunderstood an answer, but fewer when I mistook the age of an instruction for proof of its usefulness.

***

Outside my room, at the bend in the passage, a pale hauling animal would sleep above the pipes.

Its name would be Split. Nobody would be able to tell me whether it had been named for a broken coupling, a divided inheritance, or something unpleasant it had done with a load.

I would initially regard it as an impediment.

On wet nights it would extend one hind foot into the only dry part of the passage. Returning with my case under one arm, I would have to step over it. Split would watch without moving its head.

I would complain to the workshop that fed it.

The workshop would deny feeding it.

A bucket would be standing just inside their door.

Eventually I would learn where it preferred to be scratched. This would not improve its placement in the passage. It might make matters worse: Split would begin waiting for me with its jaw resting on the low wall, directly beneath the spot where I needed to put my hand while climbing the steps.

I would find myself explaining delays by saying the animal had looked uncomfortable.

This would be received without sympathy.

***

The work I would remember longest would concern a small sound.

In an old pumping station, an operator would tell me that a drain sometimes clicked before the main gauge moved.

“What kind of click?”

He would make a noise with his tongue.

His colleague would say it was nothing like that.

We would wait beside the drain. The pumps would work. Water would tremble in a loose cover plate. A bird somewhere inside the roof would drag something back and forth.

Nothing would click.

I would return twice, then a third time. On the third visit, we would hear it: two light contacts, so close together that I would at first take them for one.

The operator would lift a finger.

“That.”

There would be no immediate explanation. We would check the gauge and note the delay. Someone would suggest a loose fitting. Someone else would remember hearing it before a blockage, though they could not remember which year.

I would send the observation to the other stations supplied by the same line.

Most would report nothing. One would report a single knock. Another would reply that their drain had no cover and could not possibly make the sound described.

Weeks later, a repair crew would find a small plate moving in a housing farther uphill. It would take another visit to discover why it moved before the pressure changed at the station.

By then, the operator who had first mentioned it would be tired of discussing the matter.

I would have to catch him at lunch to tell him what we had found.

He would listen with his bowl held beneath his chin.

“Good,” he would say. “Can I stop listening for it?”

***

That is where I think I would belong: moving between people who had noticed something and people who could do something about it.

I would like the walks more than I admitted. The warm passages, the brief open crossings where lake light hurt my eyes, the workshops whose occupants knew I would accept tea if it was offered twice. I would become particular about the condition of public handrails. I would develop a dislike of one otherwise excellent tram because its timetable left me twelve minutes short of a gallery keeper’s break.

At home, I would have no grand collection. A few papers awaiting answers. A shell used to hold down the corner of a damp receipt. One expensive wooden box, purchased because I had grown tired of borrowing space in the office cupboard.

The bowl would remain beneath the dark patch in the plaster.

One evening I would come home and find water in it.

I would put down my case, stand on the stool, and feel along the wall above the stain. Then I would go downstairs to the workshop and ask whom they used for household channels.

Split would be asleep across the doorway. I would wake it badly and receive a slow, offended look.

While waiting for it to move, I would hear someone inside the workshop say that the upper passage had been smelling warmer all week.

I would take out a pencil.
