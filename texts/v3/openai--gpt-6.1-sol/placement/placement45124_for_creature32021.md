I would be the person who walks beside the old man and writes down both routes.

At first this would not be an occupation. I would be a spare member of a household, old enough to carry water, not strong enough to be the first choice for hauling a hearth stone. Someone would ask me to accompany him because he had returned late twice, and because, when questioned, he had described a crossing nobody else remembered.

I would take a little food, a lamp, and more cord than he thought necessary.

He would resent the cord.

Not openly. He would inspect the coil while I fastened it to my belt, then look away with the particular patience people use toward someone whose incompetence has become their responsibility.

Outside, he would walk faster than I expected.

***

I think I would live well away from the margin, in a house whose back wall fitted badly against a rib.

There would be a narrow space there where cold air entered. We would pack it with shed wool each winter. Each winter the shape of the space would change, and some of the wool would have to be pulled out again.

My sleeping place would be beside that wall. This would not be the warmest place, but it would have a shallow recess in which I could keep things without their being trodden on: a scraper, a chipped cup, strips of bark, the small stones used to hold those strips flat.

I would be particular about those stones. Someone would borrow one to prop up a pot and I would become unreasonably angry.

There would be other people in the house. I do not think I would have arrived with all of them, or remember a time when we had agreed to become a household. One person would know how I preferred my food without knowing where I was born. A child would have grown accustomed to sleeping with a heel pressed against my leg.

Below us, something with hooked feet would scratch at the underside of the hearth.

I would hear it most clearly when everyone else had gone to sleep. Sometimes its claws would follow a short, repeated course: three scrapes, a pause, two scrapes farther away. I would begin to recognize that course without intending to.

When it changed, I would wake.

***

The old man would teach me that a path is wider than the place where feet have worn it.

“You’ll need somewhere to fall,” he would say.

We would be examining a passage along the side of a high vein. The walking surface would be sound. Beyond it, the slope would have steepened since his last visit.

I would have written *passable*.

He would wait while I scraped the word away.

He would teach me other things badly. He would point toward a dark obstruction and say that there used to be a pool beyond it, then become irritated when I asked whether there was a pool now. He would name a household as though its doorway were still an afternoon’s walk away. Once he would lead me into a sheltered hollow and stand quietly for so long that I would begin looking for damage in the ground.

At last he would say, “They cooked here.”

There would be no house left. Only a smooth patch under an overhang, and three depressions whose arrangement might once have held a hearth.

I would write that down too.

Not on the strip we brought home for the people who needed to travel tomorrow. On another one.

***

For a while I would believe I could make a proper record.

I would devise signs for openings and closures, warmth and water, a place to rest, a place where a burden had to be passed separately. I would make them small so that more could fit on a strip. Then someone with stiff fingers and poor sight would try to read them beside a smoky lamp, and I would make them large again.

The trouble would be the time between walking a route and someone using the account.

A sheltered groove could fill with ice. A warm rise could cool. A seam I had measured with two fingers could admit a whole hand before a traveler reached it.

I would start putting the date first, then the weather.

Later I would put my name there.

It would matter that someone could come and find me.

A woman carrying membrane from the stems would ask why I had called a certain passage easy. She would have spent most of a morning coaxing her bundle through it, with nowhere dry to set it down.

I would go back carrying a bundle of the same size.

After that, I would ask people what they were taking with them before I told them where to go.

***

I do not think I would be especially brave.

I would dislike the places where wind came upward from below. Even after years, the pressure against my coat would make me bend my knees and reach for a hold before I needed one.

The old man would sometimes cross first.

On other days he would stop at a harmless dark patch because it reminded him of a place where someone had fallen.

I would learn not to hurry him through those stops. I would also learn not to agree that the ground was dangerous merely because he was afraid.

This would be difficult.

Once, while we waited, he would tell me the same story twice. In the first telling, the person who fell would be his cousin. In the second, his father.

I would keep my hands still on the bark strip.

When we returned, I would describe the patch and say that we had not tested it. I would leave the two deaths out of the route account. But that evening, while he ate, I would ask who had brought him home afterward.

Perhaps he would remember.

***

My work would become most useful when I took something away.

A knot left beside a crossing could survive the crossing itself. People trusted familiar marks, particularly when they were tired. I would carry old marking cord home in short, dirty lengths, some stiff with ice, some half swallowed by thickening tissue.

We would unravel what could be saved.

The child who slept beside me would like this task. They would sort the usable fibers and ask where each piece had been.

I would answer if I knew.

One length would have marked the approach to a household we could still see across an interval. For several seasons its people had come to us to exchange resin for food. Then the leaves had drawn apart.

The child would ask whether we could use that cord for a new crossing.

“Yes,” I would say.

There would be no reason not to. I would still leave that length until last.

***

Eventually the old man would be unable to come.

There would be several mornings when he intended to, got dressed, and sat down while I finished preparing. Then he would ask me to look at one particular place.

He would give exact instructions. They might be forty years out of date.

I would go anyway, as far as I safely could.

When I came back, I would tell him what I had found: a ridge where he remembered a hollow, a dry groove, a colony hanging beneath a torn edge. Sometimes this would annoy him. Sometimes he would question me until I understood that he had been trying to find out about something else.

I would begin bringing back small things. A husk from a plant he remembered eating. A flake of resin with a different smell. Once, some warm water wrapped in several layers, which would be cold by the time he drank it.

He would tell me I should have brought an insect from the pool instead.

***

On the morning after the new leaf opened, I would go out behind the woman and the child.

Not close enough to make an expedition of it. I would be there to examine the old approach and remove a mark if the movement had made it misleading.

I would see the grazer test the pale tissue.

The child would look back at me because I had cord on my belt.

I would feel, briefly, that I ought to do something. Give the place a mark. Make the walk useful. Bring home news more satisfying than *still waiting*.

But the junction would be taking the grazer’s weight in a way I had never seen before. A shallow depression would spread beneath its foot and slowly lift after it moved. Farther out, falling water would have begun to collect along a crease.

I would crouch on the old tissue and watch.

Before leaving, I would tie a knot beside the last firm rib. Close enough to find in poor light, far enough back that no one would mistake it for permission to cross.

At home I would make a new strip.

I would put the date first. Then the cold, the direction of the wind, the water collecting. I would record how far the grazer had gone, and that we had not followed.

The child would lean against my arm while I worked.

“Tomorrow?” they would ask.

I would leave room on the strip.
