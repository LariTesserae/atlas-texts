At first I would put myself beside one of the birches, on ground that looks firm enough to sit on. I would want the tree at my back and the basin in front of me. From there I could watch the weather arrive, see the white pipes descend, distinguish the spillway’s unchanging force from the wind moving through the sedges.

But I would keep looking down.

Water would be working somewhere beneath my seat. Now and then a small piece of the bank would settle, almost too little to notice. I would put a hand on the moss to steady myself and leave it there longer than I needed to.

That is where I begin to lose the shape of someone sitting under a tree.

My attention goes into the hand, then farther: into the roots beneath it, the cold moving along their undersides, the spaces between stones. The open basin recedes. What matters is the place where a thread of water divides around a buried rock, and one branch arrives colder than the other.

I think I would live some distance from the spillway, beneath the service road.

Not at a culvert. There is no culvert there yet. Only a low place where the gravel tracks cross an old wet seam. Water has found its way beneath them through the loose material. Above, the sedge between the wheel ruts is thick; below, there is a shallow chamber with a roof of roots and two stones almost touching.

I would fit between those stones.

My body would be longer than the chamber, but that would not trouble me. Part of me would lie in the water uphill, part in the narrow passage, part spread beneath the downhill bank. I would know the road by its pressure. A vehicle would arrive first as a faint compression, then as several hard, separate weights passing overhead. Dust and fine grit would descend afterward. I would close the openings behind my head until the water cleared.

I would be one of the animals, though not the great one beneath the spray. Mine would be a smaller territory, awkwardly joined together: the underside of the road, a hollow beside a birch root, two pools under heath, a wet run that reaches toward the intake but usually stops short of it.

In dry weather that last run would become difficult. I would go only as far as the water allowed. My front pads would feel ahead while the broad sections of my body remained in the deeper channel. Sometimes I would reach a patch of cool mud, grip a root, and wait there through the night. By morning, seepage might have gathered around me. Sometimes I would have to draw myself back.

There would be a place I particularly liked, just downhill of the road.

A flat stone forms its floor. Above it, roots have grown together so closely that little soil falls through. The water enters along one edge and leaves through a notch on the other. I can spread there without stretching any narrow part of myself. My head rests lower than my body, in the flow. A few larvae live in the roots, and a softened birch leaf occasionally comes within reach.

That would be my resting chamber. I would return to it often enough to alter it.

At first, only by passing through. A loose fragment pushed aside. A strand of root held away until another route for the water formed. Later, I would carry stones there.

I would have several pockets along my edges, some empty, some occupied. One stone would be pale and rough, one dark and smooth on a single face. I would have learned their uses by the pull they gave my body. The pale one would help me remain still in a rising stream. The dark one would sit well in the outlet notch.

I would release it there.

Water would deepen around my flank. A part of my skin that had been exposed would become submerged again. I would stay.

I would not understand the road as a road, or the shed as a shed. Yet they would enter my life with great specificity. The road would have a heavy season and a quiet season. The shed would offer a thin warmth downstream of its drain. The intake would sometimes draw water from my lowest passage so steadily that I could feel the change well uphill.

I would know the maintenance worker without seeing him.

His weight has a rhythm. He stops at the same places. He sets something down near the concrete, and a brief, sharp disturbance travels through the ground. On some visits, after he has been there, the warm outflow changes.

I withdraw when he comes close. But I would also follow water he has diverted, enter the space beneath a stone he has lifted, feed among roots exposed by his shovel. My life would repeatedly touch his work without either of us having a clear account of the other.

Once, the road would be repaired.

Fresh gravel would fill the sunken track. Under its new weight, my passage would narrow. I would be downstream when this happened, with most of my body resting in the chamber and my head under a fringe of heath.

The flow would weaken.

For a while I would wait. Water changes often; a leaf caught upstream can make an interruption. Then I would move up toward the two stones and find that the fine gravel between them no longer gave way.

I would work at it until my pads slipped.

Sand would collect behind my head. The chamber below would become shallower. Eventually I would turn aside into damp ground that had never held my whole body before.

That would take time.

I would gather myself at each constriction, ease one broad section forward, rest, then bring the next after it. Roots would scrape along my folds. Where the seam thinned, my weight would press the moss upward a little. From above, there might be only a small change in the lie of the sedges.

I would leave the dark stone in the old outlet.

For several days, perhaps longer, I would have no good resting place. Then I would reach water beneath the central strip of the road. It would be shallow but moving. Beyond it, a pocket of loose stones would open downhill.

I would spread into that pocket.

The first water to pass over me would carry peat from the passage I had made. Later it would run clearer. A root, freed from the soil, would hang against my back. On my next journey I would push it to one side.

By another spring, this would be part of my route.

So I would be there: below the place where a worker slows his vehicle because the track is always wet. Small enough to remain undiscovered, large enough to affect which patch stays wet. Carrying weight when I need it, leaving some behind when I can.

And sometimes, beneath the road, I would pause with my head in the current.

The basin would be shining overhead. I would know it through a little brightness entering at the mouth of the channel. I would draw back from that brightness, settle my pads on the stone, and let the cold water pass over the groove in my head.

Something would be coming downstream.

I would wait to feel what it was.
