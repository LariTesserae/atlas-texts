At first I would find myself beneath the washing platform, where there is just enough room to crouch if I turn my head sideways.

Someone above would be rinsing a cloth. The water would come through in separate drops, striking my neck, the back of my hand, the stone beside my knee. I would have brought a lamp and discovered that I needed both hands. Now the lamp would stand in a place that was almost level, and every time I moved I would look to see whether it was still upright.

I would be trying to find where a channel had stopped running.

That seems a likely place for me: not at the center of the square, not quite out in the forest, but underneath something everyone uses. I would hear footsteps without seeing their owners. A dropped washing paddle would sound enormous. Someone would ask whether I had found the trouble, and I would say, “Not yet,” with more annoyance than the question deserved.

For a while I think I might be the thing making the trouble—a pale root crossing the channel, thickening in the dark. Or a frog tucked into the space behind it, obliged to move because a hand has arrived. There would be a whole town above me that I knew only as pressure, vibration, occasional light.

But I keep returning to the hand.

It has a small cut across one knuckle. The nail of the thumb is split. It reaches into water reluctantly, because the water is cold and because something in there may have an edge.

I think I would be a person whose work has no single proper name.

People would bring me baskets with their bottoms giving way. I would replace the worn strips, though I would not be the best basket maker in town. Someone would ask me to look at a gutter because I happened to be passing with a knife. I would carry messages when the lower path flooded, and occasionally read a letter aloud for someone who could recognize a name but not follow the rest.

I would not be a builder. The carpenter’s daughter would make that distinction clear.

“You can put a prop under it,” she would tell me. “You cannot decide that makes it sound.”

Once, after I had propped a sagging gallery, she would make me help take it apart. I would resent this until we lifted the outer beam and found how little remained beneath its skin.

Afterward I would be slower to give reassurance.

My room would be above one of the alleys, reached through someone else’s gallery. This would mean that getting home involved a greeting, or an explanation, or at least the careful sound of passing without stopping. The household whose gallery I used would know when I had a visitor. I would know when they had boiled too little grain and were scraping the pot harder than usual.

There would be a shutter that opened against bamboo. To open it fully I would have to push a stem aside with my shoulder. In wind, the stem would tap the shutter even after I closed it.

I would keep a dry bundle in a basket above the hearth: spare paper, letters, a shirt with its cuffs still whole. The basket would have an extra lid made from an old tray. I would check it too often during rain and sometimes forget to check it when the weather cleared, which would be when a drip finally found its way inside.

I think I would have gone down-valley once.

Not on a grand departure. I would have gone with someone carrying goods, stayed longer than intended, and found work in a storehouse where the floor did not move under a loaded foot. For several weeks I would have enjoyed setting things down and finding them unchanged the next morning.

Then other things would have become apparent: the overseer’s habit of counting twice whenever I handled the stock; the cost of a room; how little anyone cared that I knew how to split bamboo without wasting half of it.

I might have returned for a sick relative. I might have used that as the reason and been relieved that no one asked for another.

Somebody would nevertheless say, “So the broad streets weren’t broad enough?”

I would have no good reply.

For a time I would draw the town.

I would use scraps left around the edges of letters, joining them with paste. I would mark stairs, bridges, the public square, the three streams. I would try to record which passages could still be used when water rose. This would seem genuinely useful.

Then the woman below my room would close her gallery to traffic after too many muddy baskets passed through. A new stair would be built behind the grain seller’s wall. A drain I had drawn as one line would turn out to divide beneath a kitchen, with one branch running through the remains of an older house.

My drawing would fill with corrections.

I would begin adding names beside the routes. Ask here before crossing. This household has rope. This door sticks in wet weather.

That would also prove unreliable. People leave. Rope gets borrowed. A person who is generous in the morning may have had enough by dusk.

Still, I would keep the papers. Now and then someone would want to see where a buried channel ought to be, and I would bring the bundle down, weighting its corners with bowls.

There would be days when I disliked every inch of the place.

A sleeve wet before breakfast. A basket returned with a broken handle. Three people asking for help while I was carrying something heavy myself. On those days I would climb beyond the last house and sit where the path widened around a boulder.

I would go there to be out of reach.

Usually someone would pass.

“Are you busy?”

“Yes.”

They would hesitate, and I would ask what they needed.

But not always. Sometimes I would stay on the stone until the cold came through my trousers.

Under the washing platform, the blockage would finally turn out to be a strip of split bamboo lodged crosswise in the channel. Leaves had caught on it, then grit, then more leaves. I would ease it loose with the hook and feel the water begin to pull at my fingers.

There would be a brief rush, loud in the cramped space.

Above me, someone would lift a foot as water spilled around a washing stone.

“Is that it?”

I would wait. More leaves would come through. A small dark seed would turn twice in the lamplight and disappear beneath the next floor.

“Yes,” I would say.

Getting out would be harder than getting in. My shoulder would catch against a support; the lamp would have to be passed up first. Someone would take it from me, then offer a hand.

I would emerge muddy on one side, with one knee stiff and a fern fragment stuck to my collar. The woman doing her washing would point it out rather than remove it.

By then it would be late enough for broth.

I would go to the cookshop where I owed a little money, carrying the broken strip of bamboo because I had forgotten to put it down.
