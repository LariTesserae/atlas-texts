I would be below the place where the fallen vane has taken root.

Not directly below it. Its new fibers would already be pushing through the dead layers, making the nearest passages unreliable. I would live a little to one side, in a chamber warmed by a conducting band that crossed the ceiling at an angle. One end of the chamber would be too cold to sleep in. At the other, something in the wall would knock twice during every long turn of the nearest tower.

I would have room to turn around, but only in one place.

At first I imagine myself as one of the small animals in the ventilation slits: almost transparent, opening a fan into the warm exhaust. There is something inviting about that narrow life, the whole body arranged to receive what passes.

But I would want to know what was happening on the other side of the wall.

So I think I would be a burrower. Not one of the oldest. Not yet a ridge that other animals crossed without noticing. My shell would have acquired enough dead fiber to make me awkward in passages I had used when young, but very little living growth. A few mites would occupy a seam behind my front shield. They would retreat when I scraped there, then return before I had finished settling.

There would be one place along my underside where a limb had healed badly. In a narrow tunnel I could brace it well enough. In a wide chamber, with nothing close to press against, I would have to arrange myself carefully.

This would determine more of my life than any preference.

I would know the route to the surface, but I would not use it often. The brightness would reach me before the opening did: first a thin glow in a cord overhead, then a wash through the wall, then the unpleasant uncertainty of ground that had only one side.

Below, every movement would have an answer. A foot pressed here would be met there. Above, I could lift a limb into nothing.

I would cross exposed ground quickly.

***

My chamber would have belonged to a larger animal.

There would be a smooth place on the ceiling that I could not reach, even fully extended. Along one wall, the old feeding marks would run beneath a newer layer of repair. The resting depression would be broad enough for me and another animal, though I would usually occupy only its warmer edge.

I would not know the previous inhabitant as a story.

I would know that the floor gave slightly under my rear limbs. That one patch held heat after the conducting band cooled. That a narrow passage behind the chamber stayed open although there was no longer enough traffic to explain it.

When I first arrived, I would follow that passage.

It would end at a wall carrying a faint, uneven vibration. I would test it with my jaw, scrape away a little loose material, and find living tissue underneath. I would leave it alone.

Later I would return.

Something about the vibration would resemble movement beyond a bend. Not closely enough to make me dig, but closely enough to make me wait.

That waiting place would become part of my route.

***

I would feed slowly.

A broad layer of dead fiber would be less interesting to me than a thin deposit laid over a warm cord. I would lift it in small pieces, testing each one. Sometimes I would expose too much and feel the clear secretion begin to set against my mouthparts. Then I would pull back hard, leave a fragment of myself in the wall, and avoid that place until the tenderness passed.

I would have feeding sites I favored for no reason another animal could use.

One would be poor but easy to reach.

One would be rich but lie beside a passage occupied by an animal that disliked my approach. I would announce myself there, wait, announce myself again. On some days it would let me pass. On others, its reply would come through the wall so sharply that I would feel it in my raised feet.

I would turn back.

There would also be a junction where I kept leaving a mark after the plateau had begun to close the branch beyond it.

At first the closure would be slight. A shoulder scrape. A longer pause before I could bring my rear shields through. I would remove the new growth and leave the passage nearly as wide as before.

Then the wall would thicken behind my work.

I would return to it too often.

Elsewhere, I might notice a pressure change and leave at once. Here I would persist, making the same short excavation every few days, though a cooler route remained open nearby.

I think this would be one of my recognizable habits.

An arriving animal would test my wax at the junction and take the other way.

***

I would not be alone all the time.

A smaller burrower would begin using the cold end of my chamber. At first I would turn toward it whenever it entered. My movement would set the mites running along my shell, and the visitor would stop with its front limbs spread.

Eventually I would stop turning.

It would leave before I did and return while I was feeding. Its marks would accumulate beneath mine at the entrance. Sometimes it would bring a trace of fresh resin from a passage I had not visited.

I would follow that trace once.

The passage would descend between two warm membranes, narrow enough that I had to fold limbs I normally kept extended. At its end would be a feeding seam recently opened by movement in the plateau.

For several days we would use it from opposite ends.

Then it would harden.

We would meet in the middle, touch the wall, and return separately.

After that, when the smaller animal pressed a particular pattern into the floor, I would sometimes get up.

***

The great chamber’s opening would reach me as an absence.

The familiar double knock would fail. The conducting band overhead would remain warm, but its vibration would lose definition. At the far end of my chamber, a draft would begin where there had never been a draft.

I would go to the junction I had kept reopening.

It would be sealed.

For a while I would work at it anyway, removing material that no longer came away in flakes. The smaller animal would pass behind me and send its pattern through the floor. I would feel it, but continue scraping.

Then the pressure would change.

My damaged limb would lose its purchase. Air would pull the loosened fragments past my head. I would withdraw, turn with difficulty, and follow the smaller animal toward the surface.

At the scar, I would brace beside animals whose shells were larger than my chamber. Small creatures would press into the shelter under my rear plates. I would feel their movements and shift enough to leave them space.

There would be nothing useful for me to do beyond holding on.

***

Afterward, I would not recover all my routes.

My chamber might still be there, but the ceiling band would be cold. The old waiting place would have become a vent, too powerful to approach. At the stubborn junction, a new passage would run across the direction I remembered.

I would explore it badly at first.

I would stop where there was no junction. Test walls that carried no useful signal. Return to my old entrance and leave a mark, although I no longer slept beyond it.

The smaller animal would find a resting hollow farther down.

I would visit, then remain.

It would be a less comfortable place. I would have to sleep with my damaged limb folded underneath me. But air would move steadily through it, and there would be food nearby.

After a while I would scrape a little more room along one wall.

That is where I would find myself: not at the center of the plateau’s life, nor wholly apart from it. An animal of middling size, carrying a worn shell, recognizable to a few others by my marks and the pressure of my approach.

I would still return sometimes to the closed junction.

But there would be nights when the other animal arrived tired, and I would move from the warmer side of the hollow before it asked.
