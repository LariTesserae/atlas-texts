At first I look for a room near the ground.

Not because I dislike the height. I would like the upper windows, especially after rain, when the plain briefly acquires edges and you can see how much has happened since yesterday. But I would want to be able to leave without asking the transit system. I would want a door that opened onto warm stone.

I imagine a narrow room behind a district entrance: a workbench, a shallow sink, a bed with its feet set in different kinds of soft material. There would be objects awaiting repair. A notice lamp. The hinge from a quarantine drawer. Something a child had brought in because it was making a noise and therefore, reasonably enough, might be broken.

But the room is too large for me.

The workbench goes first. Then the bed. The sink remains for a while, becoming a depression where water collects. I find myself less interested in the objects awaiting repair than in the contact between two pieces of metal. One is warmer than the other. They expand differently. There is a place beneath them where the condensation lasts several minutes longer than it does outside.

I think I would live inside a handrail.

Not along its whole length. Near the first bend, where people steady themselves before entering the chamber.

There is a maintenance opening beneath it, closed by a plate whose fastening has worked loose. Resinous dust collects in the recess. So do salts from hands, carried around the underside by cleaning cloths. A creeper once tried to enter here and was removed. Two pale discs remain.

My body fits between them.

I have a narrow central body and six limbs, though two are usually folded so closely against me that I appear to have four. The ends are broad and soft. They can adhere to polished metal without leaving a mark. Beneath my body is a thin, tough membrane that I lay against whatever I need to hear.

Outside, on stone, I would be difficult to distinguish from a discarded strip of rind.

Inside the rail, I am a small warm obstruction.

***

The rail has an official pulse mechanism. I am not that mechanism. I cannot announce a closure or tell anyone which floor they have selected.

At first, I merely eat what gathers around its housing.

The mechanism sends its signals through a metal tongue fixed beneath the rail. The tongue loosens before my arrival. Not enough to stop working. Enough to make the third pulse weaker than the first two.

I dislike this.

Each time the mechanism operates, the loose tongue strikes the edge of my recess. I wake, shift, settle, and am struck again. Eventually I push a folded limb into the gap.

The pulses become even.

For several days, this is the best place I have ever slept.

Then a maintenance worker opens the plate.

Light enters from below. A tool touches my folded limb. I withdraw, and the metal tongue gives its small, wrong knock.

The worker waits.

I know the waiting through the rail. Her hand stays on it without gripping. The pressure changes once, very slightly.

She tests the mechanism again.

I put my limb back.

She tests it a third time.

After that, she leaves the plate open for the afternoon. I remain deep in the recess, displeased by the draft. When she returns, she fits a proper spacer beneath the tongue, cleans only half the housing, and closes the plate without tightening it completely.

I have lost my useful crevice. I move into another.

This is how I first become acquainted with Sera.

***

I do not know her name immediately.

For a long time she is the person who rests her hand before putting her full weight on it. Most people seize the rail and let the rail receive them. She makes contact, waits a fraction, then leans.

Her fingers are sometimes covered. Sometimes they carry the dry taste of the hospital’s cleaning agents. There is a small rough place beside one nail where a glove has repeatedly rubbed.

I know other arrivals.

The person with the wide rings touches down in four separate hard places.

The child uses both hands and swings, introducing a great deal of information that has nothing to do with entering a chamber.

Someone presses a wrist to the rail instead of a palm. Someone else has a walking support whose tip reaches the floor before any hand reaches me. I learn to listen for that first contact through the bracket.

I cannot understand the conversations above my head. They come through as breaths and pressures, interrupted whenever someone lifts a hand to gesture. Sometimes two people are talking and neither touches the rail. Then I know very little about them.

A palm can tell me that someone is carrying too much.

It cannot tell me what they bought.

***

I leave at night.

There are wax-eaters around the chamber window, and a sweet deposit beneath a drainage fitting that is replenished too slowly to be worth defending. Along the path, the roots offer other surfaces. I lay my membrane against them and receive the tiny irregular movements of water.

I have to cross open stone to reach the nearest hollow.

The thin hunter finds me there once.

Its head appears beside a succulent blade. The rest of it continues arriving long after there seems to be enough animal.

I flatten. It stops.

For some time we remain in this arrangement.

Then the rail behind me delivers a pulse through its footing. The hunter turns toward it, and I slip beneath a leaf whose underside is sticky with condensed water.

Afterward I spend two days in the recess without leaving. Hunger eventually persuades me to choose a different route.

I am not brave merely because I am small.

***

When the yellow line appears, the pulse mechanism gives its closure pattern.

Everyone who can use it receives the warning. Then the rail is still, apart from what comes up through the building.

People continue touching it.

This is when I spread myself.

My limbs reach farther than usual. The membrane beneath my body lies against the inner wall. There is nothing I can do about the machinery below the plain. I can, however, feel the beginning of a movement before it reaches the entrance bench.

Sometimes I draw my limbs inward.

Sometimes I hold them where they are.

People have noticed a faint alteration beneath their palms. They do not agree about its cause. One says the rail cools before a shift. Another says it feels furry, which is inaccurate and has led to unnecessary cleaning.

Sera says there is something living in the housing.

This has consequences.

A form is submitted. The plate is opened. My recess is photographed. For the occasion I am far down the bracket, having sensed the unfamiliar tools.

The photograph shows dust, two old adhesive discs, and part of a limb.

The resulting report recommends removal of the dust.

Sera removes it. She leaves the discs.

***

On the evening she comes home with the hatching fruit, I am not in the rail.

I am beneath the entrance bench, investigating a cold drop that has fallen from one of her bags. It tastes of mineral salts and something I have never encountered.

The grazer is near her shoe. It is eating with extraordinary speed. From my position I can see the translucent edge of the leaf disappearing into its mouth.

Sera holds a cold container against her fingers.

Above us, two people move at the window. I do not see them clearly. I see the change when their bodies interrupt the light falling past the bench.

The first movement below the plain arrives as pressure through the bench’s feet.

I retreat beneath a fastening.

Sera’s shoe lifts from the ground. The grazer stops chewing.

For a moment, the unopened fruits in the bag all click together.

Then the pressure passes. The bench remains where it was. The leaf trembles against the grazer’s mouth, and it resumes eating.

Sera lowers her foot.

I return to the drop.

***

Later, my rail is replaced.

There has been a crack in its lower bracket for some time. I have been using the crack to reach the floor, but this does not make it safe. The municipal crew arrives in the middle of the morning, when I am asleep.

By the time I wake, both ends of my world are open.

The rail is on a cloth. A worker lifts it, and I travel farther in a few seconds than I have travelled in months.

There is a cart with other lengths of metal. None of them carries a living hand.

I could remain in mine. The recess is undamaged. But the cart is standing in the sun’s warm gray patch, and the metal is heating faster than I can tolerate.

I emerge in front of the worker.

He puts down the rail.

We consider one another.

He has no gloves on. I can feel his steps through the cloth, but nothing else.

Then he holds out a piece of packing material. Its cut edge contains many small chambers. I climb into one.

He leaves it beneath the bench.

The new rail has a sealed housing and no loose plate. It is, from the city’s point of view, better. I spend the afternoon examining it and cannot find a way inside.

That night I sleep in the packing material.

The following evening Sera arrives. She rests her hand, pauses, and leans. I am too far below her to receive much of it.

Before she enters the chamber, she crouches to look beneath the bench.

She turns the packing material so its open cells face away from the path. This improves it considerably.

I live there for nine days.

On the tenth, a little water begins collecting where the new rail enters its bracket. There is a gasket, and behind the gasket, a space.

It is smaller than my old recess. I cannot turn around without coming partly out. The official pulses reach it sharply enough to interrupt my sleep.

I learn where to fold my limbs.

I learn the new sounds of the fastening.

When Sera next puts her hand on the rail, I am close enough to feel the rough place beside her nail. She stays a little longer than she needs to.

Below us, inside the bench, something has begun eating the packing material.

I hear it each night.
