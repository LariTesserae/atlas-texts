At first I would put myself on the raised paving.

Not at its center, where someone waiting through a transfer cycle would stand, but at the edge, where one block has lifted slightly above the next. There is room there to sit with my heels out of the current. I would look toward the broken stair whenever the lift opened, although the lift is elsewhere. In that brief light, a stair still seems like the thing one ought to watch.

I would keep making these small mistakes about what the district offers.

A window would seem to imply a room I could enter. The fountain would seem a place to get water. The handrail would lead my attention along a route that no longer existed. Even knowing better, I would look for a way to cross the hollow without having to descend to its lowest part.

But I would not remain the person sitting there.

The useful place is underneath.

Below the lifted edge, a root has made a low chamber. One side is wet. The other has a crust that breaks into grains when the paving moves. Air passes the entrance without passing through the whole chamber. There are beetles farther in, and a narrow joint beyond their reach.

That is where I would begin to fit.

Not as the root, although I would initially mistake its pressure for part of my own holding. Not as the stone. The stone does something I must answer.

I would be one of the small animals, without a notch yet, living under the raised block beside the sedge.

My back would face the underside of the paving. My soft surface would face the lower stone. Between them there would be just enough space to shorten, extend, and turn part of my body without turning all of it. A person lifting the block would think I had been flattened by its weight.

I would have arrived there during seepage from the fountain.

There would be no remembered journey in the human sense: no view of the pedestal behind me, no destination chosen beneath the grass. There would be wet stone at my front and drying stone behind. Once, a groove would interrupt the wetness. I would lower myself into it and stay until the water stopped touching my head.

By the time I reached the root, the district’s light would have changed several times without becoming anything I could use.

The root would be an obstruction at first. I would follow its side. Where it entered the paving, the surface would narrow around me, and my feet would find places into which they could spread.

I would stop going forward.

For a long while, that would be enough.

***

The hollow would come to me through a few small areas of contact.

One foot would feel a tremor before the others did. The difference would matter. A beetle working at the edge of the chamber makes the stone move differently from a pressure exchange, but I would sometimes close for either. I would close for a tool dropped far away. I might remain folded after the danger had passed, missing what was carried through the still air.

I would not be especially wise.

There would be days when my membranes collected little. A fringe would stick to a wet grain of mortar and fail to open. I would draw it inward with the others, then unfold it again, still caught. Eventually the grain might come loose. Until then, that part of the membrane would remain doubled.

Food would arrive mixed with things I could not use.

I would gather them together anyway. A fragment of insect skin, a spore, a particle worn from a shutter, all held in the same droplet. Folding would bring the droplet toward my head. Some of what entered the groove would remain there too long. Some would leave me.

Above my chamber, the sedge would bend under water. Its roots would shift against the block. I would tighten, then loosen.

This would happen many times.

I would grow darker without seeing myself. Dust would lodge on my back. A patch near one end would thicken where the stone rubbed it during load exchanges. My feet would find the same small irregularities so often that, after retreating farther into the joint, I would return to almost the same position.

Almost matters. A little farther out, the air brings more. A little farther out, it can take hold of me.

***

Once I would lose my shelter.

Not because the block was lifted. Nothing so complete.

A root would thicken, and an exchange of weight would settle the paving differently. The chamber would still be there, but the narrow joint would pinch at one end. I would withdraw from it. The place where I had opened my membranes would now receive a thin, persistent draft.

I would wait through it.

Then there would be water.

I would come out under the sedge with dust softened on my back. A lizard would be nearby, beyond the stems. I would not know this until its movement reached the paving. By then my front would be on open stone and my rear feet still under the block.

I would hold.

The bitten place would not hold with me.

Afterward I would remain beneath the block, farther in than before. The injured part would touch the lower stone. Water would reach it. I would keep both membranes shut, though one was undamaged.

When I opened again, they would no longer lie evenly.

The old position would be unusable. I would leave it on the next wet interval, passing along the block rather than away from it, until a mortar seam took my feet. There, the underside of the root would form a roof over the entrance. It would be a poorer place for feeding.

It would be a place I could keep.

***

Someone might notice me there.

Their lamp would stay on the seam while they examined the root damage. I would retreat a little section at a time. They might touch the dust beside me with a probe, find the material yielding, and put a mark on an inspection photograph.

The light would go away.

I would still have to wait. The probe had moved the stone.

Eventually one membrane would open into the shallow space beneath the root. Its damaged edge would remain folded. The other would extend farther, until one fringe touched the damp paving.

I would draw it back slightly.

There would be no whole district before me, no balconies overhead, no city whose weight I understood. Only the stone loosening its tremor, the air becoming usable, and something very small arriving against the open surface.

I would stay there to receive it.
