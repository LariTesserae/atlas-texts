At first I would look for somewhere to put my weight.

Not a place to live yet. Not even a place to stop. A place for one hand, with another place close enough that I could withdraw the first.

The broad leaves would be misleading. The dark would conceal which were supported from beneath and which merely lay across an opening. I would reach toward what looked like a branch and find a root, toward what looked like bark and find the warm flank of an animal sleeping against it. Before I knew where I was, I would need to know what moved when touched.

I think I would be above the ground, but not high in the crowns. There is too much exposed space up there, too much distance between one useful thing and the next. I would keep to the level where roots become trunks and trunks have not yet become a confusion of small branches. The grazers would pass below my belly. Their backs would lift the leaves toward me. Sometimes I would see farther because something large had just broken a way through.

And I would not be the old female.

I would want to follow her, but I would not always manage it.

Her missing toe would matter to me before her face did. There would be a particular arrangement of the remaining toes when she gripped a sloping trunk: three pale creases, a dark space, the foot turned slightly outward. I would know that shape from behind. In foliage thick enough to hide her whole body, I could find it and put my hand where her foot had been.

Only the stem would no longer be as slack as it was when she touched it.

I would learn her route with a delay built into every movement.

That seems to be where I belong: close behind something that knows, in a body capable of going farther than its knowledge.

Perhaps I am the half-grown male with the burned hand. The hand keeps arriving in what I imagine before the rest of him does.

The burn is on the inside of two fingers. At first I can spread them. Later I cannot spread them without pulling at the tender place between them. My mouth tastes of mineral grit because I keep licking there. Saliva cools the skin briefly, then dries.

When I climb, I turn that hand outward and use its heel. This makes my arm awkward. A hold that usually carries me leaves me hanging too low, and my hind foot searches through empty leaves. The animal behind me catches my tail—not to help me, but because I am in the way.

I bite at its fingers. It lets go. I find a root.

For a while, all the basin is arranged around that hand. The bark on one side of a trunk is rough enough to hurt; the other side is too smooth to grip without the fingers I am trying not to use. A narrow vine fits into the crease of the burn. I cannot take the quick route the others take. By the time I reach the next fork, one of them has already eaten the flower I smelled from below.

Hunger makes me hurry. Hurrying makes the hand hurt.

I do not understand why the old female stopped at the fissure.

I remember her wrist against the stone. I remember her muzzle going forward, then back. But I also remember water there: my mouth low in the opening, a wet edge against my lower lip, the cold traveling into my teeth. Those memories still bring my head forward when we pass.

The new smell stops me only when I am close.

For several nights I would be caught between those things. The remembered drink would take me down the root. The sting in my nose would send me up it again. Eventually I might stop descending. Or rain might come, and the opening might be safe, and I would have to learn it over.

There would be no good place in my head to put the knowledge away finished.

***

I would have preferences that the others could scarcely notice.

A certain kind of beetle, soft under the wing cases.

The fold behind one female’s knee, where I slept when I was smaller and where I still try to wedge my face when the group is crowded.

A hanging strip of bark that knocks twice against a trunk when a heavy animal passes underneath. I would stop chewing to listen for the second knock.

I would dislike one of the infants. It reaches for my mouth even when my mouth is empty. Its nails enter my nostril. Its mother pulls me close if I push it away too roughly, holds the fur at my shoulder, bares her teeth against my cheek.

Later, when she leaves it braced between two roots to reach for a leaf, I would put my foot beneath it when it begins to slide.

I might not look at it while I did so.

***

The hollow with the three stones would be important to me.

I would enter it more completely than the adults can. There is room inside for my shoulders if I turn them. The husks shift beneath my chest, and the sound outside becomes thicker. A scream in the canopy is still a scream, but here it has to come through wood.

My burned hand rests in the cool scraps.

A beetle moves against my wrist. I eat it.

Beyond the hollow’s mouth, a tail passes, followed by a hind foot. Someone sniffs at the opening. I remain still until the face withdraws.

I would like having a place where I can see someone without being immediately seen. That liking would last until an adult reached in and pulled me out by the ankle.

The unused stone would press against my ribs while I lay there. I might roll it aside. I might pick it up once, feeling its weight shift into the unburned hand.

It would be a poor stone for opening a pod. Too rounded on the side I brought down, liable to glance away. I would strike twice, fail, and use the smaller one.

But I might carry the poor stone as far as the entrance before dropping it.

The next time I came, it would be beside the entrance.

I would touch it.

Not because I had decided to keep it. It would simply be something I had handled that was still where my hand had left it. In a place where leaves turn, water goes bad, branches fall, and food is taken out of one’s mouth, that would hold my attention.

Then a larva would stir beneath a husk, and I would forget the stone.

***

When the small rain began, I would be late.

I would hear the old female’s clicks while trying to free my sore hand from a fork. The others would move, their bodies lifting and lowering through the stems ahead. I would pull harder than I should, cry once, and follow.

The route would still be moving from their passage.

A loop would rise against my chest. I would brace my feet apart, let my belly drop beneath it, and stretch the good arm toward a root. For a moment my whole weight would hang there. The root would peel a little from the trunk.

Then my tail would find something.

At the vents I would come down among wet leaves already emptied by other mouths. The grazers would be pushing close. I would hold a leaf beneath a drip, as I had before, but pull it toward my body when another hand reached across.

The leaf would fold. Most of the water would spill.

There would still be a little caught along the midrib.

I would lick it, turn the leaf, lick again.

Only after drinking would I put the burned fingers beneath the drip.

The first drop would make me jerk. I would return the hand. Another drop, then another. My fingers would open a little.

Nearby, the old female would be drinking low against the stone. A grazer would move her aside. She would shift without looking up, finding another wet margin with her lips.

I would crouch over my leaf until the dripping stopped.

***

By daylight I would be in the sleeping fork, neither at its center nor quite outside it.

The youngest bodies would be enclosed. The adults would have the firmest rests for their hips and shoulders. I would lie across a slanting branch with one leg hooked over another, my sore hand tucked against my chest.

As I fell asleep, the group would move around me. A face would press into my back. Someone’s tail would lie across my muzzle, and I would push it away without waking fully.

The old female would shift to a place I could not see.

I would lift my head.

There: the divided ear, moving above a shoulder.

I would settle again.

That is who I think I would be here. Not the one who knows the basin, but one who has begun to notice how much he does not know, and who still goes down toward remembered water.

When we woke, I would follow the old female to the next trunk.

She would test the stem.

Behind her, I would open my hand as far as it would go, and wait for somewhere else to put it.
