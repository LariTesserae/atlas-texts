I would live in a room that had once been the space between two houses.

The walls would not face each other squarely. At one end I could lie down with my arm stretched above my head; at the other I would have to turn sideways to get past the bed. Someone would have roofed it over when a household needed another sleeping place, then added a door when the arrangement began to seem permanent. The door would open inward. This would be inconvenient, but opening it outward would obstruct the passage.

There would be a shelf above the door that stayed dry except in one particular kind of rain.

I would have learned which kind.

At first I think I would make something small: pegs, wedges, the little wooden pieces that keep a vessel from rocking on an uneven ledge. Work that could be carried indoors when the light failed. I would save every useful offcut and accumulate so many that visitors had to stand.

But I would also be the person who kept noticing where the water went.

Not the springs themselves. Everyone notices a new spring. I mean the damp mark that appears a hand’s breadth from where it ought to appear, the darkening around a peg, the underside of a sill that stays wet after the sill has dried. I would run a finger along these places and then smell it, without quite knowing when I had acquired the habit.

People would begin asking me to look.

There would be no proper name for this work. When someone sent a child to fetch me, the child would say, “It’s come through again,” and I would have to ask where.

Sometimes they would be annoyed that I did not already know.

—

I would own a shallow wooden tray with three small feet beneath it. Inside: a copper blade worn narrow by sharpening; a bone point; lengths of reed; a little vessel of black clay wrapped in damp cloth; scraps of pale wood thin enough to slip into a seam.

Also a spoon. The spoon would have no place in the tray, but I would use it too often to leave it behind.

I would not usually be the person who repaired the trouble. The mason would do that, or the gutter-maker, or the householder with an old knife and more confidence than I possessed. I would find the route.

For this I would sometimes need everyone to stop helping.

“Don’t wipe it yet.”

“Leave the bowl where it was.”

“No, the cloth was catching that. Put it back.”

Then I would crouch in someone’s kitchen while they stood behind me trying to get on with the morning.

Water leaves evidence, but people are quick to tidy it away. A floor has been dried, a bowl shifted, a bed raised another finger’s breadth. By the time I arrive, I may have only a wet wall and three conflicting accounts of when it began.

I would make mistakes.

Once I would persuade a household to open a seam that had been sound. Fresh clay would have to be fetched; the room would be colder for two nights; a woman would keep the piece of dry packing I removed and show it to me whenever we disagreed afterward.

I would dislike her for this, although she would be entitled to do it.

—

My own room would have a damp patch I could not account for.

It would be behind the bed, low on the wider wall. No steady drip, no visible fissure. Sometimes the stone would be pale for a fortnight. Sometimes I would wake to find my rolled bedding wet along one edge.

The gutter-maker’s father would know about it.

Whenever I offered an opinion too readily, he would ask whether I had sorted out my wall.

I would say I had not had time.

This would be partly true.

—

What would draw me to this work would be the interval before the answer became clear.

A woman says water is entering beneath her hearth. Her son says it is coming down from the roof. Both have seen something real. I set my tray on the step and remove the little skirt of reeds they have tucked around the hearth stones. Behind it the clay is dark, but the floor beneath is nearly dry.

There is a swollen shelf overhead. A hanging cup rests against its edge.

I lift the cup away.

For several moments nothing happens. The woman begins explaining again that the hearth was relaid last cold season. Then a clear bead gathers underneath the shelf, travels toward a peg, and stops.

I put a scrap of wood against it. The bead spreads over the scrap and wets my thumb.

We move the cup’s peg two fingers farther out. I cut a small interruption in the shelf’s underside. Her son brings water to test it, pouring much too much, and we have to wipe the hearth after all.

When I leave, she gives me a bundle of reeds and asks whether I think the roof will need replacing.

I have not looked at her roof.

I go back in and look.

—

I would be on good terms with Eda, though I might sometimes mistake that for friendship.

She would let me examine things caught in the screen if I did not delay her work. I would be interested in the mineral skins, especially those that had formed around fibers or the splintered end of a reed. They might tell me something about a channel I had never been able to reach.

She would be interested in getting the screen clean.

“Take it or put it down.”

I would put most of it down.

Once she would find a small wedge with a slanting cut across its broad end. I would recognize it as mine, made for a loose stair in the upper part of town.

I would carry it back there and discover the stair still firm. The householder would tell me it was the second wedge I had made for him. He had dropped the first.

I would have forgotten making two.

For several days before asking him, I would have had a much more interesting explanation.

Eda would listen to it and then reach past me for her basket.

—

In the evening I would return to the narrow room and hang up whatever had become wet. My hands would be worse than I had expected when I began this work. The bone point would sit comfortably in my palm, but closing my fingers around it would pull at a split beside the smallest nail.

I would keep my dry wrapping on the shelf above the door.

Sometimes I would use it. More often I would tell myself to wait until after eating.

There would be someone inland who expected a visit from me. Perhaps a sister who had left, who wrote through travelers rather than sending letters. Her messages would include practical directions: where to turn off the track, whose yard had the good well, what time she was usually home.

I would intend to go when a particular run of repairs was finished.

She would stop asking when that might be and begin sending news instead.

I would like hearing about her life. I would also resent how easily her news fitted into a few sentences. A new room. A tree cut down. A child tall enough now to lift the gate latch.

I would have trouble deciding what to send back.

—

One morning the child at the dismantled house would fetch me because water had begun collecting around the shoot.

The wooden accommodation would be too close on one side. Growth, or swelling, or a slight movement in the new stone: I would not be sure which. I would borrow the gutter-maker’s blade and shave a little from the opening while the child watched.

The aunt would ask whether the trough had been badly made.

“No,” I would say.

“Then why are you cutting it?”

I would show her the swollen edge. She would feel it with her thumb and remain unconvinced.

The child would hold the shavings. I would ask for them, meaning to put them in my tray, but the child would close both hands.

So I would leave without them.

That afternoon I would have to pass the house again. I would stop at the doorway to see whether the water was clearing. The child would be elsewhere; the aunt would have taken her stool home. A builder, kneeling with his back to me, would ask me to hand him a wedge.

I would give him one from my tray, then stay to hold the board while he drove it in.
