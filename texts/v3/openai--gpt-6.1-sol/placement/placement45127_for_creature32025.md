At first I would find myself behind the great slab where the sound thins.

Not in its deepest shadow. There would be a little room there between the slab and the floor, enough to lie in, but I would choose the place nearer its edge, where I could hear something approaching before it entered. One side of my body would touch stone. The other would face the narrow way out.

From there the roof would be hidden. I would know the bats by what reached the floor: the noise, the smell, a small dry impact beyond my feet. The nearest patch of daylight would fall on a slope I could not quite see without moving my head.

I think I would remain there long enough to mistake my stillness for belonging to the stone.

Then something would cross the dust.

A beetle, perhaps. Too far away to hear clearly, but close enough that its movement would reach the hairs above my eyes. My ears would turn before my head did. One forefoot would draw back beneath my chest.

That is where I would begin to discover what I was.

Not a watcher placed in the basin to understand it. One of the pale-backed animals, with dust in the fur over my shoulders and a darker patch inside one foreleg where I had wiped my mouth. I would have a broken whisker on the left. A healed split in one pad would make me place that foot slightly differently on a sharp edge.

I would be old enough to have preferred routes.

The gap immediately behind my resting place would be passable, but I would seldom use it. Its floor slopes toward a pocket of damp where the smell of another animal lingers. I could go that way if I needed to. Usually I would leave by the wider opening, turn around the end of the slab, and put my feet into the shallow depression along its base.

There would be no single view of the country I inhabited. A few feet of ground would become available at a time.

First the low arch, touched across the top by my whiskers. Then a place where I could stand upright. Then the underside of a shelf, too low at its far end, so that I would turn before reaching it. I would know the turn before I smelled the dry guano beyond it, though smelling it would confirm where I was.

At a narrow crossing I would pause with my chest still under cover.

Nothing would be moving on the pale floor ahead. That would not make me cross immediately. I would listen, lift my nose, wait through a flutter from the ceiling. Then I would go, quicker than I had moved among the stones, and slow again as soon as my shoulder brushed the next block.

My feeding place would be a strip along an old deposit, where the crust had dried and separated from the limestone.

I would put my nose close to it. Beneath the noise of the roost there would be a small irregular scraping. I would wait for it to stop, then move my head a little and wait for it to begin again.

One claw would go under the crust.

The piece I lifted would resist, break, and expose something soft. I would eat before it could withdraw. There would be grit in the mouthful. I would turn my head aside afterward and work my tongue against my teeth.

A familiar animal might come along the other edge of the deposit. I would smell it before seeing its back.

For a moment we would both be still. It would lower its nose where my foot had disturbed the crust. I would make the short sound in my throat, not loud enough to carry beyond the heap.

It would move on.

If it did not, I might leave. There would be other places to feed, and I would already have eaten something. My resting gap I would defend more firmly.

Later, I would go down toward water.

I would avoid the pool with the long open bank. There would be a smaller one between two buried slabs, reached through a turn so tight that my flank would brush both walls. The water there would be close beneath a ledge. I could drink with my forefeet dry and my hindquarters still in the passage.

Before lowering my head, I would smell the edge.

Once, in this place, there had been a toad.

I would not need to find the toad again to hesitate.

After drinking I might lick the damp stone beside the pool. Then I would back out, because there would not be room to turn, and carry a cool trace of moisture on my chin up through the dry passages.

There is one thing I would sometimes do that would have no urgency.

I would climb to a stone below an opening.

Not all the way to the grasses. Not into the strongest light. The stone would have a hollow at its sheltered end, and from there I could smell what came down.

On some visits there would be almost nothing: limestone warmed a little above, pigeon feathers, my own fur. On others, air would descend and stir the hairs along my spine. I would lift my muzzle. There would be scents I could not follow into familiar places.

I might put one foot beyond the hollow.

The light would catch its pale fur. My claws would curl against the stone.

Then I would draw it back.

Below me, a bat would loosen its hold. Others would begin to move, and the sound through the basin would change. I would turn toward that sound. By the time the first bodies passed beneath the opening, I would already be descending, placing each foot where it could take my weight.

There would be feeding to do along the roost’s edge. Something might fall. I would investigate a sound, find only a pebble, and continue.

Eventually I would return to the great slab.

I would smell the entrance before going in. A few grains of limestone would have shifted near the threshold. I would step over them, pass my shoulders through, and lower myself onto the dry floor.

My forefeet would lie ahead of me. My chin would rest between them. The broken whisker would catch briefly against the wall until I moved my head.

Outside, beetles would work through the debris.

I would listen for a while, then sleep.
