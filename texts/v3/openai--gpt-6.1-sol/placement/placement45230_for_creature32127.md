At first I would be difficult to place.

There would be a streak on a stone, low enough that you might mistake it for an old waterline. A scatter of chips drawn into a hollow. On the underside of a slab, a patch where the violet film had been scraped away.

You could follow these signs without finding me. Some would be days old. Some would belong to another animal. The water would have rearranged the smallest fragments, and a fallen blade might have closed the passage I had used.

I would be nearby, but nearby would not mean what it means on open ground.

Between us there might be only three paces of distance and a flooded room neither of us could enter.

***

I think I would live in the second shelter: the pale-roofed one beyond the channel.

From your rise it would appear almost intact. You would see the overlapping plates and the dark entrance beneath them. You would not see that one side of the floor had begun to settle, or that the warm seam lay off-center, close to the back wall.

My shelter would have been built by a body larger than mine.

The entrance polish would reach above my own soft margins. A roof plate I could scarcely lift would have been set there in one movement, or so it would seem to me after many attempts to shift it. Beneath the floor would be fragments fitted too tightly to draw out.

I would have occupied the place after a season of empty tides. Enough wax would remain in the narrow entrance to ease my passage. Enough warmth would remain to make me stay.

At first I would sleep where the previous animal had slept.

Later, I would move a little farther back.

***

My body would not give me a single, continuous account of the world.

Under my forward edge, the branched feelers would rest on separate pieces of stone. One would receive the faint grating of a loaded slab. Another, touching a loose chip, would receive almost nothing. A third would carry the long pressure of a chamber filling beneath the shelter.

I would lift them and set them down again.

Something that seemed safe a moment before would become uncertain when I changed the points of contact. Something that sounded dangerous would resolve into a fragment rocking harmlessly in a shallow channel.

I would spend much of my life making these small corrections.

My eyes would show me the ledge. The ground would tell me whether I could put my weight on it.

Even then I would sometimes be wrong.

One of my rear plates would have a pale division where it had grown back after a fall. In certain positions it would catch the plate beside it. I would have learned to turn farther than necessary before entering the shelter, leaving a broad gray mark on the outside stone.

You might take that mark for evidence of a much larger inhabitant.

***

The warmth would be weakening.

Not quickly. There would be no morning when the floor suddenly became cold. I would continue returning to the same place, lowering my underside onto the smooth depression, remaining there through the deep part of the tide.

But the damp would persist longer on the back wall. A surface that used to dry would stay dark. The mineral film would begin to establish itself along the edge of the warm patch.

I would scrape it away.

It would return.

For a long time that would be the whole of my response.

Then I would begin carrying stones inward.

A low wall would form around the depression. I would choose thin fragments first, then thicker ones that held their position when pressed. Some would split beneath my pads. Some would rock, and I would leave them outside beside other rejected pieces.

The wall would make the shelter more difficult to turn in. It would also hold a little more warmth.

I would rest against it before the work was finished.

***

There would be young.

Not many.

A few capsules would stand on their short stalks near the back of the shelter, where condensation gathered in the roof seam and fell most steadily. I would keep the floor around them clear, though never entirely clear. Fine grains would work loose from the wall. Water would carry scales of mineral through the rear gap.

One young animal would emerge before the others.

For several tides it would remain within reach of my extended forelimb. Its pads would be too large for its body. It would press them against the floor, lift them, and press them again, gathering grit it could not yet use.

When it moved toward the entrance, I would shift a stone.

When it moved toward the warm wall, I would stay still.

Eventually it would find the narrow space beneath my side and settle there. I would keep the plates on that flank slightly raised. The exposed fold would lose water more quickly, and I would have to move before I otherwise needed to.

This would change my feeding route.

I would visit the nearest wall too often. Its film would become thin. I would go farther out, to a submerged ledge where the water pressed cold between my plates.

You would rarely see these journeys. You might notice only that the shelter was occupied on one visit and seemed empty on the next.

***

Across the channel, the other builder would press against its floor.

The tremor would reach me incompletely. A low pulse, an interval, a second pulse altered by the water beneath us.

I would lower myself.

There would be places on my floor where an answer traveled well, and places where it disappeared into loose rubble. I would have learned them by repetition. On a broad buried plate near the entrance, I would press once, wait, then press again.

Sometimes the other animal would answer.

Sometimes a chamber would begin to drain, and its deep report would cover everything.

I do not know that I would be lonely when no answer came. I might remain lowered for a while, then return to the wall or the capsules. But when a familiar tremor did arrive, my front limbs would loosen. The folds beneath my armor would open a little farther.

That much would happen in the body, whether or not there was anything more to it.

***

If you came to my entrance, I would first know you through the floor.

Your weight would arrive in separate, abrupt loads. Gravel would shift under you. A roof stone might tremble when you leaned over it.

I would gather my feelers beneath my forward fold and wait.

Then your movement would stop.

For a while we would be still on opposite sides of the entrance: you with one hand on the stone, me with four pads spread against the floor. The young animal might come forward between us, carrying a grain stuck to the wax along its side.

If you stepped back, I would not follow.

I would reach toward the gravel your foot had displaced. There would be a small task there, immediate and possible. I would draw the chips into the hollow, test them with my weight, and leave one projecting where the young could brace itself on the way out.

Beyond us, the water would be rising.

My feeding ledge would disappear. The back wall would darken. Air would begin moving through the rear gap, passing over the capsules and out through the roof.

I would turn within the cramped shelter, carefully, to keep the damaged plate from catching.

Then I would settle near the young, with my feelers on the broad stone that carried the other inhabitant’s answer best.
