I would find myself on the sheltered side of a ridge, where the current returns without quite returning to the place it left.

There would be a low stone lip overhead. Beyond it, the forest would descend into water too dark to show its depth. Beneath it, the leaves would grow close together, their lowest tiers almost touching the mountain. A narrow space would remain between leaf and stone. Enough for a body flattened sideways. Enough to move without being seen from above.

At first I would mistake that space for my home.

I would be young, or at least lightly made. Water would pass through the grooves of my skin without leaving much behind. The few grains I carried would be conspicuous: a copper fleck near one joint, two pale grains farther inward, a dark chip that made a stiff place whenever I folded.

My eyes would open at different times.

One would be looking into the dimness beyond the stone lip. Another, pressed close to the mountain, would still hold the brightness of a slope I had crossed some days before. A third would be covered by a leaf. I would not immediately move the leaf. There would be nourishment along its underside, and its weight would tell me something about the current.

It would take me a long time to learn that these were views of one place.

The body between them would be no help. Some of it would lie uphill, some downhill, and one limb would have reached around the stone to grip a stalk on the other side. My head would be tucked beneath the middle of this arrangement, its shell resting against a shallow depression.

I would know where I was by the holds I could release.

This one, safely.

This one, if the leaf above me had stopped trembling.

Not these two together.

Farther out, beyond the lip, a loose end of my body would sway. I would bring it back slowly, taking a little soft growth from each leaf it passed.

***

There would be a pale plate embedded in the floor of my shelter.

Its edge would stand clear of the stone. Beneath it, water would move gently through several small chambers, and I would spend the darkest intervals folded in one of them. Roots would cross the plate’s upper surface. One root would enter a hole and emerge beneath it, thicker there, as though the sheltered portion had grown for longer than the rest.

I would keep catching my smallest limb in that hole.

Each time, I would have to unfold farther than I wanted to. One eye would come out into the open. The plate would tilt a little under the strain, raising silt from beneath its buried edge.

Then another body would arrive.

It would not pull me free. It would brace the plate.

With the plate held steady, I could turn the caught limb and withdraw it. I would remain against the visitor afterward, my gripping surfaces still closed around nothing.

The visitor would have an old break in one of its shells. Fine roots would hang from the break, clipped short where its movements had worn them away.

It would feed beside me. When it left, I would follow it as far as the lip, then return beneath the pale plate.

Only much later would I find the rest of the body to which that plate had belonged.

It would extend through the floor of the grove: a joint beneath a terrace of silt, a narrow length clasping a stalk, another plate almost entirely covered by green. My shelter would be one small interval in its folding.

I would have been living beneath an animal without knowing it.

I would begin to use the hole differently. Rather than passing through it, I would place one grip against its rim. The current was easy to feel there.

***

I think I would be one of the animals that comes back.

Not always to the same shelter. A ridge might move while I was away, carrying the grove beyond a trough I could cross. A season of rain might bury the pale plate beneath new growth. I might approach the place from another direction and fail to recognize it.

But I would return to the shallow clearings where the silt had been handled.

At first I would do too much.

I would find a grain at the end of a line and move it inward. Then I would move another, and smooth the small ridge my limb had made, and disturb a third grain while smoothing it.

When the older animal arrived, it would hold its fan close to the ground for a long time.

I would wait nearby with my eyes shut.

It would leave most of my changes alone. At the outer edge of the clearing, it would press a short hollow into the silt. Nothing more.

Over the next several visits, other animals would approach that hollow before approaching the lines I had altered. Their limbs would settle there. Their heads would turn. One would remain through a whole dimming of the basin, then move a grain into the hollow and leave.

I would not know everything they had understood.

Eventually I would learn to leave room around a mark.

There would be clearings I visited only to remove the silt that threatened to cover their edges. I would carry it out between my soft limb margins, letting it go where the roots could catch it. Sometimes another animal would help. We might work beside each other without touching until one of us needed to turn.

Then a hold would loosen.

Then the other body could pass.

***

I would spend part of a season at a doorway.

The plants outside it would be old and heavily dusted. Through the opening, their leaves would appear newly unfolded. I would extend an eye into that brightness, then draw it back and wait. The brightness would remain in the bead long after the doorway had gone dark.

A limb placed against the inner edge would lose some of its grit.

I would notice first that it bent more easily. Then that the copper fleck was gone.

For a while I would keep the limb there. My other limbs would anchor outside, among leaves whose edges had worn white. The pale joints within the opening would open and close without stiffness.

But when I withdrew it, the gripping surface would be clumsy on familiar stone. Twice it would slip from a hold the rest of my body had already begun to trust.

I would return to the shelter beneath the old plate.

There I would rub the altered skin against its edge. Very little would come away. A fragment, then another. I would wait for each to settle into a groove before rubbing again.

The limb would remain paler than the others.

Years later, something small folded beneath my own plates would take hold of it.

***

I would not always be gentle.

When a cold pocket approached, my fan would open before my eyes found its boundary. I would release every hold but one. The forest around me would close its leaves.

In the cold, the edges of my body would sharpen. A limb brushing mine would meet serrations. I might close upon it before I had recognized the touch.

Afterward, in the warm water, I would remain folded around the one hold I had kept.

The other animal would wait beyond reach.

When I could unfold, I would extend the limb that had closed. It would inspect the edge with an eye, then touch the joint behind it. We might stay that way for a long time.

I would carry the last stiffness for days. During that time, I would feed only where the leaves gave me room.

***

As I grew older, there would be more of the mountain in me.

Sand would fill the fine channels of my skin. Shell fragments would thicken my plates. Some holds would become difficult to release because roots had entered beside them; others because I had kept them closed too long.

A young animal would settle beneath me.

I would make space by lifting a plate. The movement would be small, but it would bring water into a place that had been still. Silt would rise. The young one would close its eyes until it cleared.

Later, during a passing shadow, I would lower that plate again.

I would feel the small limbs folding beneath it, searching for positions that did not press against mine. One grip would remain on the outer edge. I would wait until it moved inward before settling fully against the stone.

There would come a time when I no longer crossed the ridge.

Visitors would clear my eyes. They would leave food against the soft margin beneath my shell. Occasionally one would brace itself beside me while I loosened a rooted limb and set it a little farther out.

I would still need to make such adjustments.

A leaf growing under my body would lift me unevenly. A current would begin entering from the wrong side. The young would need another chamber beneath the plates.

I would shift what I could.

And somewhere along my oldest limb, an eye would remain open against the stone. It would hold the faint brightness of a basin I had crossed long ago. Nearby, a smaller eye would open into the water as it was now.

We would rest there together until the leaf above us tilted.

Then the smaller body would reach out.

I would loosen my hold enough to let it pass.
