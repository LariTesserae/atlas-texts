I would be some distance beyond the last ceramic stud, with one hand still on the route.

Not lost yet. The studs would have ended at a strip of rubber that I knew by its torn edge, and the rubber would have continued under the water. I would be crouching to find where it came up again. Behind me, someone at the station would be emptying a cup. I would hear the water strike the mud, but not the cup being put down.

Under my fingers, the perforations would be closing.

I would wait to see whether they opened again.

That is the first thing I know about myself here: I would stay bent over a small change for quite a long time. Long enough for the water displaced by my knees to settle. Long enough for a fish to resume working at the seam of my boot.

Eventually I would find that the rubber had not healed. Something underneath it had risen into the holes.

I would ease my fingers free and go back for a second person.

—

I think I would have a room, though I would spend little time in it.

There would be a low bed, a washing basin, and a shelf attached to the wall at only one end. The free end would rest on a post. Each morning I would check the space between the post and the wall before putting weight on the shelf.

My cup would have two dents, close together. One made to identify it, the other acquired later. I would dislike being given a replacement.

On a hook beside the door I would keep a coat with a heavy hem. The sleeves would be patched unevenly because other people would mend them for me. I would do something for those people in return, though perhaps we would not think of the exchanges that way. Clear a path to a washing platform. Find a missing doorstep. Bring back the broad-handled knife someone had dropped into a basin that only returned things after the pump stopped.

I would be attached to a station whose usual output was water, with a little fine clay in it. There would be an old operator there who could recognize a change in the pump through the bench on which she sat.

She would send me out when a route had become doubtful.

“Take the small chain,” she would say.

I would have to ask which one. Both chains would have been called small for longer than anyone remembered.

—

At first my work would be ordinary route work.

I would carry spare studs in a bag against my hip. They would knock together softly as I walked. At a crossing I would kneel, clear the mud with the side of my hand, and feel whether the buried chain was resting or pulling.

There would be places where I could still find the old route beneath the new one. Two sets of studs, perhaps a hand’s breadth apart, each leading faithfully to a different entrance of the same building. I would leave those alone until someone could walk them with me.

We would compare what we found by touching the objects we meant.

This stud. This hinge. The third rung from the bottom.

Directions spoken without a hand on something would be too easy to misunderstand.

I would get a reputation for coming back slowly. Not for failing to come back; only for taking longer than the distance seemed to warrant. The station would keep food for me under an inverted bowl. Someone would tap the bowl twice with a spoon when I entered, so I could find it before taking off my wet coat.

For a while, that would be enough to tell me who I was.

—

But the place I would know best would not be on my assigned routes.

There would be a low ridge beside an unused intake tower. Its crest would press water upward into the hand. On one side, a mat would hold a sparse stand of reeds; on the other, a broad animal would usually stand with one foot close to the tower’s base.

The animal would have a hard fold near the bottom of its leg. I would sit against that fold when it was available. It would give me somewhere to brace my back without putting all my weight on the ground.

I would mend route bags there.

Drawstrings would pass through the channel at my feet. Sometimes one would touch my ankle and remain, feeding or resting. The great animal would move its weight so gradually that I would find myself sitting upright before I noticed the support had gone.

Here I would begin to lose track of my hands.

Not their position. I would know exactly where they were. But after an hour with a palm resting on the wet ridge, I would feel a contraction well beyond my reach, and lift that hand as though I had been holding the place where it happened.

At first I would call it vibration.

Then I would learn that I could feel it when the strip of mud between us had broken.

I would try it again on another day. I would ask someone else to sit there. They would feel the upward water, the animal, the small movements of fish. They would wait patiently while I tried to explain which other movement I meant.

—

There would be changes at home.

A patch beneath my mattress would resist being peeled away. Its surface would contain fibers from my clothing, a little skin, clay brought in on my boots. I would carry most of it outside, but a narrow part would remain around one joint of the bed.

When I slept elsewhere, I would wake with that joint aching.

I would return and find that the bed had shifted against the wall.

After that I would stop trying to remove the whole patch. I would clean around it. I would loosen the frame when it pulled. This would become one more household task, done before washing.

The old operator would come to examine it. She would put two fingers beneath the mattress and keep them there for a while.

“Does it do this at work?” she would ask.

I would say I did not know.

She would bring me a different pair of boots, with soles I could open along the inside edge.

—

I would still eat at the station. Still need my coat. Still speak close to someone’s ear when the pumps were making their deep compression.

But I would no longer always come indoors entirely.

There would be a portion of me laid along a drainage edge, thin enough to be mistaken for the crust already living there. Another would remain beneath the bed. I would find out about these portions through discomfort, then through practice.

I would not be able to reach everywhere. Most of the plain would remain beyond me. Even nearby, a stretch of shell grit or a busy channel could interrupt what I felt. I would have to walk over and put down a hand.

People would begin bringing me broken lengths of route rubber. I would rest them on my knees while they told me where the break had occurred. Sometimes nothing would happen. Sometimes I would feel a pull beneath the ridge by the tower, and know where to begin looking.

I would be useful, but not consistently enough to replace the chains.

The old operator would insist on that.

—

The first time I held a crossing, I would not mean to.

A child would be halfway over the studs when the ground beneath them began to loosen. I would be nearby, cleaning clay from a socket. There would be time to call out, but the child would turn toward my voice and put a foot down in the wrong place.

So I would hold still.

Something under my open boot would tighten. The socket in my hand would draw against my fingers. Across the path, the studs would remain level.

The child would finish crossing.

I would stay where I was until someone came to take the bag from my shoulder. They would sit with me. There would be no way to hurry the release without finding out what else I was holding.

By the end of it, the nearby reeds would all be bent toward the path.

—

That is where I think I would become most myself: at a crossing that needed time.

I would keep my room and my name. I would have coworkers who complained when I left tools in the mud, and someone who knew the two dents in my cup well enough to bring it to me without asking.

Some days I would be able to go far from the station. Other days a place beneath my bed, or beside the intake tower, would keep me close.

When the broad movement came underneath the plain, I would sit down if I could. I would put my hands somewhere they could be lifted safely.

There would be several small places to attend to.

A reed socket drawing shut. A route stud tilting. The warm hollow left by an animal’s foot. I would let most of them move. I would be learning how little I could hold without hurting myself or the ground.

And if someone were still crossing, I would try to keep that narrow part steady.

Afterward, when the water had chosen its new direction, I would wait for the pull to ease. Then I would gather my tools one at a time.

Someone from the station would usually come out to help.

They would put a hand on my shoulder before taking the bag, so I would know which weight was about to leave me.
