At first I would be beside one of the cords that tilts a whitewashed board. Someone has asked for more light at the washing shelf, and I have pulled too far. A bright patch travels up the wall and rests on the underside of a balcony, illuminating nothing anyone needs.

“Down a little,” the woman says.

I let the cord slip. She puts her hand into the light, turns it over, and goes back to picking a splinter from her palm.

For a while I stay there holding the cord, although it has a peg and there is no reason to hold it. People have to turn around me. A child ducks under my elbow. Eventually someone tells me where to tie it.

I think I would begin as a person who has come from elsewhere. My feet would expect steps to have edges. I would look down while climbing and arrive at a landing without having noticed the basket descending toward me. I would say “Sorry” too often, including to people who had merely asked me to move.

I would have brought a small bundle: a spare shirt, a whetstone, two iron tools wrapped so their ends would not pierce the cloth. There would be somewhere I had expected to put these things. The actual place would be a portion of shelf beside a disused chimney. Before unpacking, I would have to ask whether the shelf belonged to anyone.

It would belong to someone in the morning.

—

My work would start with a broken cover.

Not a valuable vessel. A flat clay cover for a storage compartment, chipped where a thumb had lifted it for years. The missing piece would let mice reach the contents, or let damp air circulate where it was unwelcome. Somebody would put it in front of me because I had the tools.

I would try to make a neat replacement from a sherd. I would smooth its edge, test it against the break, smooth it again. When I offered it up, a woman would take it from me and turn it over.

The rough side would catch better.

I would dislike having missed that. Then I would try it her way.

There are enough broken things here to keep such a person occupied, though very few would come with permission to restore them completely. A chipped rim must still leave room for the cord tied around the jar. A patched tread must not raise one foot above the height the next foot expects. A crack beside a warm sleeping place may need to remain partly open.

I would learn to ask what a thing was doing before asking what had happened to it.

Sometimes the answers would be contradictory. The owner of a shelf would want its broken corner replaced. The person carrying grain past it would want the corner left off.

I would keep the sherd until they agreed.

—

My place would gradually become the recess beneath the stair, near enough to the court to work in daylight, but beyond the rain’s direct reach. Not the space with the hens. The smaller recess beside it, where a former doorway had been filled to waist height.

There would be a board across the infill for a bench. Under it I would keep pieces sorted badly at first—by color, because color is easy to see—and later by thickness and curve. Flat roof tile in one basket. Jar shoulders in another. Small hard fragments for wedging. A shallow bowl for pieces too useful to discard and too peculiar to classify.

Someone would borrow that bowl for soaking beans. I would find my peculiar fragments arranged on the bench in a wet little heap.

Above the bench, I would have three pegs. One for the wrapped tools, one for my shirt, one that would cease to be mine because it was conveniently placed for hanging a basket while its carrier changed grip.

I might sleep in the room behind the recess. Or I might sleep elsewhere and only slowly notice that I spent all my waking time here.

People would call toward the stair when they wanted me.

—

I would not be especially good with clay before firing. My bowls would lean. I would press too hard while trying to correct them, and the kiln workers would tell me to leave them alone.

But fired clay would suit my hands. I would like the difference between a break that could be extended with a small tap and one that demanded patient grinding. I would learn how much pressure a fragment could take, though occasionally I would learn by ruining it.

There would be a quiet, disagreeable sound when a nearly finished piece broke. I would sit with both halves in my palms for a moment before putting them down.

The girl counting blue fragments would come to inspect my baskets.

I would save blue pieces for her, which would be a mistake. She would explain that a loose piece did not count. It had to be somewhere.

“Where?”

“In the wall. Or the drain.”

If I placed one deliberately, would it count then?

She would have to think about that. Her brother would answer immediately, and she would tell him nobody had asked him.

Afterward I would leave the blue fragments in the basket. She could look whenever she wanted.

—

Eventually my feet would learn the stair. I would be able to carry a full bowl, though I would still use one hand beneath it when nobody else seemed to need to.

I would know the old woman’s pause at the channel, and wait to hear whether she wanted help. I would know which child could be trusted to fetch a narrow tool and which would bring the right tool along with several items whose absence would shortly be noticed. I would know that a complaint shouted across the court was not necessarily addressed to the person whose name began it.

There would still be things I misunderstood. I would repair something that had been left broken for a reason. Someone would return it to my bench, irritated, and explain.

My hands would acquire small white marks along the knuckles. Fine dust would remain in the folds of my tool wrapping no matter how often I shook it out. When I went aboveground, I would notice the weather first on my face.

I think that is who I would become: the person under the stair who fits old fragments into places where they are needed.

One afternoon someone would bring me a shallow dish and ask for a notch in its rim. I would turn it in my hands, looking for the safest place to cut.

“What has to pass through?”

“A little water.”

I would ask them to show me where it was going.
