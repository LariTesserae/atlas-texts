At first I would find myself on the stair where the dog sleeps.

Not because I had business there. I would be carrying something—a coil of cord, perhaps, and a bowl with its rim broken away—and would have stopped because the dog had left less room than I expected. Above me, someone would ask whether I was coming up. Below me, someone would be waiting to come down. I would turn sideways, put the bowl against the wall, and discover that I had already begun to learn the town.

I think I would live near the join between the two hollows, but not on the road that crosses it. One level below. My doorway would face a short passage that people used more often than its width justified. There would be a place outside where a shoulder pole could be rested without its load touching the ground. I would have made that place for myself, and other people would have begun using it.

The room would be older than the curtain and newer than the bowl.

For a while I might tell people I repaired handles. There would be enough truth in this: reed splints soaking in the broken bowl, cord hanging from a peg, a basket turned upside down beside my sleeping place. I would like work that could be brought to me and returned lighter than it arrived, even when I had added something to it.

But handles would not account for all the things by the door.

There would also be a straight stick with marks cut into it, several short lengths of cord, and a wooden wedge worn smooth on one side. People would borrow these. Then they would ask me to come with them, because a mark on a stick can be misread, and because the floor from which they had measured yesterday might have been raised this morning.

I would become someone who carries a height from one place to another.

Not the person who decides where a channel must turn. Not the woman who knows damp through her cheek. I would arrive after someone had said, “Here,” and before the work made that decision difficult to undo.

A woman would hold one end of my cord against the dark seam behind her water jars. I would take the other end through her doorway, past a stair, across the narrow lane. Her brother would be waiting beside the place where he intended to lay a threshold. We would have to move a drying mat. Someone would object. The cord would sag; I would tighten it; the woman would say she could not stand there all day.

That is the sort of work I would have: useful, interruptible, and without a name that everyone agreed on.

I would be known for a habit that annoyed people. Before measuring a doorway, I would ask what would have to pass through it.

“A person,” someone would say.

“What else?”

“Whatever a person carries.”

But then a grandmother would mention the grain jar, or a bed frame would be brought from a relative’s house, or the people moving in would remember that one of them could not bend easily. I would put down my stick and ask them to show me.

Sometimes I would ask too much. A man would finally tell me that he needed a doorway, not a discussion of everything he owned. I would remember this the next time and be too quiet.

I would not be especially good at carrying water. I would protect the jar so carefully on the stairs that I forgot the last movement, and its base would strike the floor with a small, accusing knock. The old woman watching would close her eyes.

“Again,” she would say.

“Shall I carry it back up?”

“No. Put it down again.”

There would be clay behind my left ear that I never quite removed. A child would tell me about it, then make a practice of checking. On the days she found none, she would seem faintly disappointed.

My room would contain a low shelf I had intended to raise. I would have measured it for the person who lived there before me, and then grown used to stooping. There would be a bright scrap of cloth folded beneath the bowl to keep it from rocking. On evenings when I wanted the cloth for something else, I would search for a chip of stone and fail to find one the right thickness.

I think I would be embarrassed by how poorly arranged my own room was.

Once, I would carry a height incorrectly.

Not by much. Enough that water, coming around a corner in the first hard rain, entered a sleeping room instead of passing it. There would be no collapse, no great public calamity. A mat would become wet. A bag of grain would have to be opened and spread. The people whose room it was would say very little while I helped them.

For several weeks afterward I would go there whenever it rained. Eventually the father would tell me I was obstructing the passage.

The error would remain as a second mark on my stick, close beside the first. I would know which was right. Someone borrowing it would have to ask.

Later, I might acquire a different task almost by accident.

When a household moved, I would stay behind a little while after their belongings had gone. There would be small things to remove from the old walls: a peg, the end of a shelf, a cord left too high for anyone to reach without help. Sometimes these would be wanted in the new room. Sometimes the people would say to leave them for whoever came next.

I would like those intervals. The room empty, but still showing where everyone had lived. A clean patch behind a jar. Smoke above the lamp niche. A child’s scratches near the floor, covered in places by later plaster.

I would not preserve all of it. Someone waiting outside would need the room, and we would have work to do.

By then I would belong to the town well enough that people called into my doorway without looking inside. They would expect me at certain jobs and forget to thank me at others. A borrowed basket would come back with onions in it. On another day it would not come back at all, and I would have to go looking.

At night, I would sometimes hear digging beside my sleeping place.

I would lie awake through several strokes, trying to decide whether the sound was nearer than it had been the night before. Then I would get up, lift the curtain, and go around to the other entrance.

The worker would be crouched in lamplight with a basket between his knees. He would look up, expecting a complaint.

I would have brought two cups. Before setting them down, I would ask him where the dry ground was.
