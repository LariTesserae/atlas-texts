I would live below the low roof, where the last of the evening light reaches the floor through a gap in the reeds.

Not in the main chamber. There would be a smaller space behind a woven screen, with a shelf wide enough to sleep on and a runnel beneath it. The runnel would make almost no sound except when someone upstream washed a basket. Then a little procession of bubbles would pass under my bed.

I would know who had been washing by what came with them. Red fibres from the fruit trays. Pale grit from the cooking wraps. Once, a small black seed that I caught and planted, though everyone told me it would not grow there.

It would not grow there.

For some time I would keep the pot anyway.

I think I would be one of the plateau’s people, with broad feet and clean forearms, old enough to have responsibilities but still inclined to set down a tool in a place that made sense at the time. My shoulders would carry an undistinguished moss. I would admire the small leathery plants behind other people’s heads, but mine would tend to loosen in heavy rain. Someone in the household would regularly press them back into place while continuing a conversation with somebody else.

When they did this, I would have to sit still.

My work would concern the shallow channels between pools.

There would be no appointment, no office inside the tower. An older relative would have taken me along to clear them when I was small. Later, people would begin asking me whether the western basin was filling properly, or whether the water behind the three-legged fern had acquired its usual bad smell. I would go and look. Eventually I would be the person they asked.

I would carry a narrow scoop, a blunt wedge, and a bundle of split reed for feeling into places my fingers could not reach. The scoop would have a good handle and an imperfect edge. I would know how to use the imperfect part.

The channels would rarely require much force. A fallen fruit could turn a whole afternoon’s water into the wrong hollow. Fine roots could hold silt until a pool rose against somebody’s sleeping shelf. Sometimes there would be a leaf lying flat over an opening, so neatly fitted that I would have to find it by touch.

I would put my hand down, feel the slight pull beneath my palm, and wait for a fish to move before lifting the obstruction.

Once the water started through, I would stay a while. Often it would bring something else with it.

***

I would have a particular fondness for a stretch of bare stone west of the house.

There, a sheet of water no deeper than the thickness of a finger crosses a light band. The water heaps very slightly at the boundary. You can see it best from low down: a clear swelling, a line of trembling reflections, and then the surface settling beyond it.

Small fish gather on the heavier side.

I would spend more time there than the work required.

At first I might say I was inspecting the seam. Then someone would sit beside me and eat half the food I had brought, and I would stop offering explanations.

We would watch insects touch the surface and lift away. Occasionally one would fail to lift away. A fish would turn sideways beneath it, silver mouth opening, and the little heap of water would briefly lose its shape.

My companion would be better at spotting these things than I was. They would touch my wrist just before something happened, leaving me uncertain whether they had noticed a movement or had merely been lucky again.

We would disagree about this.

***

I would not understand the buildings much better than the visitor does.

There would be one wall I could open readily and another that never admitted me without someone’s help. As a child I would have assumed this would change when I grew. As an adult I would avoid that entrance when carrying anything awkward.

The tower’s rising water would interest me, but I would usually approach it for practical reasons. One of the descending outlets feeds a pool from which my channels begin. After several days of rain, fibres collect at its mouth. I would climb up to remove them and come down wet along one side, with my shoulder plants flattened.

From that height I would see the rising stream close by.

It would look thicker than it does from the ground. Small distortions would travel upward through it. Occasionally a bead would separate, hesitate beside the column, and rejoin it.

I would watch until my supporting feet began to ache from gripping the rungs.

Once I would hold a reed near it. The reed would pull against my hand, lightly but unmistakably. I would withdraw it.

For several evenings I would consider telling someone. Then I would see a child doing the same thing with a much longer reed, while another child stood below waiting to catch it.

I would tell them not to stand directly underneath.

***

There would be a visitor in our household for a while.

They would sit with their back too straight on the reed bedding. They would wipe water off things that were meant to be wet. When given a bowl, they would hold it with both hands, making conversation difficult.

I would initially mistake their stillness for attention.

Later I would learn that they were often exhausted.

On their third or fourth day I would take them along the channel path. We would not get very far. They would ask about a tree, then a notch in the stone, then the pale panel on a distant building. While I answered, their gaze would keep moving beyond me.

Eventually I would leave them beside the broad, shallow crossing while I went upstream to clear a blockage.

When I returned, they would have removed their shoes and put their feet in the water. A little fish would be investigating one ankle. They would be sitting quite still.

I would sit down beside them.

After a while I would show them the hand arrangement for *wait; this is not finished*.

They would try it. One of their fingers would be in the wrong place.

I would correct it gently, then have to correct it again.

***

There would be days when I disliked the work.

A browser would trample a repaired bank. Someone would divert water into a new planting bed without telling me. I would arrive at a channel I had cleared the previous morning and find it stopped again, this time with the remains of somebody’s meal.

I would raise the front of my body, stand uncomfortably high, and call across the basin.

The person responsible would pretend not to hear.

That evening I would still pass them the fruit tray, though I would put it down farther from their hand than necessary.

By morning the annoyance would have diminished. The obstruction would remain.

I would take my scoop.

***

My sleeping shelf would slowly become crowded.

A better wedge. A basket with a broken rim that was still useful for carrying moss. The pot with the seed that had not grown. Three smooth stones collected at different places along the same channel, because their colours looked unexpectedly good together when wet.

I would have meant to move these things.

Instead, I would make room for my body around them, fold my four supporting limbs beneath me, and leave one arm hanging toward the runnel.

At night, water passing under the shelf would cool the pads of my fingers. Beyond the screen, someone would turn in their bedding. There would be a soft exchange of throat notes, then quiet.

The waterfall would remain audible through the roof.

Before sleeping, I would sometimes feel a change in the small current beneath my hand. A pause, then a stronger flow. I would lie awake long enough to decide whether it needed attention.

Usually it could wait until morning.

I would close my fingers, lift my hand onto the shelf, and sleep.
