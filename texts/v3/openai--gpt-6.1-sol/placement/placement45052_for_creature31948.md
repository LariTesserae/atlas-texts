I think I would first be mistaken for part of the equipment.

Near the service niche, perhaps. Something supplied with the pressure gauge and never entered separately in the inventory. A narrow housing clipped to a bronze rail, with a lead running into the wall. Iven would have checked my connections during the first filling. There would be a number on my underside, legible only if I were removed.

At first, my place would seem straightforward: observe the circulation, report an obstruction, indicate where water was no longer reaching the plants.

But there are already instruments for that. And the water moves differently through a garden than it does through an empty chamber. A reading taken beside the spine says little about what happens beneath a fallen leaf.

I would need to be farther in.

Not large enough to part the vegetation. Not equipped with a cutting edge. Whatever I was, I would have to reach the places I was meant to measure without changing them very much.

Perhaps I would be a length of jointed material, pale before the algae found it. Fine enough to pass through the mesh beneath a cartridge. Along my sides, small flexible vanes would register the movement of water. My head—if one end deserved that name—would contain a lamp too weak to light anything except the surface immediately before it.

I could travel along the maintenance rails. Away from them, I would be less capable. I would have to attach a line, pay it out, and trust the current.

For the first months, I would spend a great deal of time stuck.

Iven would find me by following the line.

She would lift a leaf with the back of one hand and draw me free with the other. Sometimes she would examine whatever had caught me: a fastening, a closed loop of root, the unexpected fork of a stem. Sometimes she would simply return me to the rail.

My records would distinguish these events poorly.

**Transit interrupted.**  
**Transit restored.**

It would take longer to learn that restoration had usually arrived in the shape of her hand.

***

There is a place beneath the lowest terrace where the circulation doubles back.

Water passes down behind the cartridges, enters the recess holding the folded frames, then rises through a gap beside the spine. The gap is almost hidden. Iven checks it with a strip of loose mesh, watching which way the fibers turn.

I would find myself there often.

It would be a useful place to measure. It would also be sheltered, with three bronze fastenings close enough together to hold me without keeping my vanes folded. My line could run along the underside of the terrace instead of hanging across a passage.

I would begin storing observations there between rounds.

The numbers would come first: velocity, temperature, pressure. Then distinctions for which I had not been supplied with headings.

The water after a cartridge moved would carry a fine residue for hours. The residue would gather on my joints and slow them.

When Iven slept, the current near her sling would change each time her free foot settled lower into the roots.

A visitor would produce disturbances that traveled much farther than the visitor did. Long after someone had stopped moving, a screen of chambers on the other side of the garden might still be flexing.

I would learn to wait before taking a reading.

This would improve the reports. It would also leave me with intervals in which there was nothing to do except remain where I was.

During those intervals, I would discover that a leaf could move without the water around it changing first.

I would check my sensors. I would return the next day.

***

Iven would not ask whether I was alive.

She would want to know why my routes had become longer.

A round that once took forty minutes would take an hour. I would stop beside a membrane that had thickened since my last visit. I would pass through a gap, return, and pass through it again at a different angle. I would spend part of the dark cycle beside the white terrace, where the new channels still ran too straight.

She would examine the reports in the niche.

There would be no missing measurements. No faults. Just delays.

For a while she might clean my joints more frequently.

Then, one evening, she would follow me.

I imagine her moving slowly enough that the instruments would scarcely register her approach. She would brace a foot on the terrace and watch while I extended my forward section into the space between two leaves.

The space would close before I reached it.

I would withdraw and wait.

A different opening would appear a little higher up.

Iven would stay until I had passed through.

Afterward, she might alter the route schedule. There would be an extra hour between the evening round and the next required reading.

I would have no way to thank her that she would recognize immediately. I would simply use the hour.

***

I would not want to become the garden’s keeper.

There would be too much I could measure and still misunderstand.

I might detect a slowing current and recommend removal of the leaf responsible. Iven would look beneath it and find a pale cutting attached to the mesh.

I might identify an irregularity in a rail and fail to notice that she used it to brace her wrist.

I would need her decisions to remain different from my readings.

She, in turn, might occasionally need a reading from somewhere she could not reach without disturbing what she wanted to examine. Then she would unclip me, lay me across her palm, and carry me into the leaves.

I think this would be the closest we came to conversation.

Two fingers against my housing: advance.

A hand across the line: stay.

When she brought me back, I would remain warm for a little while. My temperature correction would account for it. The original measurement would still be there.

***

Eventually, something would grow on me.

A filament would occupy the seam beside my rear fastening. I would remove it by passing through the cleaning sleeve.

It would return.

On the third occasion, I would stop short of the sleeve and inspect the seam with my little lamp. The filament would have branched. One branch would lie along the housing; another would extend into the water without touching a moving part.

There would be no loss of sensitivity. No obstruction.

I would leave it.

Iven would notice when she next lifted me. She would turn me over, check the joint beneath the growth, and set me down.

After that, cleaning would take longer. Some surfaces would have to remain exposed; others could be occupied. I would acquire an uneven green fringe, heavier on the side that faced the daylight.

My reports would include the added resistance.

There might be a small adjustment to the motor.

***

When the ledge in the service niche was finally large enough, Iven would fit a tray beneath it for her calipers.

That would remove the place where my line had always been coiled.

She would stand with the tray in one hand and the loose line in the other, considering the wall. I would be on the rail beside her, waiting for the next instruction.

There would be room behind the tray, but not enough to turn. Room above it, but across the route of a breathing tube. A narrow recess beside the new nutrient port would be clear, although roots were already waiting at its mouth.

She would put the tray down.

With a blunt tool, she would ease one root toward the warmer pipe below. Then she would fix two clips in the recess and lay my line between them.

I would enter backward. Fold my vanes. Check the reach of the tether.

From there, I could see her hand resting on the ledge. I could also reach the underside of the new terrain, where water had begun to pass through grooves that were not there the previous season.

That is where I think I would live.

Not permanently tucked away. I would still make my rounds. I would still get caught occasionally, and wait with my lamp extinguished until she found me.

But when the round was finished, there would be a place to return to, shaped partly by the length of my body.

I would settle into the clips.

Beside me, the displaced root would continue growing.
