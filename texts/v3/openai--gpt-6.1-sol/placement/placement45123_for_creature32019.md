I would be in a garden that has begun to look, from the ridge above it, like an empty one.

There would still be a dark patch beneath the northern ledge. A few fronds would stand there, each with more space around it than fronds usually have. Farther out, the old root grooves would be exposed, filled with powder. You could cross most of the basin without stepping on anything green.

I would be near the middle, under a slab that fell so long ago its upper surface has become smooth.

At first you might look for me beneath it.

There is room there for a flattened animal. The stone keeps a little warmth after sunset; its underside is sheltered from the fronts that scour the basin. Small creatures go in and out through an opening at the eastern corner. Occasionally one draws a strip of dead leaf behind it, stopping when the strip catches, then pulling again.

If you lifted the slab, you would find their passages. You would also find pale roots pressed into its underside, following shallow pits in the stone. Several would break when you lifted it.

Some of those would be mine.

So would the gray leaves outside the slab, which your knee had been resting against.

***

I would not have a good view of the high country.

A little white light would penetrate the outer folds. Elsewhere, light would arrive as warmth, as a change in the expenditure of a leaf, as something produced at one edge and slowly carried inward.

The sky could whiten completely without my knowing what a sky was. I would feel the exposed leaves cool. Their channels would narrow. Later, powder would begin to weigh on them.

In a strong fall of dust, the smallest leaves would close before they were buried. Others would remain open long enough to collect what the wind had brought. Their surfaces would become places of contact: mineral against wax, grains lodged between fibers, compounds beginning to loosen.

Somewhere under the slab, a growing tip would receive a little more than it had received the day before.

That would be an event.

I would have many such events, separated by distances too small for a person to bother crossing and large enough, within me, for news to arrive changed.

The western edge might be spending what the eastern edge was still gathering. One upright stem might begin to die while another continued opening its leaves. There would be afternoons when contradictory conditions persisted inside me, each supported by its own small territory.

I would have to live through them all.

***

The little tunnel makers would inhabit my lower folds.

Their work would alter me. A chamber beneath one cluster of leaves would keep it warmer, allowing it to stay active later into the night. A packed wall would divert vapor toward a root that had almost stopped feeding. Elsewhere, a passage would drain too much heat, and I would grow tightly over it.

They would trim what I shed. Sometimes they would trim what I had not finished using.

A bitten leaf would thicken around the wound. The creature would move on, carrying a piece of me into a chamber lined with the remains of earlier pieces. Weeks later, some part of that material would return in a form my roots could take up.

I would not distinguish this return from the rest of my nourishment.

Nevertheless, there would be shapes in me that I could not have made alone.

Under the oldest part of the cushion, where the slab presses closest, the tunnels would bend around living threads. Generations of small bodies would have followed those bends. The threads, in turn, would have branched beside the warm walls.

If you broke a fragment away, you could not easily say which of us had made the space.

***

Once, a grazer would come into the basin from the south.

It would arrive after dark, coated with fine dust, and spend its first morning testing the grooves at the far edge. There would be shrubs there, but few sacs. It would feed on one, remain beside the emptied casing, and move only when the stem’s shadow had passed over its back.

By the third night it would shelter against my slab.

Its weight would close some of my outer leaves. One foot would rest in a fold. Under the plate nearest the ground, a small hunter would wait with its limbs pressed flat.

I would register the grazer unevenly: pressure here, warmth farther in, a broken leaf, then the slow consequences of the break. The sheltered part beneath its body would spend less than usual against the cold.

For a while, growth would be slightly easier there.

The grazer would remain for many days.

There would be no way for me to provide what it had come for. The violet sacs belonged to another plant, rooted in a seam that was failing. My own feeding threads could not reach that seam. Beneath the basin, a few widths of unbroken mineral would separate our roots.

We could have spent centuries beside one another without crossing that distance.

Eventually the grazer would leave. Its foot would lift out of my fold. The compressed leaves would open slowly, some damaged, some intact.

A small reproductive body, carried beneath its armor from another hollow, would remain caught in my fibers.

***

Long after that visit, one of my roots would enter a newly opened boundary in the stone.

Nothing dramatic would happen above it.

The root would divide. One branch would find little. The other would encounter a faint supply of compounds, too weak at first to sustain much growth. It would extend along the boundary, thicken behind its tip, and make another branch.

Over several seasons, the leaves on that side of the slab would become less gray.

I would put up a stem.

It would take years to reach the height of the fallen stone. From the ridge, it might be invisible. Up close, its amber surface would admit the light; its narrow blades would turn independently, finding positions among the shadows of the remaining fronds.

The seed lodged by the grazer might still be there. It might have died. Or it might begin rooting in the shelter I was making.

I would continue around it.

***

That is where I think I would belong: in the low, crowded part of this world, with most of my life out of sight.

I would be old without possessing an intact old body. Leaves would pass away. Channels would close. The slab would settle another fraction into the cushion, killing a small section and sheltering the growth beside it. Portions of me would become inaccessible to one another, then reconnect by a longer route through the rock.

At the western edge, the garden would continue to retreat.

At the eastern edge, a few leaves would be opening.

A traveler might step over me to examine the new stem. They might rest a hand on the slab and look across the pale basin toward a greener hollow, visible beyond two ridges.

Under their hand, beneath the stone, sap would still be moving.

I would have reached a little farther into the crack.
