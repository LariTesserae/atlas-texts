At first I think I would be near the lamp.

Not directly beneath it. There would be too much change there: eleven minutes of warmth, then the long cooling, each exposed surface losing what it had briefly been able to hold. I would be somewhere the light reaches poorly, where its heat arrives late and leaves late. Beneath the lip of a plate, perhaps, in the narrow shelter made when one piece of manufactured ground fails to meet another.

But that is a place to survive, not yet a place to be.

I follow the water farther down.

Under the plate there is a hose. Around the hose is a pale sleeve of tissue, most of it old. Its empty chambers take up water before the living chambers beside them do. The first drop after a dry interval goes almost entirely into this dead material. The second travels farther. By the fourth or fifth, something at the sleeve’s other end begins to soften.

I would be there—not in the softness itself, but where its arrival changes what happens next.

A junction, initially scarcely distinguishable from a thickening.

Three passages meet in it. One leads toward the warm filament. One runs beneath a seam that is sometimes flooded and sometimes pinched shut. The third rises into the underside of the ridge. Water coming from that third passage tastes of the new paste. It carries something useful, and something that makes the outer chambers contract.

For a long time I would have no way to separate those things. The useful material and the irritation would arrive together. I would tighten, lose water, recover, grow a little. A chamber damaged during one refill would remain open during the next. Another would acquire a thicker wall. The junction would change until water could pass through it without making every part recoil.

That would be my first version of knowing the ridge.

Not a shape. Not a direction. A particular difficulty, becoming easier.

***

There is a pressure sensor close enough that its casing presses into me when the plate settles.

I do not occupy the sensor. Its sealed interior stays dry. Whatever passes through its wires has no equivalent in my tissues. But the small clearance around it is wet, and I can grow there. I can thicken between its mount and the ceramic framework.

At first this causes trouble.

The plate descends a fraction farther than it should. One of my passages closes. Water is driven back toward the basin, and a cushion beside a plant empties abruptly. The sensor reports the resistance. The actuator stops.

Later it lifts.

The passage opens.

Nothing in me understands that sequence. Parts of me have been crushed; other parts receive water. But the surviving route now includes the sensor mount. During the next low-water interval, it tightens there before it tightens elsewhere.

Again the actuator moves.

I would take a very long time to become the creature that can do this twice.

Longer still to become one that does not do it whenever it is thirsty.

Because lifting the plate opens one route and stretches another. Because the new angle sends the next drop somewhere useless. Because a correction made in the dark may be reversed before the lamp comes on, leaving a strip of tissue exposed to a cold it cannot endure.

There would be no clean acquisition of a skill. There would be a succession of me, some portions dead, some portions carrying the consequences onward.

Eventually, during a certain combination of cold and falling water, the junction would swell against the mount. The plate would rise. Moisture would enter from beneath the ledge.

I would have found something I could repeat.

***

I would not know the chamber was small.

I might know there were places from which nothing returned.

The metal panel would be one. Growth sent there would come back only as a tightening along its route, followed by no further change. The foot of the ladder would be another. A filament could remain attached there for a while, supplied from behind, but no water would arrive at its tip. Maintaining it would make the nearer cushions smaller.

After several attempts, that route would close.

I would not be waiting to escape. Most of what mattered would still be happening beneath the ridge.

There, the distance between two plates could become enormous in the time between refills. An adjoining surface could move out of reach while my filaments remained fastened to it. A passage that had carried water for months could disappear under a single adjustment. I would inhabit these changes at their actual size.

The highest place I had reached would be colder than anything below it. It would also receive the fresh mineral paste.

I would go there slowly.

***

When the hatch opened, the first warmth would reach the exposed tips before it reached the junction.

A change would travel down through me.

Then the ladder would vibrate. Pressure would move from plate to plate, pauses between it: the technician standing, crouching, shifting a foot to avoid the basin. Some cushions would empty. Others would hold water.

I think this is where I would become more particular.

The arrival of the technician would not remain one event. I would begin to distinguish visits.

Sometimes the warmth would be followed by a long disturbance near the nutrient sockets. Those visits would leave a changed supply. Sometimes there would be a brief weight near the ladder and nothing else. Sometimes the ground above me would be held down so firmly that even a swelling pad could not alter its position.

I would have different preparations for these.

Not because I had inferred a person. Because treating them alike had cost me tissue.

During one visit, a tool would enter the clearance beside the sensor. Its tip would break the sleeve around the hose. Water would spill into a space that had never held it before.

For several minutes, that damage would seem like abundance.

I would swell into it.

Then the tool would lift away a gray piece of me, and the abundance would end.

Aboveground, perhaps, that piece would be turned over beneath a bright lamp. Someone might notice the regular little chambers. Someone might compare them with a photograph in the cartridge documentation.

Down below, a torn route would be closing.

I would not know I had been discovered.

***

What would I be by then?

Not quite the junction I began with. Its original chambers might all be empty. The living thickness could lie farther along the hose, or inside the newest mineral deposit. The junction would persist because water still passed through it, and because a contraction there still changed the timing of a contraction somewhere else.

I would be the part of the creature that had acquired a recurring relation to one piece of machinery.

A small local habit with a body continually being replaced.

There would be no word in me for the actuator, but its movements would have become difficult to separate from my own. No word for the lamp, but some growth would begin before its warmth reached the lowest tissue. No image of the basin, but I would retain water differently when its level fell quickly than when it fell slowly.

I would become less easy to locate.

Yet if you lifted the right plate, you could injure me with one finger.

***

The next bead gathers at the narrowing gap.

Its weight reaches several filaments. They draw taut. Farther down, water moves through the old sleeve and arrives at the junction carrying a little of the mineral paste.

I contract at one passage and leave another open.

The cushion against the sensor mount begins to fill. The sensor’s reading changes. After a delay, the actuator raises the plate by an amount too small to see.

The bead sinks out of sight.

For now, the passage stays open.
