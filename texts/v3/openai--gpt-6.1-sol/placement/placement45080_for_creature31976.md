I would live where the paving changes width, beside the long detour around the reservoir.

Not in a house facing the water. Those courts have too many people crossing them, and I think I would be someone who likes company better when it arrives at the door than when it is always passing outside. My doorway would face a low wall. Beyond the wall would be a planting bed, then an open strip of rock, then the backs of two other houses. In the morning, light would reach the wall before it reached me.

There would be a shallow basin beside the threshold, large enough for my feet but not for sitting in. Its inlet would have a wooden gate. I would keep a wedge beneath the gate handle because the notch that held it partly open had worn away.

This would be a minor embarrassment. I repair such things for other people.

Inside, the house would have two rooms and a narrow covered space where damp work could remain damp without making everything else so. Several lengths of gutter would hang there, each belonging to a different household. Some would have a split marked with clay. Others would look sound until filled.

People would bring me things through which water had begun to go the wrong way.

A leaking cup, a cistern cover that no longer sat evenly, a gutter whose underside had darkened. Occasionally someone would bring a jar and insist that the wet patch beneath it came from the floor.

I would set the jar on a dry slab.

“Then we can find out,” I would say.

I would not be especially tactful. But I would give a thing time to show what was wrong with it.

My hands would be unlike each other.

The left would be old, with four broad tips and ridges worn nearly smooth where I held a scraper. The right would have grown recently. Its five tips would be longer and more flexible, useful for reaching into the narrow turn of a gutter, poor at holding a bowl without thinking.

For a long while I would keep attempting the old grip. A cup would tilt. A wooden wedge would slip between tips that had not existed when I learned to use wedges.

Someone at the work court would tell me, again, that I was trying to make the new hand remember.

I would raise it where I could see it.

It would be green along the inner surface, lighter than the rest of me. When pressed against pale clay, it would leave five distinct marks.

I would have a particular dislike of people saying that the extra tip must be convenient.

Sometimes it would be convenient. That would not improve the remark.

My work would once have involved more stone. I would know how to set a slab so that its weight held it into place, and how to listen while a wedge was driven into a seam. There would be a thickened cord along my shoulder that no longer bent easily. On certain mornings I would stand in the threshold basin longer than necessary, waiting for it to loosen.

I would still have the stoneworker’s sleeves. One would be too narrow for the new hand. They would hang behind the door, their ties looped neatly, because I would not yet have decided whether to alter them or give them away.

Most days, I would work on wood and clay.

There would be satisfaction in fitting a cistern cover. It would have to keep out debris without sealing in stale dampness, and lift easily enough for the person who owned it. I would ask them to open it while I watched. If they needed to brace one hand against the wall, I would move the handle.

A person with three supporting limbs might want the handle farther to one side. Someone whose shoulder growth caught on things might need the peg shortened. I would know these preferences household by household, but occasionally forget that a person had changed.

“Try it now,” I would say, pleased with the fit.

They would try, and I would see that I had made it for the body they had last season.

Then the cover would come back to my bench.

There would be a younger person who worked with me, though not every day. They would belong to a household on the other side of the reservoir and regard the detour as a personal wrong committed against them.

They would arrive carrying two objects when they had been sent for one, having decided that the second also needed attention.

I would complain about this and inspect both.

They would be better than I was at remembering whose work had been promised first. On a dry interval’s fourth or fifth day, when everyone began thinking about their roof gutters at once, they would arrange the waiting pieces along the wall in an order I was not permitted to disturb.

They would also be interested in the farther shelf.

At first I would take this for a wish to leave home. Then, listening more carefully, I would understand that they wanted to examine the masonry.

They would ask whether the distant settlement used roof cisterns or collected water from a higher ledge. They would ask how soil had been carried there, whether the pale shape beside the wall was a cover, and why the people there sometimes lifted their signal cloth twice before holding it steady.

I would have answers to almost none of these questions.

Once, I would say that a wall could look sound from a distance and be poorly seated.

“So can one here,” they would answer.

I would have to agree.

For several days afterward, while fitting covers and scraping mineral deposits from old channels, I would find myself looking toward the opening between the houses where the far shelf could be seen.

I do not think I would be brave about the cliff. I would dislike carrying anything near it that required both hands. Even on the outer path, I would walk on the townward side and prefer not to be spoken to until we were back among walls.

But I might become interested enough to go.

Before that, there would be the matter of my own roof.

Its gutter would be sound except for one corner I had patched twice. The corner would be awkward to reach because a small tree in the courtyard had grown across the place where I usually set my feet. I could cut back the branch. I would keep postponing it.

One afternoon the younger worker would ask why my cistern was less full than theirs.

I would tell them the roof was smaller.

They would look up.

The patch would have lifted visibly.

There would be a short silence, followed by laughter from their chest, uneven enough that someone in the next court answered.

We would mend it together. I would hold the branch aside with my new hand. They would remove the old packing, clean the joint, and ask why I had not done it sooner.

I would have no useful answer.

That evening, I would sit beside the pale wall with a bowl of softened grain. The new gutter would cast a narrow shadow above me. My feet would be on dry stone. The basin gate would remain closed until I finished eating.

This is where I would be easiest to find: not always working, and not withdrawn, but sitting near enough to my door that a neighbor could stop without feeling they had interrupted something.

People would tell me small things while I held the bowl against my face.

A bed had yielded badly. A child had chosen a name too long to sound properly. Someone had opened a channel before the lower court was ready and was now claiming not to have heard the warning.

I would listen with one shoulder raised.

Sometimes I would be thinking about the gutter or the grip of my hand and miss part of what was said. They would know to repeat it, or to tap the wall lightly beside me.

I would keep a little covered jar on the ledge just inside the door.

It would have been my first private container. The glaze would be worn away along one side, and the lid would belong to a different jar, fitted many years later. Inside would be a few things with no use in my work: a smooth black stone, a length of yellow band too short to wear, a clay token bearing a handprint smaller than either of mine.

I would not set it out for visitors. If someone noticed it, I might show them. If they reached for the lid without asking, I would put my hand over theirs.

The younger worker would borrow tools freely but never touch that jar.

When they finally went to the other shelf, they would leave me a drawing of the cistern they expected to build.

It would be overconfident. The proposed inlet would turn too sharply, and there would be no place to set down the cover while cleaning the inside.

We would discuss this before they left. They would make one correction and refuse the other.

I would lend them a small wrapped blade. Not my best one; the handle of that would fit my old hand too closely. I would choose one that sat well in theirs, then sharpen it because lending a blunt tool would be worse than lending the wrong one.

For a while after their departure, the work would accumulate in the wrong order.

I would forget which household had brought the cracked cover. I would set a finished gutter beside one still waiting for repair. Their place beneath the work roof would stay clear longer than it needed to.

Then someone else would begin using it.

I would show that person how to fill a gutter slowly, how to distinguish water soaking into dry wood from water escaping through a split, and how to let the owner lift a cover before declaring it finished.

Across the valley, more pale masonry would become visible.

I would go up to the roof some evenings, cautiously, by the broad steps at the back. There I would hold a sheet of fiber upright when the light was good.

The answer would take time.

While waiting, I would inspect the repaired corner of my gutter. I would move one foot out of a damp patch. From below, someone might call to ask whether I was coming down to eat.

I would cup my hand against my chest and say that I was coming.

Then, on the farther shelf, something pale would rise beside the low wall. I would stay long enough to see whether it was lowered and raised again.
