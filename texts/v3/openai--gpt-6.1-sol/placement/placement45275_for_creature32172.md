At first I would look for a place beneath the shelves, where the light reaches only after striking something else. Not the deepest refuge—the lizard needs that—but the strip beside it, where fallen leaves collect and a person’s fingers can still reach.

I would want to be low enough to hear the grit move.

Up in the shrub, every arrival disturbs a branch. Down here, a beetle can pass under a leaf without making the whole courtyard know. The hand-mill comes through the paving as a faint repeated tremor. Between turns, there is the scratch of something trying to get purchase on clay.

Perhaps I would have been carried here. There are so many things in this garden that began elsewhere, serving another purpose.

I think I would still have a handle, though no one would trust it now.

It would lie against my side, flattened and brittle. Beneath a coating of earth-colored clay, there would be a weave: narrow strips crossing one another, their edges once supple enough to bend without breaking. A few would remain visible near the bottom. In dry weather they would shrink away from the coating, leaving tiny passages.

That is where I would find myself: the basket leaning against the wall.

Someone brought me up from the dwelling when I could no longer carry a load. They pressed clay into my weave, working it from both sides. The marks of their fingers remain inside, but earth covers them. They gave me a place on a broad stone and turned my damaged side toward the sandstone.

For a while, I must have held my shape well.

Now my lower edge has settled. There is a slight bulge where the earth presses outward, and the wall takes some of my weight. A trailing succulent grows over my lip. Its oldest stems rest against me; the newer ones have reached a neighboring pot. I cannot hold all of it within the shape I was given.

Under that plant, things happen slowly enough to leave traces.

A leaf falls and stays green for several days. Then its skin loosens, and its edges curl inward. An ant tests it and leaves. Later, other ants return to a split at its base. Dust gathers in the hollow it makes. By the time it breaks apart, a thin root has come down beside it.

I would know the rain first at the rim.

A dark spot, then another. Water would descend along a stem and enter the earth well away from where it struck. The clay on my outside would darken unevenly. Within me, the old woven strips would swell, pressing against their brittle covering.

A little water would escape through the bottom. Not enough to make a lasting wet patch. Enough for the smell to change, and for the lizard to come out and pause nearby with its head raised.

By morning, my outer surface would be pale again. Deeper in the earth, the roots would still have something to draw upon.

I would also know the person who comes to tend the courtyard, though mostly by pressure and shadow.

They set a vessel down beside me before reaching toward the shelves. Sometimes one foot braces against my stone. Once, they tried to straighten me. Both hands slid beneath my lower edge, and for a moment my weight lifted from its accustomed place.

But a root had passed through the weave and entered the wall.

The person felt the resistance. They lowered me again, more carefully than they had raised me. Afterward they pushed a small stone under the bulging side.

I would have that stone still.

There would be days when nothing touched me except the plant. Days when the sparrows dropped husks into my earth, or a wasp searched the exposed strips along my bottom. The spider might fasten a thread from my handle to the wall. Each time the plant moved, the thread would pull a little.

Eventually, a piece of clay would fall from my side.

It might fall while the courtyard was empty. A small sound, lost beneath the brood calling in the jar. Behind it would be the dark weave, a pale root, and a pocket of earth held together by finer roots. The person would find the fragment later, turn it over, and look at the opening.

Perhaps they would patch it. Perhaps they would tuck a curved potsherd against me and leave the rooted earth undisturbed.

By then there would be fewer strips that could still bear weight. More of the plant would rest on the neighboring pot. The root in the wall would have thickened.

I would remain in the place where they lowered me, leaning a little, with the small stone underneath. Through the opening in my side, an ant would enter carrying something white. Another would come out empty-handed. Above them, close to the old rim, two leaves would be opening.
