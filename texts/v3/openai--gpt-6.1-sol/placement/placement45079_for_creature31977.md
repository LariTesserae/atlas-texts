At first I would choose a room.

Not one of the high rooms with a view over the cloud, but a room halfway down a long terrace, entered through a passage that turns twice. Sunlight would reach its floor for a short time in the afternoon. There would be a square window and, beneath it, the stain left by water that once came through the sill.

I would imagine sitting there, sheltered, watching the light move.

But a room is a choice made by something that needs a room. The dry center of the floor would offer little. The window would open onto distances I could not use. Even the shelter would be unreliable: what keeps out rain can keep out the water needed to live.

I would move toward the wall.

At its foot, a dark line continues around the corner and disappears beneath the threshold. Here the stone is cooler. A grain of mortar, detached from somewhere above, has come to rest in the joint. Water divides around it. On one side the passage is almost too narrow to see; on the other, a little sediment has gathered, holding moisture after the current stops.

This is closer.

I would be under the threshold, but not far under it. Far enough that the afternoon sun could not reach my back; near enough that new water would touch me before it filled the hollow behind me.

There would need to be another way out.

The threshold has cracked across its width. Its inner end rests on the floor, while the outer end is held a little higher by a chip of fallen stone. Underneath is a low, uneven space. One opening leads into the passage. Another leads into the room, where a shallow groove runs toward a broken drain.

I could fit there.

Then I would have a body: broad enough to cover a patch of wet stone, thin enough to pass beneath the lifted edge. Six feet, folded close. A pale back that dries unevenly, the ridge becoming gray before the edges do.

I would be one of the small animals, ordinary among them.

Perhaps a pad on my left side would have healed badly after being caught under a settling flake. It would still hold, though I would take longer to find purchase on smooth stone. On a stair I would tend to turn a little sideways. Nothing about this would distinguish me at a distance. Even close by, you would have to watch several movements before you noticed.

My place would be the underside of that threshold, and the length of gutter immediately outside it, and sometimes the room behind.

There would be no single boundary.

When rain reached the terrace, the gutter would widen across the passage. I could feed farther from the wall. A low spot that had been unreachable through the dry afternoon would become connected to my shelter by a film of water. I would cross it, mouth pressed to the paving, leaving a track that narrowed where the growth was thin.

I would stop often.

A shadow would pass, and my feet would tighten. The shadow might be cloud, or a bird, or the edge of a grass stem moving above the opening. I would not need to distinguish them immediately. Remaining attached would come first.

After a while, I would move again.

The city beyond this passage would reach me in small arrivals. Water from the upper lanes would bring pale grit. Water that had stood in a roof hollow would be warmer and carry fine fragments of dark soil. After a long dry interval, the first runoff might be too thick with dust to feed in. I would draw back beneath the threshold and wait while it passed.

I would know little of where any of it had been.

The distant rain would matter when it reached my feet.

In the room behind me, the drain would work badly. Its opening would take water freely at first, then catch loose flakes and swell the pool across the floor. This would be useful until it was not. The sheltered growth would thicken. More animals would arrive. We would feed along the same edges without keeping equal distances, sometimes touching, sometimes passing over a tail that had not yet moved aside.

As the pool rose, I would climb the underside of the threshold.

There, held above the water, I could remain for hours. My feet would find the pits in the stone. Water would bead against my folds. Below, small fragments would turn slowly in the current.

If the water reached me, I would move toward the outer opening. If that opening had become a strong current, I would turn back and try the crack leading into the wall.

A familiar place could become difficult in one afternoon.

I think I would remain in that part of the city for several seasons. Not because I belonged to the room, or because I recognized the passage as home, but because its wet intervals were usually long enough, its shade dependable, its exits still open.

In winter I would go deeper.

The groove behind the threshold leads beneath three steps to a drain with a low ceiling. I would join the bodies gathered there. My back would touch another animal’s underside; a foot would rest briefly against my head and then settle elsewhere. Meltwater would run below us, sometimes carrying enough warmth to make me loosen my grip.

Mostly I would stay.

When I returned, the passage would have changed. A little more of the corner would be gone. The sediment fan would extend farther into the room. Perhaps the grain that divided the current beneath the threshold would have washed away, and the water would now take the shorter route.

I would turn with it.

One year, during the mild rains, I would leave the passage and begin climbing. The water would be continuous up the lane. Other pale backs would appear at the foot of the first stair.

I would reach the lip slowly, favoring the foot that did not hold as well. Beneath the projecting stone I would pause. Above me would be another small wet surface, beyond that another riser.

For a while, enough water would remain.

I would put my forefeet over the edge.
