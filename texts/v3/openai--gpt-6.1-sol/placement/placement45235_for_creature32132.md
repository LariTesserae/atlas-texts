At first I would stay near the entrance.

Not because I wanted to leave. Because the hollow would take some time to enter me. I would have brought the wrong measures: the length of a breath, the interval between footsteps, the expectation that a thing which had begun moving would soon arrive somewhere.

I would watch a spoke descend and mistake its disappearance for an ending.

The outside air would cool one side of me. I would keep shifting to give it the other side, and each time I did, I would lose some small event in the chamber. A leaf would have turned. Water would have passed through a groove. The creature beside the gauge would no longer be beside the gauge.

Eventually I would stop shifting.

I do not think I would remain a person there for very long.

The trouble would be my height. Too much of me would lie outside the habitable layer. My head would be hot, my ankles hotter, and the middle of my body would have no means of persuading the rest to become less cumbersome. I would begin by bending. Then by resting an arm across a broad leaf, discovering that it could bear a little weight if the weight were spread.

What I wanted from a body would change.

Less depth. More underside. The ability to remain in contact without pressing.

I might first mistake myself for a fallen piece of insulation: a narrow, gray-brown length lying along the rim of a stone shelf. One end curled beneath itself. The other divided into several soft tabs. Dust would adhere to the upper surface, giving it the same red as the lanes.

But fallen insulation would not draw its end away when the next ceiling tile brightened.

I would.

Slowly enough that the distinction would take a while to establish.

There would be no particular front to me at first. A warmer end and a wetter end, perhaps. Parts that could stiffen and parts that could thin until they slid between a root and the stone beneath it. I would learn where I ended by attempting passages that were too small.

Some of those attempts would leave material behind.

A soft fringe caught in a split housing. A translucent skin along the bottom of a conduit. I would withdraw and feel the absence for several revolutions of the wheel. Later, passing that way again, I might find the abandoned piece still moist. I would touch it without knowing whether it remained mine.

My place would be on the inner wall of the little metal shelter, just below the shelf.

Not in the cup.

The cup would be too dry, and its dust too fine. Crossing it would cost me more moisture than I could spare. But the wall beneath it would have a narrow folded seam, and inside that seam the heat would be less direct. A little condensation would reach it along the underside of the shelf. Enough, sometimes, to wet three or four finger-widths of metal.

I would live along those finger-widths.

My body would become a flattened crescent there, with a tougher upper skin and a pale lower surface fitted closely to the riveted wall. When water arrived, the lower surface would loosen. Small chambers would fill. For a time I would be thicker, almost comfortable.

When the wall dried, I would contract into the seam.

I would hear very little.

What reached me would come through contact: the piston as a long compression; the loose plates as a fine, continuous irritation; the creature’s unequal steps as two pressures that did not quite resemble one another.

Its broad foot would arrive first as a spreading weight.

The prongs would make three small certainties.

I would know it was coming before the leaves at the threshold moved.

At first I would retreat completely when it entered. The creature would stand in the former place of the stool, and I would become a dark line beneath the shelf. Its hand would rest on the cup. Dust would shift above me.

Then it would leave.

Nothing would have pursued me into the seam. Nothing would have tried to peel me away.

After many visits I would remain partly unfolded.

One tab, then two.

I would not understand the cup. I would know only that the creature touched something there which did not answer through the wall. The cup’s weight remained nearly constant. It held no circulating water. Its dust made no demand I could detect.

Still, the creature came.

There would be intervals when it stood so quietly that I could distinguish the turning beneath its dead leaf from the larger movement below the floor. A slight hitch. A pause. A beginning again.

I would become accustomed to that smaller rotation.

Outside the shelter, I would do very little that anyone could call work.

I could move grit, but only a few grains at once, carrying them in folds of my underside. I could make a damp crossing a little longer by stretching across it. If a drop gathered beneath the shelf, I could touch it before it fell and lead some of it into the seam.

Once I would find a fine copper thread pressing against my skin.

For a long time I would treat it as an obstruction. I would try to pass above it, then below it. Eventually I would remain against it because there was nowhere better to go.

The thread would give me another way to feel the hollow.

A distant shoot turning. A tension changing somewhere behind the brick enclosure. I would lie still and receive these things, without being able to locate them precisely. Some would arrive from farther away than I had ever travelled.

I would not pull on the thread.

I would fit a shallow channel around it and let it remain.

My longest journey would be to the condensation ledge where the creature rested.

It would take so long that I would begin in one condition of the chamber and arrive in another. The water I had meant to follow would be gone. The leaf that had shaded my route would have lifted. I would have to shelter beneath a fallen stem until the tile above it dimmed.

At the ledge, the creature would lie nearly horizontal.

I would find its lowest plate resting on a small ridge of stone. Each piston stroke would make the plate move against the ridge. Very slightly. Not enough, perhaps, to matter.

I would settle between them.

This would not be a decision I could have explained. The space would be narrow, shaded, and close to moisture. Those would be sufficient reasons to enter it.

But when the plate bore down, my body would spread the pressure. When it lifted, I would thicken again.

The creature would move one of its short arms. A blunt crescent would touch my upper skin.

I would hold still.

It would leave me there.

Afterward, I would sometimes return to the shelter and sometimes remain beneath the ledge. I would have two places, separated by a journey I could not undertake casually. In one, I would feel the visits to the cup. In the other, I would feel the creature resting.

Neither place would need me every time.

That would suit me. I would have dry periods of my own, when even the smallest opening cost too much. I would withdraw, leaving only the tough surface exposed. The pump would continue. Water would find routes that did not include me.

And then, one day, in the shelter, a thin strip of the creature’s hand would descend past the shelf instead of folding immediately away.

It would stop beside me.

There would be moisture on it, gathered elsewhere.

I would not unfold all at once. A pale edge would emerge from the seam and touch the wet strip. Water would pass between us. The hand would remain until I had taken a little, then withdraw with some still clinging to it.

Above us, the dust in the cup would bear its shallow circular groove.

Below, I would settle into the wall, somewhat thicker than before.

I think that is what I would become: a small body with two places to live, able to recognize a particular arrival through stone.

If you looked into the shelter, you might notice a clean crescent beneath the shelf where the dust did not quite adhere. You might take it for a mark left by a missing fitting.

You would have to stay.

The pool would rise. The shelf would tremble. Eventually, three pale prongs would settle at the threshold.

And the crescent would open a little.
