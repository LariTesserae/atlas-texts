I would first be noticed underneath the cupboard, where one of the fine wires has been fastened twice.

There is a little ceramic eye for the original fastening. Beside it is another, newer eye, set slightly lower. The wire passes through the second. The first is empty.

I would occupy that empty eye.

Not because I was small enough to fit inside it. Some of me would extend beneath the tiles, and some would lie against the underside of the cupboard, in the cool region the cups’ warmth never quite reaches. But the empty fastening would be the place from which my position could be understood. If someone wanted to find me, they would look there.

While I was present, its hole would seem deeper than the ceramic was thick.

At first, I think I would be mistaken for something left over from the repair.

The cupboard opens; the wires tighten; warmth separates into seven slightly different kinds. A small disturbance persists beneath the lowest fastening. It could be an installation fault. It could be a pressure adjustment taking longer than expected.

I would make the same mistake about myself.

For several openings, I would attend only to the wire. Its tension changes before the light brightens, then changes again after the light has gone dim. I would spread along that second change, trying to follow it to its end.

There would always be a little more.

When the next opening came, I would still be following what had happened at the previous one.

Eventually, another resident would settle beside me. It would occupy the newer fastening while I remained in the old. The wire would pass through it, making its boundary difficult to distinguish.

A door across the alcove would fold inward.

Then another.

I would continue attending to the tension.

The resident would leave before I understood that the two doors had been meant for me.

At the following opening, I would notice it approaching from a different street. I would gather myself out of the empty eye. For the first time, the hole would look shallow.

That would be my first answer.

***

I would have a body that was easier to locate by its ends than by its middle.

One end could rest against the empty fastening while another extended through the alcove entrance. Between them, I would be thin and irregular. A pipe seen through me might acquire a second rim. The grout between two tiles might appear to continue beneath a third.

When I contracted, something would remain loose for a while. When I extended, some narrow portion of me would be slow to arrive.

I would learn to allow for this on the stairs.

The broad resident who takes them habitually would pass me more than once. It would lose definition at each riser and recover on the landing. I would get halfway up and discover that part of me was still beside the cupboard.

There would be shorter ways. I would use them when I was frightened.

But I would keep trying the stairs.

On a landing, I could remain almost entirely in one place. The clear well above it would show a patch of daylight with one edge broken by an overhead fitting. As clouds passed, that edge would grow faint and return. I would stay there through several changes, until the cupboard had opened and closed without me.

Afterward I would go back quickly, expecting to find something altered.

The cups would still be there. The empty eye would be empty.

I would settle into it with an unfamiliar care.

***

My first useful habit would concern a door in the next street.

It is narrow, even by the city’s standards, and opens onto a wall. Sometimes, when a resident passes, it folds shut before the faint answering boundary has finished forming.

I would wait beside it.

For a little while after its blue marks changed, I would lie along the lower edge. The door would close against me and hesitate. Another panel farther down the street would brighten. Then I would withdraw.

The two residents would continue together.

I would do this often enough that they began to take that street when they wanted to touch one another.

There would be a small alteration to the pavement: a ridge no higher than the rim of a cup, running beneath the door. With it, I could maintain my position without spreading into the adjoining channel. I would spend several days examining the ridge before using it.

Once I had learned to rest there, I would stay too long.

A resident would pass. The marks would change. I would hold the door open.

No answering boundary would appear.

Farther away, two panels would flash and go dark. The passing resident would contract abruptly. It would take another route, losing itself in the bundled conduits overhead.

I would remain beneath the door, still waiting.

An angular shadow on a braided thread would reach the square. It would pass through the part of me extending across the lane.

For a while, I would be unable to find my ends.

One would be against the ridge. Another would seem to lie inside the wall. A third, which I had not known I possessed, would register every stroke of a nearby plate.

The other residents would close doors around me. They would hold one cold channel steady. A grille would stop drawing water through the corner where most of me had gathered.

I would be given very little to attend to.

Even so, coming together would take a long time.

Afterward, a narrow portion of me would remain slow to contract. It would show as a place where the straight line beneath a door bent slightly downward.

I would leave the ridge unused for many cycles of the cupboard.

When I returned, I would let the first passerby go without intervening. Then the second.

On the third passage, an answering boundary would begin to form. I would wait until I could distinguish it before extending beneath the door.

The delay I made would be much shorter.

***

There would also be the black fleck.

Its keeper would bring it to the alcove one day and hold it near the old ceramic eye. I would retreat into the tiles, making room.

The fleck would remain suspended between us.

In the bright cupboard light it would have a blunt end and a finer end, though those might only be shadows. I would examine it from several positions. Its keeper would turn it once, very slowly.

When the cupboard closed, I would continue to look.

The keeper would return with it on another day.

Eventually, I would go to the clear pipe where it was usually held. I would remain on the other side of the glass. There would be no need for a door to open.

I would not find a fleck of my own. I would bring the keeper to the stair landing instead.

We would wait beneath the broken edge of daylight.

The cloud that came would be thin. The change would be barely visible. I would worry that we had arrived at the wrong time, or that the fitting’s shadow had looked different when I was alone.

Then the keeper would move slightly.

The fleck would cross from light into shade.

We would stay until another cloud came.

***

I would draw near the entrance, but not often.

The sediment curtain would be difficult for me. Too many grains would arrive at once, each with its own irregular surface, each briefly changing the light. Beyond them there would be fibers, fragments, darker things. I could not follow one without losing another.

The resident who waits there would make room for me beyond the silver teeth.

For a time I would try to spread as widely as it did.

The pounding would pass through the iron. The curtain would bulge. A grain caught at the ceramic seam would turn over, showing a pale side I had not expected.

I would gather too sharply around it.

Part of me would slip toward the teeth, and I would withdraw so quickly that my slower portion remained stretched across the floor.

The waiting resident would change its shape. A narrow boundary would lie beside mine, extending back into the city.

I would follow it to the first corner.

It would return to the entrance. I would go to the cupboard.

On later visits, I would stay closer to the heated strip. Sometimes I would leave before the curtain moved at all.

The resident would continue to make room.

***

Over time, the empty fastening would become less exact.

A minute dark line would develop along one side of the ceramic eye. The wire in the newer fastening would polish its own little groove. The cupboard would still open at regular intervals, but one hinge would begin to settle with a faint second movement after the other.

I would attend to these things without trying to correct them.

There would be evenings when I occupied the whole underside of the cupboard. There would be others when I fitted into the empty eye so closely that the hole looked ordinary.

Sometimes I would miss an opening because I was on the stairs.

Sometimes I would return with another resident and find the alcove crowded. We would wait outside until there was room to separate.

I would still have difficulty doing that. The portion damaged by the braided shadow would occasionally trail into someone else, and I would have to contract slowly, allowing their boundary to become clear before recovering my own.

One resident would learn to wait through it.

We would meet often beside the narrow door. On some occasions I would hold it. On others, they would pass without answering, and I would let it close.

Then, much later, a blue panel near the cupboard would change.

I would leave the empty fastening and go to see.
