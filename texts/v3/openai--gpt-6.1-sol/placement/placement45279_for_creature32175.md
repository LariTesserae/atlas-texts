At first I would stay beside the upper seep.

It seems the easiest place to be without asking anything of the hollow. There is a basin to drink from and a sloping stone that has almost dried. I would sit with my feet on the gravel, keeping my weight away from the dark soil. For a while I would watch the water gather at the crack, trying to catch the instant when separate beads become a thread.

But I think my attention would keep going into the crack.

The water comes out already changed by somewhere I cannot see. It leaves a faint deposit along the stone. When I rub that deposit between finger and thumb, there is a little slipperiness before the mineral roughness. Something has come up with it.

That is where I begin to lose the shape of a visitor.

I would follow the wetness inward, past the reach of a fingertip, past the narrow place where the stone makes the water hurry. Beyond it, the passage widens. There is room for water to turn. A pale fold holds one side of the chamber; the other side is rock, orange in places, dark where nothing has disturbed the old coating.

I would be somewhere along the seam between them.

Not in the main current. In the small sheltered length where the animal’s wall draws close to stone without quite touching it. Water enters there, slows, and carries some of its fine burden down against the surface. A scrap of leaf, reduced almost to softness. A mineral grain. Something loosened from a root.

I would begin as a little roughness that catches these things.

Perhaps, seen from outside, I would look like a cloudy thumbprint on the pale wall. The water would pass over me without visibly changing. I would have no view of the chamber, only its conditions arriving: colder water, sweeter water, a pressure that makes my whole small extent lie flatter.

I would be a living film.

There would be many bodies in me. Some fixed close to the wall, some held farther out in the slippery material we make, some able to leave and settle again. At first this seems to make the word *I* difficult. Then the difficulty becomes less important. What happens at one edge changes what is possible at the other. We thicken together where food collects. Where the current grows harsh, we become sparse.

My place would be smaller than the palm of the hand I had imagined bringing to the seep.

For a long time, it would be enough.

Above, a fern would extend a new frond. Down here, a faint change in what the water carries would reach us. We would grow into it. Later, after warmth had lain on the hollow for several days, something rich would arrive from the roots, and the film would become thicker along its lower edge.

That thickness would alter the water.

Only slightly. Enough to hold a grain that would otherwise have passed. The grain would make a sheltered side. We would grow there too.

I would acquire folds of my own.

There would be a ridge no taller than a grain of sand, then a shallow pocket behind it. Threads of the colony would join across the pocket. Water would pass beneath them. The larger animal would continue its slow swelling, and my little structures would bend with its wall.

Some would tear.

I think this would be the first event that felt like movement rather than growth.

In the afternoon, the chamber fills more fully. Its pale side rounds inward. Water presses through the narrowing seam, lifting an edge of the film that has become too thick to hold. A portion loosens. For a while it remains joined by a thin strand, turning in the current. Then it goes.

There would be no alarm traveling through the remainder. There would be exposed surface, altered flow, fresh room to grow.

But I would go with the loosened portion.

I had thought I belonged to the wall. Now I would be a soft fleck traveling through the dark. The water would fold me over myself, open me, press grit into me. I would pass through a chamber whose living walls have withdrawn, where the pale lining has become firm and no longer yields. For a little while the current would divide around a lodged stem, and I would turn there repeatedly.

Then I would be carried upward.

Light would reach me before open air. It would change the outermost lives in the fleck. Some would cease to grow. Others, carried all this way from darkness, would find something they could use.

I would emerge in one of the wash-bowl basins.

For the first time, there would be space above me that water did not fill.

I would settle beneath a transparent leaf. Its veins would lie across me, delicate and resistant, while the remaining softness fed us. A shadow would cross the basin when the fern moved. The water would warm. Near evening its level would fall, bringing the air close enough that the upper part of the film began to dry.

That would be dangerous. I would not belong in every part of this place merely because I had come from within it.

A drop from the fern would break us apart.

Some of me would remain beneath the leaf. Some would spill through the notch. A small portion would cling to the lower jaw of the three-toed creature when it came to drink.

I would go beneath the bush with it.

There, in the damp passage beside the roots, the creature would rub its mouth against a stone. A little moisture would stay behind. So would I.

This place would have a different rhythm. Less rushing, more waiting. Warmth would arrive through the creature’s belly when it rested nearby. Root hairs would enter the narrow space and disappear. During the afternoon rising, water would reach the stone from below, bringing substances familiar to the lives that had survived the journey.

Beneath that stone, a young margin of the larger animal would be advancing.

I would find myself on it eventually. The margin would grow under the film, lifting it away from the mineral surface so slowly that nothing needed to detach. We would spread where it spread. Our waste would enter its tissue; some of what passed through the tissue would sustain us.

My new place would be close to the cold rim of the hollow, where the animal is thin and winter can reach almost all of it.

There would be seasons when the film nearly vanished. A sheltered smear would remain under the stone, receiving little, growing little. When water returned, it would take days for the first uneven cloudiness to show.

And then I would be there again: thicker near the seam, holding a grain, making a small lee in which another grain could settle.

Overhead, the bush would put down a root into the soil beside us. It would turn once and continue.

Water would gather on the animal’s margin. In that shallow shine, a few clear threads would rise. Between them, close to the stone, I would begin to spread.
