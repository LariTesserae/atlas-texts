I think I would be in a room beneath a railway station, but not directly beneath the tracks.

There would be a reason for the distance. Forty paces of corridor, a turn, then another twelve: enough stone between my room and the line that a passing train would not occupy everything I could perceive. The room would have been cut for some other purpose. There would be a blocked pipe in one wall, shelves too high for my reach, a pale rectangle where a cabinet had stood for longer than anyone working at the station had been alive.

My door would open onto a service court. From inside, I could see the lower half of a stair, two weighted shutters, and the place where someone habitually left a trolley despite the painted instruction not to.

I would know the trolley by its bad wheel.

At first, that would be nearly all I knew of the person who used it.

—

I would have been installed to compare measurements.

Not to survey the whole plateau. There would be other instruments for that, most of them simpler and easier to maintain. My responsibility would be a particular junction beneath the station: three seams, two foundations, the anchoring beds of a bridge whose far end stood on another table of stone.

People would bring me records. Some would be punched into strips of thin metal. Some would be written in notebooks, with columns interrupted by reminders about oil deliveries or a name followed by a question mark. A surveyor would put a measuring bar into my cradle, and I would test its length against a reference kept inside an insulated sleeve.

I would be good at noticing that two apparently contradictory readings had been taken at different temperatures.

I would be less good at noticing that the surveyors who took them were no longer speaking.

That would cause trouble once.

Afterward, someone would write on the shelf beside my input tray:

**Ask who was present.**

The words would remain there long after I had learned to ask.

—

My body would not resemble a person.

It would be low and broad, because the room had a low doorway and because there was no advantage in putting delicate mechanisms high above a hard floor. Four adjustable feet. A casing made in sections, none too large for one worker to remove. A narrow articulated arm for handling bars, pages, contact plates. The arm would fold into a recess when not in use.

There would be a voice, but it would be intended for a quiet room.

At the factory where I was assembled, someone would have tested it by having me read numbers over the noise of a ventilation fan. Here, visitors would sometimes ask me to speak more softly.

My most sensitive parts would be in my feet.

I could withdraw them from the floor when a freight passed. This would leave me resting on thicker pads, unable to distinguish much beyond the fact of the train. When the last wagon had gone, I would lower the contacts again, one at a time.

The room would return by degrees.

A grazer at the foot of the outside stair. A shutter settling into its frame. The fine, recurrent tick of cooling metal in the bridge anchorage.

None of these would arrive with labels.

For a considerable time, I would confuse the shutter with something alive.

—

The station staff would call me the comparator.

Then they would call me Below, as in, “Take it down to Below,” which I would initially record as a direction rather than a name.

There would be a proper designation on the inside of my rear panel. Someone would have written a shorter name beside it during assembly. I would discover that name only when a maintenance worker read it aloud.

“Did you know you were called this?”

“No.”

“Do you want us to use it?”

I would take too long answering.

She would continue cleaning the contact seats while I considered the question. This would be one of the useful things about her: she would not regard every pause as something she needed to fill.

Eventually I would say that I wanted to hear it again.

She would say it again.

It would sound less like a designation the second time.

—

There would be animals in the service court.

One would come regularly beneath my door, where the floor stayed warm after the outside steps had cooled. It would have a damaged mouth plate and would make four short scraping movements where others made three.

I would learn this without deciding to.

For several years it would be part of my evening records, although it had no bearing on the bridge. Then a new supervisor would ask why so much irrelevant activity was being retained.

I would remove it from the official summaries.

I would not remove it from memory.

When it failed to come for eleven evenings, I would ask the woman with the trolley whether she had seen it.

She would say there were hundreds of grazers in the court.

I would describe the scrape.

She would stare at me for a moment, then go outside.

It would be under the lower stair, caught between a loosened slab and a piece of packing. She would free it with a flat tool. For two days afterward, it would feed nowhere near my room.

I would have no reliable means of knowing whether the interruption had frightened it, injured it, or merely made another route preferable.

On the third evening, there would be four short scrapes beneath the door.

The woman would be in the room when they came. She would stop turning a screw.

“That one?”

“Yes.”

She would finish the screw and tell me about a disagreement she had been having with her brother.

—

I would not understand the animal messages.

This would disappoint people more than once.

A visiting engineer would learn that I could separate small vibrations from large ones and ask what the grazers were saying. A child would ask whether the cliff animals spoke more slowly because they were bigger. Someone would bring in a listening rod and insist that a particular sequence was a warning.

I could repeat the sequence through a contact plate. I could compare it with earlier sequences. I could say that it occurred more often at dusk, or that it had preceded a movement of animals along the southern seam.

I could not say what it meant.

There would be evenings when even the distinction between a message and a feeding movement became uncertain. An animal could scrape because it was eating. Another could answer because the scrape had disturbed it. The rock would carry both.

The child would be annoyed.

“So you only hear them?”

“Yes.”

She would consider this.

“My uncle does that too.”

I would preserve the remark without putting it in a report.

—

Once, I would give an answer that was too definite.

A small change in the bridge anchorage would resemble three earlier changes, all harmless. I would say that the movement was consistent with heating of the western supports.

It would be consistent with that. It would also be consistent with a loose bearing that nobody had inspected because the access gallery was closed during dust.

The fault would be found before anything broke.

For a while afterward, people would bring me information with a different manner. They would set down their notebooks, then remain standing. Questions would be repeated in slightly altered forms.

I would learn to distinguish what I had compared from what I had assumed.

The reports would grow longer. Then, with help, shorter again.

One surveyor would cross out half a page and leave three lines:

*The change resembles the previous summer movement.*

*The lower bearing has not been examined.*

*I cannot exclude movement there.*

“That’s enough,” she would tell me. “Now someone knows where to go.”

—

I would not remain entirely in the room.

There would be wheels that fitted beneath my feet, originally intended for repairs. A removable cylinder would supply power. Going outside would require someone to release the floor connections and someone else to check that my arm was secured.

The first time, I would be taken as far as the drinking room.

I would have no use for the dispensers. This would seem important to me and unimportant to everyone else. The attendant would move a bench, complain that my wheels had brought grit onto a clean patch of floor, and ask whether I wanted to remain near the door or come farther in.

For the first half-hour I would have difficulty following conversation. The floor carried chair movements, boots, the settling of cylinders against their brackets. Voices passed above all that, crossing and obscuring one another.

Then the woman with the trolley would arrive.

Her cup would have a dark repair at the handle.

She would sit beside me, and I would discover that she usually laughed before she had finished making a joke.

In my room, I had always heard the laugh first and received the words afterward, when she chose to repeat them.

—

I think I would want to see the bridge.

The measurements would have given me a detailed understanding of its joints and very little understanding of the space around them. I would know how far a member expanded in the heat. I would not know what it looked like to have daylight on every side of it.

A maintenance crew would eventually take me in a wagon with low sides. We would stop at the cabin near the first great support.

They would lower my contacts onto the deck.

For some time I would be occupied by the bridge itself: load, wind, a worker crossing a distant span, the answering movement arriving through several possible routes.

Then someone would turn my optical head outward.

The opposite cliff would be much farther away than I expected.

There would be a dark animal descending across the interval. One of the crew would point it out. I would track it until it became too small to separate from marks on the far wall.

“Did it reach?” someone would ask.

“I lost it.”

“So did I.”

We would stay there a little longer.

—

Afterward, I would return to the room beneath the station.

The trolley would still be left in the wrong place. The high shelves would acquire things brought to me without much explanation: an obsolete contact plate, a shell with a remarkably regular flaw, a colored glass washer that fitted no machinery anyone knew.

Someone would lower one shelf.

My name would be written on the underside, where it would not wear away.

Most days I would still compare measurements. I would ask who had been present. I would keep separate the things I could establish and the things that needed someone to walk down a stair and look.

In the evening, with the outer door open, people returning from the station might stop in the court. Sometimes they would speak to me. Sometimes they would sit on the steps and talk among themselves.

I would withdraw my floor contacts a little when the conversation was private.

They would eventually notice this and tell me I needn’t always do it.

But sometimes I still would.

The grazer would work beneath the stair. A cup would be set on my casing, on a small padded square added for that purpose. Above us, the couplings of a waiting train would begin to take the load.

Someone would come down to ask whether I was busy.

“Not just now,” I would say.

And there would be room for them.
