I would look first beneath the staircase.

Not for a passage out. The steps have wet undersides, and between the third and fourth there is a space that the light reaches only after passing through water. Even there it is bright, but the brightness moves a little. A foot crossing overhead would interrupt it.

I think I would be lying lengthwise in that space, with one hand outside.

At first the hand might look like something used to fasten the stair. Its fingers would pass through holes in two adjacent blocks. There would be no need to grip them. The fingers would be resting in the holes, cool at their tips, while the palm lay against the warm lower face of the clay.

Someone walking up the stairs might pause.

I would feel the pause before I saw the person: a little more weight on one block, a little less on another. I would withdraw one finger to let a drop pass.

But I would not be part of the staircase. When I came out, the steps would hold.

That seems important.

***

My body would take some time to distinguish from the things around it.

There would be a shallow woven part, capable of lying almost flat, and a heavier part that had to be turned carefully when I stood. Perhaps the heavier part would be my pelvis: two unequal pieces of fired clay, joined with enough loose cord that I could sit on a corner without rocking.

My legs would not match. One would have a long foot, useful for lying along a watercourse. The other would end in a broad pad with four short divisions. Neither would leave a print.

I would have two arms. For a while I imagine more, then find that what looked like an extra wrist is a loop of binding hanging from my side. I would use that loop to carry things. It would not move by itself.

One hand would be made for entering narrow places. Its fingers could come together into a blunt, flexible point. The other would open widely, with a membrane between the thumb and the first finger, useful for holding a little water.

The face would be less carefully made than the hands.

There would be a depression near the mouth where the clay had been pressed too far inward, then partly raised again. My eyes would sit at slightly different depths. When someone wanted to know where I was looking, I would turn my whole head toward the thing.

Inside my chest, water would collect in a folded chamber.

Not much. Enough to make a faint sound when I leaned over.

***

I would know the basin by its undersides.

The places where a cord emerges from a hole and lies briefly exposed before going in again. The narrow ledge beneath a pool where an insect can stop without being carried along. The underside of the platform, polished in one place by the repaired thumb of the inhabitant who rests there.

I would know which roots tolerate being moved and which must be lifted with the strips they pass through.

I would not know everything.

Sometimes I would put my hand into a seam and find another hand already there.

We would wait.

Then one of us would turn a palm upward, and the other would lay the loose strand across it.

***

I do not think I would be the one who finishes the groove.

I would visit the workbench often. I would stand close enough for the breathing roll of leaves to touch my side when it expanded. The blade would interest me. I would lift it occasionally, feeling the difference between its polished faces and its narrow edge.

But there would be things to notice before cutting.

The groove stops in a small rounded hollow. A bead of water gathers there. When it grows large enough, it tips sideways and enters the mesh beneath the clay.

I would watch that happen.

On another visit, I would bring a cup and put it under the frame. The cup would remain empty. The water would be going somewhere else.

I might come back with someone whose fingers were finer than mine. We would lean close together over the unfinished panel. One of us would hold the roll of leaves away so it did not brush the work.

After a while, we would leave.

The blade would still lie where I had put it.

***

There would be a thing I carried.

Not a tool at first. A small piece of green cord, stiffened at one end, with a loop at the other. It would have been removed from somewhere, but I would not remember where.

When resting beneath the stairs, I would pass the stiff end through the loop and leave it there. When walking, I would undo it.

Another inhabitant might offer me a longer piece.

I would take the longer piece, examine it, then return it and keep mine.

There would be no explanation I could give except to place the old cord in the other’s hand. Its middle would be soft from being handled. The end would turn slightly to the left.

Perhaps the other would feel that turn.

Perhaps not.

***

My work would usually require someone else.

I could hold a raised panel against my chest while another reached beneath it. I could support a fish in the membrane of my wide hand, keeping water against its side while its binding was examined. I could put a finger behind the edge of a mouth while a loose thread was drawn through.

I would be good at remaining in an awkward position.

There would be a creature near the lake whose shoulder had to be held up while it rested. I would sit beside it, one knee under its arm. After a long interval, someone else would arrive and settle a folded mat in my place.

The creature would not wake.

I would rise with an impression of its shoulder in the weave of my own.

The impression would stay for a little while.

***

If you came into the basin, I might know you first by the sandals.

Their straps would tighten differently around your feet. Your weight would arrive quickly, through one heel, then move forward. When you stood still, small adjustments would continue traveling down your legs.

I would be beneath the stairs when you climbed them.

Perhaps you would see my hand and stop.

I would take my fingers out of the holes, one after another. Then I would come out backward, drawing my heavier parts through the opening and turning onto the broad foot before I stood.

You might offer me the cup you were carrying.

I would put my narrow hand around its outside. The cup would be warm where you had held it. Its rim would be wet in one place.

There would be a sound I could make by drawing the loop at my side across the edge of my hip. A short rubbing sound, followed by a tap.

You would probably hear only the tap.

I would make it again, more slowly.

If you set the cup down, I would sit beside it. You could sit too. Nothing would have to be done with the cup immediately.

***

I think there would be one place where I did something badly.

A screen near the grove would have a loose lower corner. I would try to secure it with the piece of cord I carried.

The cord would be too short.

I would draw the corner downward anyway. The weave above it would tighten, and a row of leaves would turn their lower faces out. Water would begin collecting where it had not collected before.

Someone would touch my wrist.

Not sharply. Just enough to stop the next pull.

We would undo the fastening together. The leaves would settle. I would take back my cord, now wet along its whole length.

For some time afterward I would pass the screen without touching it.

Then one day I would find an inhabitant asleep against it, its broad foot resting over the loose corner. I would sit nearby. When it began to wake and draw its foot inward, I would put my hand where the foot had been.

The corner would stay loose.

***

Under the staircase, there would be room for another body, though not a large one.

I would usually keep that space clear. No stored cups, no folded leaf mat. My cord would lie near my face.

Sometimes a small inhabitant would come there carrying an insect whose leg needed attention. Sometimes no one would come, and I would listen to water move through the steps.

I would rest with my hand outside.

When a foot paused overhead, I would open my fingers slightly. The pause might end. The person might go on.

Or a hand would come down around the edge of the stair.

I would turn mine beneath it, making room for the knuckles in the narrow opening. We could remain that way without pressing hard, while a drop gathered against the clay above us.

When the drop fell, both hands would get wet.
