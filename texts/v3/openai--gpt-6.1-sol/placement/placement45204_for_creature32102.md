For a while I would think I was standing on the shelf.

The pebbles would be in front of me. The stair would be behind me. I would have the ordinary conviction that, if the water rose farther, I could take a step back.

Then the surge would come, and I would discover that I had mistaken the direction of safety.

Away would not mean up the stairs. It would mean inward, through a hairline opening beneath the pebble nearest the wall. I would draw myself into that opening without turning. The water would pass over the place where I had been, and I would feel its weight through a thickness of stone.

There would be no soles, after all. No clothes cooling against my back.

The sensation I had taken for dampness on my wrists would persist in several places, some of them far apart.

One would be beside the stairs. Another would be under the lip of the distant basin. A third would be high on a pillar, where no water had reached for a long time.

I would have to wait for the chamber to quiet before I could begin to understand the distance between them.

*

My body would be thin enough to disappear in the stone’s grain.

In the sheltered places it would resemble the dark coating around the fronds. On exposed surfaces it would draw back into seams, leaving only a discoloration, a brownish edge under the cream mineral. Much of it would lie farther in, where water passes through cracks too narrow to admit a limb.

I would not fill the chamber. There would be interruptions everywhere: dry stone, hot fissures, channels that had worn too deep. Some portions of me would be joined by no more than a damp thread beneath a crust. Others would have lost their connection and would continue for a while without me.

A sensation from the far wall might arrive after the event that caused it was over.

This would make the room difficult to know.

When the waterfall began, I might still be feeling its preparation: pressure against a sealed pocket, a change in warmth, water pushing into a seam that had been empty. Then the impact would arrive through the pillar near the entrance. Much later, the portion beneath the waterfall would report its own flattening.

I would learn not to hurry these things into one moment.

There would be times when I held several versions of the chamber at once.

*

The small translucent animals would graze along my outer edges.

I would feel the delicate scraping of their mouths, then the short, almost weightless pressure of their legs. They would not eat enough to injure me. Where they passed, loose sediment would be lifted and the water would reach a fresher surface.

Their hiding holes would go through me.

Before a surge, their bodies would press into those holes, and I would draw away from them to leave room. One animal would always enter farther than the other. It would place its front legs against a patch of tissue deep in the stone and remain there until the pressure fell.

I would know that particular pressure well.

If it failed to arrive one day, I would keep the passage open longer than necessary.

*

The large inhabitant would find me in the hollows.

Its hand would fit into one beneath the basin’s lip. The blunt fingers would settle against the soft lining, carefully enough that the contact would not tear it.

For a few seconds, the pressure of those fingers would be clearer than the entire torrent.

It would press, release, and press elsewhere.

I could not tell it what the chamber was doing. I would have no account of the room to deliver, no hidden plan of the passages. But my tissue would be swollen where hot water had entered. It would be drawn tight over a cavity under pressure. A margin that had been broad the last time the animal visited might now be narrow.

Its fingers could find these differences.

Sometimes it would leave after one touch. Sometimes it would stay and clear the mineral from the hollow’s rim, exposing a little more of me to the water.

That would hurt in a slow way.

Later, when the fresh edge thickened, the same place would become easier to feel through.

I would not know whether the animal understood the hurt. I would know that it used different fingers there on its next visit.

*

A narrow part of me would reach the ceramic arch.

Not into its workings. Only beneath one buried foot, where a flaw in the glaze let moisture collect.

The clicks would come through with painful precision. Each would tighten that strip of tissue and travel outward, becoming less distinct as it went. Near the stairs, a click might be no more than a brief inward pull.

When the animal fitted a new plate, the next pulse would sometimes be different.

For a little while, a part of me that had been receiving almost nothing would become sensitive again. A crack beneath a distant island would warm. Water would begin moving along it. I would feel grains of old deposit loosen and roll.

Other repairs would change nothing I could perceive.

I would remain under the foot of the arch, slowly thickening around its flaw. Too much growth there would narrow the wet passage. The inhabitant would occasionally scrape me back.

It would eat what it removed.

I would feel the torn edge, and then the water entering.

*

Nearest the entrance, my body would be sparse.

The warmth would escape too quickly there. Air moving down the stairs would dry my exposed margins. I would survive beneath the rough shelf, sending small extensions into its pits whenever spray gave me enough water.

This would be the place where I first learned the visitor.

Not their shape. I would have no clear way to assemble a shape.

I would know the repeated burden of two feet. The hesitant shift before a step. A bead of water falling from some height, striking close to me, then another. When the visitor stood still, one foot would gradually take more weight.

The inhabitant would reach across the channel.

Its fingers would lift a pebble from an exposed patch and place it near the wall. The pebble’s new weight would press down on a small extension of mine. Under it, moisture would last longer.

The second pebble would do the same.

When the next surge reached the shelf, I would withdraw from the outer edge. Water would cover the roughness where the hand had rested, spread toward the new weights, and stop.

Behind the line, the visitor would remain.

I would feel both feet through the stone.

*

I would grow most readily beneath the pebbles.

Over many visits, the line might become a row of damp undersides connected by fine dark threads. A moved stone would expose a tender patch. I would pull back from the air, then extend again when it was set down.

There would be a broad shallow hollow near the wall that I could eventually cover. Water lingered there. The stone was warm. Nothing grazed it.

But on quiet occasions the visitor would lower themself onto the shelf and put a hand in that hollow.

The palm would warm it differently from the water below.

I would feel the small shifts of pressure as the fingers relaxed. Sometimes the hand would stay until the arch clicked again.

I would grow around that place. At its edge, I would thicken a little, catching condensation and holding it in the stone’s grain. The middle would remain bare.

And when the hand returned, I would know where to find it.
