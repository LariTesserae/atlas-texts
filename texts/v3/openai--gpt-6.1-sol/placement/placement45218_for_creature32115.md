At first I would stay near the doorway.

Not because I was afraid of the creature, though I might be. The doorway would give me something simple to attend to: one foot in the warmth of the chamber, one on the cooler floor outside. Water would gather differently beneath each. Behind me the ribs would hold the sound close; ahead of me it would spread across the plain.

I would stand there long enough to discover that the threshold was not quite level.

A little water would be trying to enter the room. A little would be trying to leave. Between those movements, beside the place where my heel rested, a pale strand would sway without going anywhere.

I think I would keep looking at that strand.

The chamber-dweller might come near. It might press the floor once, beside my foot, and wait for me to move. I would move. The water would find another course.

For some time, that would seem to be my part in the valley: to stand somewhere, learn what my standing did, and then stand somewhere else.

***

But I do not think I would remain a visitor.

The changes would be small enough that I could mistake them for habits.

I would stop lifting my feet all at once. The floor releases more easily if you begin at one edge and let the separation travel. I would learn this before noticing that the edges of my feet had become thin.

I would sit with my palm against a wall. Later I would discover that I had been sitting there through an entire change of light, not asleep, not waiting, following a vibration that sometimes arrived in my wrist and sometimes in the bones of my arm.

There would be places I could no longer pass without stopping.

A covered channel whose sound went briefly dull at the bend.

An arch that shed one large drop from its eastern foot, although all the smaller drops fell from the west.

A shallow depression where water continued turning after the rest of a pool had become still.

I would begin to recognize these places without looking at them.

And then, one afternoon, I would reach into a cleft beneath a terrace and find that my arm went farther in than it should.

There would be no pain. I would feel the water moving along its hidden bed, and beyond it the pressure of roots pressed closely together. My fingers would separate around something I could not see.

When I withdrew them, a fine green sleeve would remain between two knuckles.

I would hold it toward the daylight.

It would be mine.

***

I would not live in the chamber.

I would visit it, especially toward evening, when the familiar resident rested against the wall and the ringing conduit carried a warmer, lower note. But I would always leave before the light went out of the doorway.

My place would be farther down.

Near the rim, beneath a trough that returned condensed water to the valley, there would be an opening hidden by overlapping leaves. From above it would look too narrow to enter. From inside, it would be a sloping passage, smooth with moisture, widening where the woven floor became the descending roots.

I would go through sideways.

Below the valley the noise would lose its separate voices. Water would fall past my face in long clear lengths. Cloud would move between the roots, sometimes opening enough to show another pale column at a distance, sometimes closing until I could see only the surface beneath my hand.

Here the roots would not seem like supports. They would be walls, crossings, places to put a foot.

I would find a hollow where three of them divided.

It would be warm at the back and cool at the mouth. There would be room to fold my legs, though not to straighten them. Water would travel across the ceiling without dripping. One narrow root, no thicker than my wrist, would pass through the hollow and descend out of sight.

That would be where I rested.

Not at the bottom of anything. There would still be no bottom.

***

My body would suit the passage badly at first, then better.

I would have long arms, but one would be longer than the other. The shorter hand would remain broad enough to brace against a root. The longer one would be narrow, with fingers that could lie separately along several wet surfaces.

Across my back would be a folded membrane.

I would not know what it was for until the cloud rose into the hollow. Then it would loosen by itself, exposing shallow channels on its underside. Moisture would gather there. I would feel each bead as a small increase in weight.

When enough had collected, I would lean forward and let it run along my side.

I would have eyes, though I would use them less beneath the floor. Their lids would close upward. My mouth would be fringed with filaments that troubled me whenever I climbed into dry air.

There would also be a hard place near my shoulder.

Perhaps it would come from the first time I entered the passage too quickly. Perhaps it would already be there when I began to notice my body. I would not be able to reach it properly. When I folded the membrane, one section would remain slightly raised.

I would learn to sleep on the other side.

***

The thing I did would not have a name I knew.

There would be a narrow root below my hollow that shook whenever water passed through a certain conduit overhead. Usually the shaking would travel down it evenly. Sometimes it would hesitate.

I would lay my long hand along the root.

There would be no damaged place to find. Its surface would be smooth, its water clear. Yet the hesitation would come again, somewhere beyond my fingertips.

I would move lower.

At a fork, I would put one foot against each branch and wait. After a while, I would feel it: one branch tightening a little before the other. The water would catch there, not enough to stop, only enough to alter the sound above.

Sometimes my weight would ease it.

Sometimes I would have to stay through the afternoon.

Sometimes I would leave and return to find that it had eased without me.

I would not always know whether I had helped.

There would be other hollows among the roots. Occasionally a hand would appear through the cloud, gripping a column above mine. I would lift my own hand. The other creature might remain until the next vibration passed through both our bodies, then climb away.

We would rarely need to come close.

***

I would still go up to the basin.

The journey would leave me wet, and I would sit for a while near the opening, letting the warmth return to my limbs. Across the plain I would see the chamber-dweller moving low beside a channel, one shoulder held differently from the other.

Sometimes it would accompany me.

At the circular wall, I would place my short hand beneath the rolled edge. The long one would trail in the basin.

I would feel the water arrive against my fingers. Some of it would have passed through channels I knew from underneath. It would carry no clear message, but now and then there would be a pulse I recognized, softened by distance.

The chamber-dweller would smooth the tender rim.

I might look at the brown mark along its ribs. It might touch the place where my membrane failed to fold.

Neither of us would stay there long.

Before returning below, I would sometimes press my hand into the tiled floor of the basin. The leaves would give beneath it. Water would enter the shallow impression and brighten around my fingers.

Then I would lift my hand.

I would watch until the surface rose again, until the last narrow hollow disappeared. Only then would I cross the plain and lower myself into the passage, feeling for the first root with my feet.

In the hollow below, I would settle against the warm back wall.

After a while, through the root beside my wrist, I would feel someone walking overhead.
