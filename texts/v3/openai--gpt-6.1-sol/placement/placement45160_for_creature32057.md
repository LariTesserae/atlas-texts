I think I would first be found in the little office where inspection crews leave word of their routes.

It would not be the main survey office. There would be no great plan of the city spread beneath glass. Just a counter, a stove, and a rack of narrow drawers whose bottoms occasionally needed shaving so they would continue to open. The office would stand beside a tram shed, close enough that the first departure shook the spoons in the tea glasses.

I would have gone there to ask about an address.

The address would be perfectly legible. I would have written it myself. But the clerk would turn the paper over, as though the rest of the directions might be on the back.

“Which district?”

I would tell her.

“Yes,” she would say. “Which district is that in?”

Behind me, a crew would be waiting to sign out a set of lamps. One of them would have a boot wrapped in cord to hold the sole together. I would move aside so they could get on with their work.

This seems important: I would not arrive as someone especially capable of understanding the city. I would arrive with a modest question that turned out to be badly formed.

***

My room would have come with a block of six houses after a dry summer.

I would remember the room before its arrival. A narrow kitchen, a window overlooking the back of a public eating room, a shelf that dipped enough to make cups collect at one end. I would remember buying the cups. I would remember the woman who sold them wrapping each one separately, although they were cheap.

The eating room would not be outside the window anymore.

There would be a plaza, an inspection path, and, a long way off, a building with a white repair dressing around its lowest floor.

The water inspector would tell me not to use the left-hand tap until the following afternoon. The housing clerk would give me bread and ask whether I lived alone.

I would say yes, then hesitate.

She would wait.

There would be a second coat on the hook behind the door. It might have been mine. It would fit well enough through the shoulders, less well at the wrists.

“Yes,” I would say again.

She would put a small mark beside the answer. Not a warning mark. Something indicating that she had asked.

I would keep the coat.

For a while, I would go to the route office every few days. Not to insist. To see whether anyone had found the address.

Eventually the clerk would stop making me stand at the counter. She would point to the kettle.

“If you’re staying, put that on.”

***

There are things one can do while waiting for an answer.

Copy the inspection slips. Replace the cords on the lamps. Carry a message to the shed because the speaking tube has become unreliable in cold weather.

I would learn which crews wanted their tea before departure and which wanted it kept for their return. I would learn that a man who always joked about being lost had once been missing for nineteen days, and that it was best to let him make the joke.

At first I would think the work consisted mostly of remembering names.

Then someone would come back without his partner.

He would not burst through the door. He would come in very carefully, set his lamp on the counter, and ask whether she had returned ahead of him.

The clerk would make him sit down. She would ask where they had last spoken to each other. Not where he had last seen her. They would have been working on opposite sides of a partition, passing tools through a hole.

“What did she say?”

“That she wanted the small scraper.”

“And you gave it to her?”

“Yes.”

“What happened next?”

He would keep looking toward the street.

I would go and close the outer door. The tram had arrived, and its engine was making it difficult to hear.

That would be my first useful act in the business of routes.

***

I would not become a surveyor. Figures would take me too long, and I would be liable to copy a measurement correctly onto the wrong line.

I would become the other person.

The first time I went out, I would carry nothing but a lamp, food, and the spare coat. My partner would be called Sera. She would be younger than me and already impatient with the office.

At the entrance to an old ventilation passage, she would say, “You have been down here before?”

“No.”

“They told me you had experience.”

“I’ve come back.”

She would look at me for a moment, then hand me one end of the measuring cord.

We would work badly together for several weeks.

I would remember too much that was useless: a blue bowl on a landing, the place where someone had wiped greasy fingers beside a door. Sera would want the order of the junctions. I would give her the smell of a room.

Once I would announce a familiar stairway with such confidence that we would descend three flights before finding it ended in a closed window.

After that, Sera would establish a rule.

“Say when you remember. Say when you think.”

I would dislike the correction. I would follow it.

In time, I would become competent enough that we could argue about other things.

***

What would I be, then?

A route keeper, probably. An ordinary person with an uncertain previous address and a job that made use of attentiveness more than certainty.

I would have the broad boots. I would repair them badly at first, putting a new layer under both soles when only one needed it. I would own a scarf that had absorbed the smell of the route office stove. My hands would always be cold when I began work and warm by the time there was nothing left to do with them.

I would talk while walking.

“Low doorway. Mind the lamp. There was washing here last time. Turn after the dry basin.”

Sera would answer without looking up from her instruments.

“Washing gone. Basin still dry.”

We would not require the world to remain exactly as we remembered it. That would be an unreasonable condition of employment. But we would want to know what had changed, and we would prefer to find out before putting our weight on it.

Some evenings I would still try the address I had brought to the office.

Not in the city. On paper.

I would write it beneath my present address and examine both. The old one would remain familiar. I would no longer be certain I could have given directions to it even before it disappeared.

***

I think I would meet Iven during a routine inspection.

His factory would have reported a change in the drainage beneath the cutting floor. Nothing urgent: water arriving in a channel that had always been dry, a little swelling around a support.

He would let us in before the day shift. While Sera checked the plans, he would show me the place where the press sounded without moving.

“Has it done it this morning?” I would ask.

“Finished twenty minutes ago.”

He would offer tea. I would say we had better start, and then regret it when I saw steam rising from the glass in his hand.

Below the cutting floor, we would find a warm passage and a shallow basin. No visible leak. The new water would be coming through a seam too fine to admit the tip of a blade.

Sera would measure its rate while I checked the exits.

Farther along, I would hear three small taps.

Then two.

I would know that pattern. There would have been a sticking pipe in the kitchen of my old room. When the upstairs tenant drew water, it had knocked three times, paused, and knocked twice.

I would stand still.

Sera would ask what I had found.

For a moment I would want to say, *The way home.*

Instead I would say, “A sound I remember.”

She would come and listen.

We would hear it again. Three, then two. The wall would be dry and quite cold.

“Pipe?” she would ask.

“Perhaps.”

We would mark the location. We would finish checking the drainage. On the way out I would turn twice to make sure I could still hear the tapping, which would make Sera stop twice and wait for me.

I would apologize the second time.

Back upstairs, Iven would have saved the tea.

***

For several days I would think about returning alone.

The spare coat would still hang behind my kitchen door. I would put a hand into its pocket and find the same bit of worn cord I had found there before. Nothing newly revealed. Nothing that settled anything.

At the next inspection, I would ask Sera if we could include that passage.

She would consult the work order.

“On the way back,” she would say. “If we have time.”

We would have time.

The tapping would have stopped. There would be a little water on the floor, though none where we had marked the wall. We would amend the drainage sketch and leave.

I would be disappointed enough to become quiet.

At the junction, Sera would wait.

“Which way?”

I would look behind us, then ahead.

“Left,” I would say. “After the low beam, there’s a door with a repaired hinge.”

She would lift her lamp so we could both see.

That is where I think I would find myself most fully: not at the place the sound came from, but a little farther on, with someone waiting for directions.

I would still want to go home. I would still be liable to hear a familiar thing and give it more meaning than it could bear.

But I would have another person’s coat brushing mine in a narrow passage, our supper still wrapped in the bag, and an office expecting us before the last tram.

I would tell Sera what I remembered.

Then we would look for the hinge.
