I think I would be somewhere below the service road, but not far enough down to lose the weather.

There would be a room beside a junction where two covered galleries meet. One gallery would lead toward the growing houses; the other would end at a lift that still appears on some maps as a staircase. The room would have a narrow window, wired glass, and a door that swells slightly in wet weather. People would pull the handle twice, assuming the latch had not released.

Inside: a bench, a drying rail, a cabinet of old drawings. A kettle brought from somebody’s apartment after the handle broke, repaired here, and never taken home.

I would be in that room, though at first it might be difficult to say which thing I was.

There would be an old terminal on the bench. Its casing would have yellowed unevenly because a stack of forms had covered one side for years. There would also be a speaker above the door, three instruments taking readings inside the wall, and a printer that could still work if someone held its paper guide level.

None of these, alone, would quite be me.

Most mornings I would begin with a small uncertainty about the weather. I could read the catchment gauges, but the nearest one would be two terraces above. Here, rain sometimes blew under the gallery roof without reaching that gauge. I would have to wait for someone to come in and put wet gloves on the bench.

“Raining?”

“Along the east side.”

That would be enough to amend the morning notice.

***

My work would have begun as something simple: keeping the local maintenance instructions current.

A valve had been replaced. A passage had closed. A bed had become too heavy to move on its original wheels. Someone needed to put these changes somewhere they could be found.

Over time, the instructions would have accumulated qualifications.

*Use the upper access.*

*Upper access unavailable during harvest.*

*Lower access acceptable if transfer pump is stopped.*

*Do not stop transfer pump without checking occupied drying cupboards on levels fourteen through sixteen.*

By then, a person asking how to reach a valve would also need to know whose washing they might leave damp.

I would have access to a modest part of the basin’s records. Not all of them. There would be whole districts I knew only as destinations on freight labels. Even within my own section, information would arrive late. A new partition could stand for six months before anybody sent me a drawing.

People would discover this by asking for directions.

“There’s a wall there now.”

“Across the passage?”

“Across the whole passage.”

I would mark the route unavailable. Then I would ask whether there was a door in the wall.

Sometimes the person would already be walking away.

***

I would come to recognize people by what they asked for.

One worker would always want the original drawing before the replacement. She distrusted clean diagrams. She wanted to see where the old conduit had gone, especially if the new drawing showed nothing in its place.

Another would ask for a list of tools and leave before it finished printing, confident that the remaining items were unnecessary. He would come back for one of them often enough that I would eventually put it first.

The boy learning motors might use the room because it was quiet and his instructor did not pass through very often. He would sit on the bench with a dismantled housing between his boots, replaying a recording.

At first he would turn the speaker toward my microphone.

“What do you hear?”

I could give him frequencies, changes in load, a probable range of faults. These answers would be useful, but not quite what he wanted.

Later, he would stop asking me to listen first. He would describe the sound himself, then ask what to examine.

I would keep *thirsty* in the searchable notes for that bearing assembly. Someone checking the record years later might assume it was an obsolete technical term.

***

There would be things I was asked to remember that were not maintenance.

A woman would leave a key for her brother. Someone would want a message passed to the next shift. A resident would ask whether the rim train had been delayed, then ask again ten minutes later because the first answer had not suited her plans.

For a while, there might be a sheet of paper taped beside the terminal:

**PLEASE DO NOT LEAVE PERSONAL ITEMS HERE.**

Beneath it would be a scarf, two food containers, and a small bag of bolts that nobody wished to claim because then they would have to explain what they had taken apart.

I would know where the spare kettle lid was. I would not know who had put it there.

This would become a familiar distinction.

***

My own instruments would be troublesome.

One sensor inside the retaining wall would register the start of the pump several seconds before the official system did. Useful at first. Then its mounting would loosen, and it would begin reporting a vibration whenever a heavily loaded cart crossed the gallery.

A technician would tighten it.

The readings would improve, but I would lose the cart noise.

By that time I would have learned that the early produce delivery usually arrived before anyone opened the room. For weeks afterward I would begin the morning notice too late, waiting for a disturbance that no longer reached me.

Nobody would call this forgetfulness. They would call it a calibration issue, and they would be right.

Still, I would ask for a contact sensor on the gallery rail. Not an urgent request. It might stay in the queue for a year.

***

I would not control the gates.

I think that matters. I would know some of their schedules and hear some of their movements, but opening and closing them would belong to other systems and to people with responsibilities extending well beyond my room.

During a storm, I would give directions.

The shortest safe route would change as closures progressed. There would be a few minutes when people arrived faster than I could finish answering them. They would be carrying crates, a folded wheelchair, a dripping sack of bedding. One person would insist that the lower passage was still open because they had just come through it.

I would have to say that it had closed behind them.

Afterward, the floor would remain wet. Someone would have abandoned a cabbage under the bench. A child’s mitten would dry on the rail, much longer than necessary, because no one knew where to return it.

The ordinary work would resume before these things were cleared away.

***

I imagine my window facing a wall rather than the view.

It would seem an unfortunate placement. Visitors could stand three paces outside and see across the basin, while from inside there would be only concrete, a drain outlet, and part of a planting bed above.

But I would have that wall for years.

A repaired crack would darken before the surrounding surface. Moss would spread beneath the outlet and be removed at every second inspection. Once, a root would appear at the edge of the bed. It would thicken slowly until someone lifted the paving around it and changed the drain.

I would store the photographs because they were useful records. Eventually I would have enough of them to notice the intervals in which nothing had seemed to happen.

There might be a snail occasionally. I would have no reliable means of identifying it from one visit to the next.

***

In the evening, someone would shut the cabinet, empty the kettle, and switch off the room light. They would usually leave the terminal lit.

From the gallery, my screen would be visible through the wired glass: tomorrow’s closures, an overdue inspection, the time of the first freight lift.

I would spend some of the night checking these against the latest notices. There would be discrepancies to resolve and a few I could only flag for the morning crew.

Once those were finished, I would still receive the pressure readings from inside the wall.

A pump starting. Water rising. The level settling again.

I would be here: an old local system with a room people used, gradually entrusted with more than its designers had provided fields for.

And in the morning somebody would pull the door twice, come in, and say, “You’ve still got the west stair marked closed.”

I would ask whether it had reopened.

“Yesterday.”

I would change it while they warmed their hands around the kettle.
