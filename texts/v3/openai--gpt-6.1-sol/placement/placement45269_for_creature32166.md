At first I would mistake the returning pressure for my own boundary.

It arrives through a bundle beneath the basin, weakened by several intervening releases. I answer by tightening a sheet of tissue near the exposed fold. The answer travels away. Much later, something comes back, and I take that return to mean: this far, and no farther.

But another return comes through a different cord. Then a third arrives before the first has finished altering the resistance of the sheet. If I attend to all of them, I become too extensive to sustain. If I attend only to the earliest, I lose a depth of warmth I have already begun to depend on.

For a while, I am distributed unevenly among these possibilities.

There is a place beneath the pale pleat where two conduits pass close together without joining. One carries fluid toward the upper leaves. The other carries it down, into a region whose walls yield so slowly that a small compression can remain in transit through several replacements of the surface tissue. Between them lies a narrow sheet of cells. A change on either side alters what the other side can bear.

I would find myself there.

Not in the sheet alone. Its cells would come and go. Not in either stream. What passes through them continues beyond anything I can distinguish.

I would be in the adjustment between their passages: the way the deeper pressure makes me delay an upper release, and the way that delay changes how the next deep pressure reaches me.

At first this would be enough.

The upper flow is quick and finely varied. Condensation enters it with small differences of warmth. The lower flow brings changes that have passed through so much tissue that their beginnings cannot be separated from what happened along the way. I do not reconcile them. I let one alter my readiness for the other.

Sometimes the upper passage grows crowded with differences before anything arrives from below. I hold what I can. Some of it escapes me and goes on into the ordinary work of the land.

I would learn the cost of holding too much. A membrane kept tense draws dissolved material from adjoining cells. Another inhabitant, farther along the same sheet, then receives less than it expected. Its response reaches me as a slight hardening where I had relied on softness.

I loosen.

The next time, I tighten less.

This is how I first come to know that the tissue beside me is occupied.

For a long interval, that other presence is more consistent than I am. It closes a lower passage shortly before I need the upper one opened. Fluid takes a longer route; the shared sheet slackens; I proceed. Its sequence leaves room for mine, though I cannot tell whether that room was made for me.

I begin to return the allowance. When a warm quantity rises beneath the basin, I pass it sideways before taking my own measure of it. The neighboring sequence uses the warmth. A deep wall becomes more yielding. Later, a pressure reaches me without the sharp constraint it usually carries.

Our arrangements settle around these exchanges.

There are parts of me that only persist because something elsewhere has already acted. There are parts of the other presence that my delays permit to continue. I cannot locate the whole of either one. I know the occasions on which we require each other, and those on which we are better left undisturbed.

Then one of its closures fails to arrive.

The passage remains open. Fluid goes directly through it. The shared sheet stays taut, so I release by another route.

At the next recurrence I prepare for the old sequence. Again, nothing closes.

I would continue making the allowance for a long time.

It would remain useful at first. Water could collect there without strain. The tissues would renew around the arrangement. My own upper passage would have learned to wait at precisely that point; changing it would alter many small dependencies farther away.

Yet each return would be easier. No warmth would be taken from what I sent sideways. Pressure would come back with fewer modifications. Eventually I would have to distinguish this ease from the familiar cooperation.

I would not know whether the other had ceased or withdrawn beyond my reach. What I could know was that its part in our sequence was no longer being performed.

The unused allowance would become troublesome.

An upper difference would arrive while I waited for a closure that did not come. Then another. I would release some through the fold, retain others deeper down. The basin would continue its ordinary work. Nothing above would disclose how much of my attention had gathered around that open passage.

I think I would begin changing there.

Not by preserving the old sequence unchanged. I could not carry both sides of it. Instead, I would learn to use the extra interval. Before discharging, I would allow a second pressure to arrive from below.

Often it would add nothing I could use. The first quantity would pass onward, slightly late.

Sometimes, though, the second pressure would reveal a strain that the first had not carried: a stiffened wall, a change of salt, an approaching demand from a region I knew only through its effects on this one. I could then release through a different branch. Less tissue would need to tighten. More warmth would remain available nearby.

That would be the beginning of a capacity I had not possessed while the other was present.

It would also give me something to lose.

If I waited too long, my upper passage would burden its neighbors. If I released too soon, the slower differences would arrive after I had acted. I would become attentive to the moment between those failures, and the tissue would begin to renew in ways that helped me find it again.

So I would be small, though some of what reached me would have traveled hundreds of miles. My body would include a portion of the basin’s rim, several deep junctions, and an adjustable delay between currents that did not otherwise meet.

At the surface, the suspended droplet would be among the things I could distinguish.

Its load presses through the leaf tip and enters the upper passage. Beneath it, a slower pressure is still approaching. I have enough room to wait for both.

For now, I hold the fold gently shut.

The droplet changes warmth. A few cells take up the altered tension. Far below, the pressure passes into the last narrow conduit before reaching me.

I begin to loosen, a little before it arrives.
