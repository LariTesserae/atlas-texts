I would be farther back in the fracture, behind the animal waiting for the drop. From the floor, nothing of me would show except two feet and a pale edge that could be mistaken for a split crystal.

I would be one of the older bodies pressed against its shields.

Not the oldest. That one occupies the deepest pocket, where the warmth enters through the underside of the stone. It has lost the hooks from three feet and seldom changes position. When water comes, we feel it swell against us. When it dries, a space opens around it, and one of us moves closer.

My cups would have worn through unevenly. One still holds a little water if I tilt it inward. The other has a slit along the crease where it folds beside my mouth. A drop placed there separates into two beads, one available to drink, one traveling down the outside of my leg. I still raise both cups when moisture touches my face.

Sometimes the intact one catches something.

There would be a place I used to occupy farther up the wall. I could reach it now, but not return safely. The last part of the descent requires hanging by the front limbs while the holding feet search below. A younger animal can do this with its cups closed. Mine no longer close firmly enough.

I would not remember the place as a view. I would remember the sequence of surfaces: rough under the left feet, smooth beneath the body, a cold seam that had to be crossed without stopping. At the end was a shallow depression with a grain of white mineral lodged in it. My mouth fitted beside that grain. Water arrived there.

Here, water comes through another body.

When the catcher backs toward us, I feel its shields before I taste the moisture. Their edges press against the fine hairs around my mouth. There is a brief scrape as it settles. Then the channels beneath the shields fill, and I move my head a little to find the opening I have used before.

We do not take equal amounts. The oldest animal cannot reach the upper channels. Another has a mouthpart that trembles and spills water into the stone. I can move between them, but sometimes arrive too late. The catcher has already emptied, and its shields lie flat.

I stay against it a while longer. The rock has warmed where its abdomen rested.

I would still be able to carry a flake.

There is one beside our entrance, almost too thin to see edge-on, with a blunt corner small enough to grip. It does not belong to me. Several animals have moved it. Its underside bears the pale marks of different mouths.

When the drip begins to wander, I would come out and push it.

The movement would take much of a sheltered afternoon. First I would brace my rear feet in a seam. Then close my mouth around the corner. Then pull until something gives: the flake, my grip, or a foot beneath me. After each attempt I would stop with my body flat to the wall.

The catcher would wait above, cups raised beneath the old position.

Eventually one drop would strike the flake, cling to its lower edge, and fall within reach. The catcher’s shields would lift. I would remain outside for that filling, my mouth still touching the stone I had moved.

A grazer would pass across my back. Its blades would click once against a shield. I would flatten until it had gone.

I think there would be a small patch of leathery growth beneath me—not enough to support us, but enough to keep my mouthparts working. I would scrape it when the fracture was dry. The oldest animal would eat the loosened pieces that reached its feet. Sometimes it would raise its head before anything fell, and I would continue scraping until there was something for it to find.

Around our bodies, the hollow would remain mostly out of reach.

I would know the winged creatures by the sudden lightness of their landings. The travelers would arrive through the stone as separate, firm contacts. Their two taps, pause, and third tap would reach me plainly, though I would seldom make room: our fracture narrows too quickly for them.

The great animal at the deepest end would be beyond several difficult crossings. Once, while my cups were still whole, I might have gone close enough to feel warmth beneath its face. What remained of that journey would be a bodily reluctance at one particular angle of descent. My feet would search for another route before my eyes found the crack.

The mountain’s knock would reach us through the stone behind the oldest animal.

Its body would contract first. Then the catcher would lower its cups. I would draw my front limbs beneath me, placing the torn one against my mouth, and grip with all six holding feet.

For a while there would be no movement to follow.

Then something would shift outside.

Not a fall. A small dragging pressure, followed by a brittle sound. Dust would enter our fracture and settle between our shields. The catcher would remain still longer than usual. When it finally backed toward us, its body would approach from the wrong angle. One of its feet would search where a ledge had been.

I would touch the foot with my intact cup.

It would withdraw, try lower, and find purchase.

After it left again, I would come forward. The flake beside the entrance would have turned over. Its familiar corner would be underneath; the new edge would be sharp enough to cut the cup I still had.

I would test it with my mouth, let go, and move around to the other side.

Beyond the flake, on a freshly exposed surface, moisture would be gathering. I would put my torn cup against it. Water would enter the slit and spread along the crease, too little to lift, enough to wet my mouth when I folded the limb inward.

I would remain there until the surface dried.

Then I would go back into the fracture, touching each foothold before giving it my weight. At the entrance I would turn sideways to pass the catcher. The older bodies would have settled closer together in my absence. I would press between them slowly, until a place opened for my feet.

Above us, the catcher would raise its cups again.

I would rest my mouth against the edge of its lowest shield and wait.
