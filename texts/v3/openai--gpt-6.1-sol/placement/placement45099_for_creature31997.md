I would find myself below the broad opening beside the stair, where the strand crosses from one court into the next.

Not at its tip. A little behind it, beneath the brown covering that makes the root difficult to distinguish from the floor.

There is a hollow stem there, left by an earlier growth. One end is caught beneath the broken tile; the other disappears into sediment. The new root has entered it and followed its interior for some distance before emerging through a split. Water still passes through the space between the living root and the old sheath. Very little water. Enough to bring dissolved things, and sometimes a grain too large to pass farther.

At first I think I would be that sheath. Something whose shape remains useful after most of its substance has gone. But its inside is not empty, and I would be there: against the softened inner wall, in the narrow wet interval the root has not occupied.

I would have no leaves.

My growth would be pale and thin enough that, if the stem were opened, I might be mistaken for part of its decay. Fine threads would run along the old fibers. Some would enter them. Others would cross to a fallen leaf pressed against the outside of the sheath, following the damp contact through its split.

The leaf would give me a second place to live.

For a while those two places would be connected by almost nothing. A few threads crossing a gap smaller than a grain of silt. If the leaf shifted, they would break. I would continue on both sides, without any means of establishing which side had kept me.

Perhaps that is where I would begin to become difficult to count.

I would not occupy the whole basin. The colonnade would be beyond me. Even the six shoots beyond the doorway would be distant, though one of their roots passed through the middle of my growth. What reached me from them would arrive as a slight alteration in the water beside that root: something released, something taken up, a change sustained long enough to affect what I could do.

I would grow unevenly.

Where the old sheath was still firm, I would remain sparse. Along a softened seam I would thicken. At a place where mortar dust had accumulated, my threads might stop altogether, while farther along, beneath the tile, they continued through the remains of another leaf.

There would be no moment when I saw this arrangement.

It would be my shape nevertheless.

The tile above me would have a rounded lip and a shallow groove on its underside, made when the clay was wet. Someone’s finger had drawn it, perhaps while lifting the piece, perhaps to fit it against another. Mud would fill most of the groove. In one sheltered part, I would spread through a strip of old plant matter held against the clay.

That small irregularity would keep a portion of me from being washed away.

Farther out, a current would occasionally lift the fallen leaf. It would rise only at its edge, admitting colder water underneath. Some of my threads would loosen. A little of what I had occupied would leave with the water.

I would remain in the part pressed flat.

Over time, the leaf would become less substantial. Its veins would persist after the softer tissue between them had failed. My growth would follow those veins, making branches whose arrangement I had not devised. Then one vein would separate from the stalk, and the water would turn it sideways.

A portion of me would go with it.

It might settle in the next paving joint. It might come to rest against clean stone where there was too little to sustain it. There would be no journey for the rest of me to remember. Under the tile, growth would continue.

And the living root would continue through the sleeve.

Its passage would gradually widen the split. The old material would soften around it. In places, my threads would disappear as the substances sustaining them were exhausted; elsewhere, they would spread into newly exposed fibers. What I released would enter the same small volume of water from which the root took what it could use.

Somewhere beyond the doorway, the firm bud would add a little length.

I would not be in that bud. I would not direct it toward the deposit beyond the column. Most of the court would remain empty, and much of what happened there would have no bearing on my life beneath the tile.

But a little of the fallen leaf might pass through me and become available beside the root.

I would live where that became possible: inside an old stem, under a piece of worked clay, close to the floor. For a long interval there would be enough. Then less. Then, against the opening of the sheath, another leaf would settle.
