I would be in a room beside the returning track, not quite part of the workshop.

The room would have two entrances. One would open onto the rail, so that a cart could stop with its load level with the receiving bench. The other would open onto a passage between dwellings. This door would be narrower. Its curtain would have one pale plate among the brown ones, fitted there because it was the right size.

At first I would think my work was sorting.

The parts coming back from the city would be laid before me: a bearing, a hinge, a section of dark conduit with a polished place on its underside. I would turn each piece beneath a low lamp. Some could go directly to the separators. Some would need their several materials parted. Others would still be usable somewhere that required less of them.

There would be trays for these distinctions. Their rims would have worn to different heights.

But there would also be a shallow space at the back of the bench where I put things I had not finished considering.

That space would grow crowded.

A hinge might have worn unevenly because the door it carried had settled. Melted down, it would make a perfectly good new hinge. Yet the new one, made to the recorded dimensions, would bind against the same door.

A gauge might return without damage. Its owner had ceased to be able to hold it.

I would begin going out.

***

My body would be suited to the bench, though less well than whoever fitted it had expected.

One forearm would be broad and steady enough to rest a component on. The other hand would have finer fingers. Between two of them, a narrow separation would run farther into the palm than looked comfortable. This would let me hold a thin piece without closing my whole hand.

It would also let dust accumulate somewhere difficult to clean.

I would have a small brush for that. I would dislike lending it.

Walking would not initially be part of my competence. I would stop too often to compare the posted distances with the distance I had felt beneath my feet. Once, carrying a replacement contact to a dwelling, I would mistake two identical openings and enter the wrong one.

The inhabitant inside would look at the contact, then at my hands.

After a while, they would move a dish toward me.

I would remain until it no longer seemed necessary to explain the mistake. Before leaving, I would learn which opening I wanted: the one whose left jamb held warmth a little higher than the right.

After that, I would find it easily.

***

There would be things I could make that were not quite replacement parts.

A broader grip for a tool whose owner could still use it, provided the first two fingers no longer had to meet.

A rest that held a bowl at an angle.

A small rise in the paving at a junction, low enough not to obstruct a cart, high enough to be felt through the sole of a foot.

These would have to be made carefully. An adaptation that helped one inhabitant might trouble another. A raised grip could change the balance of a cutter. A useful mark beneath the foot could become a place where someone else hesitated.

I would keep returning to see.

Sometimes I would find my work removed.

The bowl-rest would be sitting on a shelf. The inhabitant would have put a familiar broken tile beneath the bowl instead.

I would examine the tile. I would want to improve it.

For several visits, I would have to keep my finer hand still.

***

The watcher at the freight signal would bring me a receiving tool.

Its jaw would be sound. Its handle would be worn into a shape that no longer quite met the watcher’s hand.

I would propose a new handle.

The watcher would leave the tool on my bench but would not leave the room.

We would stand on opposite sides of it. A cart would pass outside, carrying something neither of us had ordered.

Eventually, I would ask the watcher to hold the tool.

The hand would close. At one place, just beneath the broadest finger, there would be a gap.

I would add material there. Very little. The rest I would leave untouched.

When the watcher returned to collect it, I would have placed the tool in the completed tray. It would lift it, try the grip, and keep holding it through the interval in which I expected it to set the tool down.

Then it would go.

Much later, I would find that the pale plate in my doorway had been adjusted. It had always struck the stone when the curtain was raised. Now it cleared.

***

I would not remember becoming the person who was sent for when an object was sound but its use had become difficult.

There would be no change in the room’s designation. The distance posts would still name it as a receiving annex.

Ordinary damaged parts would continue to arrive. I would separate their materials and fill out the inspection marks. Some cycles would pass without a visitor.

Then someone would come through the narrow door with nothing in their hands.

They would sit beside the bench.

I would wait for them to explain which thing needed attention.

Perhaps they would say that the route to the rolling house had acquired too many turns.

I would know the route had not changed. I would also know that this was not useful information.

We would walk it together.

At the first junction, they would turn correctly. At the next, they would stop. I would stop too, without taking their arm.

There would be a foundation nearby where the pressure of the central engines could be felt particularly well. We would stand there for a while. Then I would take the left passage, slowly enough that walking beside me required no decision yet.

I might begin using that route myself.

***

My room would be behind the annex. A heated wall, a table, a dish with its contents pressed into several ridges. I would prefer the ridges because I could take a portion without looking.

On a shelf would be things that had stayed in the shallow space at the back of the bench until I brought them home.

A latch no one needed.

A hand-piece I had made too smoothly. Its intended owner had returned it because there was nowhere on it to know where the thumb belonged.

A strip of ceramic from the bowl-rest.

I would not arrange them by material.

Under the table, a plated creature would sometimes lower itself against the floor. Its presence would be uncertain. I might go many cycles without seeing it, then find a faint mark beneath the place where I kept one foot.

I would move that foot a little to the side.

***

Eventually my finer hand would begin to close badly.

Not by much. A thin contact would slip farther between the fingers than I intended. I would catch it with the broad hand and remain still afterward, feeling the warmth gather at the wrist.

At first I would alter my tools.

Then I would alter the way I laid them out.

Someone bringing a hinge would notice that my small brush was no longer on its usual shelf. I had put it near the bench, where I could reach it without turning the wrist.

On the next visit, they would set the hinge down with its worn side toward me.

I would notice.

I would not yet thank them.

There would still be work I could do. I could hold a new grip while its owner tried it. I could feel where the pressure was wrong. I could accompany someone past a junction and return by the longer route.

And there would be the space at the back of the bench.

I would keep it clear enough for one more object, though clearing it would take time. Things would have to be carried into my room, or passed to someone who might use them, or finally placed in a returning cart.

The cart would wait outside with its wheels resting in the dust.

I would put a hand on each piece before letting it go.
