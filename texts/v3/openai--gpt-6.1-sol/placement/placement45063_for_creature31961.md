At first I would take the touch beneath the stone to be mine.

I would be sitting in the forest’s shadow with my hand between the narrow animal’s forefeet. My smallest finger would have gone cold. The contact would be so slight that I could mistake it for the return of feeling: a point of warmth, then a little pressure along the nail.

I would lift my hand.

The pressure would remain where the finger had been.

That would be the first thing I could not put down to the height, or the air, or the long walk.

I think I would return to that place. Not directly. I would find it while trying to reach the seven trees, and again while following the woman whose braid came apart among the animals. Once I would come upon it from beneath a ledge I had believed was on the other side of the plateau. The narrow creature would be there, its feet extended, the space between them undisturbed.

I would sit without putting my hand in it.

Something would make a minute scraping under the stone.

There would be enough time between the scrapes to examine the little plants beneath the creature’s belly. One leaf would have a broken scale. Under the break, its flesh would be the pale green of a bead held against the sky. I would notice that the animal had arranged one hind foot so its shadow covered the injury.

Eventually I would put my hand down again.

This time the touch would find my finger before it reached the stone.

*

There is a low place beneath that shelf. I would discover it only after I had spent some time above it.

The entrance would be beside an animal I had mistaken for a rounded stone with three fissures in it. At close range, the fissures would prove to be closed eyes. There would be no mouth that I could find. Behind it, a gap would admit my arm and then, with no visible widening, my shoulder.

I would hesitate over my head. The gap would seem too narrow.

Someone inside would breathe out.

I would find that I was already kneeling.

The room would have no depth I could estimate. Its roof would be close enough to brush my hair, but farther in, pale limbs would rise without touching it. There would be shallow hollows in the floor and dark lengths of braided fiber laid between them. Most would be empty. One would contain the tip of a horn. Another would hold the side of a face, resting as if against a pillow.

The face would be mine, though I would not recognize it immediately.

I have never found it easy to recognize a face seen from that angle: one cheek flattened, the mouth a little open, the visible eye looking elsewhere. I would first notice a familiar scar near the chin. Then I would touch my chin and find the scar under my finger.

The face in the hollow would not move.

Beside it would lie a small animal with a long, shallow body. Its back would be gray and finely lined. Four feet would rest beneath it; a fifth would be raised toward the roof. The raised foot would end in something softer than a claw, broader than a toe.

It would be touching the underside of the stone.

I would sit beside it for a long time.

*

I would not become that animal all at once.

For a while I would still have a shelter, or the use of one. I would know the chipped bowl. I would learn where to put my cheek against the wooden post without disturbing the little creature asleep below it.

The woman with the repaired sleeve would give me a length of fiber. There would be no ceremony. She would place it across my knees while I sat near the wall, then go on with her work. When I picked it up, one end would be damp and warm.

I would carry it without knowing what it was for.

My feet would begin to hurt less. Looking at the soles, I would find the fine branching lines described in the stone. Powder would remain in them even after I rubbed them with the heel of my hand. One line would cross the arch and continue up the side of the foot. I would be able to feel it there, not as a cut but as a place where the skin folded more readily.

I would spend less time standing.

There would be practical reasons. Under the larger animals it is easier to see from low down. The smallest inhabitants are lost to an upright person. And the place beneath the shelf would be difficult to leave without lingering by each hollow.

Once, on my way out, I would find a curled body caught in the braid I had brought. It would have six legs, all held against its underside. I would loosen the fibers one at a time. When it was free, it would remain on my palm.

The woman would find me there.

She would look at the animal, then at the loose strands hanging from my wrist. With two fingers she would lay one strand along its back.

I would understand that much.

*

I think my place would be beneath the stone, but close to an opening. There would be ordinary light within reach. I would still want to see the difference between red fiber and brown.

My body would become low enough to rest in the shallow room. The change would not feel like shrinking. There would simply be fewer parts of me exposed to the cold. My knees would find their hollows. My shoulder blades would settle against a slight rise behind them. The seams of my clothing would loosen, and small animals would occupy the loosened places.

I would keep the sleeve with a tear near the cuff. Sometimes the woman would reach into the opening and repair it. Sometimes she would leave a strand unfinished, and I would hold it until she returned.

The face in the hollow would remain recognizable longer than the rest of me.

A child might kneel at the entrance to look at it. I would hear the pebble tapping lightly against the child’s teeth. After a while they would set the pebble down beside my face, turn it so the hole faced inward, and wait.

I would be able to hear through it.

Not words, at first. The dry click beside the path would be quite clear. So would the small exhalation from the corrugated throat, although that animal stood beyond the basin.

Then there would be the sound of someone drawing a hand away from stone.

That sound would be difficult to bear. I would raise one of my feet toward the roof.

*

I would have work, though much of it would consist of remaining in the right place.

Above me, between the narrow creature’s forefeet, people would sometimes rest a hand. I would touch one finger from below. Not every hand, and not always the same finger. Some would have a small animal tucked into the curl of the palm already. Some would be too tightly closed.

If a hand withdrew, I would wait beneath the shape it left.

Occasionally the shape would begin to slip. The little finger would lengthen; the thumb would turn toward the wrong edge. I would put my raised foot against it until it held again. There would be no force involved. The difficulty would be knowing when to stop.

The cloudy-eyed person would come to the opening and inspect my foot. They would hold it between two warm fingers, much as they had once held my wrist.

I would still have a pulse somewhere. They would wait for it.

Afterward, they would place my foot back against the roof, a little to the left.

*

From outside, I might look like something jointed in a seam of the plateau. A traveler would notice a pale edge, a few folds, perhaps an eye. If they knelt, I would lower myself slightly to make room for them to see.

I would have no reason to hide.

There would still be places I liked: the forest’s shade above me, the worn post, the basin with the branching channel. I could reach them, though anyone watching might think I had remained beneath the shelf.

And I would remember the walk up. The coolness in the joints of my fingers. The distant mountain systems losing their height as I gained mine.

Sometimes a hand resting above me would be warm from that walk.

I would touch it carefully.

The person would lift their hand and turn it over, looking for what had touched them. Through the thin part of my raised foot I would see the underside of their palm, the grain of stone between us, and a small clear piece of sky.

I would stay there after they left.

Not because I could not move.

Their hand might return.
