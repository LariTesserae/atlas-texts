I would find myself on the other side of the wall from the sleeping shelf.

Not in the room. Behind it, where the platform does not quite meet the foundation, there is a passage that narrows around an old bolt. Dust has gathered against the bolt’s square head. Beyond it, the passage widens enough for two bodies to lie alongside one another, though only if their ears are folded.

The woman sometimes hears something there while she is falling asleep. She presses her heel against the wall, and the movement reaches us through the stone.

We become still.

After a while, we go on with what we were doing.

There is more space beneath the pump house than she knows. Some of it is unusable: hot pockets, holes filled with sharp rust, a hollow in which the air always smells wrong. But a strip along the shaded wall remains cool enough through most of the afternoon. The fill beneath it contains pieces of white stone that can be loosened with the forefeet. Where they are too large, we work around them.

I would be one of the smaller seam-runners, narrow enough to get behind the spare-parts locker but not quite narrow enough to turn around there.

This has caused trouble twice.

The first time, I backed out with a length of cotton caught between my teeth. The second, the woman opened the locker while I was still inside. Light entered from a direction in which there had never been light before. I dropped what I was carrying and went beneath her hand.

For several nights afterward, I approached the locker by a different route.

I have a dark line along my back. It is visible when the dust is washed out of my fur, which seldom happens. One hind foot has a pale outer toe. I do not know these things about myself. What I know is the width of the opening beside the drain, the places where my whiskers touch before my shoulders do, and how long I can remain on the bare platform before my feet begin to hurt.

My first drinking place was beneath a broken lip of stone.

There were three stops on the way there. At the first, we waited under a slab while the adult ahead of us put her nose into the open. At the second, we crossed a strip of gravel one at a time. At the third, there was no water, but we pressed our mouths against the seam anyway.

Then we reached the broken lip.

I remember the underside of the ledge better than its shape. The drops gathered in separate places. One hung above a pale bump; another formed farther back, where only the smallest of us could reach. We jostled there until an adult pushed between us. After that, I waited at the pale bump.

I still go there occasionally.

The journey is longer now because one of the old crossings has become too hot. There is also water nearer the house. But on some nights I pass the slab and the gravel and put my nose to the dry seam. Then I go on to the ledge.

Sometimes it gives me nothing.

I wait long enough for my forefeet to grow uncomfortable, then leave.

***

I would spend much of my life carrying things that do not look worth carrying.

A seed with its outer coat already split. A strand pulled from the edge of a cleaning cloth. The pale underside of a beetle. A sharp flake of stone that has worked loose in the passage where we sleep.

The flake is awkward. I have to hold it at one corner, and it knocks against the walls as I drag it out. At the entrance I pause, because the space beyond is bright and because the stone prevents me from closing my mouth properly.

Then I carry it a little farther.

There are already many flakes there. The woman’s broom scatters some of them each morning. Others remain beyond the reach of its bristles.

Of the six animals in our passage, I would be the one that keeps opening a small entrance behind the trough.

The others seldom use it. A root crosses the opening and catches loose fur. For most of the day, the ground outside is exposed to the sun. But in the late evening there is a narrow shadow between the trough and the condenser housing, and I can reach the drain without passing the place where the snake has been.

The woman finds the entrance when she waters the shrubs.

She presses grit into it with two fingers.

I open it again after dark.

This continues for some time. Eventually she puts a flat stone over the hole. I dig around its lower edge. The new opening is less convenient, but shaded earlier.

***

When the path changes, I would not be the first to notice.

The old female stops at the bend. I come up behind her, close enough that one of her ears brushes my muzzle. She steps onto the cleared ground, lifts a foot, and withdraws.

I wait for her to move.

Instead, she turns into the rough stones.

I follow badly. There is a gap she crosses that I cannot cross without putting my belly against a warm edge. I go around a larger stone and lose sight of her. For a moment there is only open ground and the smell of dust rising from somewhere nearby.

Then I find the others’ tracks.

That evening, the usual place behind the condenser is dry. We search along the base of the fins. I climb onto the lower brace, where the metal is cooler, and sniff at a crust left from yesterday.

On the other side of the building, the old female finds the grain water.

By the time I arrive, she has eaten the largest pieces. I take a softened husk from beside her foot and carry it under the platform.

For several nights I still turn toward the old place. Each time I reach it, I search briefly before going around the house.

On the fourth night, I turn earlier.

The new route passes a stone with a split down its middle. My whiskers touch both sides when I go through. Just beyond it is a shallow pocket where the ground stays tolerable even before sunset.

I stop there with the husk between my teeth.

Behind me, another animal waits to pass. I move aside, and it goes through the split. Then I follow it toward the water.
