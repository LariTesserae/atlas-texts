At first I would be carried.

Not far above the bottom, where there might be room to fall, but within the moving thickness just over it. I would touch stone and lose it before the touch became a place. A grain would strike me. Another would turn me over. For a while there would be no useful difference between my body and the small things traveling beside it.

Then one part of me would hold.

The rest would pull in the current. I would be stretched between that small attachment and the water, with no understanding of either. Another part would make contact. There would be less pulling.

That would be the beginning of where I lived.

I would be the animal beneath the shelf, but not quite the animal as it appears from above. From above there is a margin, a covering, a shape that can be mistaken for something discarded. Where I find myself is lower: in the shallow space where the underside meets stone. Here, a roughness too small to show through the mud can give me purchase. A seam can admit water. A slight convexity can leave part of my body unsupported.

I would know the shelf by these differences.

For a long time I would occupy very little of it. Mud would gather against me, and I would feed where it settled. My threads would extend farther than my body. One would reach into a pit; another would lie along the base of a grain too large to move. Their returns would be separate and uneven. There would be food in one groove and only mineral dust in another.

I would grow into the spaces this feeding made possible.

No whole view of the lowland would arrive. The channel would reach me as stronger pressure along one margin, then as an interval of grit striking the exposed folds. The shoulder above me would matter because water came down from it differently. A hollow nearby might collect mud that later spilled across my breathing passages. These would be places in the only sense available to me: differences that required different movements.

Even the stone would not remain one thing.

As I spread, a surface that had been beyond my reach would become something to hold. A depression would become a sheltered opening. A ridge would divide the underside into two areas that could release separately. When I moved, I would lose some of these arrangements and acquire others, very slowly, without carrying an image of what had been left behind.

Perhaps I would stay for years.

The deposit above me would change colors. Pale shell dust would lodge in the mucus over my back, then be loosened and carried away. Darker mud would settle into the shallow folds. I would bear what happened nearby without seeing it: the exposure of a stone, the erosion of a bank, a small richness of dead plankton arriving from water I had never occupied.

Some of it would feed me. Much of it would merely become weight.

When the covering thickened, I would contract beneath it. A patch would slide away. Water would reach a passage that had begun to close. Elsewhere the mud would remain, and I would work there next.

There would be no single moment when the whole body was safe or exposed. One edge might feed while another stayed folded against a pebble. One part might be firmly attached while a neighboring part searched beneath the deposit. The body would live by these unequal conditions. I would not gather them into a judgment of the tide. I would close here, extend there, keep water moving through a narrow space.

Once, a fish would take a piece of me.

The pull would lift several points of attachment before others held. My margin would contract around a place that no longer reached the stone. For a while water would enter there too freely. Sediment would collect in the wound. The feeding grooves nearest it would remain shut.

Later, the edge would grow again, though differently. A thread emerging beside the notch would reach a pit that the old margin had covered. That small change would persist after the injury ceased to require a response.

I would be an old animal without knowing that I was old.

There would be a shelf I had occupied longer than any of the sediment presently resting on it. There would be parts of my body that had thinned and thickened many times. Beneath the rim, young would develop in recesses held clear of the grit. Their small movements would press against me. Then some would be absent.

One might remain nearby, under the same stone. Its threads might eventually reach into sediment mine had already crossed. We would share the shelter without recognizing its extent or one another.

And I think this is where I would find myself most completely: not during the violence of the strongest current, but just after it weakened.

The cover would still be shifting. Fine grains would descend through water no eye could have made useful. Along my rim, pressure would ease unevenly. A closed groove would open. One thread would emerge, adhere to something, and begin its return.

Elsewhere, the shelf would be bare. Elsewhere, the channel would still carry its low veil of mud. I would know neither.

I would have a little stone beneath me, room for water to pass, and this one burden coming slowly inward.
