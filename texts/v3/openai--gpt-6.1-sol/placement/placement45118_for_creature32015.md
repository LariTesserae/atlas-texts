I would live near an interruption, but not at its edge.

My house would be the fourth one along a short street that approaches the shelter and then turns aside. From the kitchen door I could see the last stretch of paving. I could not see someone enter the interruption unless I went out beyond the garden wall. This would matter. There would have been years when I watched too closely.

The house would have two rooms, a drying alcove, and a courtyard shared with people whose names appear on my household cloth. In the courtyard, we would grow a bitter leaf that tolerates poor light. One corner would remain bare because something beneath it shifts whenever the nearest cistern is full. We would keep trying bowls there, then moving them.

I think at first I would be a keeper of records.

Not one of the people responsible for the long rows of rods in the city court. My work would be nearer to use: shelter boards, household strips, accounts of delayed journeys. I would copy a board when its surface became too crowded, carry the old one indoors, and make sure the new board preserved the marks of people who had entered but had not yet come out.

There would be a cupboard for this, built on a foundation tested in wet weather and dry. Its shelves would have shallow lips. Even so, twice a year I would take everything out and check whether loose objects still settled toward the same corner.

I would have a handwriting that other people found easy to read. This would be one of the reasons I was given the work, and it would become a nuisance. People would bring me names to put on bowls and arguments to phrase for the court. A neighbor would ask me to write a message to someone she had quarreled with, then stand over my shoulder rejecting every sentence.

“No. That makes it sound as though I want him to come.”

“Do you?”

“That is not what the message is about.”

I would be useful without being particularly patient.

At the shelter, the first task each morning would be to uncover the water jar and smell it. Then I would check the bench, the roof ties, the paving, and the board. Marks made in damp weather can swell into one another. A count of three might become a count of eight. Direction lines might vanish beneath a traveler’s thumbprint.

I would not correct these things from memory alone. I would leave a small space beside them and write what I remembered, including whether I had actually seen the travelers.

This would make the boards untidy.

Someone would complain that the old keeper had used less room.

The old keeper would still be alive, living in the next house but one, and would agree.

—

Yet keeping records would not be enough to describe what I did.

A person returning through an interruption might need water before questions. They might need help lowering their pack. They might be angry, because the crossing had cost them a day, or because everyone they had hoped to see had died while they walked.

The shelter would not tell me which.

So I would learn to sit without immediately reaching for the board.

I would keep dry mitts in a covered box. A little food, too, though the household would argue about how much. We could not keep a fresh meal waiting for every possible arrival. Thin cakes would do for most people. Broth could be fetched.

There would be a folded cloth beneath the bench, intended for a wet traveler to sit on. The cloth would belong to the shelter, but someone would always take it home to mend, and for days afterward no one would know where it was.

Much of the work would be like that. Not the measurement of lost time, but finding a cloth.

I would come to know the different ways people first looked at the avenue after emerging. Some checked the sky. Some turned to inspect the shelter they had just left, as if they might catch it doing something. Some looked down at their hands.

I would not know what any of these gestures meant until they spoke.

One woman would return carrying a basket of translucent stalks gathered that morning. Her household would have moved away six years earlier. The stalks would still be fresh.

I would bring her into our kitchen because she would not want to put the basket down in the shelter.

We would eat them that evening. This would trouble one of the children.

“If they’re six years old—”

“They aren’t,” the woman would say.

The child would look at me, wanting the person who kept the boards to settle it.

I would ask for another bowl.

Later, the woman would help wash up. She would know where cups ought to be kept in a kitchen like ours, and would be irritated by where we kept them. That irritation would let us talk more easily than our careful questions had.

—

I would have come to this work through a loss, probably, but not one clean enough to explain me.

Someone from my household would have gone through the interruption and failed to return. An older sister, perhaps. She had intended to collect a repaired loom part from the far street. She had taken food for two days and complained about the weight of the part before she had even picked it up.

For a while I would remember her departure in unreasonable detail. Whether her sleeve had been turned back. Whether I had warmed the handle of the tool I passed her. Whether she had said she would return before the next rain.

Then details would begin to differ whenever I told the story.

I would stop telling it so often.

Her household strip would remain in our dry box. There would be later strips with fewer of the same names. Eventually I would be one of the people named to receive her, rather than one of the dependents she had meant to come home to.

I might become the shelter keeper partly to avoid having to ask someone else whether she had arrived.

But after some years, I would learn to leave the shelter.

I would need to help with fuel. I would want to hear music in another house. Sometimes I would take the long ordinary road to a neighboring street, leaving my duties with someone who checked the jar less thoroughly but spoke more kindly to frightened people.

On those journeys I would carry the same loose pebbles as anyone else. I would not have any special understanding of the ground. Once, tired and preoccupied, I would continue too far into smooth country before recognizing what was happening.

Returning would take most of the afternoon.

The following morning I would enter the incident in the local record without mentioning how frightened I had been. When the old keeper read it, she would ask how many pebbles I had used.

“All of them.”

She would sit beside me for a while. Then she would tell me that the pouch I carried was too small.

—

There is another part of the work I would gradually take on.

The old boards would be stored in bundles, separated by thin pieces of fired clay. Most would never need to be consulted. Their marks would concern households whose obligations had been transferred, roads no longer maintained, journeys completed long ago.

But sometimes a person would return with a strip no one recognized.

Then I would sit on the floor with the bundles open around me, looking for the shape of a mark.

The labor would make my neck ache. Dust would settle on my cuffs. Someone would bring a lamp and tell me to stop for the evening.

Occasionally I would find what I needed.

More often I would find something adjacent: the name of a court where the traveler had lived, a note that a receiving household had moved, a mark copied differently by an earlier keeper. Enough to send a message along an avenue. Enough to make the next question less vague.

I would dislike being thanked before anything was settled.

The records would contain errors I had made myself. I would recognize an omission years after copying a board, or discover that I had attached an account to the wrong household because two strips had dried together. There would be no use defending these mistakes. I would amend what I could and go to the people affected.

This, more than solitude or learning, would shape me: having to knock on a door and explain that someone had been waiting in the wrong place because of my work.

—

I think I would remain human.

The cold would hurt my hands. I would have a tooth that bothered me before rain, though not reliably enough to be useful. I would sometimes resent an arrival because it interrupted supper, and then be ashamed while fetching the extra bowl.

I would have people I preferred, people I avoided, and a child in the household who considered my cupboard more interesting than I did. I would show them how to handle the boards without rubbing the marks. They would show me that an old board, held at an angle to the window, carried shallow scratches we had not copied.

For several days we would look at everything that way.

Most of the scratches would turn out to be damage.

One would not.

We would bring that board to the old keeper, and she would remember the person who made it: a man who had pressed hard because his hands were shaking, though she could no longer remember why.

I would add her account on a separate piece, with the date and her name.

Then I would put both away.

By evening there would be ordinary work to finish. The covered jar would need filling. A roof mat would have slipped over one corner of the shelter. I would take a ladder, ask someone to hold it, and spend longer than expected getting the binding right.

From there I could see the short street, our courtyard, the lamp already lit in the kitchen. Beyond the shelter the avenue would lie empty.

I would look along it once before climbing down.

At home, someone would ask whether anybody had come through.

“Not today.”

They would move a drying rack so I could reach my place. Supper would be thickening in the pot, and I would be asked to stir it while the others brought in the bowls.
