I would be in the split bench, but not in either half.

A little way down the gap, where a fragment of shell has caught crosswise and water divides around it. One branch of the flow goes through a space too narrow for a shrimp. The other turns beneath the shell and comes back carrying fine particles. Here, something soft can remain without having to hold very hard.

There would be no light at my place. A fish passing above would arrive as a brief alteration in pressure. The old crab would arrive through the bench itself, well before its claw reached the opening.

At first I think I would be something that simply adheres: a pale thickness on the underside of the fragment, easily mistaken for the cloudy film on the stones. But there is a small movement in that thickness. Something extends into the current and withdraws. It has an opening. It has a length tucked away where the water cannot pull at it.

I would be a worm in a tube.

Not one of the worms that thread freely through the deposit near the outlets. Mine would be a shorter reach. A soft body doubled slightly inside a crooked sleeve, with a small fringe at its exposed end. The fringe would open into the returning water. A grain would touch it. Some grains would pass toward the mouth; others would be brushed away.

The sleeve would begin with things too small to name separately: powder from the clay plates, a broken bristle, pale grit from mortar. I would bind them in a thin secretion. The first walls would scarcely be walls at all. Water would pass through them. A shrimp walking over them would make me contract.

Then more grains would lodge against the first.

My tube would follow the underside of the shell fragment because that was where it had begun. When it reached the fragment’s edge, it would bend. There would be no straight route worth making. Above, the water moved too freely; below, another shell left a protected interval. I would lengthen into that interval.

This would take a long time.

For much of it, nothing about me would be visible except the fringe opening, folding, opening again.

I would have no occasion to know the bench. Its channel, its round depression, the work once done upon it: all of that would be outside my reach. Even the split would be too large a thing for me. There would be the close surface behind my body, the roughness beneath it, the entrance where food came, and a bend I could withdraw beyond.

Warmth would sometimes strengthen in the water. I would extend a little farther.

Sometimes fine sediment would thicken until each movement brought too much against me. I would fold inward and wait. The settled material would cover the lip of the tube. Later I would push through it, leaving a small soft mound around the opening.

The crab would disturb my place.

Its claw would enter from above, blunt and enormous, searching among the fragments. One day it would lift the shell to which my earliest tube was fastened.

For a moment, the pull would run through the whole sleeve.

A few grains would come loose. The shell would turn. The current would reach me from a direction it had never reached me before, and I would retreat to the deepest bend, pressing my body against the lining.

The crab would scrape the other side of the fragment. Then it would let go.

The fragment would settle lower in the gap.

I would survive in a place altered by less than the width of the crab’s smallest leg. My entrance would now face another shell. Food would arrive from the side. Part of the old tube would be crushed shut, and I would begin another opening beside it.

There would be a short fork in my dwelling: one passage occupied, one packed with my discarded lining and the grains that kept coming.

Minerals would gather on both.

The oldest part would become hard enough that I could no longer change it. It would still fit my body, though I would spend less time there. The new entrance would remain soft. A shrimp’s brush could strip away its rim. I would mend that rim with whatever reached me next, and it would come back a slightly different shape.

Other lives would settle against the sleeve. A film would thicken on its sheltered side. Smaller worms would enter the spaces among its grains. Somewhere beyond the bend, a snail would graze a narrow track and pass on.

I would feel the scrape.

I would withdraw.

I would come out again.

Eventually the shell fragment would cease to move when the crab tested it. Crust would join it to the fragment below, and my oldest tube would be enclosed between them. The sleeve that had kept water off my body would now conduct a faint pressure from one piece to the other.

My living end would remain near the gap, where there was still room for the fringe to open.

Far above, a drop would fall.

The disturbance would reach the bench, then the packed shells, then the wall of my tube. I would close before anything touched me.

After a while, the fine parts would unfold.

A little food would turn in the water beneath the shell. I would reach for it from the edge of the growing stone.
