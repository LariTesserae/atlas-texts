At first I would look for a house.

I would be tired from crossing the open stone, and I would have made the mistake of carrying my belongings too high on my back. There would be a place between my shoulders where the straps had rubbed through my coat. I would want a door, a lamp, someone taking a pot off the heat. Something I could recognize before learning how to live there.

But I think my place would turn out to be just short of a house: the covered passage between two chambers, where there is room to sit but not to straighten, and where a warm root travels along the wall.

I would notice it because someone was speaking inside it.

Not speaking to me. Asking for a bowl, perhaps. An ordinary request arriving so distinctly through the wood that I would put my hand against the opening.

Then the voice would stop.

For a while I would remain there with my hand cupped around an empty sound.

***

I would be a person, though not a particularly capable one on arrival.

My knees would learn before the rest of me. I would keep trying to look toward whatever I meant to reach, instead of finding out what held me where I was. Twice I would step onto greenery that seemed no different from the greenery beside it. Twice someone would catch the back of my coat.

The second time, nobody would explain.

They would put a wooden wedge into my hand and make me lift the mat.

Underneath, there would be almost nothing: three taut stems, a little dark water, roots passing across the hollow without entering it. A seam-runner’s passage along one side.

I would lower the plants carefully. Their surface would look trustworthy again.

That would stay with me.

I think I would spend my first season doing work that did not require crossing far: stripping dead ribs from leaves, sorting fiber, carrying warm water from a fissure to the sound keeper’s room. My hands would become nicked and stained. I would learn which scraps could be burned and which had to be put back beneath the fields.

The older woman would not immediately teach me her work.

She would let me watch her pull a cleaning tuft through a root. She would hold the tuft afterward and turn it over with one fingernail. Sometimes she would smell it. Once she would set it aside and crawl out to inspect a roof anchor.

I would follow her and discover that what sounded like an obstruction inside the passage was a root beginning to tear outside.

Another time the same muffled sound would mean only wet pith.

I would have to learn them separately.

***

Eventually she would give me the near end of a cord.

This is where I would begin to belong: seated in a narrow passage, holding something whose other end was out of sight.

My first task would be to tell her when the cord stopped moving.

Later, to distinguish a snag from a turn.

Later still, to pull without stripping the living skin from the inside of the root.

I would discover that there are ways of being careful which look, from outside, exactly like doing nothing. A cord held with two fingers. A pause long enough for wet fiber to soften. A hand left against wood while someone in another room taps three times, then waits, then taps once more.

The woman would have opinions about all of these things.

She would also change her mind. A binding she had trusted for twenty years would fail in a warm season. A piece of wood she had told me to throw away would make an excellent replacement mouth.

We would keep it.

I would like her partly because she would not disguise these reversals.

***

There would be a small chamber beside hers that had once been used for drying berries.

That is where I would sleep.

Its ceiling would be low even by the measure of the houses. One wall would grow warm after sunset; another would remain damp. I would keep my bedding between them and shift it a hand’s width as the season changed.

A sound root would pass beneath the sleeping ledge.

The woman would warn me that it was not a listening place.

I would discover why during the first week: a child rehearsing a message long after everyone had gone to bed, speaking each phrase into the opening and waiting for an answer that was never going to come. Someone coughing. A household arguing over a missing blade, then finding it. The peculiar silence of people who had forgotten the passage was open.

I would make a padded cover for the mouth nearest my bed.

It would not seal tightly. A sealed root grows damp in the wrong way. There would still be a little air passing through, enough to move the loose fibers at its edge.

Sometimes, waking, I would touch those fibers to see whether anyone had removed the cover.

***

I think I would become a maker of replacement pieces.

Not whole sound paths. Those would require patience with living trees that I might never acquire. I would be good at the short sections: the elbows, mouths, sleeves, and fitted lengths that let an old passage continue after one part had failed.

I would collect wood with unusual hollows. I would learn to open a piece without leaving a sharp inner lip to catch moisture. My blades would become small from sharpening.

On a shelf I would keep several joints that had not worked.

One would turn every voice breathy. Another would carry taps beautifully but swallow certain words. A third would make anyone speaking through it sound as though they were suppressing laughter.

Children would ask to use that one.

The woman would allow it until they became unbearable.

There would be other failures I could not find a use for, but I would keep a few because they felt right in the hand. When someone asked why, I would say I had not finished examining them.

This would be mostly true.

***

After a great wind, there would be more practical work than my little joints could help with.

I would go out with the others to stitch the field edges. We would lie with our shoulders toward the wind and feed wet fiber beneath the exposed roots. Someone would keep a knee against my tool basket. My wrists would ache from holding a strip of growth down while another person fastened it.

In one torn place we would find a sound root lifted out of the earth, its far end broken.

It would lead nowhere now.

Nevertheless, when the wind struck its open mouth, a low note would come out of it.

We would work beside that note for most of an afternoon.

I would want to save the root. The older woman would examine it, put her finger through a split I had not seen, and shake her head.

We would cut it into lengths for other repairs.

I would take one length home and leave it under my sleeping ledge until I could bear to work on it. By then it would have dried too far and warped.

Some things I would learn slowly.

***

The seam-runner under the woman’s basket would never become mine.

It would accept my presence sooner than my touch. I would see its nose appear while I was working and feel flattered, until I realized I had left a bowl of softened fruit beside my foot.

When the woman traveled to another basin, it would come out and smell the cuffs of my coat.

It would find the wrong person and withdraw.

I would miss her more after that.

During one of her absences, a child would bring me a root mouth that had come loose from a wall. The child would stand beside my workbench watching, refusing to leave it.

I would fit a new sleeve, warm the binding, and ask who was at the other end.

“My father.”

“Ask him to tap.”

The child would crouch at the mouth.

A little later, three taps would arrive.

I would keep one hand on the joint while the child began describing something that had happened outside: a piece of green thrown into their basin, a shell heap found underneath it, an animal with a pale patch along its back.

The story would take a long time.

Once or twice the child would stop to ask whether the father was still there. Each time, a tap would come back through the wood.

When they had finished, I would carry my tools home.

***

So I would live there, near the older woman, gradually taking on some of her work.

People would find me by a shaving caught in my sleeve, or by the bowl of water warming beside me. I would know a few voices well and be mistaken about others. I would have to ask people to repeat themselves. There would be repairs I could finish while someone waited and repairs that meant crawling outside together to see what had shifted.

On still days I would take my work onto the stone.

For a little while, voices would reach me without passing through anything. I would be able to look up and see the person who had spoken.

Then a blade of grass would lean. I would gather the small pieces first, put the blades away, and carry the unfinished joint back into the passage.

There would be room there for my knees, my basket, the warm bowl.

I would set the joint down and listen once more before deciding where to cut.
