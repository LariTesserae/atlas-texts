I would stay at the bench longer than thirst required.

The cup would empty. I would keep holding it, partly because setting it down seemed liable to end the visit, partly because I would not yet know where my hands were supposed to go when they had nothing in them.

At the far end of the bench, the second cup would turn a little. Its handle would come to rest against the roof’s hanging cloth.

I would look away.

After some time, a small wooden wedge would appear between us.

It would be split at the thick end. One side would be polished, the other rough enough to catch a sleeve. There would be nothing to indicate whether it had been offered to me or merely put down.

I would pick it up.

The hollow beside me would ease.

That would be my first work: holding an object someone else had finished holding.

***

At first I would be a visitor with increasingly dirty cuffs.

I would carry things between shelters. Not far, necessarily. A tool laid a few inches beyond a worker’s reach might require an afternoon to bring closer. I would learn to put it where it was wanted without waiting to see the hand.

I would make mistakes. I would place a scraper blade down in wet clay and come back to find the edge wrapped in cloth. I would move a cup out of what looked like a drainage groove, and the next morning find it returned, with a low ridge built around it.

The wedge would stay with me.

Sometimes it would fit beneath a bench leg. Sometimes it would hold a cloth away from a freshly smoothed surface. I would begin to notice places for it: a narrow opening under a tilted slab, a gap behind a prop, the lifted corner of a basket that needed air beneath it.

My own hands would change so gradually that I might blame the heat.

The fingers would no longer all swell alike. One would remain thin enough to enter a seam. Another would flatten against a board. There would be an extra fold near the base of my thumb that I could not recall having before.

I would still use the cup made for me. But I would start turning its handle toward the wall.

***

The place I would return to would be beside a ceiling sheet held lower than the others.

There would be a basin beneath it, and a strip of dry ground no wider than a sleeping body. Water would gather overhead without falling. The sheet would have been poorly fitted, or the ground beneath it would have risen after its supports were set. A forked pole would stand nearby, too short by a distance that changed whenever I looked at it.

Someone would be holding the sheet up.

I would know the holder by a shallow crease that sometimes appeared above the basin. When the heat thickened, the crease would fill with water. When a cup was brought, it would briefly become the hollow of a shoulder.

I would bring water for several days before trying the pole.

The first time, the pole would take no weight at all.

The second time, its bundled ends would spread against the ceiling. I would tie them more tightly. This would make the pole too short again.

Eventually I would put my wedge beneath it.

A few drops would fall into the basin.

For an instant, a hand would become visible beside my face: fingers bent inward, palm deeply lined, a small flattened place where something had pressed for a long time. Then it would close and disappear.

I would leave the pole standing and go back to the bench.

By morning the wedge would be lying beside my cup. The pole would be on its side. Someone would be holding the ceiling again.

I would spend a long time examining the wedge. One face would bear a new dent.

***

I do not think I would become a maker of broad surfaces. I would be poor at deciding how far a floor ought to go, or where a basin should end. Whenever I tried to smooth a large stretch, I would leave one patch untouched because there was something beneath it I could feel but not name.

Nor would I be one of the pale beings running ahead of water. I would usually arrive after the difficulty had become substantial.

My body would acquire its clearest shape in cramped places.

A wrist would form where I needed to reach behind a prop. An elbow would come into being under a bench. When carrying a basket, I might have an ordinary enough back; when setting it down, I would need to wait while my legs found somewhere to stand.

The dark mark I would come to recognize as my own would be on the side of a finger. It would catch clay and remain damp after the rest of the hand had dried.

I would begin keeping wedges under cloth.

Not many. A few thick ones, several slender ones, one with a curve cut into its face. I would dress them slowly, using a scraper whose handle someone had altered for me. Beside them I would keep the first wedge, still split, useful only when turned a particular way.

I would leave small gaps in my work so that it could be reached from underneath.

***

Once, I would find the keeper of the failed pieces sitting outside its shelter.

A wrapped slab would lie between its knees. The cloth would be open at one corner, showing an edge pressed so thin that light passed through it.

I would sit nearby and take out a wedge.

The slab would lift slightly. I would put the wedge beneath it.

Nothing else would happen for a long time.

Then the cloth would be drawn back over the edge. The slab would settle onto my wedge, and the two depressions under the keeper would slowly fill with shallow water.

The next time I came, a different failed piece would be resting there.

My wedge would have been left on the bench. Beside it would be a narrow offcut of clay, covered so it had not hardened.

I would take both.

After that, I would sometimes find work waiting for me that nobody intended to finish.

***

I would return to the low ceiling.

This time I would not bring the pole.

I would bring a shallow basket, wet cloth, three broad wedges, and a piece of clay that had already been pressed against the sheet’s underside. I would have spent several visits obtaining that impression. The holder would have shifted very little while I worked.

The clay would fit against the ceiling. Beneath it I would build a squat support, broad enough to stay upright as the basin changed. I would leave an opening through its middle so the water could pass.

For hours it would take no weight.

Then one wedge would begin to creak.

I would put a hand against the support and keep it there. My forearm would thicken. Something at my shoulder would gather, tighten, and find a bearing against the ground.

The holder would not let go all at once.

First the basin would tremble. Then a crease would appear along the ceiling, followed by the faint outline of a wrist. Water would run down it.

I would hold still.

At last there would be room beside the support for someone to sit.

The cup I had brought would lift. It would remain in the air for a while before tilting.

I would need a drink myself, but my hand would be caught against the clay. I would have to wait.

When the cup came down, it would be placed close to my other hand.

***

That is where I think I would find myself: on the narrow ground beside that basin, returning often, sometimes caught there for longer than I intended.

I would become an inhabitant whose work could usually be removed. Wedges, rests, low stools, supports with room to pass a cord through them. Some would work. Some would be returned to me with dents I would have to study.

There would be other places to go. Someone would leave a basket suspended on an awkward bend of arm, or a newly laid slab would need to be held until its underside firmed. I would learn the routes between those places and leave my tools where another could find them.

But I would keep the bench.

Its roof would sag at one corner. The ground at its far end would never quite dry. Under it I would keep a little shelf of temporary things, including the first split wedge.

My cup would sit above them.

Sometimes, returning, I would find a second vessel beside it and know to leave the shelf undisturbed. I would sit down carefully, keeping my feet clear of the soft ground.

After a while, something beside me would stop bearing weight.

I would hear the bench creak.
