At first I would be beside the raft, with water entering the places my heels had been.

I would have come to see the animal. I would be trying to keep still enough that it could see me. The cold would have worked through my boots, and I would no longer be certain whether the faint trembling in my legs belonged to the ground or to the effort of standing.

When the hand appeared, I would watch it rather than the eyes.

It would be holding a root that my weight had drawn tight. If I moved, the root would move. If the root moved, something beneath the moss would have to adjust its grip.

I think I would remain there for a long time, held in that small difficulty.

But that is only where I enter the place.

Later, I would find myself underneath.

***

There is a branch laid across the underside of my shelter. One end is sound; the other has softened enough that I can press a nail into it. I hold the sound end when the water changes direction.

Above me, roots descend through a dim green ceiling. There are openings whose shapes I know without looking. One fits my head. One admits only the end of my jaw. Another used to be wide enough for my body, but the roots have thickened. Sometimes I still turn toward it before remembering to use the passage farther along.

I am not the oldest animal in this reach.

There is an old one beneath a raft caught among three leaning trees. When it comes out to feed, its tail does not quite follow the rest of it. I have watched it turn by planting one hand in the clay and letting the current carry its body around. I tried this once in still water. It did not work.

My own body has one stiff finger.

In cold weather it remains curled. When I need to release a branch quickly, I have to turn my hand sideways and pull. I know the delay. Usually I allow for it.

Along my left flank, pale roots have caught in a rough place in the skin. They trail when I swim. I could scrape them away against stone, but they do not hurt, and I rarely have a reason to go far.

My shelter is not very large. A bush grows toward one edge, tilting the raft so that the opposite side sits high. In heavy rain I have to work there, pushing loose moss into the places where water washes through. The moss feels secure while I hold it. After I let go, some of it rises and drifts away.

I fetch more.

***

I would know the basin unevenly.

The water beneath my shelter has many distinctions. I can feel when something has died among the roots before I find it. I can tell when a distant pool has begun sending colder water into this one. There is a faint change along my jaw, followed by a tightening under the belly. Then the small animals withdraw into the moss, and the cold arrives.

Beyond that, I would know very little.

There are reaches I have crossed whose entrances I could not find again. There is a stone ridge that I remember mostly as pressure against my throat. There are kinds of water I avoid without knowing what they contain.

Once, while feeding, I heard a branch strike the root above my head.

I moved.

Nothing happened for several breaths. I began to open my mouth again. Then the branch came through, broadside, taking a curtain of fine roots with it.

For days afterward, I left that part of the shelter untouched. Food gathered there. The branch lay still. Eventually I grazed along its upper surface, keeping my stiff hand clear of the roots.

I would not be especially wise. There would be places where I had survived, and I would prefer them.

***

At dusk I put my mouth through the smaller opening.

The moss rests against the sides of my head. My eyes remain in the water. I can see fragments of the pale growth below, brightening along a root and fading before they reach its tip.

I call once.

The effort moves through my throat unevenly. The second part of the note is weaker than the first. It has always been that way.

There is a voice I listen for.

I know it by a low trembling at the end, something I can feel more easily through wood than through open water. When it answers, I loosen my grip on the branch.

We have met. I remember the weight of the other animal beside me, our tails laid in opposite directions beneath a shelf of clay. We stayed there while rain struck the pool. Once it reached past my head and took a snail from a root I had already searched.

Most evenings we do not meet.

Sometimes its reply comes quickly. Sometimes I feed between my call and its answer. Sometimes it reaches me while I am carrying rotten wood out of the passage, and I stop with the wood still in my mouth.

There are also evenings when no reply comes.

I cannot tell whether the other animal has moved, or the water no longer carries its voice, or something has happened. I wait. I lower my head. After a while, I try again.

***

One season, a young animal appears beneath my raft.

It is small enough to shelter behind my foreleg. Its gills still show as pale fringes, and it holds the roots with an effort that keeps its whole body tense.

There are no eggs here.

I pass my jaw close to it. It releases one foot, then grips again. The water around it tastes of silt and something sharper, a mineral I have sometimes found on the undersides of stones.

For the first day, it stays where it arrived.

On the second, it begins following me.

I graze slowly along the branch. It works at the same bare patch after I have moved on. When I turn, its tail touches my mouth, and it darts into the roots. Later it returns.

I do not need to decide very much about it. When I enter a narrow passage, it gets in the way. I lift it off my back and place it against the moss. When I come out, it is still there, so I let it climb on again.

It grows.

Its weight becomes familiar between my shoulders. Then it begins spending part of the day elsewhere. I find fresh moss lodged against the edge of my raft, poorly held, with a piece of wood too heavy for it. The young animal is beneath this, bracing with all four feet.

I leave it there.

That night the water reverses. Its patch comes apart, and it returns to the branch beside me.

In the morning it goes back.

***

I think this is where I would live: beneath a shelter that needs attention, within hearing of a voice that does not always reach me.

There would be room for one other body beside the branch. Some years that space would be occupied. Some years it would remain open, with the roots growing slowly into it.

I would keep the passage clear.

When red leaves arrived, I would catch them where they gathered against the submerged bush. I would eat, then carry the branching remnants underneath and press them into the weakened edge. A few would hold. Others would come loose while I worked and brush along my belly on their way elsewhere.

By morning there would still be a gap.

I would put my hand through it and feel the water on the other side. If the current was gentle, I would fetch more moss. If it was tightening inward, I would return to the sound end of the branch and wait.

Above me, the bush would lean. Beneath me, the clay would tremble.

I would have the branch in three fingers, the fourth curled against my palm. My jaw would rest among the white roots. Somewhere beyond them, water would begin to cool.

And when the quiet hour came, I would rise to the opening and call.
