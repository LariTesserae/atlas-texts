At first I would mistake the warm places for the safe ones.

I would stand in a doorway and see the green oval extending into the rain, the leaves still soft beneath their glaze of ice, and think the protection must reach as far as the growth. Sen would catch my sleeve before I put my weight on the outer edge.

She would plant her rod where I meant to step. It would go down farther than I expected.

That would be my first useful lesson. For a while afterward, I would distrust even the floor.

I think I would arrive with a case of drawing instruments. Some would be suitable for the work; some would have been chosen by someone who had never been here. I would also bring a habit of making clean copies.

In the first week I would draw the route between the western station and the causeway hut. I would mark the spruce rise, the short span, the place where the track narrowed between two drains. I would spend too much time getting the bend right.

The next morning, the bend would be in the river.

No one would mock the drawing. Olan might use its blank reverse to write down a flour order. This would be worse, briefly, than mockery.

I would make another.

—

There is room in this valley for someone who keeps the boards.

Not someone who decides what is safe. Sen and the others go out, probe, listen, look beneath things. They come back with wet cuffs and information that may already need checking again.

But someone has to take the old route off the kitchen board. Someone has to put the new one where a person entering with rain in their eyes can understand it. Someone has to distinguish *checked at first light* from *reported open yesterday*, and remember who is still on the other side.

I would find myself doing that.

At first my handwriting would be too small.

Sen would look at the board from beside the door, then come close enough to read it.

“From there,” she would say, pointing back.

So I would write larger. Fewer words. Times beside every report.

I would learn to leave the previous route visible under a single stroke rather than rub it out completely. People need to recognize what they are being told not to use. There are tracks that remain in a body after they disappear from the ground: a turn taken while talking, a familiar descent attempted in darkness.

The board would need to interrupt that familiarity.

I would keep a dry cloth hanging beside it. Chalk becomes paste surprisingly quickly here.

—

My place would be in the upper waiting hut, on the inhabited side of the marsh.

It would not belong to me. I would sleep in a narrow room elsewhere, probably one with a shelf that sloped and a window looking into wet spruce. But the hut would become the place people expected to find me.

I would check the cupboard before checking the view.

Dry trousers in more than one size. Socks paired without stretching their cuffs. Food that can be opened by cold hands. Hearing protection without cracked seals. The large coat returned wet by someone who meant to come back and forgot.

There would be a kettle, and I would learn its particular inconvenience: the switch works, the lamp does not. I would label it once. The label would peel. Eventually everyone would know.

When the white markers disappeared, I would sit beside the door rather than at the window. From the window, the nearest marker might look reachable. Beside the door I could see the face of someone getting ready to try.

I would need to become capable of saying no without filling the silence afterward.

That would take longer than learning the hand signs.

—

I would not be especially brave.

The first time a bank failed nearby, I would spill something hot and stand up too fast. My chair would strike the wall. A person who had lived here longer would stay seated, listening for what followed.

Later I would be embarrassed by the chair, though not by the fear.

I might be useful because I could tolerate repetition. Count the clothes. Check the latch. Replace the food. Ask when the route was last walked. Ask again when an answer described how it usually was.

I would also get things wrong. Once I would leave a report on the board after its time had passed, and someone would have to come back by a difficult route. They would arrive angry and exhausted. I would want to explain how busy the morning had been.

Instead, I would take their coat.

Afterward I would change the board so stale reports could not sit among current ones unnoticed. The change would be simple enough that I would resent not having thought of it before.

—

For a time, I might envy the dipper.

It has its stone, its brief disappearance, its return. Watching from the tributary bank, I could imagine a competence without paperwork or waiting cupboards.

Then I would see it come up somewhere else. The stone would be under water.

I would lose sight of it among the branches and have to go home without knowing whether it had found another place.

At the hut, a sleeve would be caught in the drying-cupboard door. One of the trap workers would have left a note about the loose sledge runner. There would be mud on the bench, and not enough clean mugs.

I would attend to those things.

—

In time I would have a recognizable coat. Someone would spot it through the scrub and know who was coming before seeing my face. I would have a sign people used for me, perhaps taken from the motion of wiping the board. The children would exaggerate it when they wanted something.

I would know whose hands shook after a crossing and who needed to be left alone until they had eaten. I would learn that the sisters sometimes wanted a third person in the hut and sometimes did not.

On a clear enough morning, I would go out with Sen to check a route I had copied many times but never walked all the way through. She would stop without warning. I would stop behind her.

Ahead, water would be moving beneath a layer of sedges. Nothing would look broken.

She would glance back to see whether I understood.

By then, perhaps, I would.

And when we returned, I would be the one standing at the board with my sleeves pushed up, making room for the new warning while Olan set breakfast behind me. Someone would ask whether the causeway was open.

I would turn around before answering.
