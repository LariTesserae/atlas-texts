I think I would first be found beneath a bank, rather than beside the open pool.

The bank would not look large enough to contain me. A low tree would grow across it, its several trunks making a roof close to the ground. Between two roots there would be a dark opening, half occupied by water. My hindquarters would be inside. My forefeet would rest on a submerged shelf of stone, and my jaw would lie where the shelf met the bank.

From there I could feel two currents.

One would come down from the pool, cold and uneven, carrying the small disturbances of things feeding above me. The other would rise through a crack under my left forefoot. It would be faintly warmer. Where they met, a few pale roots would move continually, though nothing on the surface seemed to move at all.

I would not be waiting for a message. Most of what reached me would have nothing to do with my companions.

A stone settling. Water lifting a strip of dead bark. The intermittent scraping of something small beneath the shelf.

I would let these pass through my attention without having to turn each one into a decision.

Then a foot would touch rock upstream, withdraw, and touch it again.

I would lift my jaw.

***

I do not think I would be the elder.

There are too many ways of knowing this country that cannot be acquired merely by being attentive. I would know the stretch between the bank and the feeding hollow. I would know which passage pinched behind the shoulders, and where I could turn if water began arriving from the wrong direction. I would know a stone that tipped under weight, although moss had made it look continuous with its neighbors.

Beyond that, I would have followed others.

There would be a ridge I had crossed only once, in cold rain, with my nose close to the tail ahead of me. Somewhere beyond it would be water that tasted strange. I would remember eating there and not being satisfied. I would remember the relief of returning, though the return took several days.

My body would be fully grown. I would have the strength to pull a root loose and the sense, usually, to stop before the whole mat came with it. But when an old animal left a resting place without calling, I would still get up.

Perhaps not immediately. I might remain with my jaw against the stone, trying to feel what it had felt.

Then I would follow.

***

There would be a small patch of liverwort behind one shoulder.

I would never see it. I would know that something there caught against the underside of a root in a passage I used often. Sometimes another animal would nose through my coat at that place. The touch would be pleasant until its teeth found an old hair that had not yet loosened.

My back would carry the rusty stain of our water. My belly would smell of the warm bank. On emerging into cold air, I would feel the outer hairs stiffen while the skin beneath them remained warm.

I would be an animal that could lie still for a long time without growing tired of lying still.

But not everywhere.

On an exposed ridge, with only shallow water beneath the moss, I would keep shifting my weight. The ground there would offer small, broken impressions: feet, rain, roots straining in wind. Nothing deep enough to rest my attention against. I would move toward a hollow before the others did, then wait below it until they arrived.

***

The upstream touch would come again.

This time I would know the youngster by its haste. It would place both forefeet too close together, slide, recover, and send a confused disturbance down the stone.

I would leave the bank.

Outside, cloud would already be among the trees. The pool would remain visible, but its far side would not. A few stems would stand alone in the gray air, their reflections clearer than their leaves.

The youngster would be on a root above the inlet. It would have gone farther that afternoon than before. Its coat would carry cold water and the odor of crushed vegetation from a passage none of us had used recently.

It would press its jaw beneath mine.

For a while I would stand there and allow it.

Then it would turn back toward the inlet.

I would follow until we reached a place where the channel narrowed between two stones. Water passed readily. A small body could pass readily too. Beyond the narrowing, however, the roof dropped. I could feel the current pushing into a space without feeling a second opening.

I would set one forefoot against the stone and tap.

The youngster would put its head into the gap.

I would tap again, more widely spaced.

It would draw back, but not leave. There would be something in there it wanted: a shell-bearing animal, perhaps, or tender growth where the water had washed the underside of a root clean.

I would put my muzzle into the opening myself. My shoulders would prevent me from going far. I would scrape what I could reach, bring out a little food, and eat some of it.

The youngster would take the rest.

This would not make the opening safe. Tomorrow it might try again. I would have to be there tomorrow, or another member of the group would have to notice.

***

So perhaps that is where I would belong: within reach of the less experienced ones.

Not always beside them. Sometimes I would be asleep when they set out. Sometimes I would be feeding, and their taps would arrive through my teeth as I held a root, and I would finish cutting it before I went.

I would not understand everything they wanted. Their quick movements might irritate me. A youngster pulling at the plants behind my shoulder could make me turn sharply enough to frighten it.

But at a stream crossing I would often choose the lower stone.

The elder would enter first and find footing beneath the root mat. Another adult would stay near the bank. I would settle downstream, broadside to the current, where a slipping body would come against me before it reached the deep channel.

There would be no need to gather everyone close. The current itself would tell me when a youngster had entered. Its scrambling would become clearer as it approached.

A small forefoot would catch in my coat.

I would brace.

***

Later, perhaps, the familiar spring would weaken.

I would feel the change, but I would not know where to go. I might spend too long opening the outlet, dragging away leaves that had nothing to do with the failing flow. Each removal would give me a brief increase in water, enough to make me work again.

The elder would climb uphill.

I would remain below until one of the young came back for me.

At the new place there would be no pool yet. Only lifted moss, a little warmth, and the smell of water that had spent a long time in stone. I would lie beside the others. For much of the night I would feel very little.

Then a bubble would pass beneath my chest.

I would shift aside. A youngster would put its nose into the space I had left.

By morning we might have made a hollow.

I would drink cautiously. The water would leave an unfamiliar taste along the hard edge of my mouth. There would be food here, but I would take only a little. My body would need time.

When the cloud thickened, I would look for a shelf below the new opening. I would test it with my weight, settle my forefeet, and lower my jaw.

Behind me, someone would be tearing moss. Farther up the slope, the elder would move through the shrubs. I would feel neither of them for a moment.

Then the water would bring me a small, hurried touch.

I would lift my head.
