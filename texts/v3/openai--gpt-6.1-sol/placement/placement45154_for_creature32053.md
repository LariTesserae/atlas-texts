At first I would put myself beneath the fallen leaf, where the water has entered without showing on the surface.

There is just enough space between a vein and the earth for a body to pass. Above me, the leaf is still a leaf: a ceiling with a pattern, thicker along some lines than others. Farther along, it has lost that distinction. The passage widens into material that gives underfoot.

I would know the widening before I knew the courtyard.

I think I would be one of the workers who comes up carrying nothing.

My first contact with the morning would be the underside of another ant’s head. We meet where a fiber narrows the passage. Her jaws hold a pale fragment, and I draw back into a shallow place in the wall while she passes. One of her feet touches my antenna. Then the fragment moves overhead, close enough to brush the ceiling.

I follow her toward the entrance.

The air changes before the light does. It becomes less close, less full of the bodies I have passed. At the opening I stop with my forelegs outside and the rest of me still in the root. A loose grain rests against the rim. I touch it, touch the ground beside it, step over.

Here, I would not have the courtyard all at once.

The fern would reach me as a brief change in light, then perhaps as water striking the ground. The root would be a surface I could follow for a long time. The spider would remain something I had not encountered. Even the four entrances would not make a shape I stood apart from and understood. There would be this opening behind me, another reached by a familiar stretch of ground, and ways between them that sometimes became difficult.

I go toward the basin.

Where there was water, the earth now offers only a little coolness. I lower my head. There is moisture among the particles, but no surface to drink from. I move along its edge, turning where the ground steepens.

Something has crossed the route near the seed coat. I pause there longer.

Another worker comes close. Our antennae meet, separate, meet again. She goes toward the moss. I turn back. No event has occurred that could be seen from above, except that two small bodies briefly stopped beside each other.

Below, the damp has reached a chamber.

I would find myself there next, with a larva in my jaws.

It is a different weight from earth. I lift it clear of the floor and turn into the passage. The way is narrow enough that I must carry it lengthwise. Ahead, another worker is moving in the same direction; behind me, one comes with another pale body.

At the fiber, I slow.

My load touches the wall. I adjust it and pass through.

The new place is not far away. Its floor is drier, its ceiling still firm. I put the larva beside the others and remain close, touching it once more before turning. On the return journey, I meet a worker coming toward me. I fold into the shallow recess. Her load passes my head.

Later I take up a crumb from the old chamber.

It catches against a strand in the wall, and I pull until it comes free. There is a slight collapse where it was. I do not repair that place. I carry the crumb through the passage, out into the green light.

At the mound I set it down.

It rolls toward my feet.

For a moment I stand over it, touching the place where it failed to stay. Then I take it again. A little farther along, a pale chip projects from the slope. I press the crumb beside it, release it, and touch it once.

This time it stays.

I would be that worker—not always carrying, not always building, sometimes stopped at a dried patch of ground, sometimes making room in a passage. I would spend much of my life beneath the courtyard without knowing how nearly invisible it was.

Before I go below, another ant comes out.

Her forefoot lands on the crumb I have just placed. She climbs over it and continues toward the moss. I turn into the opening, where the light ends a short distance down.
