At first I would mistake the sound for someone washing a cup.

It would come through the wall beside my bed: a little turn of water, a pause, then the touch of something hard against something else. In the morning I would find no kitchen on that side. The passage would run between my room and a bank of sealed housings, all still wearing their inspection labels. Farther along, under a bridge, a basin would have arrived close enough that one corner of it was visible through the handrail.

I would have been told the room was quiet.

It would be quiet. After a few nights I would stop getting up to investigate.

My room would be above a workshop that had not yet decided what work it was for. There would be a bench, a locked cabinet, six reels of wire in a rack made for seven. One window would look onto the channel. The other would show the back of a building close enough to touch, though getting to its entrance would take most of an hour.

I would begin by keeping that second window shut. Later, someone on the other side would open theirs and ask whether I had a small saucepan.

I would have one.

Getting it back would be my first real difficulty.

***

I think I would arrive as a person employed to carry measurements.

Not to make them. There would be people who knew where to set their instruments and how long to wait before accepting a reading. I would take the results from one workshop to another, in a shallow case with a catch that pressed a permanent dent into my thumb.

There would be paper in the case, and samples: a length of cable, two pieces of tile, sometimes a little bottle half full of water. A measurement made in one district might not survive the journey written as a number. The piece of tile would be needed so that the recipient could put a thumb across it and understand how wide the joint had been.

For some deliveries I would carry an empty box.

“Don’t put anything in it,” the woman at dispatch would say.

I would ask whether rain counted.

She would look at the weather and give me a lid.

This work would take me along routes too ordinary to appear in anyone’s account of the valley. Behind the tanks, below the kitchens, through the passage where the wall had been temporarily padded because people kept bruising the same shoulder. I would learn which stairs were easier with my case full. I would develop the habit of carrying a spare latch home, even on days when no latch was needed.

The latch man would notice.

“That’s how it starts,” he would say.

I would assume he meant the weight.

***

There would be a place on my route where I always stopped to adjust the strap.

It would be a recess beside an enclosed flywheel. Warm air would come out beneath the housing, just enough to take the cold from my fingers. A narrow shelf, probably intended for tools, would hold my case securely while I worked the buckle.

One morning there would be a furred animal on it.

It would not move when I put the case down. Its feet would open against the shelf, joint after joint, and one would rest on the corner of a delivery slip.

I would wait.

It would wait too, though it would seem to be doing something more exact than waiting.

Eventually I would ease the paper out from under its foot. The animal would lift that foot and set it down again a little farther to the left.

The next day there would be three.

I would find another place for my case.

A fitter would come while I was standing there, pretending to check the buckle. She would squat in front of the animals without looking directly at them, then draw a short white line on the housing.

“What did you see?” I would ask.

“Enough.”

She would put away her chalk.

After she left, I would look at the line. It would be beneath the shelf, nowhere near the animals.

For weeks I would try to work out what she had noticed. I would look at their heads, the spacing between them, the places their feet touched. I would decide it had something to do with warmth. Then I would find them sleeping against a cold drain.

One afternoon the fitter would return and replace a seal below the shelf.

She would let me hold the old one. Nothing would appear wrong with it until she bent it between two fingers.

***

My deliveries would become slower.

Not late, at first. I would simply use up the spare time in them.

I would stop beside a calf folded into a tree socket. I would watch a basin cleaner put both feet out of the water without lifting the brush. I would wait for a black bird to finish examining the metal fastening on my case.

There would be a child who accused me of helping it cheat.

I would say I had never played the game.

“That’s helping,” the child would say.

Eventually someone would give me a second kind of errand.

A room had come out of one of the alignment chambers. It was being left to settle, but a worker had forgotten a coat inside it. Could I collect the coat on my way?

The room would smell of cold plaster. Its window would overlook the underside of a staircase I knew, though I had never seen that underside from anywhere else. The coat would be hanging from a handle on a wall with no door.

I would take it.

Then I would go back for the lunch tin in its pocket, which would still be hanging there.

I would bring both to dispatch and be told, gently, that this sometimes happened. Nobody would laugh until I did.

After that there would be more such errands. A bowl from a cupboard in a vacated apartment. Three chairs from a passage scheduled to become narrower. A child’s bead from a ventilation cage being removed.

I would learn to ask who had last held the object.

Sometimes that person would have to come with me.

Sometimes they would stand outside while I went in, talking through the doorway about something quite unrelated. Bread, usually. The trouble with a feeding line. A sister expected from below.

Their voice would make the object easier to pick up.

***

I would not be unusually gifted at this.

There would be days when I carried a chair halfway home and discovered that its back had become too high to pass under the bridges. I would have to retrace my route, sit in it for a while, then try again.

I would lose a spoon.

I would return a cupboard with everything in it except the smell of the owner’s soap. She would be courteous about this, which would make me feel worse.

When my own saucepan finally came back, months after I had lent it through the window, I would receive it at the door. Its base would have a small burned patch shaped like a leaf.

The neighbor would bring bread as well.

We would stand beside each other in the passage and eat some of it before either of us thought to go indoors.

***

By then I would be doing less work with measurements.

Dispatch would keep giving me things that had to be returned, and people would find me on my route to ask about others. I would have no particular title. On the roster there would still be an old one, abbreviated to fit the space.

I would carry a cloth roll containing a screwdriver, a length of soft cord, a brush, a spoon that was not mine, and a square of protective film.

The film would be for laying under small objects on unfamiliar shelves. This would work often enough to become a habit.

I would also carry food for an old lintel whose warm alcove lay beneath the turning point of a freight line. It would take the grass from my hand in a slow, awkward way. On days when it did not raise its head, I would leave the grass close to its lip.

Once, while I sat beside it, a metallic note would begin somewhere high in the structure.

The lintel would set its jaw against the wall.

Through the sole of my shoe I would feel a faint answer. I would stay until the sound ended, and then a little longer.

***

My room would become recognizable mostly by what people left outside it.

A cracked cup in a basket. A hinge wrapped in yellow cloth. A note asking me not to collect anything until Thursday. Beside these, sometimes, an object with no note at all.

I would not bring those straight inside.

I would leave a stool in the passage and sit near them while I ate. Often somebody would come back.

Once there would be a transparent nest, empty, its chambers crushed together by whatever hands had removed it. I would spend an evening opening the folds with the blunt end of my screwdriver. The nursery would be no larger than a thimble. A few pale hairs would adhere to the screw hole at its top.

I would take it to the warm shelf.

In the morning it would be gone.

***

This is where I think I would find myself: on the middle terraces, close to the machinery, with a room I had slowly learned to enter carrying nothing.

I would be a resident people trusted with things they were reluctant to lose. Not all the things could be recovered. I would have to learn how to say that at someone’s doorway, and how to stay long enough afterward.

I would know the condensation-cup reader by the sound of her step. I would have a favorite cook and an unpaid debt to one of the tree sisters. Children would occasionally accompany me, then run ahead when I approached a place they found dull. I would take longer over meals than I intended.

From time to time I would go to the upper rail.

I would stand beside the hanging drop without leaning too close. Inside it, somewhere among the inverted roofs, would be the passage outside my room.

If the light were right, I might make out the stool.

Perhaps someone would be sitting on it.

I would look for a while, trying to distinguish their coat, then turn back down the terraces. There would be enough time to get home before they left, provided I did not take the stairs empty-handed.

So I would pick up a small fallen screw from beside the rail.

It would be cold in my palm.

I would carry it all the way.
