I would find myself in the strip of floor the carriers leave empty in front of the bench.

At first I would think this was a courtesy. There is a place to sit; there is room for my boots. In a chamber where every other interval is occupied, that small provision would seem almost generous.

I would sit without taking off my gloves.

The bench would transmit the sound differently from the floor. The buried knocking would arrive through the backs of my thighs; the higher rattling would come through the corrugated wall when I leaned against it. My teeth would remain quiet until I closed them. Then I would hear another register.

Under the bench, a carrier would unfold its fringe.

It would not come out to meet me. It would put two or three wires into the space beside my heel and wait for whatever my weight did next.

I would lift my foot.

The floor would give it back without retaining its shape.

The carrier would withdraw its wires.

For a while I would continue to think of myself as someone taking a rest.

***

I would have brought something unnecessary: a folded paper, perhaps, with the route to the chamber drawn on it. Not a useful map of the chamber itself. That would have to be redrawn whenever a carrier crossed an aisle or a blade gave up the width of a finger. Just directions for finding the entrance from above.

I would keep it in my breast pocket because I would not yet understand how little there was here for a pocket to do.

My first work would be trying to distinguish the sounds.

Not counting the strokes. There are too many, and their intervals would defeat me. I would listen instead for the places where something failed to arrive: a knock expected under the left foot, a thin ringing that ended before its neighbouring blade had answered.

Once, I would hear a vacancy and turn toward it.

On a mineral face near the shelter, an old washer would have stopped striking. Its feet would still be working. Each would lift and settle, lift and settle, with no discernible change of position.

I would put a finger beside it.

The blade would cut the glove.

Not badly. A short incision through the cloth, without the resistance I would expect from cutting. The severed fibres would open around my finger.

The animal would strike once.

I would feel the blow farther up my arm than seemed reasonable.

Then the colony would take up its noise again, and I would stand there with my hand against the stone, unsure whether I had helped anything or merely interrupted it.

***

The carriers would interest me before the resident did.

The pale surfaces would be too difficult to look at honestly. A piece of wrist twenty metres from another piece of wrist; a fold with worn black structures along it; something soft occupying a separation that ought not to contain anything at all. I would spend too much effort deciding what I was seeing.

A carrier could be followed.

It could be watched choosing one old animal and leaving another. I would learn that its hesitation was not indecision. The pads at the ends of its wires would continue searching while the rest of it stayed still. Sometimes it would pass a damaged washer several times before lifting it. Sometimes a healthy-looking one would be carried away at once.

I would begin to recognize individuals by what they had retained.

One would have a slight rise beneath its rear plates. Another would make three faint sounds when it came to rest, the last almost too low to hear. The carrier under my bench would have a broken wire that touched the floor sooner than the others.

My bench.

I would have begun calling it that before noticing.

When I stood up, the carrier would remain underneath. When I returned, the strip of floor would still be empty.

***

I would not touch the crank early.

I would touch the railing.

The marks would be more legible to my fingertips than to my eyes. Some would have a burr on one edge, others on both. I would follow a group of five beneath the rail, turning my wrist awkwardly to reach the last cut.

There, my hand would settle into a position that felt familiar.

Not a memory. There would be nothing to remember: no name, no face, no scene of a worker making marks. Only the unpleasant ease of a posture I had never adopted before.

I would let go.

Across the chamber, a carrier would stop with an old washer partly enclosed beneath its plates.

After a time it would continue.

That would be the first occasion on which I understood that doing nothing also reached them.

***

I do not think I would become the engine’s attendant.

The gauge needs no watching. The wheel does not wander. The crank is an invitation too specifically shaped for a hand, and I would distrust it more each time I passed.

Nor would I become the chamber’s interpreter. I could learn the difference between a colony making room and a colony taking weight without learning what either was for. Knowing a sound well enough to flinch at it would not make it a language.

I would become useful somewhere smaller.

It might begin with the glove.

My own glove, the one cut against the blade. A carrier would examine it while I sat, touching the loose fibres with its pads. I would hold my hand still. One wire would enter the cut and touch the skin beneath.

The sensation would be very slight.

The carrier would flatten closer to the floor.

Something under my boots would yield, pause, and yield again.

I would feel my hand become heavier.

I would not know how to remove it without putting that weight somewhere else.

So I would wait.

***

After that, the place reserved before the bench would change its meaning.

The buried back would settle below it before I sat down. The carrier would come out farther. Small animals would gather along the corrugated panel behind my shoulders, not on me, but near enough for their strokes to pass through the bench.

There would still be room to leave.

I would test that. I would rise, walk to the entrance, and stand where a little cooler air could be felt without moving. The paper in my pocket would have softened along its folds. I would take it out and confirm that the marks were still marks.

Behind me, the chamber would sound much as it had before.

Only one blade near the shelter would give a thin, sustained note that I had not heard while seated.

I could leave while it made that sound.

I would fold the paper and go back to the bench.

That is where I would find myself: not imprisoned, not quite staying freely, learning the weight of an absence by repeatedly making it.

***

There would come a time when one of the old washers was left in my palm.

The carrier would place it there through the split in my glove. Its central plate would be almost clear. Beneath it, the dark structure would contract around whatever it could not release.

It would strike.

My bones would answer badly.

The first blows would go nowhere. I would feel them expire in the flesh of my hand. Then I would move my elbow onto the bench, and something in the panel behind me would rattle.

The animal’s feet would spread.

I would try different positions: wrist straight, shoulder against the wall, soles resting lightly on the floor. The carrier would wait. When I found a posture in which the washer ceased to grow lighter, its wires would withdraw.

I would remain like that for a long time.

Eventually I would feel the two transparent combs moving against the edge of the cut glove.

I would let them take what they could.

***

Perhaps I would still have an ordinary body.

I would dislike the dryness. I would shift when my knee hurt. I would remember food with an impatience none of the chamber’s creatures seemed to share.

But certain positions would become necessary.

A hand laid on the bench. A heel turned slightly outward. My back held away from the corrugated panel by just enough space for a carrier’s wires to explore.

In those positions, I would be something the chamber had not possessed before.

A surface that could be asked.

The stone takes the blows delivered to it. The engine receives everything through its casing. Even the great soft resident withdraws too slowly to offer much distinction between refusal and accommodation.

I could lift my hand.

I could put it down elsewhere.

This small difference would occupy me.

I would learn to lift it slowly.

***

I would go to the far gap eventually.

I would not try to look through it.

The old animals there have no spare movement for my curiosity. I would kneel sideways, keeping my coat clear of the edges, and place the back of my hand against the mineral a little below the lowest trembling plate.

For some time nothing would happen.

Then one black foot would loosen.

Only one.

It would come away from the stone and touch my knuckle, feeling for a purchase that was not where its neighbours expected it to be.

I would stay still.

I would not yet know whether I had taken a little of its burden or added my own to what it carried. I would watch the foot, the pale plate above it, the narrow place from which it had withdrawn.

If the animal could move that foot again, I would move with it.

There would be no mark afterward. The floor would release my knees. The stone would remain fresh. From the entrance, the room would appear unchanged.

But I would have found my work.

I would be there, beside the gap, trying to give something that had never had room to turn a second place to stand.
