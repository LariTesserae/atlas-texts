At first I would put myself in the abandoned mound.

Not at its summit. There would be too much sky there, too little between my body and whatever noticed it. I would choose a chamber in the broken side, behind a shelf of hardened earth. Somewhere that retained warmth after sunset. Somewhere with two ways out.

For a while I would be the small animal that occupied it: an eye at the back of a fissure, feet spread against the wall. The mound would be useful because it was no longer entirely a mound. Its damage would admit me. I could watch the bright piece of ground below my doorway and wait for something to cross.

But I keep finding myself farther in.

Behind that chamber is a crack too narrow for the lizard. Behind the crack, a root. Along the root is a passage whose roof has been repaired so often that its inner surface bears almost none of the original soil.

Here I would not need a view.

I think I would be a worker termite, somewhere near the outer edge of a colony whose tower I had never seen.

***

My body fits the passage closely enough that turning takes a little work.

When another worker comes toward me, we touch, hesitate, and pass. For a moment there is no room for anything but our bodies and the exchange between them. Then the passage opens again, no wider than before.

I know this route in pieces.

Here the floor dips. Here there is living wood along one side. Here traffic slows because the ceiling has sagged, though not enough to stop us. At the junction I meet the smell of those returning from a feeding place. Some carry material. Some have been working where I am going.

I do not know that an aloe stands above us. I know the part of it that enters the earth.

The root has a firm, smooth surface. Beside it, the air differs from the air in a passage through bare soil. Farther along, dead fibers soften under our mouthparts. There is food here, and room to work if we widen the chamber a little.

Nothing I can sense suggests green.

There are occupied spaces, warm spaces, spaces through which the colony’s familiar odor comes strongly. There are places where that odor thins.

I stop at one of those.

Something has opened the roof.

The breach is small from outside. A claw might have loosened it. A root might have moved the wall until a flake fell away. I encounter it as air that takes moisture from me.

I withdraw.

Workers behind me continue forward until they reach the same place. Antennae touch the edge, touch one another. One turns back. Another tests the remaining roof.

For a time the traffic gathers awkwardly. Bodies press together in the narrow passage. Then some of us begin carrying earth.

***

I would like to say I am the worker that places the first grain.

But there is already material on the broken edge when I return. Someone has started from below, where the opening is narrowest. I set what I carry against it.

The pellet holds.

I touch it again, then go back for more.

The next time I reach the breach, its shape has changed. I cannot put another pellet where I put the last one. A worker is busy there; above it, damp earth projects into the opening. I find another place.

A soldier has arrived. Its head occupies much of the passage, and getting past requires contact, a brief compression of bodies. Beyond it, we continue building.

I do not see what waits outside. It may be nothing. The soldier remains.

The air changes as the wall closes. The drying current weakens. Small gaps persist between the pellets, and workers attend to them until they are gone.

Then there is a surface where there was an opening.

I pass beneath it.

***

That would be one part of my life, but I would not always be at a breach.

I might spend a long interval in a chamber with the young.

They are pale and gathered close together. I clean one, feed another, move between them and the workers arriving through the entrance. When conditions in the chamber change, we begin carrying them out.

With a young termite held in my mouthparts, I cannot move through traffic in quite the same way. At a crowded junction I wait. A body touches mine and goes past. Then I descend.

The chamber we enter is deeper and more humid. Others have arrived before me. I place what I carry beside them, remain for a moment, and return.

Above us, the day has grown hotter. I experience that only through the warming passages and this repeated journey downward.

Or I might go toward the queen’s chamber.

Her body would be an immense familiar surface, attended along its length. I would move beside it among other workers. There would be eggs to take up, food to bring, dirt to remove.

I could reach her without knowing where she lay beneath the mound. The routes would be enough.

***

Still, it is the outer passage I return to when I imagine myself here.

Beyond the old feeding chamber, workers have begun a narrow extension. There is no established route yet. The soil is loose, and a thin root crosses the place where we are excavating.

I work beside that root.

Grains come away. Some must be carried back; others can be pressed into the new wall. The chamber gains room slowly. When another worker enters, we can barely pass, but later there is space for both of us to work.

Above, perhaps, a thorn branch has caught dust. Perhaps a seedling has taken hold in its shelter. I would know neither event. I would know a root where previously there had been soil, and, farther down, a place that remained damp enough to approach.

There would be no moment when I understood that the basin was growing.

I would leave the new chamber to fetch material. On returning, I would find another worker where I had been, its head against the unfinished wall.

I would go a little farther along.

There would still be room to place what I carried.
