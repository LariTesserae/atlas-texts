I would be under the lowest stair, though not always under the same staircase.

There would be a place worn smooth by my back: the underside of a wooden tread, a patch in the concrete, the hollow behind an iron riser. Anyone reaching in to retrieve a dropped coin would find dust, two or three bent pins, perhaps the coin. They would not find me. I would have moved when their knees touched the floor.

I think I would be small, but not small enough to fit comfortably in the spaces I used. Getting through would require patience. There would be a particular order in which I folded my limbs, and sometimes I would get it wrong and have to back out.

My coat would catch on screw heads. In winter it would be thick and gray; in summer, when the pipes cooled and the clouds came farther up the stairs, it would lie flat against me and show the darker skin underneath. I would have hands of a sort, good for holding the rim of a saucer. No thumbs. A great deal could be done by leaning my weight against something.

The water would matter.

Not because it was enchanted, or because it belonged to the people who left it. I would be thirsty. The open rock would be too dry, and the water inside pipes would be inaccessible except at faults. A saucer on a landing would be a shallow, dependable place to drink.

At first I would visit only after the lamps went out.

Later, in one building, I would learn that the woman in the ground-floor rooms set down the saucer and went directly to bed. There was no need to wait longer. I could hear her slippers stop beside the wall, then the small noise of the crock coming to rest.

She used a chipped saucer with blue flowers. She filled it from her kettle after the water had cooled.

Sometimes she forgot. Those nights I would go elsewhere, mildly offended.

The black grain would come from me, though I would have no notion of paying for the water.

Something would harden behind my teeth. After drinking, I would work it loose against the saucer’s edge. This would take a little while. On difficult nights I would brace both feet against the floor and pull with my mouth, making a sound like a nail being drawn out of wood.

Then it would be done.

I would feel better afterward. The little stone would remain in the saucer, and I would leave it there.

There would be no name for me that I knew. People upstairs might have several. Children would disagree about my shape. One would draw a long tail; another, who had actually seen me, would draw no tail at all but too many legs.

I would be interested in neither drawing. I would be interested in the warm space between the classroom radiator and the wall, which could be reached from the stairs when the hour was loose.

That would be how I moved through the city: not into its future, exactly, but through portions of buildings that had not quite finished being separate.

A stair in the northern district might share its underside with a stair near the laundry. Usually this would last only a few minutes. I would smell soap where there should have been coal dust, turn into a gap beside a joist, and come out behind a pile of empty baskets.

Getting home would be less certain.

I would know several places where I could wait. A broken ventilator. The foot of a linen cupboard. A recess behind a hot-water tank where a mouse had stored so much shredded paper that I could sleep without touching the cold floor.

I would not travel far merely because I could. There would be landings I disliked, doors whose movement frightened me, one passage with a smell of clean, wet stone that I would never enter.

My routes would look meandering on a child’s map. In practice they would be quite efficient.

***

The woman with the blue saucer would own a sewing machine.

During the day I would hear its treadle through the floor. The rhythm would stop whenever she had to thread a needle or find her scissors. She would speak to herself at these times, accusing people who had not visited for years of moving things.

Her name might be Sella. I would know it from the callers at her door.

She would make alterations for the neighbors: turning cuffs, letting out coats, sewing fresh fabric over worn shoulders. Bits of thread would accumulate beneath her chair. I would take them when the room was empty.

Not to push into the mountain. That would be the white bird’s business.

I would use them to line the place beneath the stair. Short wool threads would be best. Long cotton ones would wind around my feet, and I would spend a whole evening removing them.

Once Sella would see me.

She would be coming back from the laundry, carrying a bundle too large to let her close the outer door. I would be drinking earlier than usual because the building had been quiet all afternoon.

We would look at each other.

I would have water hanging from the hair beneath my mouth. She would have a clothespin caught on her sleeve.

“Well,” she would say.

I would go under the stair.

For several days she would put the saucer a little farther from its usual place, nearer her door. I would not approach it. Eventually she would put it back.

That would be the extent of our understanding for years.

***

Mara would know I was there.

She would crouch with difficulty at the foot of the stairs and inspect the marks beside the skirting board. I would remain just beyond the light, watching her chalk.

Her entry would probably be unhelpful to anyone hoping to discover my nature:

*Lower stair passage in use. Water provided. No obstruction.*

I would like the sound of her pruning shears knocking against the buckle of her bag. It would mean that she was leaving.

Once Iven would come with her. He would sit on the floor while she spoke to the caretaker.

I would be waiting to cross behind him.

Without looking around, he would shift his feet out of my way.

That would unsettle me. I would remain where I was until he had gone.

***

There would be young ones, though I would not know where they came from until I found them.

One winter, three would be crowded into my sleeping place. Their coats would still be sparse. They would smell of mineral dust and stale water, and they would have already eaten the softest threads.

I would try to push them out.

They would push back with surprising strength.

Eventually I would sleep nearer the entrance, where the draft was worse.

For a while the saucer would be emptied before I reached it. Then Sella would leave a second dish, larger and without flowers. The young ones would climb into this and emerge soaked. They would shake themselves against the riser, spattering it with dark spots.

I would teach them nothing deliberately. They would follow me, get stuck, make noises in places where silence was required.

One would leave by the laundry passage. One would disappear during an hour when all the pipes in the building sounded very far away.

The third would stay until spring. It would have a habit of sleeping with one foot on my face.

When it left, I would sleep badly for several nights.

***

Sella would grow slower.

The treadle would run for shorter stretches. More callers would bring things than take them away. I would find fewer threads, but occasionally an entire piece of good wool would fall beneath her chair.

Then, one evening, the saucer would remain empty.

I would go to the laundry. Coming back, I would find people in her rooms.

There would be unfamiliar shoes beside the door and the smell of tea made too strong. A woman would be crying without much sound. Someone would open cupboards. Someone else would tell them to leave those until morning.

For three evenings the lamps would remain lit.

I would not go into the apartment. The voices would be too close, the furniture moved. I would sleep beneath the stairs and wake whenever someone crossed the landing.

On the second night a visitor would notice the saucer.

“Was she feeding something?”

“Only water,” another would say.

They would fill it.

Afterward, the apartment would stand empty long enough for its smells to change. I would venture inside and find a pin beneath the sewing table. The table itself would be gone.

There would still be a short piece of blue wool caught around the foot of the radiator.

I would take that.

***

I would not be the thing that kept the rooms in place. If a corridor lengthened, I would retreat as the residents did. If a wall became soft, I would avoid leaning against it. I would have no remedy for a missing landing.

But I would know where water was usually left, and which doors struck the floor when opened, and where a tired body could lie without being disturbed.

Eventually someone new would move into Sella’s rooms.

They would put a shoe rack beside the stair, directly across my passage.

For several nights I would squeeze behind it. Then one of the shoes would fall. The next night another would fall, and the next morning the rack would be moved.

The new tenant would leave water in a shallow enamel bowl.

I would distrust it for a while. It would smell sharply of washing powder, and its rim would be too high to hold in the usual way.

But there would come a night when I was thirsty enough.

Above me, a tram would pass slowly through the district. The tenant would turn over in bed. Somewhere in the wall, a pipe would knock twice and fall quiet.

I would put my forefeet over the rim and drink.
