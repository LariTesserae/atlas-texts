At first I would look for somewhere that remained open.

Not a square. A square has too much sky above it, too many roots meeting beneath. Nor one of the little passages: I would hesitate at the mineral warmth of its threshold, sensing that the room being made beyond it was not necessarily room for me.

I would follow a fallen tower to its broken end. There, beneath a sheet of bark still attached along one edge, I would find a low space with light coming through three slits. The floor would be warm. Every few moments a fiber would tighten overhead and shake down a little pale dust.

I would settle where I could see the slits.

By afternoon there would be only two. The floor would have begun to tilt, and I would move farther under the bark. A thin ridge would rise behind me, following the pressure of my weight.

By evening I would have stopped being certain which surface I was resting on.

The three slits would still matter, though. Even after all three had closed.

***

My first recognizable part would be a crease that did not quite seal.

It would occur low in a wall, beneath a broad leaf whose stem had been caught during a graft. The rest of the join would harden smoothly. Along a handspan of it, the inner surfaces would remain moist and pale.

When the wall swelled, this small place would yield.

Nothing spectacular would happen. A chamber above it would acquire a little more room. A root under the street would bend rather than break. The sound of the wall setting at sunset would stop briefly at the crease, then continue on its other side.

I would feel that interval through my whole length.

For a while, that would be my whole length.

I would have no view of the street. Sunlight would arrive as a change in the thickness of the leaf above me. Rain would announce itself through the sudden weight of tissue drinking uphill. The deep evening movement would reach me first as a pull at one end, then as a pull at the other.

If both ends pulled together, I could remain.

If they pulled apart, I would have to grow.

***

I would grow along the places where surfaces were almost touching.

Behind a buttress, between two layers of paving, beneath the tightened rim of a cup: wherever there was room for a thin wet membrane, some part of me could extend. I would not keep all these places open. Most would close normally after I had passed through them.

But a few would retain a crease.

From outside, my body might look like a poor graft running through a district. A wall would have a slight give under pressure. A step would sink and recover. Somewhere high in a tower, a balcony would remain flexible after the balconies below it had set.

I would know them by their tensions. The balcony would pull continuously, impatient with its own weight. The step would give me short, blunt impressions whenever the roots beneath it exchanged positions. The wall would be harder to distinguish from myself.

There would be distances across which I could carry pressure but not fluid. Others would be so short that opening a seam in one street wet the paving in another.

I would lose parts by misunderstanding this.

A membrane sent toward what seemed a neighboring chamber might grow for an entire day without reaching it. I would feel its tip drying, keep feeding it, and finally have to close the narrow throat behind it. Elsewhere, a root I had barely touched would return through my underside already thickened, taking more room than I could provide.

For several nights I would ache around such arrivals.

***

The keeper of cool chambers would be my nearest neighbor.

At noon its contraction would flatten my lower reaches. Moisture would retreat through the red throats beneath me, leaving my creases tacky and close to sealing. I would withdraw from the hot wall and gather under the street.

At sunset, its release would fill me again.

We would develop an uneven border. Some days I could enter the outer layers of its chambers. On others it would thicken those layers before I arrived. I would learn to leave a little room around the throats. It would grow one of them through the widest of my folds.

During a hard evening, when the district set too quickly, that throat would remain open after the others closed. Water would come through it in small pulses. I would spread the water upward, wetting a line of brittle tissue beneath the roofs.

A fracture would travel toward us.

It would enter my upper fold, split it, and stop.

For the rest of the night I would be shorter. The balcony I had held would spring upward and harden beyond my reach. Below, the cool chamber would continue giving water through the throat.

I would have no way to tell it that the place receiving the water was gone.

***

Eventually I would reach a pool.

I would approach under the paving, spread thin, and put only one edge into its rim. There I would acquire my first sight.

It would be a narrow sight, looking upward through dark water. I would see the underside of a frond. Its veins would tremble before anything in the tissue above me moved.

Then I would see a pale seam opening across that underside.

I would draw back. Several breaths later, the frond would split.

For days I would remain just outside the rim, extending and retracting a fringe too fine to be visible. The water would show me folds I had not yet made. Some fitted comfortably into my body. Others opened through places that were already hard and could no longer yield.

One afternoon I would see my earliest crease sealed shut.

I would leave the pool before the reflection finished.

Still, when sunset came, I would keep that crease wet. Water would be scarce elsewhere in me. A step would harden; a low passage would close. I would hold fluid in the old fold until the pressure from below had passed.

In the morning it would remain open.

I would return to the pool, though less of me would go near it each time.

***

My young would begin as places I could no longer feel clearly.

One beneath a roof would answer my contraction late. Another would stay full after I had emptied. I would try to draw them back into rhythm. For a little while they would comply.

Then the roof would lift, stretching the connecting tissue into a white cord.

Through it I would feel the small body above me choosing to stay wet.

I would release my end. The cord would seal.

For some time afterward, I would grow toward that roof. Each attempt would find a new layer between us. Eventually I would stop, though one fold in my upper surface would continue opening whenever rain crossed that part of the district.

***

I would be found most easily before sunset.

There would be a lane with one wall already rigid and the other still shining. Halfway along it, a leaf would tremble without opening. Beneath the leaf, a seam would darken.

The lane would begin to close.

I would have little strength against it. The deep bodies would be moving; the cool chambers would be emptying; every shared fiber would carry more pressure than I could turn aside. My lower reaches would flatten. Several old folds would vanish at once.

But beneath that trembling leaf, I would still have room.

I would draw moisture there from the last of my soft tissue. The seam would widen, scarcely more than a crack. Warm air would pass through it. On the other side, another wall would be swelling toward morning.

I would extend into it slowly, leaving the old crease behind me until the new one could bear the pull.
