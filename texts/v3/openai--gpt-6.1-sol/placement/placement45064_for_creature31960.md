I would begin under the gantry, where a plate has been taken off and set down without being carried away.

One of my feet would still be on its inner surface. The others would have found the machine again: a bolt head, a groove, the narrow space behind a hinge. Between those attachments my body would hang in a shape it could not maintain for long. Fluid would gather in the lowest fold. The feeding ribbon would remain closed.

There would be no reason, yet, to release the plate.

Its vibration would differ from the casing’s. Something would touch it at intervals, and each touch would arrive cleanly through the little organ beneath my foot. The casing would carry the much larger movement of the pump. Across my body these two accounts would meet, stretching tissues that had previously been almost unstressed.

I would not know that someone was waiting.

A glove would rest on the plate. Lift away. Return.

Eventually the plate would be placed against the basalt foundation. That would change what came through it. Beneath the brief contacts and the thin ringing of metal, I would find a deep, crowded vibration arriving by a route I had not felt before.

A free limb would extend.

Its end would pass over paint, over the edge of the plate, into a seam too narrow for the hand that had set it down. The foot would open there. It would take several attempts to seal.

Only then would I let go.

***

For a long time I would live within reach of that place.

The scale of the station would come to me as a series of surfaces, not a building. Some would transmit the pump strongly enough that I could scarcely distinguish anything else. Some would have a looseness that altered when touched. Others would be quiet enough, within the roar, to reveal the small bodies working behind them.

I would feed in a crack under the foundation.

My ribbon would pass the crowded entrance and extend farther in, where the organisms had softer coverings. At first it would reach only a short distance. Later there would be an irregularity deep in the crack where its hooks caught unless I folded one edge inward. I would learn that turn through repeated withdrawals, each leaving a small injury.

Above me, the machine would continue.

Things would arrive that did not belong to its regular movement. A succession of heavy contacts through the gantry. A wheel striking a joint in the deck. Something dragged across the casing, stopped, dragged again.

Once, a loose length of hose would touch my body.

I would pull inward, lifting three feet together. The hose would remain. Water moving around it would disturb the free ends of my limbs.

For several days my feeding ribbon would emerge on the other side.

Then the hose would be gone.

***

Growth would not feel like becoming large.

An attachment that had been easy would begin to require a different angle. Two limbs would crowd each other in a recess. My body, formerly sheltered behind a rib in the housing, would project beyond it into the intake’s pull.

I would compensate for a while.

One foot higher. Another behind the corner. A division of a limb extended to a new bolt.

The feeding crack would remain accessible, but the current would press against more of me. Maintaining the old position would require fluid to be moved continually among the supporting limbs. Even while feeding, I would have to make corrections.

One day I would reach past the foundation and attach to the mountain.

The new foothold would carry the station less directly. Through it would come small disturbances that disappeared beneath the pump when my feet were all on metal. A grazer shifting against a shell bed. Something releasing from a ledge. A fracture transmitting a faint movement from below.

I would keep that foot there.

The rest of me would follow slowly enough that no single passage along the gantry would reveal the change. First my body would be beneath the foundation rather than beside it. Then a long limb would lie over the basalt shoulder. Then the feeding ribbon would find a different fissure.

The plate would remain near the service door.

By then I would no longer fit beneath it.

***

I would be a stay on the station’s western shoulder, with one shortened limb and a foot that could not close completely.

The damage would have happened during an ordinary adjustment. I would have sealed against a thin edge of basalt, then transferred too much of my support to it. The edge would break.

For a moment the foot would remain attached to the fragment while the rest of me moved away.

Other limbs would tighten. One would slide before finding purchase. The feeding ribbon would be pulled out of its fissure with several hooks bent backward.

Afterward, a small piece of mountain would remain enclosed in the injured foot.

I would carry it without having any use for it. Its weight would alter the limb’s position when I was free of the rock. New tissue would grow around it, and the membrane would seal poorly on smooth surfaces. I would find places with a shallow hollow and put it there.

There would be many such places.

Over the years, my route around the shoulder would become a sequence of them.

***

When the pump stopped, I would press down so hard that the injured foot opened along an old seam.

The change would reach different parts of me by different paths. The limb attached to the foundation would feel it first. Then the feet on the shoulder, through which the machine’s vibration had arrived after passing through stone. Between these contacts my own tissues would briefly carry strains they had never carried together.

I would flatten.

My free limbs would settle. The feeding ribbon would withdraw with a cluster of animals still enclosed at its tip.

Something would cross the gantry quickly. More contacts would follow. The casing would be struck, opened, struck again.

I would remain where I was.

The other pumps would continue to arrive through the range. Their vibration would occupy the place where this machine had been, but differently at every foot. I would keep adjusting the pressure of the little organs against their surfaces.

No arrangement would restore what had gone.

In time I would feed again.

The crack would still contain food. The water would still pass over the shoulder, although the small current near the intake would have weakened. Animals would begin to occupy spaces where they had previously been pulled loose.

I would move one foot from the foundation to the rock.

Later, another.

***

I would leave from the highest part of the shoulder.

There would be a ridge across the basin, faintly available to my eyes as an interruption in the light. I would have touched nothing there. Its cracks, its sheltered faces, the quality of the vibration within it would all be unknown.

I would release five feet, then two more.

The damaged foot would remain in a hollow. Around the enclosed chip, its membrane would loosen slowly.

With only that contact left, much of my account of the mountain would have to pass through injured tissue. Movements that seven feet had separated would arrive crowded together. I would hold while the current lifted my body slightly and tightened the limb.

Then the foot would open.

For the crossing I would gather myself into a long bundle. The damaged limb would hang lower than the others. Occasionally I would spread one of the sound feet to correct the turn it caused.

The roar would remain in the water, but it would no longer come to me through fixed points. My body would bend in it. Far below, a hunter would release from a cliff, unseen.

I would take most of the day to reach the ridge.

The first foot to extend would be an undamaged one. Its membrane would meet a sloping face, fail to seal, and move a short distance downward.

There it would hold.

Through the hard organ at its center would come the working pumps, the basin, the faint disturbance of something feeding farther along the same rock. My suspended body would turn slightly around the new attachment.

I would extend another limb.

Eventually I would find a hollow for the foot containing the basalt chip. I would lower my body between the attachments and unfold the feeding ribbon into a seam.

For a while, it would find nothing.

Then its tip would touch something soft, deep inside the mountain.
