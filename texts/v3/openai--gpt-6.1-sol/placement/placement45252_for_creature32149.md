At first I put myself in one of the high rooms, with a shutter opening toward the plateau. There would be a table beneath the window, and I would know which distant patch was a herd and which was a field.

But I don’t think I would stay there. The room is too far from the places where people have to stop. By the time someone reached me, they would have forgotten half of what they meant to ask, or become so annoyed by the climb that they would insist they had asked something else.

I find myself farther down, beside a covered cistern, in a room that used to hold harness.

There are still wooden pegs in the wall. The landlord will not let me remove them, because he expects that someday it will hold harness again. I hang my coat on one and a bag of writing things on another. Two are too high to use without standing on the stool. When people notice this, they suggest shelves.

Nobody offers the wood.

The room has a window, but its best light comes through the door in the afternoon. I keep the door open when the weather allows. A strip of wool nailed along its lower edge drags over the sill and has to be lifted with my foot when I close it. This is one of several repairs I keep meaning to make.

I earn most of my living by writing things down.

Not laws, usually. Lists. An agreement concerning three jars of seed. The names of animals sent to a distant basin and the names of those that came back. A letter to a son who has been gone long enough that his mother no longer knows whether to begin with affection or complaint.

She dictates both. I read them back. She removes the affection, then asks me to put it in again.

I know the marks used for water shares in this quarter, and some of those used across the ridge. The difference has brought me work and got me into trouble. Once I copied a mark as I had learned it at home, and gave a household an afternoon it did not own. The mistake was caught before the thaw. Still, for two seasons a man greeted me by asking whether I had any afternoons for sale.

I am not the water keeper. I sit two steps below her during disputes and record what is agreed.

Sometimes she asks me to read an older agreement aloud. Sometimes she tells me not to read the part I have just begun, because everyone remembers that part and it is the next line they are arguing about.

She has a better memory than I do. Mine depends on folded sheets, scratches on boards, and knowing which bundle is tied with which cord. I dislike lending a document without its wrapping. Other people dislike being made to wait while I find the proper wrapping. We manage.

For a while, this seems enough: a person with a stool and a useful familiarity with names.

Then I begin to notice how often I leave the stool.

An old man comes to ask whether the room above his daughter’s kitchen is still included in a promise made before she married. He describes the entrance poorly. I have to go and see it. A woman needs a letter read to her brother, who cannot get down the steps. I take the letter uphill. Two neighbors disagree over a channel that passes beneath a threshold, and neither will accept a drawing made by the other.

I kneel at the threshold with a bit of string.

The stone is damp enough to darken my knees. One of the neighbors holds the lamp. The other tells her to hold it lower. They have apparently been disagreeing about lamps for years.

I could pretend that I enjoy these visits because they teach me the country. Sometimes they do. More often, I wish people could explain a doorway without requiring me to crawl through it. But by the time I return to the cistern room, I know what I am writing down.

That matters to me. It matters even when nobody notices the difference.

I would have a carrying frame, though not a good one. The upper crosspiece presses through my coat. A harness maker has offered to alter it in exchange for writing to his cousin; we are each waiting for the other to begin.

On days when there is little work, I take small loads between households. Nothing that requires great strength: a pot with a lid that must be held level, clean wool, a bundle of repaired shoes. People assume someone who works with papers has no reason to be going their way. Often I do not. I go anyway, for the payment or because I have been sitting too long.

I learn to recognize the landings where a load can be set down without inconveniencing anyone. One has a view over several roofs and a scrap of basin beyond them. In late afternoon the animals out there cast shadows long enough to distinguish. Nearer by, a woman shakes bedding from a window. I wait until she has finished before picking up the shoes.

There would be people who find me tiresome.

I ask for names when they think the names are obvious. I want to know whether a promise concerns the room, the household occupying it, or whoever next marries into that household. At a feast, someone imitates me asking a bridegroom whether his vows include use of the storage passage. It is a good imitation. I laugh, and hear the story repeated for months.

There would also be people who trust me more than they should.

A letter arrives from elsewhere, and I am asked what the writer really means. I can say that the handwriting is hurried, that a sentence has been crossed out, that the address may have changed. I cannot say whether someone is happy simply because they have written that they are well.

I would learn to be firm about this. Not immediately.

Once, wanting to comfort a woman, I would tell her that her daughter sounded content. Later I would read a second letter and realize how little the first had said. After that, when people asked me to find assurances between the lines, I would read the lines again.

In winter I move my table away from the window. There is a warm pipe from the room above, where the landlord’s sister cooks. She knows when I have visitors because their talk rises through a gap beside it. Sometimes a bowl comes down. Sometimes a request comes first.

I mend my own cuffs badly. I have one blue lining, bought secondhand, and its previous owner’s name is still sewn into it. I mean to remove that too.

Years pass. The cistern takes longer to fill.

At first the change produces more work. Agreements are examined. Shares are lent for a season and not returned. Someone says an old measure was larger than the present one, and brings a vessel down from an attic to prove it. I write until my fingers stiffen.

Later there is less work, because there are fewer people.

I carry a letter to a house where the roof is being taken apart. The woman who lives there asks me to read it before the men remove the last beam. We stand in the courtyard. Her nephew is doing well. He wants her to come before winter. She makes me read his description of the room twice.

When my own room must be left, I discover how much I have accumulated beneath the table.

Some papers belong to families already gone. Some concern debts paid years ago. There are practice marks made by children, a recipe I took down for someone who never returned, and an angry letter its writer decided not to send. I cannot carry everything. For several evenings people come to claim what is theirs. A few stay to help me sort.

The landlord takes his pegs back.

I leave him the table, which will not fit through the passage to my new room without being dismantled. Across the ridge I have been offered a bench beside a warmer wall. It belongs to a woman who says there will be plenty of room, provided I do not spread things out.

My first customer there is a child carrying a water jar.

She has been sent to find out which outlet her household may use. I recognize the family name, but not the mark she describes. We go down together to look.

At the turning she puts the jar against her hip and steps aside for someone coming uphill with a shutter across his back. I follow her into the doorway. There is just enough space for both of us, if I hold my bag in front of me.

I think that is where I would be.
