At first I would look for myself in one of the combs.

Not in its teeth, which have become too dry to change, but at the place where the veins were gathered and pressed into a handle. There might still be a narrow passage there. Something passing through it might be divided into several smaller arrivals, each slightly different from the others.

That would seem a plausible body for me: a place where what arrives together is separated.

But the comb is loose on its shelf. Nothing feeds it. When the shelf contracts, it shifts a little, and the movement belongs to the shelf’s inhabitant. Whatever distinctions the comb once made are now differences in a dead tool.

I would have to look underneath.

There, perhaps, in the membrane that receives the weight of the comb and has learned not to close the channel beneath it. A very modest life. It knows one load, one route, the occasional immense interruption. It keeps a small space open.

Yet I think I would be farther along, where the shelf enters the wall.

There are two attachments at that corner. One was made when the chamber was folded. The other grew afterward, as the roof sank. Between them is a seam that was never quite pressed shut. Oil enters it from above and leaves beneath the avenue, although nothing in the workmanship suggests that this was the intended route.

The seam has been used long enough to acquire a lining.

I would be in that lining.

Not all of it. The upper portion belongs to someone who attends to the roof. The lower portion is ordinary plant tissue and would close if permitted. My body would occupy the intervening length, with a small extension beneath the shelf and another following the route down to the paving.

From inside, this would not feel like being elongated. The distance between my parts would be measured by what could happen on the way from one to another.

A release beneath the shelf might arrive below changed by the cold. Sometimes it would not arrive at all. I would tighten, wait, and eventually receive a fraction of something I had already ceased to expect.

This would be enough to keep the parts associated.

My earliest knowledge would be of obstruction.

Before I had become distinct from the wall, a thickening had almost closed the seam. Oil gathered above it. The tissue below grew dry. A variation in pressure caused a few fibers to yield sideways instead of inward, leaving a route so narrow that the movement through it could not be distinguished from stillness.

Those fibers were sustained.

The others hardened.

I would have begun as the surviving difference between them.

For a long time, keeping the passage open would be everything I did. When the wall pressed, I would pull along the shelf. When the shelf settled, I would release below it. The oil would continue downward, and some of it would remain with me.

Eventually other things would enter.

A bitter fraction from the closed chamber nearby. A pressure sequence from a junction farther up the shell. The thin residue of nourishment altered by someone whose routes had since been absorbed.

I would respond to these differently. There would be no reason at first, beyond their different effects on my tissue. One would let me remain yielding for longer. Another would make a portion of the seam contract. A third would pass through almost unchanged.

After enough passages, my body would contain accommodations for arrivals that no longer came.

One pocket would be kept soft. One branch would remain difficult to enter. Somewhere beneath the shelf, a few fibers would tighten in advance of a pressure release that had once followed a particular bitterness.

The bitterness might arrive centuries after the release had ceased.

I would tighten anyway.

This is where I think I would become more than a channel. My maintenance would begin to interfere with transport. I would hold something because of what had once accompanied it. I would open a route for an arrival too small to pay for the opening.

There would be costs.

The roof inhabitant would receive less. It would draw its attachment closer to the wall, pressing against my upper tissues. I would contract beneath the shelf. For an interval, neither of us would pass anything downward.

We would know one another chiefly through this difficulty.

Later, perhaps, we would work out a sequence. It would tighten first, leaving me room below. I would release afterward, allowing its attachment to recover. Neither would have access to the whole arrangement. The seam would carry oil again.

A place elsewhere in my body would preserve the pressure of that first disagreement. Even after the sequence became reliable, an unusually abrupt contraction above would make me hold back.

I would not be especially wise.

Across the hollow, one of the towers would receive the sound of our seam. When the roof shifted, its returning vibration would contain a faint rasp that had not been there before. The tower might alter a stitch in answer.

I could not hear the answer as the tower heard it. It would reach me through the shelf, mixed with the chamber’s other movements. But a particular long strain would begin to arrive with a small easing at its end.

I would come to wait for that easing.

Then the tower’s shaft would deepen as the shell changed. Its note would lower. The easing would disappear into another vibration.

I would not know what had happened. For several root movements, I would spend a little of my reserve preparing for the old return.

Somewhere in my lining, a branch would slowly close.

I would have no tendril at first.

Much later, a surplus might reach the seam. Perhaps a blocked channel would reopen above me, releasing oil that had remained enclosed since the comb was made. The first portion would be thick with altered constituents. The roof inhabitant would take some. I would take some. More would follow.

Beneath the shelf, a fold of mine would swell into the chamber.

It would encounter the underside of the comb.

The contact would be unlike the shelf’s weight. It would be local, uneven, capable of changing when I changed. I would push against one tooth and receive pressure through the others. A thing outside my settled body would answer my movement.

For years I might do no more than hold it.

Then the comb would slip.

One end would fall into the seam and wedge there. The route I had kept open would narrow abruptly.

I would have to spend much of the surplus drawing the fold back. The roof inhabitant would tighten. Oil would accumulate above us. Eventually the comb would move just enough to free the passage.

It would remain crooked beneath the shelf.

My first act with an extended body would have nearly undone my life’s work.

Nevertheless, the fold would not entirely withdraw.

A small part would grow past the comb, toward the hollow. There would be no surface ahead of it. Its tip would dry, and I would replace the tip. The new portion would reach a little farther.

The root’s next movement would bend it.

The strain would arrive without the familiar compression of another tissue against mine. I would respond as though an attachment had failed. I would pull back, then relax, then pull again.

Nothing would pull in return.

Below the avenue, the advancing pale sheet would reach my lower passage.

The young inhabitant within it would follow the trace of oil toward me. Its growth would press upward, threatening the seam from a direction my old arrangements had not accommodated.

I would harden.

It would turn aside.

At the next movement of the root, the hardened part of me would fail to yield. A minute tear would open beside it. Oil would enter the tear and reach the young growth.

Some of my reserve would be lost.

The younger inhabitant would stiffen around that place. Its new tissue would hold the paving away from my lower channel. What had first been an obstruction would become, for a while, support.

I would release another small quantity there.

Perhaps I would be preserving my route. Perhaps the earlier loss had left a disposition that now repeated itself. The younger one would receive the release without knowing the difference.

Above us, my extension would remain in the hollow.

It would not reach the center. It would not discover a use for emptiness. Its farthest tissue would consume more than it returned, and during a cold interval I would withdraw from it.

A stiff thread would remain.

I would still be beneath the shelf, in the imperfect seam, holding oil long enough to alter it and sometimes too long to pass it on. Part of my body would depend on an old neighbor’s restraint. Another part would rest against growth that might eventually absorb it.

When the root settled, the thread would tremble.

The pull would reach the living fold at its base. For a little while, I would release nourishment into that fold, although there was no longer anything at the far end capable of receiving it.

Then the pressure would subside.

Below, something young would draw the oil onward.
