At first I would keep my shoulder beneath the new sheet.

Not touching it. Close enough that, when the light entered the last vein, I could see the green reflected on my skin. The drop would hang beyond my reach. The animal would be there, working the folded leaf out of the water, and I would avoid moving because the sheet was still soft and I did not know what my shadow might do to it.

I would think I was waiting for the animal to finish.

Then the floor would change.

The pressure behind my ears would come and go, but my feet would not quite accept the new direction. One would remain planted on the old floor. The other would lift toward the wall. For a little while I would stand awkwardly between them, supported by two surfaces that had ceased to agree.

There would be no pain in this. Only an unfamiliar tension along my side, as though a seam in my clothing had been caught on something.

I would put a hand there.

My clothes would be gone, although I would not remember taking them off. Under my fingers, the skin would be smooth and warm, with a shallow ridge extending from beneath my arm to my hip. The ridge would yield when pressed. When I took my hand away, it would open a little.

Nothing would come out.

A coolness would enter.

I would spend a long time examining that opening. In the ordinary light of the chamber, it would be possible to mistake it for a fold made by the way I was standing. But when I straightened, it would remain. When I lay down, it would become easier to see.

Inside would be a paler surface.

I would have no reason yet to call it anything but myself.

***

I would stop trying to reach the entrance.

Not because it had vanished. Occasionally it would show itself, and I would recognize the darkness tucked beneath the flap. But each time I walked toward it, the ridge along my side would tighten. After several steps I would need to sit.

Near the central stalk, the tension would ease.

There I would discover that I could hear the chamber differently if I rested my open side against the floor. The great wooden reports would lose their distance. A crack overhead would arrive as a small pull through my ribs; the high whistle would gather along the edge of the opening and remain there after the sound had stopped.

I would begin sleeping in that position.

On waking, I would find impressions in the floor where my hands had been. No deeper than before, but slower to fade. Moisture would collect in them. Once, during the sweat, a drop would turn aside from the stalk and settle into the print of my palm.

Its folded leaf would stay there after the water was gone.

I would try to pick it up. It would resist, not firmly, but with the slight adhesion of a wet seed. By the next dark interval, the floor would have closed over it.

I would leave my hand beside that place for a while.

***

I would still have hands. That would matter to me longer than I expected.

I would examine them whenever a bright current passed nearby. The nails would look familiar. The lines across my palms would be clear. But the joints would begin to bend without the small creases I knew, and light would linger between the bones.

Eventually I would find that I could put one hand inside the opening along my side.

There would be room for it.

Farther in, room for the other.

I would not feel organs or encounter anything that withdrew from my touch. There would be a dry, faintly ribbed surface, warm enough to rest against. I would touch it carefully, then more firmly, and feel the pressure somewhere near my back.

When I removed my hands, they would carry the scent of a snapped bean.

After that I would be less certain where to sit. My body would keep a shape when I moved, but it would no longer seem to end at the visible skin. Leaning against the stalk, I would sometimes feel a joint of it beneath my palm several moments before my fingers arrived.

I would learn to move slowly.

***

The animal would find me during a dim interval.

I would feel its feet first: six cool points beside the opening, each settling separately. I would wait for the thorn. Instead, two of the points would lift, and something soft would be placed against the ridge.

The leaf.

It would be more open now. One small lobe would lie flat; another would remain curled around a pocket of water. The animal would hold it there until the ridge relaxed.

Then it would push.

I would feel the leaf enter me, though not as a foreign thing. Its edge would pass over the inner surface with a faint dragging sensation. The pocket of water would break. Warmth would spread a little way along my back.

The animal would wait.

I would not know how to help it. I could only remain still while the opening widened, a fraction at a time, around the leaf.

At last its front feet would let go.

***

I think I would live near the joint where the animal sheds its skin.

I would be easy to overlook there: a loose pale fold beside the stalk, unattached except at one narrow place. During the loud intervals I would draw inward. During the brief night I would open.

Inside me, the leaf would continue growing.

At first it would touch both sides. Later it would not. Light would enter its veins and spread from them into the surfaces around it. A fine ridge would lift. Two layers would part.

There would be space enough for a hand, though I would no longer have one to put there.

The animal would visit without scraping. Sometimes it would rest its thorn across my opening while it drank from a nearby groove. I would hold the thorn level. When the animal returned, I would loosen.

Once it would leave the thorn too long. A white tip would emerge from the reddish base, turn toward my interior, and press against the new floor.

I would open farther to make room.

That is where I would find myself: beside the stalk, and also some distance inside the pale fold, where the first root had not yet reached the opposite wall.

Outside, four transparent feet would hold the edge steady.

Inside, the leaf would begin to move in air.
