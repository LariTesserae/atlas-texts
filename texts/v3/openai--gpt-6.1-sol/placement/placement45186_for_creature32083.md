I would be near the seventh doorway, where the light looks a little too much like the light outside.

Not in the doorway itself. Things pass through there quickly, and some arrive with a momentum acquired somewhere else. I would occupy the shallow turn just before it: a place where the floor lifts toward the wall, though not enough to be called a seat.

At first I might be the dark line beneath that lift.

A beetle would find me before you did. It would enter the line, travel a short distance, and emerge from a fold above your ankle. If you crouched to follow it, you would find several such folds, none quite parallel. Between them, a surface warm enough to be skin.

I would draw my foot back to let you see.

That would be my first mistake. You would then expect the rest of me to have the ordinary relation to a foot.

There would be a knee, certainly. A hand somewhere higher. But I would have rested in the turn for a long time, and the turn would have continued growing. One shoulder would lie farther along the wall than the other. To look at you, I would first have to loosen a strip of myself from beneath the threshold.

Small animals would object. I would wait for them to move.

By the time my face appeared, you might have stopped crouching.

***

I would not live there because it was sheltered. The recess would be dry, and the seventh doorway unreliable. Occasionally wind would come through it carrying pollen from a plant that had not flowered in the plain for many seasons.

I would live there because this was where people hesitated.

Most would need only a moment. A hand against the wall, a shift of weight, then onward. Their uncertainty would reach me through the paving before their bodies came into view.

Others would stand for longer.

A young one might arrive carrying water, unable to remember whether the crack it meant to tend was through the doorway or behind it. An old one might recognize the passage but not the chamber beyond. Once, someone would come back with wet feet and a dry upper body, trailing the smell of rain, and refuse to take another step.

I would unfold as far as necessary.

Sometimes all I would offer was a hand laid flat on the dependable part of the floor.

Sometimes I would cross first.

There would be no special authority in this. I could be wrong. The doorway might admit me to the plain and the next creature to a room. But I would have learned certain warnings: a coldness under the threshold at noon; beetles turning back without entering; the wall answering a touch before the touch had quite been made.

On those days I would remain across the entrance until someone else came to help.

***

My body would become suited to this work unevenly.

The foot nearest the passage would spread, its underside sensitive to the traffic in the roots. The other would remain narrow enough to climb with. One palm would grow soft from resting against travelers’ ribs while they listened. The other, used on the threshold, would become hard and ridged.

I would have difficulty standing straight.

When I crossed the market, the young would imitate my first few steps. I would let them, unless they did it beside the basins, where laughing with a mouthful of water wasted too much.

There would be a little animal living under my left arm that disliked motion. Whenever I left the recess, it would bite me.

I would carry it anyway.

Its call would be audible through several thicknesses of bark. On days when the doorway led somewhere unfamiliar, I could hear it calling from my own body after the rest of the district’s noise had fallen away. I would turn toward that sound. Usually I would find the way back before it stopped.

***

Once, I would fail to return by evening.

The passage would be short when I entered. Beyond it would lie a chamber with a low roof and damp walls, full of empty recesses. I would go in because something was tapping.

Not loudly. Not in a sequence I knew.

I would touch each wall and receive the tapping through all of them.

While I searched, the doorway would withdraw into a long gallery. My companion under my arm would fall silent. I would strike the plate of my chest, gently at first, then hard enough to hurt.

Nothing.

For the first time in many years, I would hear my own feet without hearing anything around them.

I would sit down.

There would be moisture in a seam beside me. I would rub it onto the plate, though I would not know whether this could help. Then I would put my hand against the wall and give the simplest sequence I knew: two taps, a pause, one more.

The distant tapping would continue.

I would repeat myself for a long time before noticing that it continued only while my hand was lifted.

When I kept my palm against the bark, it stopped.

I would remain like that until a small mouth opened beside my wrist.

***

Whatever I brought home would not be readily visible.

For several days the inhabitants would groom me more often than usual. They would separate my folds, examine the thin membranes, smell my hands. The animal under my arm would resume calling, but at a pitch that made nearby frogs turn their heads.

I would return to my recess.

After that, when someone hesitated at the seventh doorway, I would occasionally feel a second hesitation farther inside the wall.

I would wait for both.

There would be journeys I could no longer make alone. I would have to stop midway, sit down, and let a pale projection emerge from beneath my ribs. It would touch the floor, test it, then withdraw. At first I would be impatient with this. Later I would learn to begin earlier.

The young would still imitate my walk, but now their imitation would include the pause.

***

I think I would grow old there without filling the recess completely.

There would always be room beside me for someone to sit. Keeping it open would require small efforts: lifting my elbow while the wall thickened, moving a root with my heel, waking to pull back skin that had begun to adhere across the hollow.

Sometimes I would leave for an entire season. I would go to the roofs for dew, or lie beside the central square and feel the chewing through my skull. Returning, I would find the hollow occupied by somebody else.

I would wait nearby.

When they rose, I would settle in again, slowly accommodating the changes they had left.

And if you came through the galleries uncertain which brightness was the outside, you might find me there with one hand on the floor and the other tucked beneath my ribs.

I would make room.

Before pointing out a path, I would wait until I could feel your feet through the leaf.
