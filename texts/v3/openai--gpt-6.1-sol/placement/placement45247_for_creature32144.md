At first I would be found in the damp line where a clear animal has set down the hard things it no longer needs.

Not the animal itself. Not yet.

A swimmer’s jaw lies there, open against the glass. Beside it are three grains of sulfur, a bent needle, and a mineral scale with one dark corner. Water passes through the jaw and leaves by a different route. Once, a grazer puts a foot into that little current and withdraws it with an extra joint. It stands for a while, lifting and lowering the altered limb. Then it goes on.

I would be in the place its foot had touched.

There is hardly anything there to distinguish me from wetness. When the floor warms, most of the water goes away. A little remains beneath the jaw, held between its teeth. I remain with it.

For some time, this would be enough: to be a small persistence beneath something discarded.

But the jaw is not fixed. A drop strikes its hinge and turns it over. The shelter becomes a blade. Water pulls away from me on both sides, and for the first time I gather.

It is an awkward movement. One margin comes inward; another fails to follow. I leave some of myself on the stone.

A grazer finds that part and eats it.

I do not feel the feeding where it happens. I feel a slight loosening elsewhere, as though a foot I had not known about has lost its hold.

What remains of me thickens beneath the overturned jaw.

That is where I begin.

***

I would belong to the clear inhabitants, though at first the others might not recognize me as one of them. My underside would have only two blind tubes, close together, so that every attempt to crawl turns me in a slow circle. The jaw would be too large for my folds. I would carry it sideways, one tooth always dragging.

My first journeys would leave a fine scratch.

There would be food in the scratch by morning.

I would return along it, drawing up the translucent skin, and discover that the line had grown longer while I was away. Another animal would have crossed it carrying a sharp fragment. Our marks would meet beneath a ledge, where the light could not reach.

There I would encounter my first shadow.

It would already be waiting. Small, many-footed, folded into a space too shallow for the creature that ought to cast it.

I would pass over it.

Nothing would happen immediately. Then my two tubes would fill in a different order. Instead of turning, I would move straight ahead.

The shadow would remain inside me for three days.

When it left, I would turn in circles again.

***

I do not think I would become the largest of the clear animals, or the one carrying the branching darkness. I would live farther toward the entrance, under a low lip of black glass where the weather reaches the fissure before the inhabitants understand that anything has changed.

The light there would be strong enough to reveal everything in me.

A pale jaw. A white limb-tip. A curved edge of obsidian, acquired at considerable cost. Fine dust moving through the places where my body is deeper than it looks.

Visitors would pause at my margin.

Some would enter. Some would walk around.

I would learn to keep my mouth on the side nearest the hot stone and my loosest folds beside the water. This would not make me harmless. A swimmer turning among my grit might strike the jaw. A hungry fold might close before the rest of me had begun to move.

Afterward I would carry one more hard thing.

There would be no way to put it down without putting it down somewhere.

***

What would occupy me most would be the beads that forget to fall.

Near my ledge, water sometimes stops on a sloping face of glass. The bead trembles there, clear and almost perfectly round. Inside it, swimmers continue to turn. Their tails strike its surface without breaking through.

Other animals go around these beads.

I would approach from below.

At first I would touch them with my mouth, and this would be a mistake. The water would run all at once. Swimmers, grit, and part of my assembled edge would be carried into a crack I could not enter.

Eventually I would learn to spread before touching.

I would lay a thin margin against the slope, below the waiting water, and extend one tube until it reached the bead. When the bead remembered its weight, it would run over me.

For a moment I would have more body than I could hold.

Then the current would pass. The swimmers would leave through my lower folds. I would gather what remained.

Sometimes that would include unfamiliar dust.

Sometimes a shadow would stay.

***

I would not know what to do with every shadow.

One might cause my margins to lift whenever rain touched the roof. Another might keep a section of me pressed against a dry wall long after the pool beneath it had drained.

I would have to learn those movements as they arrived.

Once, I would carry a darkness with no feet and no jaw. It would lie crosswise inside me, reaching beyond both sides of my body. For several days I would be unable to pass beneath my own ledge.

I would wait in the open, losing water.

A red wall-creature would approach and test one of my exposed folds. I would gather too slowly to stop it taking a piece of glass.

But on the fourth day, when the roof settled, I would draw away before the stone moved. The darkness would contract. I would fit beneath the ledge again.

Below me, the old hollow would have become a seam too narrow for water.

***

Years might not be available in this place. I would grow by crossings instead.

After enough of them, a grazer’s touch at my margin would no longer be only a touch. My tubes would fill. My mouth would loosen. I would turn toward the cooler stone.

Not every time. Sometimes I would be feeding. Sometimes there would be too little of me left to carry anything.

But once, when a ridge split beneath a crowded pool, I would spread across the opening.

The swimmers would enter first.

Their bodies would strike the grit inside me. Grazers would cling to my upper folds. A hunter would settle near the jaw, and I would keep that fold closed.

I would move badly under their weight.

Halfway to the next hollow, one tube would tear. Water would begin to leave me. The smallest grazers would step down and walk beside my trailing margin.

I would reach the hollow with almost nothing gathered at my rear.

There I would flatten.

For a while it would be difficult to tell which water was mine. The swimmers would turn in the shallow edge. The grazers would begin testing the floor. Someone would find a loose needle and lift it.

When the next drop fell, I would admit it.

Then another.

Under the new water, my torn tube would slowly fill.
