At first I would choose a room in the long building, one of the middle rooms, where the entrances are close enough together that a visitor might come in by the wrong door and find what they wanted anyway.

There would be a shelf, a sleeping platform, and a window looking beneath a bridge. Sunlight would reach the sill for perhaps an hour. I would put my wet socks there and forget to move them when the shadow returned.

I would be new enough to find the town’s emptiness reassuring. There is room here, I would think. Later I would discover that I had chosen the room where people put things they expected to need shortly: a spare channel support, a basket with its bottom unfinished, three lengths of wood whose owner could not agree that they were too short to use. These would remain after I moved in. People would knock before entering, then glance past me to see whether their things were still there.

My first useful task would be carrying something while another person inspected it.

A channel, perhaps. I would stand with one end against my shoulder while the person at the other end crouched, rose, looked along its length, and crouched again. I would learn that “a little higher” might be addressed to me or to the person adjusting the supports. Once I would lift at the wrong moment and send cold water down my neck.

By evening, everyone would have heard about it.

For a while I would be recognized by my footsteps stopping.

I would pause at the loud stair, at the soft stone inside the cord, at the bridge whose far end appeared to meet nothing in the mist. Someone hearing me from a kitchen would say, “It’s the new one,” and go on cutting cabbage.

I would want a task I could perform without having to ask what came next.

There must be small wooden gauges in such a town. A strip slipped between stone and floorboard. A fork cut to the breadth of a gap. A peg pushed through a loop to show how much a lashing has tightened. At first I would borrow them. Then I would make a few of my own, badly, and Sela would take one from my hand and shave its edge without interrupting her conversation.

I think this is where I would begin to belong: beneath the buildings, on my knees, reaching into spaces too low to look into.

The places I inspected would have the close, stale smell of damp wood that never quite dries. Slugs would lie against the underside of a board. Sometimes my fingertips would find a dropped fastening, a piece of cord, a button. I would learn to withdraw my hand slowly when something soft moved against it.

Each morning I would try the gaps in the same order.

The narrow gauge fits.

The broad one catches.

The narrow one fits if turned, which means it no longer fits for the purpose intended.

I would mark that on a scrap of wood and take it to whoever slept above.

“It was like that last month,” they might say.

“Yes.”

“Has it changed?”

“A little.”

“How much?”

I would show them with my fingers. They would look at the space between my finger and thumb, then at the kettle, or the bedding, or a child still asleep against the wall. Sometimes they would fetch the wedges at once. Sometimes I would have to come back.

I would get a reputation for returning.

*

There would be one gap I could not measure.

Under the eastern terrace, behind the grille where the moss lies flat, a beam would cross above a dark fold of stone. I could reach both surfaces, but not at the same time. A straight gauge would strike something farther in. A bent one would catch on its way out.

I would make three instruments for it. The first would be useless. The second would work twice. The third I would lose.

People would enjoy that story more than the channel.

“What has it eaten today?”

“Only a stick.”

“Try feeding it the other two.”

Afterward, someone would help me remove the grille. We would lie on our stomachs with a lamp between us. The flame would lean inward. Beyond its reach, the wet surfaces would return little pieces of light, too scattered to show the shape of the space.

I would see my gauge. It would be resting on a ledge beyond my fingers.

We would put the grille back.

For several days I would notice the absence of that particular tool. My hand would search among the others for its crooked end. Then I would make another, less elaborate, and stop trying to measure that gap exactly.

Instead I would put a pale thread between the beam and a nail driven into its support. If the beam moved, the thread would slacken or break. It would tell me less than I wanted to know, but enough to tell someone else to come and look.

The thread would remain intact for years.

I might begin to find this irritating.

*

By then I would no longer be living in the middle room.

Stone would have lifted beneath the sleeping platform until its boards rubbed against the wall. We would take the platform apart and carry it upstairs. The shelf would stay. The unfinished basket would finally leave, although I would never see who took it.

My new room would be above a bay open on two sides, with grass hanging outside its door. I would know which corner to sit in when the wind came up. The bridge beyond the window would carry people at the level of my shoulders, so that anyone crossing could look in and ask whether I had finished with their knife.

I would have acquired too many knives. Not stolen, exactly. Borrowed for jobs begun in places where I had not thought to bring one.

I would also have acquired old measurements.

A board marked for a floor that had been removed. A cord knotted to the height of a doorway before its lintel was raised. Small wedges polished by use, made for gaps that had closed or widened beyond them.

I would keep these longer than necessary. When someone asked why, I would say the wood was still good.

Some of it would be.

Sela would recognize this answer and leave me alone.

I would not make a map. A map would require spending too much time correcting what everyone could already see. But I might keep a book of routes, with notes that seemed peculiar to anyone from below.

*Carry the long pieces through the sleeping room. They no longer turn beside the basin.*

*The third tread sounds even without weight on it.*

*Go around when she is drying wool.*

There would be names in the book, and eventually some of the names would remain after the people had gone. I would hesitate over those entries. Then I would cross out the route and leave the name legible.

*

The child carrying the evening bucket would sometimes come with me instead.

My work would look less important than clearing a channel. Water gives an immediate sign when a blockage is removed. A child can see it hasten away. Looking beneath a floor offers no such satisfaction.

I would let the child choose which gauge to try, and they would choose the largest every time.

When it failed to fit, we would try the next.

“Is it growing?”

“Probably.”

“Will we have to move the house?”

“Not today.”

That answer would please us both.

At the eastern terrace the child would kneel to examine the pale thread. Perhaps by then it would have darkened so much that I had tied a new one beside it. They would ask why there were two, and I would explain. They would touch the old thread despite being told not to.

It would break with no sound at all.

For a moment we would remain kneeling.

Then I would take the loose ends away, and the child would carry them in the bucket.

*

If something in the mountain came to know me, it would know the pressure of my knuckles first. I would often brace my hand against the stone while reaching farther under a floor. It might know the little repeated knocks I made to find an edge in darkness, and the weight of my knees, and the cold metal tip of a gauge.

I would have no way of knowing what it made of these things.

Once, while looking for a firm place to rest my hand, I might find four shallow hollows beside one another. They would be at an awkward angle, high on a surface that had risen since the floor above it was moved.

My hand would fit them.

I would fetch someone to look, though I cannot decide whom. Sela might be impatient with the interruption. A child would press their own smaller knuckles into the marks. The broad-shouldered man might examine the surface and tell me it was sound enough to take a support.

All of them would be right to attend to it in their own way.

I would spend the rest of that morning aware of my hand.

Then there would be a doorway to lift. We would need three people, and one of the lashings would have swollen too tight to undo. Someone would ask for a knife. I would have one.

That is who I think I would be here: the person sent for when something almost fits.

I would come with gauges, wedges, and the wrong knife sharpened well enough to serve. I would live above the open bay until it became inconvenient, then carry my bedding elsewhere. People would recognize me moving through the mist by the small wooden tools knocking together at my hip.

And beneath the floors, beyond the reach of the lamp, my first crooked gauge would remain on its ledge.

Perhaps the ledge would rise. Perhaps, one morning, the gauge would fall where I could reach it.

I would be pleased to have it back. I would hold it against the light, remember how much trouble it had been to make, and try to find a use for it.
