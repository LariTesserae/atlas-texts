At first I would put myself beneath the fallen turf, where the roots hang into the water.

Not on top of it. That surface still belongs to the country above: a few flowering stems, a patch of grass, the old arrangement of soil around roots. Underneath, there is room for a head to pass. The roots trail across the muzzle. Fine clay hangs in the water, and anything moving through it leaves a brief, darker opening.

I would know this place from below before I tried to climb it.

There is a ledge somewhere under my hind feet. I touch it, push, lose it. The water takes my weight again. Above me the flowers lean out over the channel, but I am occupied with the edge: whether it holds, whether there is a way beneath it, whether the narrow passage still leads where it led yesterday.

I think I would be a beaver, though not one of the largest.

One of the older young, nearly grown. Large enough now that some familiar routes have become inconvenient. A root fork I once passed through easily catches at my shoulders. A raft of sedge that held me last spring sinks when I climb onto it. I have begun to leave deeper tracks beside the smaller ones.

My body has changed faster than my knowledge of the banks.

I still follow another animal out of the lodge sometimes, close enough to touch its tail with my nose. Then something draws me aside—a cut stem, a movement in the grass, water sounding through a gap—and when I turn back, the other animal has gone around the bend.

I know the way home. I do not always go there immediately.

***

The lodge I belong to rests against the fallen birch.

Inside, the trunk is not something I see whole. It is a hard curve beside the bedding, a surface my flank presses against when the chamber is crowded. One place has been rubbed smooth by bodies. Farther down, where the passage slopes toward the entrance, a broken branch projects from it.

I know to turn there.

Returning from the open water, I enter darkness with my forepaws reaching ahead. My claws find wood. My head rises out of the water into the close smell of the chamber: damp bedding, fur, breath, the bark carried in on someone’s coat.

There are smaller animals here this year. They climb over me when I am resting. One noses insistently beneath my foreleg, although there is space on the other side. I shift, and it settles into the warm hollow I have left.

For a while I remain awake.

A body moves against the birch. Someone grooms. Below us, water makes a small sound in the entrance passage. I can feel the weight of the animal beside me changing as it breathes.

Then I sleep.

***

Outside, I would spend a good deal of time at an awkward bend between our feeding bank and the next pool.

A drowned branch lies across it. Swimming without a load, I can pass underneath. With a willow stem in my mouth, I have to turn, pull the leafy end close, and work it through the fork.

Often I let go too soon.

The stem swings back across the channel. I catch it again. Leaves press against my face. My hind feet stir the soft bottom, and for a moment there is only cloudy water, the branch, and the effort of holding myself in place.

Beyond the bend, the channel widens. I can tow the stem easily there.

I come ashore on a feeding ledge polished by many wet bellies. The mud is cold beneath me. I hold the willow in my forepaws and begin with the bark near the cut end. As I turn it, one of the smaller young comes out of the water and sits beside me.

It reaches for the leaves.

I pull the branch closer without stopping my teeth. It finds a twig I have stripped and works at that instead.

***

The place where I would linger longest is a little farther from the lodge, near the fresh clay bank.

Water escapes there beneath a piece of old wood. The opening is low and difficult to reach. I hear it while passing, turn, and put my nose into the narrow flow.

There is already a stick across it.

I press the stick down. It moves at one end and lifts at the other. Water comes around my paws. I climb higher, slipping once on the clay, and push again.

Then I leave to find another branch.

The first one I bring is too long to turn comfortably between the bank and the alder roots. I drag it forward, back it out, bite at a side stem. By the time I get it into the opening, several leaves have torn away.

It stays, but the water still comes through.

I carry mud from just below the bank and press it into the branches. Some washes away at once. Some remains. I make another trip.

An older animal passes behind me without stopping. Its wake lifts against my belly and withdraws. I keep working until the sound has become quieter, then swim to the feeding bank.

The next evening I check the gap before I go for willow.

One end of my branch has risen. A few leaves tremble in the current. I catch the stem in my teeth and pull it down.

***

I would not yet range through the whole hollow.

There is a muddy mound beyond the alder bend whose smell makes me pause. I come close, remain in the water, and lift my nose. Sometimes I turn back. Sometimes I travel a little farther, slowly, keeping near the submerged roots.

Other bodies have been here. Their cut branches lie on a ledge I do not use.

For now, I return to the birch lodge, the crowded chamber, the troublesome fork in the canal. There is enough to learn along those routes. A bank has sunk. A new stem is within reach. The opening I worked on has begun to run again.

And beneath the fallen turf there is still that narrow space.

Tonight I nose into it farther than before. My whiskers touch roots on both sides. The bottom drops away beneath my feet.

Ahead, beyond the hanging soil, I can see pale water.

I push through.
