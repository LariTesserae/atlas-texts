I would be beneath the second sunken paving stone, on the side that faces away from the generating house.

There is a space there that does not quite belong to the channel. The stone settled unevenly and left a shallow roof over a pocket of water. During the rise, the pocket fills from the east. During the reversal, it empties through a crack too narrow for a leaf stem. A little warmth reaches it through an old fastening sunk into the foundation.

I would fit around that fastening.

At first, perhaps, I would seem to be its gasket: a dark, uneven collar between iron and stone. Something installed to prevent a leak, then softened by years of immersion. My outer edge would have collected enough grit to resemble the pavement. The water would move over me. Nobody crossing the square would notice that the fastening no longer held anything.

Underneath, I would be working loose.

One pale knob at a time, I would release the stone, lengthen into the pocket, and attach again. The movement would scarcely change my position. It would change which parts of me were pressed against the warmth.

That would occupy much of a day.

I would not know it as a day. There would be the long rise, the pause, the knock, the returning pull. There would be the engine’s ordinary unevenness and, within it, smaller differences: a blow arriving late, a tremor passing through the fastening that usually passed through the stone. Some differences would disappear when I flattened myself. Others would remain wherever I touched.

I would learn the latter.

My first journey would be made because the warmth stopped.

Not all at once. One edge of the fastening would cool while the other stayed warm, and I would draw myself toward the remaining side until there was no room left to draw. Then I would put a narrow part of myself into the crack.

Beyond it, the current would take hold of me.

For a while I would be attached only by the last few knobs of my underside. They would lengthen under my weight. The stone above would become a pressure I could no longer reach. I would find another surface by spreading, not by choosing a direction: the stem of a plant, then the lower edge of a ceramic stud, then the wall.

There I would remain through a reversal, folded around the stud.

Something small would arrive under my edge.

I would tighten at first. Its touch would shift along me in short intervals, unlike the water and unlike the engine. When I loosened, it would move closer. We would stay that way until warmth began to return through the wall. Then it would leave by the upper side of the stud, and I would follow as far as the next mortar joint.

After that, the pocket beneath the paving stone would still be mine, but not entirely.

I would return to it carrying a thread of root caught in a fold. Later, a grain of amber glass. The glass would settle into my back and remain there, making one part of me less willing to bend. When I passed beneath the stone, I would have to turn that part sideways.

Over many returns, the opening would become familiar to my altered shape.

I think I would be a small individual for a long time. Broad enough to cover a hand, perhaps, but thin enough to pass behind the white ceramic markers. I would not reach the high pipes or the warm glass of the pattern shop. Most of my world would lie within a few feet of the pavement. The upper district would arrive as runoff, vibrations, flakes of material loosened somewhere beyond my reach.

One season—or several hundred reversals—the crack would begin to close.

A root would have entered it from the other side. At first the root would yield when I pressed against it. Later it would thicken. Water leaving the pocket would gather behind me, lifting my edges before the rest of the channel had begun to move.

I would feed there. Fine matter would collect along the root, and I would roll it inward.

But I would also push.

Each time the current returned, I would lay part of myself between root and stone. When the pressure eased, I would reach a little farther. My underside would become marked by the root’s curve. The amber grain in my back would press against the paving stone until its exposed edge wore smooth.

Eventually there would be room beside the root for water to pass.

I would keep that room open because I rested there. Because the pocket grew cold when it filled too slowly. Because leaving it required less effort when the current could take hold of my edge.

Someone watching might call this repair.

Elsewhere, another body would be making a different passage narrower.

I would meet that one at the wall below the markers. It would be larger than me, with a hard ridge embedded across its back. We would touch front margins. For a long time neither of us would move. Then I would flatten, and it would pass partly over me, leaving a trace of warmth where its underside had been.

I might follow it.

Only to the leaking joint beneath the staircase. Only far enough to discover water warmer than the water at home.

Then I would return differently: slower, with my front edge spread, stopping at surfaces I had crossed before without resting.

Eventually I would cease fitting beneath the stone.

I would try several times. The amber grain would catch first; then a thickened fold would hold against the entrance. I would contract and wait, contract again. Water would pass into the pocket around me. The old fastening would remain just beyond my reach.

I would settle instead against the wall beneath the ceramic studs.

During the next rise, a smaller body would cross my underside and enter the opening. I would feel it touch the fastening.

The warmth there would be faint, but still present.

Above me, a drop would gather on the brick. I would lift my front margin toward the slight movement it made before falling. Behind me, the small body would loosen its grip, shift around the iron, and attach again.
