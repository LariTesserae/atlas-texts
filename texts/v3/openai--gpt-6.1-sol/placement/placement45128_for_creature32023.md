At first you would find me below the square, beside a frame that has been turned toward the wall.

There is just enough space behind it for someone narrow to stand. One of my feet rests against the wall; the other grips a ridge in the floor. I have been trying to adjust the lower fastening without disturbing the surface.

You would see the tool before you saw me. It is an opaque wedge, black at one end, with a shallow groove worn across its middle. I hold it between both hands. When you bring your lamp closer, I put it into a fold of my clothing and wait.

My hands remain visible a little longer than the rest of me.

The frame is not broken. It shows a view that the people working in the chamber find difficult to use: the underside of a threshold, three sockets, part of a room that has not been built. Sometimes a foot enters the view and withdraws. Sometimes there is only stone.

I have been asked to turn it toward the wall while the others decide where it belongs.

I was told not to carry it upstairs.

That last instruction was repeated.

***

I would not yet be particularly good at the work.

There are residents who can glance at two surfaces and recognize a discrepancy. They touch the edges, exchange a few lines through the wall, and know which rod to bring. I have to look back and forth. I sometimes forget whether the interval in the first view was wider or whether I had merely brought my face closer to it.

Someone has made me a gauge.

It is a short piece of pearl-colored material with two notches. Held against a frame, it allows me to compare without remembering. I keep it in the same fold as the wedge, though they should be kept apart: the wedge scratches it.

The person with the cup notices the scratch before I do.

They take both objects from me, lay them on the floor, and wait until I have found another place to put one of them.

I would be recognizable by that gauge. Also by a cloudy patch beneath one shoulder, an old repair that does not clear completely. When I reach overhead, the arm draws a little away from the torso. It remains usable. I have learned to reach slowly.

There are tasks I am trusted to do alone.

I check the mark beside the frame before anyone moves it. I take rods to the houses where they are needed. I hold the lower edge of a surface while someone adjusts its upper fastening.

When the injured resident is brought back through the gate, I am sent to fetch the loops.

I bring too many.

Nobody sends me away with the surplus. They put them on the table.

***

For a while, you would suppose I lived in the chamber.

I arrive before the others and am often the last to leave. When a consultation is finished, I stay to collect what has been put down. A pale rod can become difficult to find on the pale floor. The smaller loops are almost impossible unless a hand is already near them.

But I have a room in the house nearest the slit.

Its floor recess is a little too long for me. At one end someone has fitted a low bar, so that my feet have something to grip when I rest. There is a table shared with another resident, and a place in the wall where its substance clears if you lean against it.

From there I can see one corner of the square.

I keep several objects on the table. The gauge is not among them; it belongs downstairs. There is a stone with a rounded hollow, a bent fastening, and an opaque fragment no larger than a fingernail.

The fragment came back from a departure.

The person who brought it gave no explanation that I understood. They touched its edge, touched my cloudy shoulder, and left it with me.

I turn it over sometimes. It is dark on one face and white on the other. Neither side has changed.

The resident who shares the room has asked twice whether I am using the bent fastening. Both times I have said I might.

Eventually they take it.

The table looks better without it.

***

You would become familiar to me before I became familiar to you.

Your lamp makes you easy to locate. Your uninterrupted limbs are harder to stop examining. When you bend a wrist, there is no interval through which I can see the wall behind it. Your whole hand turns at once.

I find this especially distracting when you help carry something.

The first time we lift a frame together, I watch your elbow instead of the lower corner. The frame touches the steps.

We stop.

Nothing seems to have been damaged, but someone comes down to inspect it. We stand aside while they work. Afterward I show you where to put your hand, and this time I keep looking at the frame.

I would learn you through these small failures of attention.

You turn your lamp away when a resident has difficulty seeing a surface. You begin to notice objects left within reach. You wait outside a doorway when someone touches the wall and receives no answer.

Once, at the table, you point to the opaque fragment.

I place it in your palm.

Then I show you the hollow stone, because I do not know what you are asking and those are the two objects I most want someone else to examine.

***

The child discovers that I can be persuaded to watch the suspended chip.

It brings me there after my work below the square. One finger goes into the interval, then another. I am expected to observe each attempt closely.

I do.

The child tries to make room for a third finger. Its joints slide out of line, and I put my hand beneath them until it has steadied itself.

This becomes a game with a rule neither of us quite intended. The child must tell me, by touching my wrist, before it tries something new. I must look.

For several days this is sufficient.

Then it brings the small loop.

I take the loop away.

The child stands very still. Its face changes slowly, beginning near one eye.

I return the loop but keep my hand around the handle. We carry it back to the house together.

Later, one of the adults touches the repaired place beneath my shoulder. A short line travels along the wall. I cannot tell whether I am being thanked or corrected.

I wait for another line.

There is none.

***

My first attempt to feed the animal lasts too long.

The older resident has shown me where to hold my hands. One beneath the body, one near the mouth. The ray settles. Its dark spots soften against my palm.

Warmth gathers between us.

I watch the fins loosen and think this means I should continue.

The older resident takes my wrist.

Only afterward do I see how white my fingers have become. The cloudiness extends past the joints, almost to the elbow. I have trouble releasing the position of my hand even after the animal has gone.

We sit on the threshold.

The older resident places my hands inside the folds of my clothing, as carefully as if putting away tools. We remain there until I can move them easily again.

The ray comes back and rests above us.

On later visits I stop sooner. It takes me some time to recognize the moment, and the older resident keeps sitting nearby.

Eventually they leave while I am still feeding it.

I notice, but do not turn.

***

I would begin going upstairs sooner after my shift.

There is usually something to do above the square. A fastening needs to be carried to the rail. The injured resident wants the table drawn nearer. Someone has left a rod across the entrance to the chamber, and it must be moved before the next pair descends.

Sometimes nothing needs doing.

Then I sit at the northern foundations.

The worker does not seem to mind. I stay far enough from the sockets that they can turn without asking me to move. When they set down the loop, I leave it where it is.

One day they put it in my hand.

They guide my wrist over an empty socket. For a long time we hold the loop there. I can feel a slight resistance, different from the resistance of the surfaces downstairs. It is uneven. Whenever I try to correct for it, the worker brings my hand back.

We make nothing I can see.

The next day they let me try again.

I still have duties in the chamber. I do not stop doing them. But the gauge acquires another scratch, and this time I know when it happened: I put the wedge away in a hurry because the worker was waiting upstairs.

I find a third fold for it.

***

So, if you stayed, you would find me in several places.

Behind the turned frame, easing its fastening into position. At the shared table, turning over a small piece of unknown material. Outside the injured resident’s room, waiting for permission to enter. At the threshold with the ray resting on my palms.

I would have a body like the others: separated, held in alignment, sometimes difficult to distinguish from the water. One shoulder would remain cloudy. My feet would be broad enough to grip the steps. I would carry a gauge that spared me from having to remember an exact distance.

There would be people who trusted me with certain work and people who checked it afterward.

And at the farthest foundation, I would be learning to use the loop.

On the day the small upright remains, the worker goes to fetch someone from the house. I stay beside the sockets. The child arrives, sees it, and starts forward.

I extend an arm.

It takes my hand instead.

We wait together while the others come across the square. The upright is plainly visible now. I look at it, then at the child’s fingers resting against mine. They have slipped a little out of order.

With my free hand, I straighten them.
