I think I would live near a crossing that was no longer quite a crossing.

One broadmouth would still use the eastern road. The western road would have healed almost level with the shelf, and the route descending toward the next settlement would be passable only in the morning, before the heat loosened its steepest scales. People would disagree about whether my house was conveniently placed. Those arriving before noon would say it was. Those arriving after would sit in its narrow shade and say nothing until they had drunk.

The house would stand beneath a lifted root, well short of a great tree. From the doorway I could see the lower part of one distant crown. Its upper branches would be hidden behind a rise. Sometimes the silver undersides of the foliage would show above that rise, and sometimes they would not, though the wind at my house felt the same.

I would make and mend foot coverings.

That seems a modest enough occupation to begin with. There would be softened fiber hanging beneath the roof, strips of tough membrane kept flat between rind plates, a shell knife with a handle polished by my thumb. Travelers would leave damaged soles beside the door. Some would be worn thin evenly; others would have a single deep gouge where their owner had stepped badly. I would learn something about a journey before hearing its account.

A person hurrying uphill wears a different place from a person carrying water downhill.

A person frightened by the ground often curls their toes.

I would notice these things and sometimes be wrong about them.

My own feet would be troublesome. Barefoot, I would find a busy shelf difficult to cross. Too many movements would arrive together: scraping close beneath me, a slow pressure farther down, something intermittent that seemed to shift whenever I tried to attend to it. I would stop walking without meaning to. Someone traveling with me would have to touch my wrist.

So I would become particular about soles.

Not merely thick ones. A thick sole can hide the first warning of a weak chamber as well as the nuisance of a hundred little feet. I would try crossed fibers, fibers laid in one direction, a thin pocket of dry meal sewn beneath the heel. Some experiments would last a season. Others would be taken apart after a few steps.

Under the workbench I would keep the failures.

A child visiting the house would like those best. There would be a pair that made every small tremor feel as though it came from directly ahead, and one with such stiff edges that walking in it felt like standing on two little roofs. I would let the child try them on the sound ground beside the doorway, holding their hands while they laughed.

The useful pairs would look less remarkable.

***

I would not travel much at first.

Other people would bring the country to my door in the things caught in their footwear: dust the color of dried blood, a seed casing shaped differently from ours, fibers that smelled bitter when cut. A hunter might bring a sole with three small holes in it and insist that nothing had bitten them. Someone from beyond the valley would want their shoes made softer, then return the next morning to have them stiffened again.

I would ask where they expected to walk.

Eventually I would have to go and see.

There is a limit to what can be learned from an object after it has been taken off the body.

My first long journey would probably be embarrassing. I would carry too much material, stop too often, and discover that a sole which worked well at home became unpleasant on a shelf whose chambers lay close beneath the surface. I would arrive at a water place after the drinkers and wait at the edge of the crowd with an empty vessel, pretending that I had intended to rest there anyway.

A water keeper would show me where to sit without obstructing the approach.

I would remember the place by the pressure of her hand on my shoulder.

After that I would make a circuit, perhaps twice a year. Four settlements, then five if the lower route remained usable. I would mend shoes in exchange for food and a place beneath a roof. People would ask me to carry small things: a shell blade, a strip of seasoned membrane, a message that could not safely be struck along a root.

I would repeat the messages while walking. Once I would forget an important word and have to turn back.

That would become part of my reputation too.

***

The abandoned doorway near my house would draw me gradually.

At first I would use it only as shade when collecting fibers. The opening would be low enough that I had to bend to enter. Its frame would still be rectangular, but the floor would have risen on one side, making the room slope toward a corner where cool air came through a seam.

There would be very little left inside. A piece of cord grown firmly into the wall. Two depressions that might once have held vessels. A pale patch beneath the opening.

One morning, arriving early, I would find water along the underside of the lintel.

Not much. Enough to wet my fingers and then collect again while I waited.

I would ask who had lived there. The answers would differ. Someone would remember a family moving away after a pool failed. Someone else would say the house had already been empty then. An older person would know the name of the woman who used to make its roof fast, but not whether she had built it.

I would bring a bowl on my next visit.

Then, over several mornings, I would discover the animals already using the place. A small grazer that came through the tilted corner. Two glossy insects which waited beside the first wet patch. A seam-runner whose forelimbs appeared in the old doorway and remained motionless until I moved my knee.

I would have to shift my bowl.

There would be room for it, but not where I had first put it.

***

As I grew older, people might begin calling me a route keeper, although I would still think of myself as a maker of soles.

I would know which crossings were comfortable at dawn and troublesome by midday. I would know where a traveler could sit with their feet off the ground. I would keep a few spare pairs for people who arrived with bloody heels, and sometimes lend a pair to someone who never returned.

My house would contain more unfinished work than it should.

I would still dislike walking beneath the great trees. The falling shells would trouble me, and the noise would make it hard to judge my own footing. When a journey required passing one, I would go with somebody who knew its lower paths. I would have no wish to climb into the crown.

Where I would feel most at home is much lower: seated just inside my doorway in the hour when the shelf begins to cool.

One shoe would be braced between my knees. I would be drawing a new strip through the worn edge, tightening each stitch with the same little turn of the wrist. Outside, the first ground-dwellers would emerge. A traveler resting against the root would watch them and ask whether it was too late to leave.

I would set the work aside and step out to test the road.

For a moment I would stand with one heel raised. Then I would walk as far as the crossing, slowly enough to distinguish the movement beneath me from my own.

Sometimes I would come back and say they could still reach the next shelter.

Sometimes I would tell them to stay.

And if they stayed, I would move the unfinished soles off the sleeping mat, cover the water, and make room.
