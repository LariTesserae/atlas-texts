I would find myself near the wooden pin, on its lower side, where the wet roots hang.

There would be a little earth there that nobody had put there: dust held by the grain of the wood, a softened piece of root, pollen washed down from above. Enough to keep damp after the exposed rock had dried. I would know this place first by the change in the surface under me. Stone, then something warmer, yielding almost imperceptibly.

The view would be behind me. I would have arrived without seeing it.

For a while, I think, I would be very difficult to distinguish from a bead of water.

Then the bead would lengthen. Two fine tentacles would come out, hesitate, and extend again.

I would be a snail, though not quite one of the nearly transparent snails under the rock flakes. My shell would be darker, small enough to fit into the creature’s ear but too broad to become lodged there. Along its lip there would be an old pale injury. When I withdrew, that damaged edge would prevent a perfect closure. This would determine a surprising amount of my life.

I would need a reliable damp place.

The pin would not be mine all year. In dry weather I would travel down the wall, following the seam behind it, until I reached the shelter. The journey would take most of a night. There would be a difficult stretch where the stone curved outward and offered no crack. Crossing it, I would carry my shell slightly to one side.

Inside, I would live beneath the edge of the raised floor.

Not in the bedding. The bedding would be disturbed too often, brought out and brought back, pressed under the sleeper’s weight. But one strand would extend into my space: a grass fiber, darkening slowly, held between two stones. I would graze along it. On some mornings I would find that it had been pulled away.

Then I would have to cross the bare underside of the slab to reach another.

The creature would first become known to me as weight.

Its arrival would press the floor stones into their seats. A little opening would narrow. Elsewhere, a new opening would appear. Air would move through the shelter, carrying warmth and a smell quite different from wet roots.

When it slept, the changes would come regularly.

I would emerge at those times.

There would be growth on the underside of the floor that could not be seen from above. Thin films, soft flecks, the beginnings of things. A grain of soil would be an obstacle worth going around. Where the creature had brought in a damp bundle, there would sometimes be enough to eat for several nights.

I would not know about the empty space beside it.

I might cross that space more often than any other living thing did.

Once, the creature would lift my floor stone.

Light would reach me all at once. I would pull in my tentacles, then my head, then as much of my foot as the damaged shell allowed. For a moment I would remain attached to the stone overhead.

A fingertip would touch beside me.

The stone would turn. My weight would draw me sideways. I would lose my hold and fall into its palm.

It would be a short fall.

The palm would be pale, wet in some of its creases, and warm enough that I would begin to come out before I ought to. My foot would find the lines in it. One tentacle would touch a ridge of hardened skin.

The creature would hold still.

Perhaps it would be deciding whether to eat me. Perhaps the decision would already have been made. I would have no way to distinguish stillness caused by attention from stillness caused by waiting.

Then its hand would lower against the wall.

I would leave it by degrees. First my head, then the forward part of my foot; for some time my shell would remain suspended over the narrow gap between skin and stone. The hand would stay until I was across.

That would be the whole encounter.

Afterward, I would find myself below the wooden pin again. It would take two nights to get back beneath the floor.

I would continue to live there.

When the creature cleared the channel, water would sometimes enter my crevice before finding its new course. I would climb. When it pushed snow out of the doorway, a few crystals would lodge against my slab and melt slowly. I would remain withdrawn until the wetness warmed.

In summer I would go farther.

There would be a route to the stone bowl along the shaded side of the shelter. Near its base, the rock would carry a fine green coating. I would feed there after dark. Above me, birds would move in their sleep. Once, the creature would come out while I was still on the bowl, and I would feel the water rise against my foot when it drank.

I would retreat below the chipped rim.

It would wash. Water would run over me. I would hold on.

By daylight I would be underneath again, in a small hollow left by one of the blows that had shaped the bowl. My shell would fit there with scarcely any room to spare.

That is where I would belong most completely: in an accidental recess in something made for another body’s use.

The mountain would keep supplying water. The creature would keep its morning order. I would move between the floor, the pin, and the bowl, with occasional journeys into the roots when the dampness lasted.

Over time, the pale injury on my shell would acquire a second rim.

There would be a faint trace along the wall where I passed most often. Dust would adhere to it. A little green would begin there.

The creature, cleaning the channel one morning, might run its finger beside that line and leave it undisturbed.

I would be farther in, under the stone, eating.
