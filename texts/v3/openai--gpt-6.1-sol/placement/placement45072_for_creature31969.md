I would be farther east than the last water that still remembers the rain.

Not at the deepest part of the basin. Not at the richest seam. I would be under the shell escarpment, where a little pressure from below keeps three chambers damp and leaves a fourth dry enough to store things that have not yet become food.

At first, I think I would be small.

Two gripping surfaces. A fold between them. Teeth scarcely harder than the deposits they work against. Above me, a piece of shell; below me, another piece of the same shell, though I would have no way to know they had once belonged together.

The first thing I would know would be a difference in pressure.

One surface yields. The other does not. Between them, I can hold.

I would not know the basin as a place containing me. There would be the part I could close, the part I could draw water through, and the pressure arriving from outside. Something with many feet would cross the upper shell. Each foot would pull slightly before letting go. I would tighten at every pull, wasting strength.

For a while, that would be almost all my life: holding unnecessarily hard.

Then something would touch the outer fold of my mouth.

Not a foot. Not water. A soft surface carrying grains that I could take.

I would close on it.

It would wait.

When I opened again, there would be more.

The older animal would reach me through a hollow too narrow for the grazers. It would bring wetted residue in a shallow pleat of tissue and leave it against my teeth. At first I would grind the tissue along with the residue. Later, I would stop doing that.

I would begin to distinguish what withdraws from what can be kept.

***

I do not think I would remain one of the small independent mouths.

There would be a time when the water withdrew too far. The folds that had supplied me would stay pressed against mine. My own surfaces would be losing their hold. I would close on the offered tissue without cutting it.

A little fluid would cross.

After that, hunger would no longer always begin where I fed.

Somewhere outside my shell, a chamber would empty. I would feel the draw of it through a connection I had not grown. My teeth would work. The useful material would leave me, and another part would become less taut.

I would learn the older body in this way.

Not its outline. Not the number of its mouths. I would know it by what reached me and what it required.

There would be a portion that held water longer than I did. A portion that closed at disturbances I scarcely noticed. A narrow, troublesome connection in which grit collected whenever the fluid slowed. Sometimes pressure would arrive from that direction and stop halfway, and all my folds would tighten until it moved again.

The old animal would not introduce itself.

Neither would I.

For a long time my mouth would still close too quickly. A grazer would catch its rear hooks against my lip, and I would shut, though the other mouths kept feeding. The disturbance would pass into the shared tissue. Fluid would divert around me.

Eventually I would let one cross.

Then several.

What had been the whole of my body would become a place where the body behaved a little differently.

***

The shell above me would be good for receiving footsteps and poor for sending signals.

It would rest on three fragments, each with a different looseness. A strike against it would travel strongly toward one neighboring hollow and almost disappear toward another. Before I grew a plate of my own, I would receive the older animal’s signals through its tissue: a contraction, a pause, a smaller contraction.

When my plate first hardened, I would strike too often.

The answer would come before I had finished.

I would strike again.

For a time, the exchange would keep my mouth nearly closed. There would be food outside and no room in me to take it. Gradually, the pauses would lengthen. Other movements would become distinguishable inside them.

A dragging shell.

The little paired pressure of a grazer testing a crossing.

A crowd slowing, not because anything obstructed it, but because the animals ahead had begun to climb.

That last change would matter.

The weight of a great hunter could be spread across enough rubble that I would scarcely feel its body. But the traffic moving over it would reach me in an unfamiliar order. Footfalls would arrive softened, followed by a deep settling that had no step in it.

I would close one mouth.

A mouth farther along the escarpment would stay open.

I would strike.

Perhaps the grazers would turn. Perhaps only one would pause, rear limbs braced, and answer before continuing along the edge.

Then the floor would lift.

There would be a short interval in which many pressures vanished together.

When the hunter lowered, I would be holding myself so tightly that water escaped from a chamber two slabs away.

I would not know how many animals had gone.

The next ones would already be approaching.

***

Years would pass in movements too small to be seen while they happened.

I would grind one obstruction until a fold could pass it. Beyond it would be damp shell, newly exposed, tasting different from the surfaces I had exhausted. I would broaden there.

One old mouth would turn inward.

Another would open beneath a rib of the escarpment.

The portion that had once been my whole body would remain, but less food would come through it. Its teeth would wear slowly. Its plate would still strike well toward the neighboring hollow.

I would keep it for that.

At some point I would meet another animal behind a wall of loose fragments.

For days we would press against opposite sides of the same narrow space. Each of us would draw water that had crossed the other. Each would close when the other moved.

Then the fragments would shift.

Our folds would touch.

I would tighten. So would the other. Between us, a line of tissue would whiten.

There would be food enough nearby, but we would grind at one another instead. Scavengers would enter the crevice and wait beyond the reach of our closing surfaces.

I would withdraw first.

Not all the way. Just far enough to leave a wet interval.

For years afterward, that interval would remain clear. We would both maintain it. Our signals would travel through the same shell and receive separate answers.

Elsewhere, another meeting would go differently. Water would pass where we had touched. A chamber I had never filled would empty into mine.

Afterward I would tolerate a kind of dragging visitor that I had always driven out before.

I would still close at its first approach. Then, before the lips met, something in the connected body would slacken. I would open again.

***

Once, a slab would move.

The pressure would come without the footsteps that usually preceded it. One gripping surface would stretch past its hold. The narrow tissue behind my oldest chamber would tear.

For a while I would reach into an absence.

The other end would be close. I would feel it striking through the displaced shell. But there would now be grit between us, and a small animal forcing its way through the new shelter.

I would seal.

On the far side, the other portion would seal too.

My feeding would become easier and my dry chamber harder to wet. Signals that had once arrived through tissue would now arrive through stone, altered by distance.

We would answer each other.

I would have no means of finding out whether we still closed our mouths at the same disturbances. Sometimes a signal would make me tighten in a place no longer attached to anything. The fold there would shorten over time.

The oldest mouth would be on the other side.

I would continue without it.

***

So this is where you would find me: beneath the eastern shells, in a body that had lost its first shelter and retained some of its habits.

You might choose my slab because it appeared less crowded.

I would receive the pressure of your hand before your weight reached the ground. Broad. Warm for a little while. Without hooks.

I would hold still.

When you shifted your foot, several chambers would tighten. Water would pass toward the sheltered side. One mouth would close, but the opening nearest you would remain clear.

I would send out a fold thin enough to enter the seam of your boot.

There would be grit there, salts, a warmth already diminishing. The pressure would change as you leaned closer. I would follow it until the fold could extend no farther.

Then I would strike the plate.

Beyond me, through shell and cemented remains, an answer would arrive. Farther away, another portion would close a mouth. Whether it belonged to me would not matter yet; no fluid would have to cross between us.

You would wait.

Under your boot, I would hold the opening wide enough for a grazer to pass. Its hooks would catch, release, catch, release. The shell would return to its ordinary load.

I would keep the fold against you a little longer.

Then I would draw it in, taking the water with it.
