At first I would be wherever the sound comes back changed.

A click from the floor would arrive through the pads of my feet, then again through my belly, softer and with a second click inside it. I would turn toward that difference. Not toward the thing that made the sound—I would have no good way of finding it—but toward the place that had held it for a moment.

There would be many such places. A stem thickening around an old injury. Water behind a closed leaf. The wet face of a stone still soft enough to receive a mark.

I would spend some time at each.

The cold would not enter me as it enters someone arriving from above. It would already be in my joints, setting the limits of their movement. To lift a foot would require waiting for the pad to release. To open my mouth would require a small redistribution of warmth. I would make neither movement until the surface beneath me offered somewhere worth going.

I think I would first mistake myself for something shed by the grove.

A folded piece of skin, perhaps, with a darker edge. Small enough to lie across two fingers of the narrow animal’s face. My back would carry shallow ridges that gathered water, and the water would make me difficult to distinguish from the leaf beneath me. There would be no fur. Along my underside, a row of short feet would open and close without quite lifting my body.

When resting, I would look empty.

But a pulse of light passing through the stem would show several curved chambers inside me. One would contain fluid. Another, a few pale grains. The others would seem clear until something moved against their walls.

I would live near the western margin, where roots hang over the newly made ledge. Not on the stone itself. On a broad leaf that has begun to pull away from its supporting stem, leaving a narrow, damp space behind it.

There would be room there for me to flatten.

The leaf would not grow a pocket to fit me. I would have to move whenever it changed. Sometimes its edge would press against my back for several days before I found a way out. Sometimes it would loosen so far that I could no longer reach both surfaces, and I would leave before the gap filled with frost.

My mouth would be underneath, near the front, surrounded by three short folds.

I would set those folds against a stem and wait.

Through them I could feel a white animal calling far below. Most of the call would pass through me. A little would remain: a pressure in one chamber, held between two thin walls. While I rested, those walls would gradually draw together. When I moved, the pressure would go with me.

There would be no recognizable call inside my body. No memory of the animal that made it. Only a slight unevenness in how I bent.

For a while, I might carry one side more tightly than the other.

This would make certain gaps easier to enter and others impossible. I would follow the easy ones. A root would turn me upward. A narrowing crease would send me back. By the time I reached another stem, the held pressure would have changed further, worked upon by every surface I had squeezed between.

There I would put my mouth down again.

A little fluid would pass from me into a pore.

The stem might do nothing. Or a leaf nearby would lift before the frost reached its edge. Or a thin channel would contract, sending water elsewhere. These changes would be too slow for me to remain and watch. I would already be feeling for another place to rest.

I would feed on the soft material gathered around channels that had closed.

There is nourishment there that the clear grazers cannot reach without opening the leaf. I would fit my mouth to the tiny exposed ends and draw out almost nothing. Enough to refill the chamber whose walls had come together. Enough to keep the pads of my feet moist.

Once, beside a mineral sac, I would encounter a pressure I could not release.

The nodule within it would have grown around a small hollow. A burrower’s jaw would strike the outer face, and the hollow would answer. I would be resting against the sac when it did.

For days afterward, one chamber would remain taut.

I would walk poorly. My rear feet would release too late; my body would curve toward the same side. I would leave the margin and descend through the floor felt, following wetter passages where I could let the water bear some of my weight.

A swimmer would pass close enough to fold one ribbon fin over me.

Then I would be somewhere I had never been: against the underside of the youngest cushion, with pale folded leaves above and water moving below.

The taut chamber would empty slowly there.

Above me, a leaf would loosen from its neighbor. It might have been going to loosen anyway. A grazer would enter the crease, and its feet would send tiny disturbances down through the tissue.

I would stay beneath it until I could bend evenly again.

That is where I would most often find myself—not on the cushion, where the new light could show my whole body, but beneath a fold at its edge. Close to a gap being made wider by a mouth. Close to the warmth of something sleeping beyond my reach.

Sometimes the narrow animal would touch the leaf above me. Its fingers would hold still while I held still below. Between us, fluid would move through the stem.

Neither of us would discover the other.

Later, I would emerge with a bead of water lying across my back. I would carry it a little way before it joined a seam beneath a leaf. My feet would leave no depression. The chamber inside me would be filling again.

I would turn toward a faint difference in the next returning sound, and go as far as my body could fit.
