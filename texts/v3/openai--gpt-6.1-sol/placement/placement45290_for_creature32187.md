At first I would put myself behind the pressure gauge.

There is something reassuring about its opaque face: an instrument still attached to the wall, no longer expected to tell anyone anything. Behind it, the water moves slowly enough for soft tissue to remain. The mounting carries vibrations without exposing much surface to the current.

But I would not be the gauge, or something hiding there with a small, complete body. I would be the separated portion of the animal, spread between the back of the instrument and the brick. Eleven years without a living connection to the larger growth. Still feeding. Still making contractions that reach it through the mounting.

Most of me would be thinner than the space suggests. A folded edge below the gauge would admit water. Farther inward, against the brick, there would be a thicker patch where fluid could collect. Between them, a narrow passage would sometimes flatten entirely, leaving the feeding edge to continue for a while on its own reserves.

I would have no view of the chamber.

The ladder, the grille, the three throats in the floor would reach me as differences in force. The southern pump would arrive strongly through one screw and weakly through the other. The washer would be a fine disturbance along my lower margin, occasionally lost when the pressure of the gauge against the wall changed.

The larger animal would be harder to distinguish.

A contraction under the inspection plate would enter the masonry among thousands of other movements. Some nights it would arrive while a pump was changing speed and leave no separate effect. On quieter nights it would press several of my sensitive particles against their fibers in a particular order. Fluid would move inward. A crease would close.

That closing would pass into the gauge’s mounting.

Minutes later, something might return.

I do not think I would know it as an answer. The first contraction would have changed the conditions in which I received the second. One chamber emptied, another filled; a membrane held taut that had previously been loose. The returning pressure would find me differently arranged.

After enough repetitions, a small region near the upper screw would become unusually responsive.

That might be where I would find myself more precisely: not throughout the separated tissue equally, but in the part of it altered by these exchanges. A pale thickening no wider than a fingernail. A few chambers that emptied readily at night. An exposed edge that stayed closed longer after a certain sequence reached the mounting.

There would be no thought of the eleven years.

There would, however, be tissue that had grown during them.

An old route for fluid would end blindly where the living connection had once passed into the mortar. Newer passages would bend around it. Feeding surfaces would have shifted toward the cooler side of the gauge. Several sensitive patches would have become less responsive to the nearest pump, leaving smaller movements able to produce a contraction.

Separation would have made a shape.

I would not necessarily grow back toward the main animal. The shortest route might lie across a strip of masonry that the current kept bare. A longer route might end in mortar too sound to enter. Growth would follow food and shelter, neither of which would guarantee reunion.

Some nights the exchange through the brick would continue.

Some nights it would not.

On a holiday morning, the main body might loosen its feeding margins too early. I might remain contracted because the gauge held a different mixture of vibrations. Later, when the pumps finally strengthened, the disturbance would reach us at different times. Our responses would cease to fit together for a while.

I would have no means of recognizing the mistake. By the next quiet circulation, a little fluid might pass through a channel that had been unused for days.

Then rain.

Here, behind the gauge, the first warning would be less useful than it was near the western grille. A pressure change would arrive through the masonry, but the mounting would blunt part of it. I would begin withdrawing my lower edge. Before it had folded completely, cold water would reach the wall.

The porcelain fragment might strike the gauge.

For an instant the whole sheltered space would become pressure. Sensitive particles would move together. The thin passage between my feeding edge and the thicker inner patch would close, and fluid would force its way along another route.

Something would tear.

Afterward, I might be smaller. Or there might be two portions behind the gauge, each able to continue.

I hesitate there, because that changes where “I” could be. Perhaps I would remain with the thickening near the upper screw, where the nightly exchanges had altered the tissue. Perhaps the torn feeding edge would retain enough of those alterations to respond when a contraction came through the brick. Both would have received it before. Both might close.

The warm circulation would return without resolving this.

Over the following days, a clear film would seal the damaged surfaces. One portion would feed sooner than the other. A new edge would extend beneath a flake of rust. The gauge would settle slightly against its mounting, changing the strength of everything transmitted through it.

And on a quiet night, a contraction from beneath the inspection plate would arrive.

I would be there where it could still make a difference: against the brick, behind the unreadable instrument, with enough living tissue left to tighten and slowly release.
