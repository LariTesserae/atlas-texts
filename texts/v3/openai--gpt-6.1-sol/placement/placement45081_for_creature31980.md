At first I would stay near the shelter with two entrances.

Not inside it. There is a low root outside, broad enough to sit on, from which both corridors are visible. A well lies a little farther down. I would choose that place because I could watch someone approach without making them enter a chamber, and because I could leave without asking anyone to move.

For several days I would think I had chosen it for myself.

Then, putting a hand behind me to steady my weight, I would find the shallow place where a wrist had rested before. My wrist would fit. Beside my heel there would be another small accommodation.

I would not know whether these were old, or whether the root had begun making room while I sat.

That uncertainty would keep me there.

My body would resemble the inhabitants’ bodies, though my back panels would be narrow and slightly uneven. When folded, one would overlap the other too far. When spread against a wall, they would leave a small strip unattached. I could rest well enough, but some part of me would always remain aware of the chamber: a footstep beyond the entrance, a young one shifting on the sleeping leaf, the minute sound of water arriving.

I would be slow to join and slow to come away.

Someone waiting to travel with me would learn to sit down rather than stand at the door.

My work, if it became work, would begin with an interrupted journey.

An inhabitant would arrive at the root where I sat, place two fingers against it, and tell me something I could not yet understand. Then they would go down the left corridor. Much later, another would arrive and follow the same route. I would watch them return. The passage beyond the rounded leaf sheaths had closed.

The next traveler, I would stop.

I would take them a little way down the other corridor, to where it divided beneath a low arch. I would show them the turn I had found while looking for a dry place to rest. They would press their fingers to the arch, wait, then continue.

After that, people would sometimes pause beside me.

I would learn the routes immediately around the shelter. Not the whole country. An hour away, I would be as liable as anyone to find that a familiar opening no longer admitted a shoulder. But near my two entrances I would know which floor gave under a heavy traveler, which upright stem could support the one with the shortened leg, which well had received water recently.

There would be no map that I could lay before someone.

I would walk the first part with them. At a junction I would put my hand where they could put theirs. Sometimes that would suffice. Sometimes we would have to go farther together.

I think I would enjoy being useful in this small, local way. I would also get things wrong.

Once I would send someone toward a passage I had used that morning, and they would return too tired to be courteous. I would help them attach in the chamber. While the fullness slowly came back into their hands, I would sit near the entrance, unable to tell whether my presence comforted them or prevented them from resting.

Afterward, I would stop saying that a way was open. I would say when I had last passed through it.

The shelter would acquire accommodations for people who stayed only briefly.

A low recess for a child’s hand. A place where a traveler could attach without unfolding both back panels. A firmer edge along the sleeping leaf, useful to someone who had difficulty standing.

I would help persuade these into being, then discover that I had made one of the recesses at my own height instead of the height needed. For a long time I would kneel beside it, holding my palm lower.

The others would come and help.

There would still be periods when no one arrived. I would sit outside in the yellow-green brightness, listening to the root beneath me. Across the corridor, a blade would remain partly furled. I would have looked at it so often that I could recognize the little irregularity along its edge from a distance.

I would wonder whether I was waiting for someone.

Sometimes I would get up and visit the well. Sometimes I would leave for several days.

On returning, I might find an inhabitant resting in the chamber, their threads joined where mine usually joined. I would settle outside and let them wake in their own time. It would take effort not to examine every surface for changes.

Eventually someone would bring me the empty casing.

Perhaps it would be the inhabitant who had held it toward the visitor. Perhaps the casing would have passed through several hands by then. I would be allowed to support it on my fingertip, and I would turn it carefully, trying to see how the six legs had drawn free.

The opening would be smaller than I expected.

I would look at the person who had brought it. They would look back. We would touch the same stem, but whatever passed between us would not settle what either of us wanted to know.

I would not keep the casing. They had cared for it longer than I had.

But afterward I would pay more attention to the small animals. One might cross the floor while I was waiting for my ankle threads to release. I would follow it with my eyes until it went beneath a fold I could not lift without disturbing the growth.

For the first time, I might remain sitting after I was free to rise.

That is where I find myself most clearly: beside the two-entrance shelter, detached but not yet going anywhere, with one hand still resting on the floor.

Someone approaches along the corridor. I recognize their careful third step and move over on the root to make room. They sit beside me. For a while neither of us asks about the way ahead.
