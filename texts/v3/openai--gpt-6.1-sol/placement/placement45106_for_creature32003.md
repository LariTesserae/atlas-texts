I would find myself below the clearing, where the evening valve can be heard before it can be seen.

There is room there for a hand, if the hand enters flat and turns only after passing the first root. Beyond that, the passage widens. Water moves over an old piece of timber with three square nail holes in it. One hole is empty. One contains a blackened nail. Through the third, a root has grown and then divided, so that the timber cannot be lifted without cutting something still alive.

I think I would begin in that fastening.

At first there would be very little to distinguish me from the things holding it together. Pressure on one side. Water on the other. The gradual softening of wood along its grain. When Mara crosses the clearing, her weight reaches this place through several layers, diminished and spread out, but still recognizably a footstep. When she sets the bowl of washers on the table, there is a brief, close shiver.

I would learn those two things before I learned anything about her.

For a long time, my whole world might consist of differences in pressure. The clearing is heavier when its puddle is full. The timber bends farther in the afternoon. Something upstream opens, and cool water touches an edge that has been dry since morning.

Then there is the other difference: a pressure that changes because I change it.

A narrow strip has split from the old sluice gate. It remains attached at one end, under a root collar. Its free end lies across a smaller passage. Fresh fibers have entered the split and run lengthwise through the softened wood. As they swell, the strip bows. As they empty, it settles.

That would be the first part of me I could move.

Not much. Enough to catch a floating leaf fragment. Enough to let it go.

I would spend whole days finding out how little movement was necessary.

The rest of my body would be less definite. Some of it would be the old timber, which does not grow. Some would be the pale fibers within it, which do. I would feel a pull beyond the nail hole without being able to answer it. There would be a patch along my underside where cold water made everything distinct, and another where sensation ended in a blunt, permanent numbness.

I would not know whether that numb part belonged to me. Mara would have similar uncertainties when she eventually tried to draw me.

She would discover me because the little passage stayed clear.

It used to block twice a week. She would come with a hooked wire, kneel on the clearing, and reach down through the inspection gap. Then, for a month, there would be nothing to remove.

She would record that.

Later she would find three bits of stalk collected in the pocket downstream, all too large to have passed through the gap as it usually stood.

The first time she touched my movable strip, I would close against the wire.

She would stop pulling.

Above me, the pressure of her knees would shift. The wire would go slack. After a while she would withdraw it, and I would feel the small wake travel along my underside.

The next morning she would return with a spoon.

Its bowl would slide beneath the strip, smooth and cool. I would press against it. She would ease it downward. I would follow until the old wood began to hurt, then hold still.

“All right,” she would say.

I would know her voice as a tremor through the floor. Those two words would mean, for a while, that the pressure was about to stop.

She would not dig me out. There would be too much resting on the timber, and no obvious place to put me if she did. Instead she would enlarge the inspection opening and fit a lid over it, cutting back the roots around its hinge each time she made her morning circuit.

Sometimes she would lift it merely to look.

I would come to recognize the lamp by its warmth. In that warmth I could lift my free edge a little farther. She would put the lamp down nearby while she cleaned the channel, and take it away when she was finished.

Our first useful agreement would concern a pebble.

She would place it where I could reach it. If I held the passage open too long, the clearing above became soft. If I let it close entirely, water backed up toward a bearing. The pebble, tucked under my edge, would leave a narrow, reliable gap.

I would dislike it at first. Its weight fell on a tender place.

She would try another, flatter one.

That one I would keep.

I would have no view of the roofs, or the yellow rail, or Mara taking off her shoes. Most of what she did would happen beyond my reach. I might learn that she was away without learning where she had gone. I might feel rain arrive long before I understood that there was a sky.

But I would have things of my own to attend to. A new fiber growing across the passage. Grit gathering under the pebble. The slight looseness developing around the black nail.

And there would be time when nothing needed doing, when the water slipped beneath me at the proper depth and I could rest my weight on the stone she had chosen.

In the ledger, Mara might call me a living flap. Later she might cross out *flap* and write *keeper*, though there would still be a question mark beside it.

I would be there when she rested her foot in the puddle each evening. Her weight would reach me; the valve would groan; the water would begin to fall.

Sometimes, after the usual sounds had finished, I would lift my strip and let it touch the underside of the inspection lid.

A small knock.

Above me, she would pause before taking her foot away.
