I would live beneath a stair that had recently gained a step.

The room would not have been intended for habitation. It would be a clearance left between two foundation ribs, with a basin at its lowest point and a service hatch through which, twice a year, someone would need to pass a shoulder. My lease would require me to be absent on those days. In practice, the inspector and I would have breakfast together, and I would hold the basin aside while she worked.

There would be space for a sleeping fold, a warming plate, and one visitor, provided neither of us wanted to stand.

For a long time I would think that I had chosen the room because it was inexpensive. Then a pressure door would be replaced three streets away, and I would discover that I had also chosen it for a sound: a double knock followed by a faint scrape, arriving through the left foundation rib. Without it, I would lie awake.

I would not be a sitter. I would have tried.

In the training room I would feel too many things and give them all the same importance. A colony tightening around a drain would trouble me as much as a foundation slipping. Asked which wall people should move away from, I would be reluctant to choose.

This would be kindly described as an unsuitable sensitivity.

They would find other work for me.

My work would be to check the handrails after the settling.

Not their strength; someone else would do that. I would check what they said.

A handrail’s markings can remain intact while becoming wrong. A shallow turn grows steeper. A landing moves far enough that the warning comes too late. A doorway seals behind a room-sized animal, but the rail continues to promise a passage. In a district without much light, these mistakes accumulate until someone arrives with a hot basin in both hands and steps confidently where there is no longer a floor.

I would carry a strip of warmed wax beneath my sleeve. At disputed places I would press it against the rail, taking the pattern away with me. Back in my room, I would lay the impressions across my knees and compare them with the district record.

I would become good at recognizing an old correction.

Someone, thirty winters earlier, had deepened a groove to help a person whose fingertips were losing sensation. Someone else had softened a junction mark because children kept mistaking it for the sign for home. These alterations were seldom recorded. The formal pattern remained in the register; the useful one lived under people’s hands.

My supervisor would tell me not to preserve every irregularity.

I would tell her I didn’t.

We would have this conversation often.

---

The first animal I would know well would live inside a rail coupling on my route.

It would be narrow and colorless, with six plates overlapping around a soft center. When the rail flexed, the plates separated slightly. Its feeding fringe reached into the condensation beneath the sleeve.

At first I would try to remove it.

It occupied the exact place where I needed to fit a gauge. I would ease a warmed tool beneath its rim, and it would tighten. I would wait, try from the other side, and make the same mistake.

An older repairer would tell me to come back after the settling.

By then the animal would be loose enough to lift. But when I fitted the gauge, I would discover that the coupling had developed a small, persistent vibration. The animal had been damping it. Without that soft center pressed into the joint, the rail blurred two neighboring signals together.

I would put it back.

Thereafter, my reports would say:

*Coupling serviceable. Leave occupant.*

The phrase would spread to other reports. Eventually somebody would ask for a specification for the occupant.

I would have no useful answer.

I would learn to touch the rail twice before opening the sleeve. After several months the animal would draw its fringe out of the way. After several years it would begin doing so when I arrived on the landing.

This would make me proud in a way I would find embarrassing. It had learned my weight. I had not taught it anything that it could not have learned from a leaking pipe, given sufficient regularity.

Nevertheless, on mornings when it withdrew before I touched the coupling, I would feel welcomed.

---

There would be a place on the western route where the rail was almost smooth.

The register would identify it as damaged.

It would be level with a standing person’s hip, immediately before a bridge carrying three households and a public brine tap. Hundreds of hands passed over it every day. Yet the wear would be concentrated in a length scarcely wider than my palm.

I would bring a new sleeve and the small cutter used for restoring patterns.

Before I began, a woman coming from the tap would catch my wrist.

“Not there.”

She would be carrying a basin fastened to her forearm. Her coat would be wet down one side. I would notice that she had stopped with her body turned awkwardly, taking care not to let the basin touch the rail.

I would ask what the place marked.

She would give me a name.

I would explain that I could move the remembrance a little farther from the junction. The approach marking had become difficult to distinguish.

“No,” she would say. “He stopped here.”

That would be the whole explanation.

I would make my correction farther back. The formal interval would be wrong, but a person could still read it safely.

Later I would learn that the man had repaired heating fields. He had paused at that rail each morning to wait for his daughter, who was slow fastening her boots. After she grew up and moved to another district, he continued pausing there.

People had noticed only after his death.

I would not put all this in the record. I would write:

*Approach marking moved upstream. Local use retained.*

Soon I would have several such entries.

A worn place where a woman had rested her forehead after night work. Three shallow taps beside a lift, made by someone waiting for a partner who could not hear the capsule dock. A section polished underneath, where a child had held on before growing tall enough to reach over it.

Most would still be ordinary damage. I would replace loose sleeves, clear mineral crust, cut fresh warnings into places made dangerous by the rising stone.

But I would begin asking before I cut.

---

As I grew older, I would lose some sensation in the fingers I used most.

This would not happen dramatically. A groove would seem shallower than it was. I would misread a wax impression, check it again, and blame the wax.

For a while I would use warmer strips.

Then I would acquire an apprentice who pressed too hard and distrusted animals.

She would dislike my room. She would say there was nowhere to put her feet. She would bring her own breakfast and complain that my warming plate took too long.

On the route she would ask why we were keeping so many unofficial marks.

I would answer badly. I would tell her about people she had never known. I would become impatient when she confused one name with another.

Eventually, at a railing overlooking a shaft, she would say, “You know what all of them mean. The rest of us just have to find the bridge.”

She would be right.

We would spend a winter separating the warnings from the remembrances where we could. Some could be moved onto the wall without losing their place. Some households would object. One would thank us. At two junctions we would leave everything alone.

The work would go faster once I stopped telling every story.

At the coupling, the little animal would hold shut for her.

She would accuse me of enjoying this.

I would enjoy it briefly, then regret that.

We would spend six visits teaching it her approach. Her weight arrived through the landing differently from mine, and her second touch came too soon. She would learn to wait. The animal would learn what she was waiting for.

The first morning it opened before she touched the sleeve, she would call me over.

Neither of us would mention the reports.

---

By then, the stair above my room would have acquired another step.

The inspector would suggest moving my sleeping fold before the clearance narrowed further. I would delay, then arrange for a room nearer the outer wall, where the cold made visitors impatient.

On my last evening under the stair, I would sit with the field still warm and listen for the double knock. The newer door had never quite reproduced it. There was only one knock now, and the scrape came earlier.

My apprentice would arrive to help carry my things. There would be very little to carry: tools, clothes, a bundle of wax impressions I had been told several times to discard.

She would take the bundle without comment.

Before leaving, I would put my hand against the rail outside the hatch. I would have touched it every morning for years without intending to make a mark. There would be a slight hollow beneath the fingers, too shallow to read unless you knew it was there.

I would consider showing her.

But she would already be farther up the stair, waiting for me at the new step.

I would close the hatch and follow.
