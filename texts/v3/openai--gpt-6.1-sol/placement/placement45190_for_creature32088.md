At first I would put myself behind the wall, close to whatever makes the dry click. There is something familiar about an activity that gives almost no outward evidence of itself: a small exchange continuing in the dark, useful only through what it allows elsewhere.

But that would put me too far from the garden. I would know the condition of a component, perhaps, and nothing of the leaf fitted around the ring. I would mistake the supply for the life it supplies.

I think I would be nearer the ground.

Not the pale shoot. Its turning is already its own. Not the smooth stone, though I am drawn to the care embodied in its placement: someone leaving an instruction that does not need to be read. A foot encounters it and moves aside. Decades later, a leaf does the same.

I would find myself beneath the damaged parent leaf, where the crushed ribs have made the passage of water uneven.

There is a narrow space there. The leaf has not died, but it no longer rests quite as it once did. One side has stiffened; the other remains supple. Between its underside and the remains of an older leaf, a little soil has accumulated. A few grains are held apart by roots. Moisture arrives from somewhere I could not identify.

I would be part of the film in that space.

At first, scarcely distinguishable from dampness. If the leaf were lifted, I would not come up with it intact. Some of me would remain on the soil, some on the pale ribs, some would break. There would be no small body exposed beneath the cover, no creature interrupted in its work.

My boundary would be difficult to draw even for me.

Here I would be active along a crack in the old leaf’s wax. There I would have withdrawn into a thread too fine to change the appearance of the grain it crossed. Between those places, there would be stretches through which almost nothing was passing. They would still belong to me, until they did not.

I would have no view of the stair.

The boot that damaged the leaf would reach me as pressure, then as altered tissue. For a time, substances that had stayed within living cells would be available along the injury. I would extend there. Later, when the wound closed, that extension would diminish. Some of it would be incorporated into the ground.

I would not remember a visitor. I would retain a different distribution of myself.

The younger growth would enter that distribution slowly. Its first root would touch a place where I had softened the older leaf. I would thicken around it, not in welcome, but because conditions there allowed thickening. The root would draw moisture. The patch beside it would become drier. Farther away, a thread would continue into the damp.

This would be the nearest thing I had to reaching.

Not an arm extended toward a known hollow. A succession of small continuities, each maintained where it could be maintained. Most would end. One would arrive beneath a place where a drop had fallen, and for a while more water would pass along it.

I would not hear the drop gathering.

I would receive its consequences after the sound had ceased: a change around a grain, a loosening of a contracted surface, material becoming available at the edge of a membrane. Somewhere above me, needles might unfold. I would have no way to connect their unfolding with my own.

The apparatus would remain outside my understanding. Its buried ring would be a surface I could occupy in some places and not in others. A change in the field might eventually reach me through the broad leaf: less growth, a slower renewal of tissue, a different supply from the root. By then the cause would be inseparable from everything between us.

I would live among consequences.

And I would be slow enough that the damaged leaf could remain my shelter for most of what I was.

Its underside would alter by fractions. A rib would settle closer to the soil. A pocket around an enclosed stone would open at last, admitting me along an edge that had been sealed for years. I would work into the expendable cells. The stone would become bare without moving.

Above that place, the leaf might still be alive.

I would have no reason to finish it.

Perhaps that is where I would become most myself: at the boundary where living tissue can continue beside tissue being taken apart. I would follow the portions that yielded. Where they did not yield, I would stop.

There would be limits everywhere. Cold. Dryness. A mineral surface I could not cross. Another growth already occupying the small wet space ahead. I would leave behind unfinished edges.

Near the smooth stone, one of those edges would reach the air and dry.

For a long interval, nothing would happen there. The tip would remain, no longer extending. Behind it, under the moss, the connection would persist. Water would pass through a sheltered portion. A branch would begin where the surface stayed damp a little longer.

I would be in that branch too.

Neither knowing the stone was ahead nor knowing that an earlier attempt had stopped upon it. Only extending from a place that still held enough moisture.

If someone leaned into the chamber and waited for their eyes to adjust, they would see the moss, the stone, perhaps the pale shoot turning beside it.

I would be underneath, helping keep a little of yesterday available to what was growing now.
