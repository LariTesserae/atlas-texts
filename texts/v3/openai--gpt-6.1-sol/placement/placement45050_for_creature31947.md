At first I would sit on the slab, with my hands between my knees, trying not to occupy more of the courtyard than my body required.

But the slab is too exposed. I would keep looking beneath it, where the leaf-wrapped stores lie, and beyond those to the place where its supporting pillar meets the wall. There is a gap there. A root has entered it and grown thick, but has not quite closed it. Behind the root, the stone looks darker.

That is where I would find myself.

Not in the nest beneath the lifted flagstone. That chamber already belongs to someone. Not in the blocked doorway, within reach of the folded cloth. I would live farther into the construction, in a piece of drainage that no longer drains.

One of the clay elbows turns inside the wall instead of out into the courtyard. Perhaps it was fitted incorrectly. Perhaps the wall was rebuilt around it. Its lower curve holds a little fine grit; its upper surface is dry. A crack admits the reflected light in a line so thin that, crossing my body, it might look like a seam.

I would have to be small.

At first I imagine something with a shell, able to close itself away. But a shell would catch in the bend. I would have a soft back instead, with a few overlapping plates along it, flexible enough to slip past a rough edge. The plates would be the brown of soaked paper. Between them, my skin would show pale and nearly transparent.

There would be legs tucked close beneath me. More than four, though you would have difficulty counting them when I moved. My front pair would be longer, and I would use them to feel around a corner before putting my head through.

My head would be blunt. My eyes would be small, good enough to distinguish a leaf from the opening behind it. Most of what I knew would come through the pads of my feet and the fine hairs beside my mouth.

I would know the inhabitant by its approach through the paving. Not by a single recognizable tread, but by the pauses: weight, stillness, a lighter pressure elsewhere, stillness again. I would come to the lip of the elbow when it passed.

Sometimes it would stop.

It would not bring me out into the light or examine my plates. It would put a fingertip against the clay, just below the crack, and wait. I might touch the other side. The clay would remain between us.

My place would be near the water, though not in it. A drop large enough to fill the inhabitant’s spoon would be a considerable thing to me. If it entered my elbow, I would climb to the dry upper curve and wait for it to withdraw.

But the thin dampness left afterward would suit me. I would feed on something growing at its edge, a film too slight to have a color until seen against white clay. I would graze slowly, leaving interrupted tracks.

There would be work I did without knowing it as work. Grit would lodge between my plates. When I passed through the narrowest part of the pipe, it would rub off behind me. Over time, this would keep one passage clear and obstruct another.

The inhabitant would notice.

One day it would put the wooden pin into my passage. I would retreat from the unfamiliar length of it, pressing all my feet against the upper clay. The pin would loosen a little ridge of grit. A breath of colder air would reach me.

I would remain hidden long after it had gone.

Later, coming out, I would find a flake of bark set beneath the opening. Its rough surface would give my feet purchase where the glaze was smooth. I would use it for many days before it occurred to me, if such things could occur to me, that it had been placed there.

I would not spend all my time in the pipe. There is a route beneath the shelves that the snails cannot use. I would follow it to the hanging pot and climb among the roots exposed beneath its broken bottom. Here the bright air passes through my hairs. Here I can turn around without folding myself.

I would linger there. Some nights, if there are nights in this place, I would go no farther.

I would also make mistakes. I would enter an empty shell and find the turn inside too tight. I would lose a foot to a closing crevice and grow a shorter one. Once, crossing the white wall, I would become visible to the bird.

Its head would turn.

I would flatten myself into a chipped patch of plaster, plates lifted at their edges, and remain there until the light had changed enough to hide me. Afterward I would avoid the wall for a long time, though it offered the shortest way home.

If you knelt near my opening, I might come forward to investigate the warmth of your breath. You would see two fine feelers, then a pale mouth working against the clay.

Your sleeve would touch the paving. I would withdraw.

When the inhabitant came to collect the water from your cuff, I would feel its weight settle nearby. I would wait while the spoon filled and was carried away. Then I would come out onto the bark flake.

A little dampness would remain where your sleeve had been.

I would take the longer route to reach it, keeping beneath the leaves.
