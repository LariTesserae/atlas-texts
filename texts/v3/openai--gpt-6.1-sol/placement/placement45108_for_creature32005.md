At first I would be in the passage beneath someone’s floor.

Not trapped. There would be room to turn my head, though not to turn it while also keeping my knees where they were. Water would run along the lower side of the passage. I would have one foot above it and the other in it, because I had put that foot down before discovering which of the two dark surfaces was stone.

Someone would be waiting at the opening.

I would know this from the cord lying against my ankle. It would move a little, then stop. The person holding it would know better than to tug while I was reaching.

I would be looking for a bead.

It would have gone farther than anyone expected. Dropped through a floor seam, it had struck a sloping chip and rolled beneath the partition into the next household’s drain. By the time they sent for me, its owner would have described it several times: pale, drilled slightly off center, with one flat side that was not easy to see.

All the loose things in that passage would have a flat side. I would bring back a fragment of tile first.

Then a stone with a hole that did not go through.

The third thing would be the bead.

I would hold it in my mouth while I came out, not because it was especially valuable but because my hands were needed. Its owner would wash it before touching it to her sleeve.

For a while, I think, that would be my place in the city: being sent into the part of a room that its occupants could no longer reach.

I would not be the smallest person. There would be children much smaller, and some adults with narrower shoulders. But I would be good at remembering the arrangement behind an opening. I would remember that a passage widened beyond the second turn, or that the wall on the left was stacked rather than mortared, and should not be used to push against.

People would gradually stop describing these things to me.

This would please me more than it ought to.

***

My own room would be above a drain, with a low shelf along one wall and a corner in which it was possible to sit without feeling the water through the floor. I would have inherited the shelf from someone who had used it for bowls. Two shallow hollows would remain where their bases had rested.

I would keep my flint in one hollow and nothing in the other.

Not a memorial, exactly. There would simply be no object I owned that sat well in it.

The room would have a door made of three unequal flakes tied together. It would close properly only when lifted a little. Someone would offer to repair it, and I would say there was no need. Later, carrying food in one hand and a borrowed wedge in the other, I would be unable to open it and would have to wait for a neighbor.

The neighbor would not mention the earlier offer.

For several days I would dislike them for this.

I would eat while sitting on the shelf, with my heels against the opposite wall. My settling vessel would produce a soft sheet that tore if folded too soon. I would prefer the firmer food from the upper houses but claim that mine had a finer texture.

There would be somebody who knew the difference between my preferences and my claims. They would bring me a piece of the firm food now and then, without making an occasion of it.

When they visited, we would work the seams of our clothes. Sometimes they would put their wrist in my hand to tell me something and leave it there afterward. I would have to loosen my grip to return to the sleeve.

***

Eventually I would become too broad through the chest for the passage beneath the floor.

I would discover this before anyone else did. I would go in to retrieve something ordinary—a wedge, perhaps—and reach the turn where I had always moved one shoulder ahead of the other. This time the movement would not work.

Backing out would be slow. I would have to leave the wedge.

At the entrance, I would tell the person waiting that it had gone too far.

The next day a child would bring it back.

There would be a period when I continued to receive requests and had to decide which ones I could still manage. I would learn to ask about openings I once knew without asking. The answers would occasionally irritate me. People would hold their hands apart to show widths I remembered exactly.

One household would ask whether I could go with their daughter instead.

She would already have made the journey once. They wanted someone outside who understood the route.

I would describe the loose wall to her, then ask her to show me how she intended to pass it. She would put her wrist forward impatiently. I would touch the instruction again, more slowly.

She would retrieve what they needed. On the way out, she would stop somewhere I could not see.

The cord would lie still in my fingers.

I would nearly pull it.

Then she would continue, and emerge carrying an additional chip she thought might be useful for their doorstep. We would disagree about that. She would keep the chip.

Afterward I would begin carrying cords of different lengths.

***

The work would not have a name at first. A person needed to move a basin through an unlit lane. An old man had stopped trusting the familiar groove beside his door because a repair had changed its edge. Two neighbors wanted to exchange shelves, but neither shelf could pass through the other’s entrance without being tilted in a particular order.

I would go ahead, or remain at the narrowest place, and let them know when to move.

I would have to learn not to give the next instruction too soon. Knowing what came after a turn would make me impatient with the person still making it.

A basin would break that way.

I would have both hands on its carrier’s wrist when we heard it go. For a moment neither of us would move. Then they would make the refusal gesture, although I had not yet asked anything.

I would help collect the pieces. Most would become packing beneath a repaired stair. One curved piece would remain in the household, holding food.

They would not ask for my help again for a long time.

***

I would also be asked to guide the forked tool when a body was passed into a recess.

This would be different work. There would be no one inside to answer.

The first time, I would keep explaining the angle to the two people holding the shaft. One of them would finally take my hand and tell me that they understood.

After that I would stand where I could see the edge of the wrapping. I would touch a wrist only when something needed changing.

I would learn which openings had space beyond them and which required the body to be turned. That knowledge would make people seek me out. Some would want to know well in advance.

One woman would ask me to look at a cavity while she was still able to come along. She would lean beside me, her knee against my knee, and inspect the opening.

“Not this one,” she would say against my ear.

I would find her another.

She would live long enough that I had to check it twice for changes.

***

So I think I would become a person who knew the backs of walls.

Not all of them. A few passages in my quarter, several elsewhere, one difficult opening near the square. I would occasionally be taken to a place I had never seen and expected to know what to do. I would disappoint people there.

I would still visit the passage where I had retrieved the bead. The child who could enter it would grow, and another would take her place. I would stand outside, holding the cord loosely enough that it could move without passing an instruction.

When the great vibration came from above, I would put my free hand on the wall.

There would be someone beyond the turn. I would feel the cord shift as they stopped.

I could not see the ceiling over them. I could not be certain whether the loose partition had moved. I would wait through the noise with my hand against the stone, until the ordinary trembling of the sluice could be felt again.

Then I would give the signal to come back.

At the opening, they would hand me whatever they had gone in for. I would set it aside before examining their hands.

If there was no damage, they would be impatient to leave. They would have other things to do. I would let them go, coil the cord, and take the recovered object to its owner.

On the way home I would pass a notice carved into the wall beside a low crossing. The lettering would be deep enough to read with a fingertip:

**WAIT FOR THE PERSON AHEAD TO TURN.**

I would know why it had been put there.

I would still need to touch it sometimes.
