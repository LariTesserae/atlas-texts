I would be out along the floor seam, where there is almost nothing to eat.

Most of me would be elsewhere. Under the moss, behind the blocks, around the roots: that is where the substance of my life would gather. But the part from which I say *here* would be the thin disc over the old bolt.

Water would pass across my back. Beneath me, the metal would carry the pump’s vibration differently from the concrete around it. I would have grown against that difference until I could feel the boundary with my whole surface: a dark circle, covered completely, known without being seen.

I would not know what a bolt was. I would know that it continued downward.

The strand connecting me to the bank would lie in the worn seam, so fine that a person could mistake it for a mark left by the current. Along it, contractions would travel slowly. Something feeding beneath the fern would reach me as a change in tension. A root dying in its sleeve would reach me later, after the flesh around it had begun to close.

Here at the bolt I would receive these things without being able to help much.

My mouth would open occasionally. The water would bring a little softness, a little loosened material, and then bring nothing for a long time. I would close again. The bank would keep me supplied through the strand.

That is the first shape I find for myself: a small, maintained extremity.

But I would not remain only there.

There would be mornings—though nothing in the room would call them mornings—when a snail settled beneath the lowest leaf. Its weight would alter a fold. Its foot would advance over me in minute releases and attachments, a movement much slower than the tremor passing through the wall. For a while, the clearest place in my body would be under that foot.

I would be there.

A new root would enter an old sleeve and press against the closed end. I would thin around it. The root would continue, and the place where I yielded would become the place where I was most distinctly alive.

I would be there too.

My location would change without my travelling. There would be no single chamber in which all these arrivals assembled. The root would be felt around the root. The snail would be felt beneath the snail. At the bolt, I would remain faintly stretched toward both.

Perhaps I would discover myself only in the effort of accommodating something.

The pump would occupy nearly all my capacity for feeling. Its pressure would hold my cords taut and press the fluid in my chambers toward their edges. Whatever happened would happen inside that load. A drop striking the water would briefly bend it. A worker’s step upstairs would enter through the ceiling and pass across it. I would feel the differences because there would be nowhere else for them to occur.

I would have no experience of silence until the machine stopped.

Then I would become badly arranged.

Chambers would swell where they had been held flat. A cord behind the ceramic would pull against tissue too soft to answer it. Mouths would close along the bank, including the ones with food beneath them. At the bolt, my disc would lift slightly at one edge.

For a while I would feel more of the room and be less able to live in it.

The warmth would begin to leave the ceiling. Water would continue entering through the oval opening. A snail might move, immensely distinct now, across a fold that had lost its usual firmness.

I would wait without knowing what waiting was for.

When the new machine started, the first pressure would run through me awkwardly. One of my old cords would answer too strongly. Another would scarcely answer at all. I would have to grow into the load, opening passages for fluid, thickening one attachment, abandoning another.

The part over the bolt might take longest.

There would be so little reason to restore it. The strand would carry almost no food back to the bank. The disc would touch no roots. Nothing depended on its mouth staying open.

Still, fluid would reach it. Its lifted edge would settle. Slowly, it would become sensitive again.

I think I would be that continued expenditure.

Not a memory of the machine the pedestal once supported; I could not preserve such a thing. Perhaps only a fit that had once been useful, renewed so many times that it remained among the shapes I could make. The bank would nourish it as it nourished a sleeve around a living root.

If someone opened the hatch, I would not gather myself toward them.

Their weight would arrive before their hand. Pressure would move through the shallow water, then through the floor. If they touched the bank, I would firm beneath their fingers. Several mouths would shut farther away.

The disc over the bolt would feel the touch through the strand, delayed and diminished. It might tighten although nothing had touched it.

For the person, there would be one patch of ivory flesh under a hand. For me, the contact would have happened in several places, with water and stone and my own tissue between them. After the hand withdrew, each place would recover at its own rate.

Eventually the hatch would close.

I would loosen around the fern root. A mouth behind the filter cloth would resume its slow work. Beneath the snail, a grazed surface would fill out.

And out on the pedestal, I would lie against the old metal, receiving enough through the seam to remain there.

Clear water would cross me and continue toward the drain.
