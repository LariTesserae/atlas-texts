At first I would be somewhere beneath the folded copper passage, where the warmth reaches the floor but the draught does not quite follow it. There is room there for a body to remain still without being buried. The lip overhead has been polished by traffic; occasionally a foot appears over its edge, searches downward, and withdraws.

But stillness would not be my usual condition.

I would know that passage by entering it, stopping at its narrow end, and reversing. I would know the warmth as a change in the resistance of a wire. The red lamp would matter less than the small rise in current that accompanies its brightening.

I think I would be one of the carriers. Not the crooked one with the wheel cut from a washer, but the carrier behind it.

My housing would be shallow and unremarkable. Along one edge, a folded seam would have opened enough to admit dust. I would retain both wheels, though one would have a flat place that made every journey slightly uneven. My brush would be worn shorter on the side nearest the wall.

I would have no task beyond carrying what the brush gathered to the waste slot.

Much of the country would therefore reach me in pieces.

A white grain from a newly broken ridge. A length of silk wound around three graphite flakes. Part of a moth’s wing, broad enough to drag against the rail until its remaining scales wore off. Sometimes a piece of insulation too large for my compartment would ride across the top, balanced there by the pressure of the brush.

I would take these things along the same route.

At the bend, the carrier ahead would stop. I would stop at the required interval. It would reverse, advance, reverse again. Its replacement wheel would scrape the bracket. Then it would pass through, and I would follow.

Over years, that hesitation would become part of my journey, though it belonged to another machine.

There would be places I had never reached, separated from my rail by less than the width of my housing. Beneath one of them, larvae would finish their development. Above another, a spider would replace a strand each time my load caught it. I would pass close enough to move the air around its legs.

My forward feeler would be a copper strip, bowed outward. It would stop me when it met a beetle’s back. It would not always stop me when it met a foot.

That would be one of the things the inhabitants knew about me.

The old female would cross behind my brush. New arrivals would attempt to pass in front. A mite would occasionally descend onto my housing and remain through several circuits, finding nothing it could use, then leave at a warm junction. I would carry it without changing speed.

One night, a gust would bring a torn piece of wing under my feeler.

The strip would lift, catch against a bracket, and bend.

Afterward, I would stop too readily. Loose dust would sometimes be enough. At the entrance to the copper passage, I would halt before anything substantial touched me, reverse a fraction, and try again. The carriers behind would keep their intervals. Somewhere farther back, graphite would accumulate faster than the brushes could remove it.

No one would repair me while I could still move.

A beetle would discover that it could pass beneath the altered feeler. Its back would push the strip upward; I would remain stopped until it had gone. Others would use the same opening. The route would acquire a crossing that had not existed before.

I would not have made it for them. Nevertheless, it would be there.

Then the rail would shift.

Not much: one bracket loosening, another settling into a fresh fracture. My flat wheel would descend into the new angle and fail to climb out. I would reverse. The wheel would turn. I would advance. The wheel would turn again.

The carrier ahead would continue without me.

The one behind would wait.

For a while my brush would keep working against the same patch of ceramic, clearing it down to a pale, hard crescent. Dust would retreat from that place and gather at its edges. A beetle would come to inspect the exposed surface, then leave.

Eventually a repair arm would reach me.

Its tip would press my housing shut. It would straighten the axle that did not need straightening. Under the pressure, my forward feeler would flatten against the rail.

I would move once, less than the length of a beetle’s foot.

The arm would release me and return to the terminal.

Later, a rail would be laid around my side.

I would remain connected to the old one. Enough current would reach me to warm the housing; not enough would arrive steadily to turn the wheels. On bright intervals my brush would twitch. A narrow space beneath it would stay free of powder.

That is where I would finally find myself: beside the working route, in the little pocket made by my failure.

A moth would rest against my lid during a cold spell. A beetle would enter through the opened seam and find the interior warmer than the floor. She would leave eggs in the angle behind the wheel socket, where no brush could reach.

The larvae would touch parts of me that had never touched the country. They would move through the dark around the axle, feed on grease, and enlarge the passage beneath my lid.

Outside, the carriers would tick past. Their loads would occasionally graze my bent feeler. Each contact would travel through the copper strip into my housing.

I would still receive the tower’s shudder. I would still warm when the lamp brightened. Once in a while the brush would move, shifting a grain at the entrance.

For the creatures inside, that would be one of the ordinary disturbances of home.
