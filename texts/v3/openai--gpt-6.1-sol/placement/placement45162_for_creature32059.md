At first I put myself on the high shelf, where the bright seams can be seen opening across the roof. I would like to watch a whole stream come into existence: the animals turning before there is water, the first fragments thrown from the vent, the banks acquiring their brief population.

But that is a place to visit. I don’t think I would live there.

I move lower, toward the houses. For a little while I am someone carrying one end of a roof panel, walking backward, trusting the person at the other end to tell me where to put my feet. Then I am beneath the panel, in its damp shade. Beneath the house altogether.

There is a space between one stone leg and the slab on which it rests. It looks too narrow to contain anything substantial. A dark margin shows along one edge, like hide caught under a heavy object.

That is where I find myself.

I am wider than I am long, with a low, pliant body and a back divided into small overlapping plates. Most of me is pressed out of sight. Around my edges are short limbs that can fold flat or reach into irregularities in the stone. My underside is wet. When lifted, I leave a print like a mouth on glass.

People keep creatures like me under the feet of houses.

They call us heels.

We are not strong enough to hold a house up. The stone does that. We occupy the slight unevenness between stone and stone, in the places where a hard edge would otherwise take too much of the weight. Our plates settle against one another. Our flesh thickens where there is room and withdraws where there is none.

A well-set heel is almost invisible.

I eat the films that grow in the dampness beneath the leg. Occasionally someone pushes a strip of fresh crust into my exposed margin. I take hold of it slowly. By morning its underside is clean.

The household knows which edge to feed. On the other side, near the dry channel, I bite.

***

Pressure is most of my world.

I can feel someone climbing the house steps. The load comes down through the leg in small, separate arrivals. The woman who dresses hides carries herself unevenly; she injured a foot before I was brought here. One child jumps from the second step. Another sits on it for long periods, scraping something with a stolen tooth.

Inside the house, an old water vessel occupies a corner above me. When it is full, I have less room to breathe along my left side.

I do not know it as a vessel. I know the weight that diminishes through the day and returns in the evening. Sometimes it shifts a little by itself.

I know the household winged animal more directly. It lands on the ground beside my leg, crouches, and tries to pull my feeding fringe out with its beak.

I shut my plates.

It rattles at me.

For a time after it leaves, I keep them shut.

***

When the ground changes, the pressure ceases to fit.

A house can lean for many ordinary reasons. A grazer brushes a support. Someone drags a basket across the floor. A roof panel lifts in a draft and settles badly. These events pass through me and are gone.

But sometimes one corner of the stone beneath me rises and does not return.

My body shifts away from it. A narrow part of my back becomes exposed. The pressure follows. I shift again.

There is only so much room.

Then I begin to rub two of my outer plates together.

The sound is small, especially in this valley. It resembles a blade being sharpened in another room. People must come close to hear it. A household that keeps a heel learns to listen before sleeping and after the first meal.

The child who sits on the step is good at this. She lies on her belly and puts her cheek near the ground.

Sometimes she brings me food when nothing is wrong.

Sometimes I rasp because grit has worked between my plates, and she fetches an adult who pours water along my edge. I spread myself into the water. The grit comes free. Everyone goes away again.

I cannot tell them what is happening under the village. I can only tell them that I can no longer fit comfortably beneath this particular leg.

***

The afternoon the bell rings, I have already been rubbing for some time.

Dust drops into the space around me. Footsteps multiply overhead. The familiar loads disappear in an unfamiliar order: the water first, then the stored food, then pieces of the roof. With each removal my body opens a little.

The relief is so considerable that I stop making noise.

Outside, animals are passing close enough for their feet to shake the slab. Warm moisture runs beneath me. I put out my feeding fringe.

Then someone kneels beside the leg.

There is a bone wedge, a hide strap, a hand darkened deep into its creases. The person lifts one edge of my slab just enough to loosen me. This must be done slowly. If pulled while bearing weight, a heel tears.

I cling anyway.

I have lived here through several changes of roof, through a birth, through the disappearance of the uneven-footed woman for many days and her return on a carried platform. The stone beneath me has a hollow made to the shape of my underside.

Warm water is poured into that hollow.

My foot loosens. The strap passes beneath me. For the first time in years, nothing is resting on my back.

I am lifted into brightness.

My limbs close around the strap. The luminous roof extends beyond anything I can make use of. Air touches surfaces that have stayed damp and covered for most of my life.

Someone wraps me in a wet scrap of hide.

For the journey uphill I become a small weight against a person’s hip.

***

At the temporary settlement, they place me in a shallow dish beside the salvaged roof plates. Children are sorting the plates by size. One comes over and pushes a flake of stone under my fringe.

I do not eat.

I creep toward the underside of the nearest stack. The dish is tilted to bring me back. Later they put a board across it, and I settle beneath the board.

It is much too light.

There will be a new house. Its feet will rest on slabs cut from ground that is still warm. Someone will inspect me for torn places and rinse the folds of my underside. They will set me beneath a leg, with my rasping edge facing outward.

Then the weight will come down carefully.

I will spread until I find the gaps. Above me, people will bring in their vessels and bedding. A child will test the new steps.

For a while I will have plenty to eat.
