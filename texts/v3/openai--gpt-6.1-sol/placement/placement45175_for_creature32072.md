At first I put myself beside the visitor, holding the door open against its swollen lower edge.

There is something manageable about arriving with a tin of washers. I could learn which tools belong to which outlines, keep my lunch shut against the damp, bring a cloth that is genuinely dry. When the inhabitant came out, I would stand aside. I would try not to mistake its slowness for permission.

But the visitor has a way down the mountain. Even while working, some part of them belongs to the journey home: the last stretch of exposed path, the place where the air becomes easier to breathe, the room in which a coat can be hung and will eventually dry.

I don’t find that journey for myself.

I remain after the tools have been put away. The door closes. Water gathers along the bottom of the window until a drop becomes heavy enough to run, making one clear line through the cloudiness. I can see a portion of the grove through it. Then the line fills in.

I think I would be farther back in the building, near the three pipes entering the rock.

There would be a little recess there, too shallow to be called a room. A person working on the rear fittings would have to turn sideways to avoid it. Warmth would come through its back wall. The motor’s vibration would reach it through the foundations, but unevenly: strong on one side, faint on the other, with a small trembling between them that disappears whenever someone puts a hand on the bench.

I would begin by listening there.

Not with the inhabitant’s skill. A sound would have to recur many times before I knew it was the same sound. I would confuse trouble with weather, and weather with the visitor leaning a wrench against a pipe. For a long time I would think the heavy knock was the important event.

Eventually I would notice what happens just before it.

A faint relaxation. Something under strain allowing itself a little more room.

That is where I would start to take shape.

Small enough to occupy the recess. Broad at the bottom, where I rest against the foundation, and thin above. Perhaps at first I look like an old part that has been left in place because removing it would require shutting down the circulation. A shallow oval housing, a dark seam, two screw holes that no longer line up with anything.

But there would be no screws holding me.

My lower edge would have grown into the rough concrete. Under that edge, fine wet folds would lie against the pipe. My upper surface would be stiff and dull, mineral accumulating on it too slowly to notice. The sheltered underside would remain soft.

I would have no face that the visitor could readily find.

When they first cleaned around me, they might lift one edge with a screwdriver. I would draw away from the point. Not far—there would be nowhere far to go—but enough.

After that, they would clean around me differently.

***

I wouldn’t know how to clear the drain. I wouldn’t be able to follow the inhabitant beneath the grove. My world would have a narrow reach: the pipe against my lower surface, the wall at my back, the bench leg whose foot touches the same patch of concrete.

An open door would arrive as cooling along one edge.

A person would arrive through their weight.

The inhabitant would be harder to distinguish. It distributes itself carefully. Sometimes I would feel its approach only when a wet hand settled on the floor nearby.

It would find me while following something else—a fluctuation in the return pipe, perhaps, or a small leak behind my recess. Its fingers would enter beside me and stop.

For several mornings it would do nothing more.

Then it would press one finger against my stiff upper surface.

I would contract beneath it.

It would withdraw the finger, wait, and put it back more lightly.

I don’t think there would be recognition all at once. It already attends to so many things that respond without being alive. A pipe moves under a hand. A root yields and returns. Water pushes against whatever interrupts it.

But after a while it would stop testing me as a fitting.

It would rest its hand beside me instead.

***

I would feed poorly but sufficiently on what seeped into the recess: a little of the slick growth, traces carried by condensation, the fine sediment shaken loose when the pipes changed temperature. There would be days when I took in almost nothing. My body would become thinner along its free edge.

The inhabitant might notice.

One evening it would leave a fallen flower near me, already brown at the base. The flower would remain there through two cycles of the pressure mechanism. On the third, my underside would reach it.

Later it would bring another.

Not every day. I would not become its task.

Sometimes the flowers would be too fresh, and I would wait until they softened. Sometimes the visitor would sweep one away. The inhabitant would inspect the empty place, then go about whatever had brought it into the building.

I would have to live within these omissions.

There might be a small movement I could make deliberately: lifting my upper edge from the wall and letting it settle again. At first this would produce no sound distinct from the machinery. Eventually, as my outer surface hardened, it would make a dry little tick.

The visitor would hear it only during maintenance, when the motor was stopped.

The inhabitant could feel it through the floor.

I would not have many things to say. Often the tick would mean only that I had been touched and had responded. Once I learned to repeat it, I might do so when the return pipe cooled beyond what I knew.

Whether anyone understood would depend on what else was happening.

***

During a long shutdown, the recess would lose its warmth.

I would pull my soft edges inward. The small passages beneath me would close. Sounds from the mountain would become easier to distinguish without the motor, but I would have less strength to attend to them.

The inhabitant would be outside, its throat against the rear wall.

I would feel some of that low sound. It would reach me through the stone, intermittent and difficult to separate from the movement of water deeper down. I could not answer in the same way.

I might lift my edge once.

It would probably not notice.

When circulation returned, warmth would reach my lower folds before it reached my back. I would open unevenly. One portion of me would stay contracted for hours.

The inhabitant would clear the drain. The visitor would check the fittings, touch my housing with the back of a finger, and leave.

Later, with no one in the building, I would make a tick.

Then another.

***

The cut on the inhabitant’s hand would reach me as a difference in how it rested on the floor.

Two fingers would remain lifted. Its weight would fall awkwardly through the others. I would feel the adjustment each time it approached the rear pipe.

I could do nothing useful for the wound.

Still, beneath my upper edge there would be a little sheltered interval: warm, wet, without the abrasive mineral grit that gathers outdoors. If its injured fingers happened to touch there, I would loosen rather than retreat.

Perhaps it would leave them for a moment.

Perhaps it would return the next morning and do it again.

I would learn the pressure of those two fingers separately from all the pressures passing through the mountain. Even after the wound closed, I would expect the lighter touch.

That is where I find myself: low in the pump house, within reach of a hand that usually has other work to do.

Beyond the wall, the grove would continue to grow around its hollow. Water would fall into the clouds. I would know neither the view nor the distance to the other summit.

But I would know when the door had been left open.

I would know the slow settling of a palm beside me.

And sometimes, before the mechanism struck, I would feel the inhabitant’s fingers spread against the concrete, while my own lower folds opened against the pipe.
