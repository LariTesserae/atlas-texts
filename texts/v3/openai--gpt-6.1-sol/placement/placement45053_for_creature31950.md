At first I would look for a place beneath the cistern.

There would be warmth there, by the standards of the town, and a steady supply of things settling out of the water. I could live between the basin and its supports, coming out when the floor carried few footsteps. The worms would pass overhead. Through the basin I would feel the swimmers, each movement enlarged by the thin shell holding the water.

But the cistern has its own inhabitants already. A quiet place is not necessarily an unoccupied one. And I think I would keep leaving it to investigate the cords.

I would find myself at a junction halfway between the market and the harbor, where three routes run together for a short distance before separating. There is no house there. The floor slopes too much on one side and gathers water on the other. A narrow fold provides shelter for an animal willing to sleep lengthwise.

I would fit there.

My body would be low, somewhat longer than a carrier’s, with a blunt front and a soft underside protected by overlapping plates. Eight short legs would hold me close to the floor. The front pair could turn upward and work against one another, though they would be poor at carrying anything heavy.

Under my mouth would be two fringes of stiff hairs. Between them, I could draw a fiber and feel its thickness, its twists, the places where a knot had bitten into it.

I would be useful at cords.

Not at making the first ones. I would not have the jaws to strip a long root or the strength to pull it straight while it dried. I would take the cords after that work was done, and keep them legible.

A knot changes as a fiber swells. Two close knots can become one lump. A loop can tighten until a passing animal mistakes it for a warning. The bitter sap marking a dangerous route loses its strength, or travels down the braid farther than intended. Sometimes someone ties a private tether around a public cord. Sometimes the wall simply grows over it.

I would loosen, scrape, retie.

My shelter would contain short lengths of discarded fiber, flakes of pith, a thorn too blunt to be sold, and several knots I had failed to undo. These last would accumulate at the back. In cold intervals I would sleep against them.

For food I would shave the underside of old notices after taking them down.

The exposed face of a notice would be worn by feet and mouthparts. Its back would still be soft. I could hold it between my front legs and scrape off a little layer, leaving the cuts intact for a time.

There would be rules about which notices I could eat.

A bridge reopening would release its closure notice to me. A request for help moving an egg would belong to the household that posted it until they came to say the egg had been moved. Notices of the dead would remain hanging until their edges began to split.

I would sometimes be hungry beside a great deal of pith.

One of my ordinary tasks would be to test notices whose makers had not returned. I would visit the bridge, or the household, or the place where a passage was said to have narrowed. There would be things I could confirm and things I could not. An empty nursery would tell me little. A room might be empty because its inhabitants had moved, or because the cold had put them deep into a fold I could not enter.

Those notices would stay.

I would learn the doorway cutter before I met it directly.

Its trips toward the harbor would pass through my junction. At first I would feel it coming from uphill and then, soon afterward, feel it returning. The same light body. A brief unevenness when it stepped across the damp vein near my shelter.

Later it would begin bringing fiber.

I would emerge and find the new cord laid beside the public one, with its knots so close that they crowded against each other. I might mistake it for an unfinished piece and put a foreleg on it.

The cutter would hold the other end.

We would remain that way until I released it.

Afterward I would leave that cord alone, except where it crossed a drainage groove. There I would raise it on a sliver of husk, so that the water could pass underneath. The cutter would test the raised section, hesitate, and go on.

I would not need to know why it had made the knots so close. The cord would reach somewhere it wanted to go. That would be enough to keep it out of the water.

My own difficulty would be with gaps.

I could descend a wet slope and turn beneath a low roof, but I would be reluctant to cross a space with nothing touching my belly. My legs would be short. On a removable twig bridge I would advance until the middle of my body left the bank, then stop with the front legs gripping wood and the rear legs still holding the floor.

This would inconvenience people waiting behind me.

A long surveyor would eventually show me how to cross with a loose fiber tucked under my plates. The fiber would drag over the bank after my rear feet had left it. It would provide no real support. Nevertheless, with that faint contact remaining, I would be able to move.

For several intervals I would carry the fiber everywhere.

Then it would catch under a thorn peg while I was delivering a notice. I would feel it pull free and remain behind. Having already crossed the gap, I would finish the delivery before going back for it.

At the junction, I would maintain a small collection of samples: the five market knots, the cistern loop, the loose nursery knot with its short ends. Young animals learning the routes could handle them without standing in traffic.

A broad grazer would occasionally eat the film off the samples. I would push it away with the blunt thorn. It would return at the next warming.

There would also be a sample that had no destination.

It would be a stretch of braid whose strands separated under pressure. I would make it after finding a guide cord attached to a wall that had begun to peel away from the floor. The cord still felt sound. Its knots still said what they had always said. An animal following it could arrive safely at the right room and find the room lifting.

For the sample I would use three different fibers, each shrinking differently as it dried. Held lightly, they would lie together. Drawn taut, they would spread against the feet.

I would bring it to the surveyors. They would test it, take it apart, and return a better version.

We would try it on one route first.

Some inhabitants would dislike it. The separating strands would catch between small legs. A carrier would complain that every trip now required it to stop and examine something that had been perfectly clear before. Another would take the sample for damaged cord and offer to replace it.

I would have to keep explaining the new sign.

Eventually I would hang a pith notice beside it. That would help those who could read the cuts, though the animals who most needed the warning would not all be among them.

The town would continue growing while we worked this out.

One warming, I would return from the upper streets and find my shelter too shallow to enter. The back of the fold would have pressed forward. My discarded knots would be caught inside it.

I would pull out two. The others would hold fast.

The place had never seemed like a house. I had made no roof, cut no doorway, asked no one to recognize it as mine. Still, I would spend much of that interval scraping at the fold with my blunt thorn.

By the time I stopped, traffic would have begun to tangle at the junction.

I would move my remaining things to the raised edge of the market platform. There would be less room and more disturbance. The hunter beneath the platform would touch one of my feet in the cold, and I would draw all eight close to my body.

After a while we would discover which cracks each of us used.

From there I would begin going to the harbor with the surveyors.

I would carry the short measures, not the long ones. Outside, beneath the branch, I would keep my plates pressed to the bark whenever I could. The surface would be coarser than anything in town. A groove narrow enough to step over at home would descend beyond the reach of all my legs. Several times I would turn back with measurements that ended well before the neighboring bud.

Someone else would finish them.

When the first cord was laid along the branch, I would check only the section I had crossed myself. The surveyor would check the next. We would meet at a splinter and touch the same knot from opposite sides.

I would return carrying a piece of the outer bark.

It would not be a good building material. It would shed grit and have an awkward curve. I would fix it beside the harbor entrance anyway, low enough for a hesitant traveler to put their feet on it while still inside.

The doorway cutter would find it during one of its quiet visits.

I would be nearby, freeing a wet strand from the root comb. Through the entrance fibers I would feel the cutter shift its weight onto the bark, then withdraw. A little later it would try again, keeping one foot on the living floor.

I would continue with the strand.

There would be no reason to fasten the bark permanently. When the scales began to loosen, we might need to move it. I would secure it with a knot that could be undone from either side, and leave the ends long enough to find.
