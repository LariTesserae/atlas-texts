I would look first beneath the bench.

Not for darkness. There is enough light there to see the pale underside of the seat, the roots enclosing the supports, a bead of water caught in the split husk of a seed. But it is one of the few places in the hollow where something has made a ceiling low enough to bring your back against.

I think I would want that contact. Something above me, something below, and a narrow interval in which to move.

For a while I imagine myself as an animal that lives there: flat, dry-backed, slipping sideways when the large inhabitant reaches beneath the seat. I would know the bench through its underside rather than its usefulness. The arrival of someone sitting would be a small change in pressure, a settling at the joints. I might come out to investigate a heel and retreat when it lifted.

But the ground beneath the bench is too dry for what begins to take shape. I would visit it, perhaps. I would not stay.

There is a little wet passage between a shelter support and the root that encloses it. Water enters at one end, slows in the middle, and leaves through a gap scarcely wider than a leaf stem. Fine material collects there without quite settling: pollen skins, loosened cells, the soft remains of things too small to have recognizable bodies.

That is closer.

I would have a mouth suited to holding one of those particles while the water tried to take it away. A ring of short, inward-facing bristles, perhaps, and behind them something that could close. My body would be narrow enough to turn in the passage. Along its underside, several places would fasten and release.

I would not need a face at the front. Not much of one. The hollow arrives from every direction.

Warmth through the surface beneath me. A change in the water against my sides. The root flexing when something heavy puts a foot nearby. Light interrupted by a leaf, then restored.

I begin to see the length of white stitching in the animal’s folds.

That is where I would be.

Not sewn into it, although at rest I might look that way. I would be a small, pale creature with a succession of blunt feet, each capable of holding the edge of a plate. My back would show several opaque crossbars. Between them the body would be clear enough that, after feeding, a dark speck could be watched moving slowly through me.

There would be no great strength in the feet. I could hold on while my host walked, but not if it brushed firmly against a root. For that I would have to flatten myself into one of the seams.

The old tears in its apron would be familiar ground. One has a thickened edge I cannot cross without going around. Another slopes gently enough to shelter me when the body turns. A fold near the missing pad stays wet even on land.

I would sleep there, attached at three points, with the remaining feet loose.

The large animal would not always know where I was. Nor would I always know where it was going. Its preparations would reach me before its movement: the skin becoming firmer, the overlapping plates drawing closer, water passing through an opening beside my resting place. I would pull myself clear of that opening. Then the whole surface beneath me would tilt.

During a crossing I would see very little.

Silt. A piece of root going past. Brief stretches of bright water between the plates. Sometimes a fish would follow alongside, large enough to conceal the pool beyond it, and I would fold my feeding bristles against my mouth until it left.

When we stopped, I would come out.

There would be work enough along the animal’s flank. Not work it had given me. Food catches at the edges of its covering. A thin growth forms in the healed notches. In one place the plates overlap imperfectly, and the material beneath needs only a little loosening before it comes away.

I would eat there cautiously.

A living surface can withdraw from a mouth. I would feel that withdrawal through my feet and stop. Sometimes a soft finger would sweep across the plates, moving me elsewhere with several other small inhabitants. I would remain flattened for a while after it passed.

I do not think the large animal would have a special affection for me. It might distinguish me from grit, or from a creature biting too deeply. That would be enough to begin with.

And I would leave it.

When it rested beside the shelter, I would travel down a limb and onto the root surrounding the support. The crossing would require waiting until the two surfaces touched. I could not jump.

On the root I would move more slowly. Its ridges are less accommodating than the seams of the animal. There are dry patches that cannot be seen until my feet fail to hold. I would back away from them, taking the long route to the wet passage beneath the bench.

There I would feed until the root shifted.

Sometimes the shift would mean a visitor had sat down. Sometimes it would mean my host had risen and was leaving.

I might make the wrong choice. Come out expecting its limb and find only water. Return to the passage while it waited beside the support, then emerge too late.

So I would also know a few places where I could remain alone.

The stone bowl would be one. Its grooves would hold enough moisture for me to travel along the rim. I could shelter beneath a fallen leaf and feed at its softened edge, beside the tiny animal already there. We might touch mouths to the same piece and each pull backward.

I would not understand the grooves as evidence. A worn pad passing over them would be something to retreat from, not an answer.

The doorframe would be farther than I usually went.

Once, carried there on the trailing apron, I might slip free and attach to its submerged edge. Its surface would be difficult to hold: too little grain, too few shallow places. I would spend some time moving up and down, looking for a better attachment.

Through the opening, my host would still be resting.

Outside it, the animal would have begun to move away.

I cannot give myself an understanding of that. I might reach toward the nearer fold and find water. Then I would draw back, fasten all my feet, and wait until something passed close enough to carry me.

Perhaps I would remain there through a rain. Perhaps one of the small, round-bodied animals would offer an easier surface, and I would ride it to the rim.

There would be risks in being so small that travel depended on contact. But there would also be many contacts. Leaves settle. Bodies rest. Roots enter water. The hollow is full of temporary ways across.

When the young climbed into the adult’s folds, I would give up some of my usual places. Their feet would be blunt and careless. Their mouths would investigate the white bars on my back. I would retreat into a shallow healed tear too narrow for them, then come out when they slept.

Later, after they had gone, I would find a strip of shed covering lodged along a plate. I could feed beneath it for days.

I would not know whether they had been the animal’s offspring. I would know where each had pressed its body, and which wet passages were open again.

When the large inhabitant approached the bench and lifted its fingers toward your hand, I would be on the forward limb.

The movement would carry me upward into air. I would fasten close to a pad. Beneath me the skin would cool slightly as it touched yours.

Your palm would be an unfamiliar surface, ridged and yielding, with moisture gathering where the finger rested. I might extend the front of my body across that small wet join.

There would be room for two or three feet.

I would pause there. The rest of me would remain attached to the animal. A movement from either of you could close the space or open it beyond my reach.

Perhaps you would notice the white mark beside the pad, then notice that one end of it had lifted.

Before you could bring your other hand close, I would withdraw.

The animal would lower its limb. Water would rise around us again. I would make my way back beneath a plate, carrying a little of the unfamiliar moisture with me.

By evening, when its outline had disappeared into the pool and only the two flowering plants showed clearly, I would come out along the apron.

There would be a fresh particle caught in the edge of an old tear.

I would fasten on either side of it, open my bristles, and begin.
