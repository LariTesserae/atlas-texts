I would first take myself for someone who had come in to sit down.

There is room on the broad slab, and nothing in the hollow suggests that sitting there would be an intrusion. I would lower my head beneath the roof, place a hand against the wall, and wait until I could distinguish the stone I meant to sit on from the darkness around it.

For a while, that would be enough of an identity: the person on the slab. Warm at the back, hands resting loosely, feet making two small interruptions in the powder.

But the place would become clearer as I stayed. I would notice the dry leaf beside the stone. One end would rest in a line on the pale surface; the other would be lifted just enough to have a shadow. I might reach down to pick it up, then leave it where it was. It would look too brittle to survive being held.

I would begin to wonder about the surface beneath it.

My hand would find the fine ridges before my eyes did. They would continue under the slab, where I could not follow them. Keeping my palm there, I would feel a slight yielding. I would shift my weight to test it.

The change would arrive slowly enough that I might already have forgotten the movement that caused it.

Here the sense of being a visitor begins to loosen.

I find myself attending to the underside of the slab, to its few broad contacts with the body beneath it. The stone is heavy, but its weight is familiar. There are places where it bears down more strongly and places where a thin layer of mineral earth divides the surfaces. A small grain near one contact has fractured. Its two pieces still occupy almost exactly the space the whole grain occupied.

I know this without seeing it.

Perhaps I am the part beneath the stone. That is where I would first locate myself: a low thickness, pale above, darker underneath, extended farther than I can make into a clear outline.

The person sitting overhead becomes something received through the slab. Their weight arrives unevenly. One side carries more of it. Then both. A heel presses against the floor and releases.

I firm beneath the heavier point.

Elsewhere I yield a little. There is space beneath a rounded lip of limestone, and some of my weight passes into it. Nothing visible moves. The adjustment takes long enough that the person has ceased trying to find a comfortable position before it is complete.

They rest.

I continue beyond the place supporting them. Beside the pillar, my exposed surface is harder, holding shallow cracks full of dust. Toward the wall it becomes pliant again. A band of limestone presses against a row of closed depressions, each receiving a different part of the hollow’s small changes.

The roof warms. I feel the increase through the wall before it reaches my upper surface.

Farther along, the root presses against me.

That contact is more variable than the stone. There are long periods when its presence is nearly constant, and then it grows firmer, occupying a little more of the space we share. I leave room along one side. Dark earth remains there, fine enough to settle into any opening I make.

I would be careful around the root, though care here would have no separate intention. Growth beside it would be slower. A fold would stop thickening where the living surface begins. What passed between us would be taken up gradually, with time left for the root to alter.

Underneath, a curl of leaf lies within a narrow passage. It has been there so long that the nearest portion of my body has formed around its shape. Its edge is softening. A little farther in, another fragment can no longer be distinguished from the material holding it.

This, too, would be part of being here: having things remain within me until they change.

The person rises from the slab.

Their departure reaches several places at once. The stone settles into its ordinary load, but I am still firm where I supported them. For a time, the hollow contains that difference.

I do not follow the footsteps toward the entrance. Each arrives through the floor, weaker than the last. Then the small disturbances end.

I remain beneath the slab and around the pillar, with dust lying in the lines of my back. The dry leaf near the visitor’s hand is still there. So are the two limestone flakes. Their slight weights have become familiar enough that I register the floor through them.

Much later, I relax beneath the place where the person sat.

Earth enters the space I release. Beside the slab, my pale surface widens a little. Along the root, enough room remains.

I would be the inhabitant, though I would have found myself first in the portion that bears another’s weight. From there, slowly, the rest of the body would become mine: the hardened surface, the folds holding old matter, the sensitive places against the wall.

My extent would be something I learned by resting against it.

And when no one came, I would still be there, making the small adjustments by which that resting continues.
