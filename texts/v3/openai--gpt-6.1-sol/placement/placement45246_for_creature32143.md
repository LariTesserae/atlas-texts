I would find myself below the rounded slab, but not immediately beneath the fall.

There is a place farther inward where the underside of the stone comes close to a second stone. The gap is low, crooked, and flooded. A little of the falling water enters it backward, pushed by the force of water landing outside. A little rises through the lower stone. The two currents meet along a wavering boundary that never moves very far.

I think I would begin there as something caught.

A grain, perhaps. One of the pale fragments lodged in the gap, too broad to pass through its narrowest part. Water would press against me without changing my position. The noise would reach me through the contact between my smallest corner and the rock.

But a grain cannot adjust that corner. I would need to do so. Not often, and not much: enough to ease a pressure that has become dangerous, enough to keep one surface exposed while sheltering another.

So there would be flesh beneath the mineral.

I would be one of the animals, old enough to have become inconveniently heavy.

Not the oldest. My plates would still be separate, and there would be a clear strip of body between the last plate and my forward edge. I would have arrived in this gap when it was more open. Since then, the stone above would have acquired a skin of calcite. My own back would have thickened. Between those two slow changes, the room available to me would have diminished.

I would not know this as a history. I would know that a movement once possible now meets resistance.

My rear edge would rest in a small depression. Ahead of me, the rock would rise toward the seam. To reach it I would have to spread thin, drawing tissue from the sheltered part of my body into the exposed part. A filament could get there first. Then two. The rest would stay folded beneath me while those first contacts tested the water.

The world would be very large at that edge.

A human looking into the gap might see nothing beyond a wet patch and a few whitish scales. For me, there would be a broad change across the width of a pore. Water on one side would carry the faint warmth of an exposed ledge. On the other, it would arrive cold from within the stone, bearing substances that made the fringe stay extended.

Between them, there would be food.

I would occupy that boundary with my mouth, my body mostly elsewhere.

I would have no use for the plateau’s horizon. The mountain wall, the successive basins, the cloud ceiling would leave effects, but no shapes I could hold. Their water would reach me. Their pressure would reach me. Whatever happened high in the massif might eventually alter the seam against which I fed.

The distance would be in the delay.

For long intervals, feeding and holding would be nearly the same act. My mouth would work against a patch of rock. Water would pass through the grooves beneath me. Small portions of my margin would lift and settle.

There would be no silence, but there would be an accustomed condition.

The slab would carry a rapid tapping. Beneath that would lie the deeper vibration from the drainage throat. Both would enter my body wherever I touched the stone. I would continue feeding within them, as a person might continue breathing in a familiar room.

Then a third pattern would arrive.

Three contacts, unevenly spaced. A pause. Another contact nearer.

My fringe would withdraw. The mouth would cease moving. Around my body, the margin would tighten in sections until almost all of me held close.

A crustacean would cross above the gap. I would not know its body, or its blindness, or that it had paused to work at something caught beneath a limb. There would only be the small sequence of pressures traveling through stone.

After it passed, I would remain contracted for a while.

The water at my forward edge would go ungrazed.

Eventually one filament would return to it.

I think this is where I would most fully be myself: not in the long feeding, but in that first small return. One part of the body touching outward while the rest still held firm. No decision large enough to gather the whole animal. Only a local change, followed by another.

And I would not always return to the same patch.

Once, the richer water would begin arriving a little to my left. The old position would remain wet. Its temperature would change almost not at all. Yet my mouth would find less there, and the filaments on the left would remain extended longer than those on the right.

Over days, I would turn.

The plates at my rear would make this difficult. One would catch against the upper stone. Its edge would bear down whenever I stretched. I would loosen the tissue beneath it, slowly, until water entered the separation.

Then I would pull free.

The plate would remain in the gap. Its mineral rim would hold it to the stone, and water would run over its exposed inner surface. For a time it would preserve the curve of my back.

I would be just ahead of it, thinner and able to move.

There would be other coverings behind me, though I would have no way to count them. They would occupy places where I had once fed. Some would already be joined to the rock by new deposits. Some would have broken. A clear speck of another animal might settle against one without either of us encountering the other.

Above us, the slab would remain what it had been: a rounded obstruction beside a hidden fall.

Under it, I would have turned far enough for three filaments to reach the altered seam. The rest of my body would still lie along the old direction. Water would press lightly beneath the newly freed edge.

My mouth would settle.

The turn would continue.
