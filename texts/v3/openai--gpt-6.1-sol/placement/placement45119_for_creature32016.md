I would find a room above the freight shed, reached by an outside stair that is easier to climb in the afternoon. In the morning the bottom three treads yield differently, and until I learned them I would carry my tea with both hands.

The room would have been an office once. There would still be a pale rectangle on the wall where a timetable had prevented the bark from darkening. Two screw heads would remain visible near the top. The lower ones would have gone in too deep to retrieve.

I think I would arrive with a job that sounded more definite than it was.

Someone would have written to say that the maintenance office needed help bringing its records into order. I would imagine shelves, a desk, perhaps a few weeks spent copying figures. I would bring good paper. It would curl during the first night.

The records would turn out to be in several places. The office would hold repair permits and water allocations. The pressing works would keep its batch books. Sella would have her notebooks, which she would allow me to consult at her kitchen table but not to take away. The inspector would carry a substantial part of the remaining archive in his head.

There would also be things written directly on things: a date inside a cupboard, initials beneath a bridge, three short strokes beside a drain. Nobody would agree about the strokes. One person would remember a pressure release. Another would say they marked the depth of an old obstruction. The canal keeper would remember making them, but not why.

At first I would enter both explanations.

Later I would learn to leave space.

***

My desk would stand away from the wall on four pieces of certified board. The woman who showed me the room would have put them there.

“Otherwise it’ll settle toward the window,” she would say.

The window would face the unfinished end of the canal. From my chair I could see the place where its banks lost their straightness and became two low, uneven rises under the green. On cold evenings, people crossing there would disappear from the knees down into mist.

I would work for the maintenance office three days a week. On another morning I would help at the municipal scale, reading out the weights while the shoe repairer adjusted its mechanism. He would distrust my handwriting.

“That’s a seven?”

“Yes.”

“Make it more of one.”

He would give me a blunt knife after watching me try to remove a shoot with the edge of a ruler. The knife would have a dark handle polished on one side. It would be too small to feel important until I had carried it for a month.

My hands would take longer to change. At first the fine lines beside my nails would wash out. Then one would remain.

***

What I would become good at, slowly, is finding the older measurement.

A resident would come in because a doorway had narrowed. The inspector would measure it and say it was still within tolerance. The resident would say that was not the point; her brother’s chair had passed through it last winter.

I would look for the winter measurement.

Sometimes there would be one. Sometimes the only record would be a repair to the hinge, or a note about a cold patch on the inside wall. Once there would be a pencil sketch on the back of an invoice for machine oil, with the width written between two arrows.

I would learn which differences mattered to whom.

The pressing works could accept a small change in its storage room. A woman using a walking frame could not accept the same change in a passage. The permitted width would be written in the same column, but eventually I would begin adding another.

I would call it *clearance needed*. The inspector would approve of that. It would sound like something the office was qualified to know.

There would be arguments about how to measure it.

I would attend them. I would probably become tiresome about bringing the actual chair, cupboard, bicycle, or walking frame rather than accepting everyone’s recollection of its size.

***

The woman from the house being lowered would come to the office to ask about her currants.

I would have no answer. The application would be filed correctly and awaiting a decision.

She would sit down anyway, because she had walked farther than she intended. While I found her a cup, she would ask whether the old number had been removed from the doorway yet.

I would say I did not know.

“Don’t let them lose it,” she would say. “It’s the enamel one. They don’t make those now.”

The next time I went to the house, the foreman would show it to me in a tin with the door fittings. White numbers on dark blue, chipped at the screw holes. He would have thought of it already.

I would feel a little foolish for checking, though not sorry.

After that, the office would acquire a shallow drawer for things whose paperwork was complete but whose destination was not: the enamel number, a key to a demolished cupboard, two brass hooks from a room that had become too low to use. Each would have a label tied on with cord.

Some would be collected quickly. Some would remain long enough that I recognized their owners’ footsteps on the stair.

The drawer would not be part of my duties. Nobody would object to it until we needed the space.

***

I would not be particularly skilled with living surfaces.

A child could probably tell sooner than I could whether an exposed patch was healthy. I would consult people too often at first, then once fail to consult them when I should.

There would be a damp place under my window. I would record its dimensions for several mornings, pleased with the consistency of my method. When Sella came to see it, she would put her probe into the lower edge and tell me to fetch someone from the maintenance office.

“Next time, before the measuring,” she would say.

The repair would leave a raised seam beside the sill. For a while I would notice it whenever I sat down. Later I would rest my elbow just to the left of it without thinking.

I would stay through that winter.

By spring the maintenance office would ask whether I meant to remain, because there was no sense repairing the freight-shed stair for an occupant who was about to leave. I would say yes before considering whether the stair was a sufficient reason.

It would not be the reason. I would find it difficult to name the reason precisely.

I would have a cup at Sella’s house by then, and know which days the scale usually required adjustment. Someone would have asked me to feed two pots in a kitchen window while they visited their daughter beyond the ridge. I would have returned a borrowed saw and been told to keep it until the next job.

***

Years later, I would still spend much of my time looking up what a place had been like before someone noticed it changing.

But I would also go out with the inspector. I would hold the end of the tape, shift a chair, ask someone to walk through a doorway once more. My notes would contain fewer complete sentences. I would know when a figure needed an explanation and when it needed another pair of hands.

In the room above the freight shed, I would keep a narrow space beside the desk for visitors. The wall would encroach on it gradually. Once a year I would move the desk a little.

One evening the woman from the old house would come up carrying a small covered bowl. Her currants would finally have borne enough to spare.

She would pause at the top of the stair to get her breath. I would clear the papers from the other chair.

While we talked, the machinery below us would change to its night setting. The cup nearest the edge of the desk would give a faint rattle. I would move it inward, scarcely interrupting what I was saying.

Outside, the collector leaves would be lifting. I would still have several measurements to copy.

They could wait until morning.
