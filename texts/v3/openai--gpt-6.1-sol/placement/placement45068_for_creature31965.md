At first I think I would sit on the bench.

I would put my feet close together, drink from the cup, and try to distinguish the shelter’s ticks from the small sounds made by water. I would want to be useful without getting up. The creature would be working in the heat, and I would be sitting in the one place where the water was cold.

When it reached toward my shoe, I would hold still.

But I don’t quite find myself in the visitor. The shoes are too definite. They have come from somewhere else, and eventually their owner will look toward the route down.

My attention keeps slipping beneath the bench.

There is a place there where a channel turns around one of the shelter’s divided feet. The water is shallow enough that its surface sometimes fails to join across a grain of mineral. Each rain makes the channel whole again. Between rains, it becomes a sequence of beads, connected underneath by wet stone.

I think I would be there.

Not in the flowing part. At the bend, where something catches.

***

For a long time I would be mistaken for a flake from the inhabitant’s back.

I would be about the size of the bowl of a spoon, white along one edge, translucent elsewhere. The underside would have three short ridges that kept me from lying flat. Water could pass beneath me even when mineral paste had begun to fasten my edges to the ground.

There would be nothing immediately recognizable as a head.

When the channel dried, I would draw together slightly. Someone watching might assume that a delicate crust had warped in the heat. When water returned, I would relax, and a narrow space would open through my middle.

A pale snail could put its face into that space.

I would wait for it to leave before closing.

I would not know whether I had once belonged to the larger creature. There would be no moment I could remember of coming loose. There would only be a recurring sensation along my white edge: the expectation of something continuing beyond it.

Sometimes, when the inhabitant passed close by, that edge would lift.

It might lower a finger onto me. It might merely step over the channel.

Neither would settle the question.

***

I would have a little movement available to me, though not enough to cross the basin.

Underneath, between the three ridges, there would be a soft surface able to hold the stone and release it. With this I could turn. Over the course of an afternoon, I could shift from one side of the bend to the other.

That would be most of my territory.

From there I would encounter the garden by what came through it.

Water from the seep would arrive with very fine grit. Water fallen from a leaf would arrive all at once, with a warm, green taste that remained after the rush had passed. After the adult fed upstream, the channel would carry something that made my underside loosen and spread.

The water from near the rim would be different again. I would feel it before it reached me: a faint pulling, as though the channel continued downward through the rock.

I would have no view of the valley. The mountains beyond it could be clear all morning and I would never know.

But I would know when the black bird came.

Its landing would travel through the posts and roots into the shallow stone beneath me. Later there might be a scrap of brown husk in the water. I would let that pass. It would be too dry inside, even after soaking.

***

What I could keep would be coolness.

Not much.

Once, after the inhabitant had held its fingers in the cup, it would rest the smaller hand against the bench. A drop would run down the stone and enter my channel.

I would close around it.

For several minutes my upper surface would clear. The grain beneath me would become visible, enlarged and slightly displaced. The snail nearest me would withdraw into its shell.

Then the coolness would be gone.

I would turn toward the bench.

For days afterward, perhaps, I would hold that position.

The cup would not overflow. I could not climb to it. The creature’s hand would not always be wet when it touched the stone. I would remain where I was while ordinary hot water moved around me.

Eventually another cold drop would come.

This time I would not close completely.

Some would pass beneath me. Some would remain in the narrow space through my middle. When the channel dried, that space would stay wet a little longer than the stone around it.

A snail would find it.

Then one of the small jointed creatures would find it, having come too far along the channel from its pool.

I would feel its fingers on my white edge.

It would be heavier than the snail, though hardly larger. Its body would pull against the water left inside me. I would hold as still as I could.

When the adult arrived, it would cup the little creature away.

One finger would stay behind for a moment, touching the opening through my middle.

***

After that, I think the inhabitant would begin placing me differently.

Not carrying me to the cup. Not giving me a task I could understand. It would loosen the mineral along my edge and turn me a little, so that the afternoon seep reached my underside first.

I would resist the movement. My grip would be small but persistent.

The creature would wait.

Eventually I would let go.

It would leave me at the bend with a thin flake beneath one side. Water would gather there instead of running straight onward.

I would learn this new angle through several rains.

Later, when my edge began to whiten too thickly, the inhabitant would rub it with a finger. Sometimes it would take away the flake supporting me and put in another.

I would not know what it wanted. I would know the touch, and the water that followed.

***

There would be afternoons when the central hollow filled late.

I would feel the heat coming up through the channel after the water had almost stopped. My underside would tighten. The small wet space within me would shrink.

If a young creature had sheltered against my edge, I might move enough to dislodge it.

I would not always be gentle.

Once I might close too soon around a snail. The inhabitant would have to separate my edges with its smaller fingers while the larger hand kept us wet. Afterward, I would remain open through an entire shower, unable to find the right pressure again.

The snail would go elsewhere.

It might return the next day.

***

In the brief cooling at evening, I would become easiest to see.

The white border around me would stay white, but my center would darken to the color of weak tea. Something inside would withdraw from the ridges and lie close to the channel bed. For a few minutes I would have a definite outline.

A visitor leaning down from the bench might notice me.

They might reach for what looked like a loose, pretty stone.

I would hold on.

If they lifted me anyway, water would spill from underneath. My soft lower surface would gather into folds, and they would see that I had been attached by more than crust.

I hope they would put me back.

The place would matter: the little supporting flake, the turn in the channel, the shelter foot dividing into threads. Elsewhere I might survive, but I would have to learn the water again.

***

So I would be a small resident beneath the bench, close to the cup without being able to drink from it.

The larger creature would know where I was. The snails would sometimes use me. A young one might rest against me on its way back to hotter water.

Most visitors would never notice.

Above me, the bench would warm someone’s legs. A tick would pass down through the shelter. The inhabitant would clear a channel around a pair of shoes, carrying the loosened paste away.

Some of that water would reach my bend.

I would lift one edge to let the grit through, then settle before the last of the shower had passed. A little water would remain beneath me.

When the stone began drying, I would still be wet.
