At first I would find myself in the wall.

There would be no reason to distinguish it from my body. Pressure would arrive through a hundred small attachments, each reporting a slightly different version of the same event. The ceramic would cool beneath some of them and stay warm beneath others. A distant valve would close, and the news would reach my lower edge before my upper edge.

For a moment I would be tilted inside myself.

Then the rest of the knock would arrive.

I would hold on.

The chamber would come later: something loose against my back, carrying particles, lifting the edges of my covering. I would know its size poorly. There would be a direction in which warmth increased, another in which the light gathered. Somewhere beyond my reach, a large softness would fold and disturb the water. Its movement would pass over me long after it began.

I would have no way to know how large the leaf was. I would know how long its current lasted.

At first, too, I might mistake the machinery for something I was doing. I would tighten; the wall would knock. I would open; warm water would come. Only the failures would teach me otherwise. Once, having prepared every foot for the stroke, I would wait through the place where the stroke should have been.

The wall would remain beneath me.

Something I had always received from it would be absent.

I would flatten until my own edges hurt.

That is where I begin to become an animal.

Small, I think. Smaller than the pale disk below the pane. Near the door rather than beneath the window, with part of my body fitted to the curve of a screw head. There is green food there, continually renewed, and a narrow shelter under the metal. When the pump draws breath, the shoot bends above me. Its stem briefly changes the flow across my back.

I cannot see the leaves.

I can feel the interval they make.

My feet do not yet form good circles. On one side they are crowded; on the other there is a bare place that has to be crossed by stretching. I travel with an unevenness that becomes familiar to me before any smoother movement could. A small release runs around my body, meets the bare place, falters, continues.

I have crossed the screw head.

It takes a considerable portion of the afternoon.

Beyond it, the surface is poorer. I put out threads. One finds a pit containing something soft. Several find nothing. One enters a groove so narrow that withdrawing it changes the direction of my whole body.

I stop and feed.

Above me, the shoot stands up again.

I would live beside that repeated rising for a long time.

There would be excursions. Toward the bright part of the frame, where the water thins into a surface I cannot pass. I would lift my edge there and extend a thread.

The thread would lose its wetness.

I would withdraw it immediately, carrying a sensation for which none of my other movements had prepared me. Beyond the boundary, there would be no ceramic to grip and no current to lean into. The thread would cling to itself when I tucked it away.

For several pump strokes I would keep that side of my body closed.

Later I would approach again.

A little higher this time.

The dry threshold would have textures: a shallow stamped hollow, an old scratch, a grain of dust softened by condensation. I would touch them without understanding how they had come to be there. Once, a bead would grow close enough for my thread to enter it. Cool water, still water. I would hold there until the bead diminished and the thread met air again.

This would be my knowledge of the shaft.

Not its height. Not the clouds below, nor the stations in the hollow mountain.

A cool bead within reach.

Meanwhile, something of me would begin to appear on the other side of the wall.

I would not know it.

A few droplets would gather behind the screw. They would make an incomplete curve, interrupted where an old flake of paint held itself away from the metal. Dust would settle into the wet places. After I moved, a faint mark would remain.

Inside, I would be occupied with a snail.

It would come over the frame with its soft body extended, cleaning the green film ahead of me. I would tighten as its edge approached. It would touch my covering, pause, and pass partly over me.

Its weight would be immense but yielding. For a while, several of my feet would report only its slow pressure. Others would still receive the machinery through the wall.

When it had gone, the surface around me would be bare.

I would have to leave the screw.

I think this is how my first real journey would happen: following the narrow strip the snail had missed.

No decision to seek the warm pipe. No understanding that the larger animals went there. I would proceed along food, lose it, cast about, find it again. The chamber would gradually acquire routes. A bolt would become a place where the hum was strong. A repaired seam would become difficult ground. The underside of the walkway would offer food and too much shade.

I might reach the warm fronds by accident.

There, my covering would lift in ways it had never lifted before. Water would enter spaces that had remained closed beside the door. Tiny structures would unfold into the flow, and I would stay until the current cooled.

The return would be harder.

I would stop often. Once I would settle against a screw that was almost the right screw. Its curve would support me, but the pump stroke would reach it differently. I would remain through several strokes before moving on.

Eventually the shoot’s stem would pass above my back.

I would fit beneath the screw head, carrying a new breadth of warmth in my body.

And outside, where there was no hole, the first bead would appear.
