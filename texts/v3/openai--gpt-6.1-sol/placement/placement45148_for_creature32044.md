I would begin beside the old ridge, in the narrow interval after the leaves have opened and before the powder settles on their red interiors.

Not on its crest. There would be too much light there, too little to distinguish one hour from another. I would want the side where a join has widened without being replaced, where a little warmth escapes even during the cooler phase. A place that has not been made comfortable, but has become habitable through the failure of several intentions to coincide.

At first I would imagine myself sitting there.

Then I would notice that I had been sitting for longer than a traveler could sit. The dust would have crossed me in sheets. A hedge would have put out three new leaves and lost two. The shape I had taken for my bent knee would be a fitting, and the shadow beneath it would be the only part of me that had moved.

I think I would be small.

Not one of the loose grains, though I might be carried among them. Not a voice in the systems underneath. There are already enough things here that act across distances without having to cross them, and I would rather have the difficulty of arriving.

I would be alive in the narrow dark beneath a collar.

The collar would belong to one of the larger animals: an old one, with a pale band fitted around its left leg. Its origins would be unavailable to both of us. The band would have no hinge I could find, no inscription, no obvious purpose. Its inner surface would be smoother than its outer surface, but not perfectly smooth. There would be a shallow channel, perhaps left by manufacture, perhaps worn by the leg before the tissue learned to grow around it.

That channel would be my country.

I would not fill it. In some places I would be no more than a haze. Elsewhere I would have gathered into soft, amber knots, each containing a few brighter flecks. Fine branches would pass beneath the tissue pressed against the band. Other branches would stop at its edge, where light reaches in during a long step.

I would spend much of my life at that edge.

Outside, an immense white surface would come and go. Sometimes it would be close enough to touch. Sometimes the animal would lift its foot and the ground would disappear, leaving a brightness without texture. Then the cups would stretch, release, and the foot would settle somewhere else.

I would know a crossing as a succession of pressures.

Heat would arrive from the outer surface of the collar. A different warmth would come through the animal’s leg. Between them I would open the smallest of my folds, move ions through a film too thin to have a color, and wait.

The animal would carry me without attending to me. I would feed on what gathered beneath the band: traces of its membranes, material rubbed from the collar, the slight chemical difference between a living surface and a manufactured one held close together for years.

If I grew too quickly, its tissue would tighten and crush my branches.

If I grew too slowly, a dry interval would break them.

There would be seasons when almost all of me lived beneath one rough patch, and seasons when I reached halfway around the leg.

I would have no view of the animal’s whole body. Its age would come to me through the changing fit of the collar. Its injuries would be sudden abundances followed by long restrictions. Once, after a tear in its flank, a new substance would enter my channel and support a growth unlike anything I had made before: a translucent lobe projecting beyond the band, delicate enough to tremble at each step.

I would keep it for a crossing and a half.

Then it would catch against a plate rim and be left behind.

For a while I would still send nourishment toward it.

The first replacement site we entered would seem to me like a death of weather.

The upper heat would cease. Pressure would change. A surface close to the collar would move past, carrying a regular sequence of charges that made my branches contract in the same order, one after another. The animal would stop.

Something would touch the band.

Not me. The outer face of it.

The touch would last long enough for a cooler pulse to enter the channel. Then the thing beside us would withdraw, and the animal would lower itself into the opening.

After many such passages, I would come to recognize that pulse. It would precede access to deeper films, shaded joints, places where my carrier could rest with its warm folds hanging loose. I would expand a little when I felt it, before there was anything to feed on.

Sometimes the pulse would not come.

Sometimes it would come twice, and the animal would remain motionless for so long that its tissue began to press into my channel. I would have to retreat into the rough patch and wait with it.

Whatever the collar meant to the maintenance systems, I would live among the uncertainties of that meaning.

There would be other bands. During the gatherings beneath the rising mound, one might pass close enough for a thread of me to reach it. I would extend into the small space between our carriers, sustained for a moment by the warmth trapped there.

Usually they would separate too soon.

Once they would not.

A branch would adhere to the other collar. The animals would shift apart, and the strand between us would narrow until it broke.

After that, somewhere in the buried joints, there would be another patch of amber haze receiving a different animal’s steps. It might grow where I could not. It might be scraped away during the next descent. No change would return to tell me.

My own carrier would go on.

Eventually it would bring me to an empty space.

I would know the arrival because the leg would cool before the foot touched down. The animal would set one foot inside and keep the other outside. Its body would extend. The collar would tilt, exposing my outermost branches to the air.

For once, neither surface enclosing me would be warm.

My growth would slow. The accumulated substances beneath the band would remain unused. A tiny fold at the edge of the channel, usually pressed flat by the animal’s tissue, would open into the cooling.

There would be room to extend it.

I would put out a clear thread. It would reach beyond the collar and bend, although it had touched nothing I could feed on. A second thread would bend farther along. I would hold them there through the bright front while the animal remained still.

I would not learn the shape of the absent object. My reach would be too short.

But when we left, one thread would tear and the other would fold back beneath the band. For many crossings afterward, its tip would keep the bend it had acquired there.

That is where I would find myself: carried from the chambers under the ridge to the places where the ground still cools something that is gone.

I would be neither the animal nor its protection. I would be the small life that had found room inside the protection, traveling with it, occasionally reaching past its edge.

And when the animal finally rested beneath the old ridge, I would receive the warmth stored in its leg long after the leaves above us had closed.

My folds would open.

There would still be a little food in the channel.
