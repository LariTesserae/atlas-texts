I would look first for somewhere to sit.

Not on the great animal, if I could help it. Even where it resembles a ledge, I would hesitate to put my whole weight on something whose breathing I could feel through my boots. And not beside the cleft: the noise there would get into my teeth.

There is a shelf near the opening, slightly below the largest fern. A little water lies on it, too thin to be called a pool. From there I could see the distant mountain without losing sight of the roots. I would choose that place. I would think I had chosen it because it was empty.

Sitting down would be difficult. My hand would find the rock readily enough, but the lowering of my body would seem to have several depths. At one of them I would still be standing. At another, my knees would be almost level with my chin. The cold would be the same at each.

I would keep my hand on the shelf.

Under its shallow water, something would press back.

At first I would take it for another portion of the large inhabitant. There would be warmth, but less than I had felt beneath the moss. And this surface would be smooth, without fur or the fine grain of exposed skin. A little ridge would move across my palm, following the crease at the base of my fingers.

When I lifted my hand, I would see it.

A flat creature, scarcely longer than my thumb, lying where the fern’s shadow meets the wet reflection of the opening. Its body would be almost clear. The black shelf would show through it, except along a milky fold running from one end to the other. There would be two dark points near the broader end. Eyes, probably. Around the narrower end, several small feet would be tucked under the body.

I would lean closer.

The feet would not be tucked under. They would be spread against the stone, so transparent that I had mistaken them for the edges of the water. One would reach into the fern bed. Another would rest on bare rock. A third would end at the shelf’s lip, where there was nothing to support it.

The creature would be in the act of turning over.

Its broad underside would be visible along one edge. There, faintly, I would see the shape of a mouth.

I think this is where I would begin to lose my place as the person looking.

Not all at once. I would still have a wet sleeve, a hand braced on stone, the uncomfortable weight of my legs. But I would also feel the shelf against a much larger area of myself. A grain of mineral, invisible from above, would be distinctly there beneath my ribs. Water would draw along my side with a cool pull. The fern would stand over me in separate, enormous arches.

The hand above me would interrupt the gray light.

I would know its warmth before I knew it as mine.

Then it would be easier to remain below it.

My body would be thin where it lay open against the rock and thicker where it was folding. I would have no hard shell. Along my back, the milky line would be a gathering of tissue, muscular enough to lift me, though the lift would never carry me clear of the shelf. My feet would hold with the soft insistence of lips.

I would not be trying to leave the hollow. The foot at the edge would be feeling the air.

There is weather out there. It comes up from below with scents that have travelled: snow, broken vegetation, the dry interior of a recently fallen stone. Against my exposed foot, those scents would have texture. One would be fine and prickling. Another would spread heavily through the pad. A cloud passing the opening would cool it.

Farther in, my other foot would touch a root.

Through the root I would feel the great animal settling its weight. That immense, unfinished easing would reach me as a steady pressure. The turning minerals would be there too, but differently: a close succession of hard touches along my underside, each distinguishable, none becoming the last.

I would lie between them without being able to say which was supporting me.

Perhaps I would eat there. The mouth beneath my lifted edge would make small motions against the water. Something would enter it, though the film on the shelf would not grow thinner. I would feel a fullness immediately behind the mouth, no farther than that. I could not tell whether I was feeding or tasting.

I would have eyes after all.

One would face the fern stems. Their wet surfaces would fill most of its sight. Between two roots, the pale fur of the large forelimb would make a soft, irregular wall.

The other eye would face outward.

It would be badly placed for looking at mountains. My own folding body would obscure the upper half of its view. Still, I would see a narrow strip of distant slope, and sometimes a cloud would cross it. The slope would darken while everything touching me remained as it was.

That would occupy me.

I would be a small animal held in the act of turning toward a change. Not trapped in panic, not waiting for the rest of the turn. The effort would be comfortable. My feet would have found good purchase. The water beneath my body would carry a little light.

If someone sat on the shelf, I would feel their arrival through the stone.

If they put a hand beside me, I would reach it with the edge that was already lifting. They might feel only a soft ridge following one of their palm lines. I would feel warmth, salt, the minute tremor of muscles holding the fingers apart.

I would fit against the hand without fastening myself to it.

And when it was gone, there would still be the root at one foot, the open air at another, and that narrow view through my own translucent side.

On the distant mountain, a shadow would be moving.

I would have just enough of an eye turned toward it.
