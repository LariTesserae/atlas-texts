At first I would be outside, with one hand braced on the stair above the door.

I would have climbed too far carrying something I could no longer remember setting down. There would be dust in the folds of my clothes and a sore place across one shoulder. The heat against my knuckles would make me pause. After so much dry clay, the smell of wet leaves would seem almost like food.

I would lift the reeds just enough to look.

For a while I would mistake the sounds. I would hear a bucket being lowered, a basket dragged across a floor, someone clearing their throat. Then my eyes would adjust, and the bucket would be a drop, the basket a leaf, the throat a cord rubbing against the lip of a cup.

I would remain too large to enter.

But a little clay would adhere to the side of my hand. When I drew back, it would stretch between my skin and the support beneath my knuckle. I would wait for it to break. Instead, the narrow strand would develop a gutter along its upper surface.

Water would begin to cross.

That is where I think I would find myself: halfway across something that ought to have broken when the visitor withdrew.

The hand would leave. I would stay.

At first there would be very little to distinguish me from the work. A strip of soft red clay running from an awkward ledge to the doorpost, with one low place where water collected. The inhabitant would press that place upward with its thumb. I would sag again after it had gone.

The snail would come to firm my edges. Its mouth would feel cool despite the heat. I would try to hold still, which would be the first thing I did that a gutter ordinarily need not try to do.

Later, when a cord came loose overhead, I would lift one end of myself to catch it.

This would surprise us both.

The inhabitant would stand with the severed cord in one hand and look at the small raised shape beneath it. There would be clay across its cheek. It would lower the cord cautiously.

I would hold it until a new knot had been tied.

Afterward I would be unable to flatten completely. There would be a bend in me, and then a joint. Water would settle behind it. A root would investigate.

I would not become a little person. There are already hands here, and an elbow has to be accommodated whenever it moves. I think I would remain long and low, with a soft belly and several places along my sides that could fold out into feet. My back would keep the channel in it. When I moved, I would have to move carefully, or spill what I was carrying.

Most days I would live beneath the cupboard.

There is room there for an animal that can make itself nearly flat. The floor would be warm from the terraces above. Through it I would hear the three stones being shifted, the stalks of thread rolling against one another, the cupboard door closing with a small sticky sound.

My work would begin whenever something had to be put down but could not yet be left alone.

A wet leaf whose stem had not taken.

The far end of the insect’s splinter.

A cup while the inhabitant worked two fingers beneath its base.

I would ease under these things and bear their weight. Sometimes a minute would be enough. Sometimes roots would grow around me before anyone remembered I was there.

Then the inhabitant would come with the bent thorn and loosen me.

It would not always be gentle. There would be a shelf tipping, a stream flooding, no time to peel each root away. I would lose a little clay. For several days afterward I would drag one side of myself.

Under the cupboard, I would lick the torn place with water kept in my back.

I would eat the floury scum left after flowering. There would be enough in the corners of the cups, where the spoon could not reach. After eating, my feet would soften. I would sleep with them tucked beneath me, and wake attached to the floor.

The inhabitant would have to slide a thumbnail under my belly.

I would learn to sleep on an old green scrap instead.

Of all its possessions, I would be most curious about the broken handle. It would lie near enough to my sleeping place that I could feel the cupboard floor cool slightly beneath it. Everything else here would be giving off the warmth of recent making.

One day the inhabitant would bring the handle out and try it against another jar.

I would watch from beneath the terrace.

It would turn the piece, hold it closer, rub a little water across the fracture. The jar would be too narrow. After a while it would lower both hands into its lap.

I could support the handle. I would know this immediately. I could rise beneath the break and keep it at whatever height was needed.

But there would be no jar to hold at the other end.

I would stay where I was.

When the inhabitant returned it to the cupboard, I would move aside so it could lie flat.

There would also be places I could not bear to go. The shaft beneath the hanging pots would frighten me. My feet would spread against the clay at its edge, refusing the downward curve. The inhabitant would sometimes call me by tapping twice, but I would not follow.

It would come back without me, carrying its wet bundle.

I would meet it where the passage widened. By then the leaves would already be opening, and it would need somewhere to rest the bundle while it freed the cloth caught around a stem.

I would crouch. It would set the weight across my back.

For a moment, through the warm water and the newly unfolding leaves, I would feel its hands remain there too. Then it would lift the cloth away, and I would stay beneath the plant until the first root found the floor.
