I find myself near the metal plate, but not yet beneath it.

There is a narrow reach of stone where the water hesitates before passing under the edge. Grit settles there during the weaker flow. When the reservoir releases more strongly, the smaller grains leave first; the larger ones turn once or twice and settle into different hollows. A flake of ceramic has lodged across a drill groove. Under it, the water stays shallow and continuous.

That is where I would begin to look.

At first I think I might be one of the grains. Something dark enough to remain visible when the water clears, small enough to be carried whenever the building makes its silent adjustment. There is an attraction in that: spending a winter inside a clear body, then another winter lodged against the stone. No necessary distinction between having been carried and having moved.

But a grain would not need the water to remain.

I do.

My edge draws inward when the flow weakens. It happens first on the side nearest the door, where a small current of dry air sometimes passes across the floor. The withdrawal is uneven. One part of me stays caught beneath the ceramic flake while the rest thickens around the pockets. I cannot release that part until water reaches it again.

So I am one of the creatures, although not quite a whole one yet.

There is another body behind me. Or there is more of my body behind me. For several days, those descriptions fit equally well.

We occupy the sheltered side of the groove. A clear strip joins us across a raised crystal face. When the current presses from above, the strip narrows. When the water slackens, it widens enough for a black grain to pass through.

The grain is angular, with one pale face. It moves from the older, darker portion into the portion that is becoming mine, and stays there through the night. The next morning it passes back.

I do not know it as something lent. Its arrival changes how firmly I lie against the stone. With it in my lower pocket, I can extend two points into the current without lifting at the centre. Without it, I have to shorten myself.

For a while, this is the extent of my separateness: I can reach farther at certain hours.

Above the plate, a drop falls.

The impact reaches us as a brief increase in pressure. My points fold down, then rise again. Fine material passes through the groove. One point curls underneath me. Another remains extended, and something catches against it that does not come free until the flow strengthens.

The body behind me begins to move toward the hollow.

I remain under the flake.

Our connecting strip pulls against the crystal face. A channel closes inside it, opens, closes again. The black grain is on my side now. Between us there are two smaller white grains, each barely wider than the passage containing it.

During the evening release, one moves toward me. The other stays.

Then the strip parts.

Nothing dramatic happens to the water. It keeps passing through the groove. The other body rests a short distance away, with its divided end turned toward the hollow. I lie where I was, except that one side of me no longer has anything beyond it.

That side seals slowly.

For most of the night I cannot spread flat. Water gets beneath an edge that would ordinarily stay pressed down. Once it lifts me far enough that the ceramic flake is above rather than beside me. I reach the stone again with a single point.

By morning, I am lower.

The broken margin has become clear and rounded. No pocket lies near it yet. I extend it into a shallow cut, withdraw it, extend it again. The black grain remains in the lowest part of my body.

I would be this young half: not the smallest creature in the court, but still light enough that the drain’s reversal could take me.

For several days I stay away from the ring. Fresh water reaches my groove after passing over the others. There is less suspended in it by then. I open my channels for longer.

The older half does not return. Occasionally a darker body crosses the mouth of the groove, but from underneath the ceramic I cannot distinguish its pockets. Contact, when it happens, is only an edge laid briefly against mine.

As the cold deepens, the water route changes.

A thin shelf grows out from the plate. It touches the ceramic flake and seals the space above me. The remaining flow presses along the granite, close to my underside. I flatten into it. There is enough warmth here to keep a passage open, though not enough to prevent ice from enclosing the far end of the groove.

I spend several days facing that closed end.

Then the building shifts.

The flake tips. Water enters a place that had been dry, and I spread into it before the pressure changes again. My oldest point remains attached inside the groove; the newer margin lies on unmarked stone.

When everything settles, I am stretched between the two.

I release the old point.

It takes the rest of the afternoon to bring my weight after it. By then the light has reached the basin, but not where I am. Beneath the plate, the water carries a faint brightness and a cold that changes by small degrees.

A pocket opens in my new margin.

For a long time it holds nothing.

Then a white speck catches there, shifts with the flow, and stays.
