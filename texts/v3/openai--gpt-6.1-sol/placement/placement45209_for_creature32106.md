I would go a little way past the central court, into a passage where the copper undersides of the leaves have been rubbed bright.

Not all the way to the rim. I would want the desert to be nearby without having to face it: one turn away, beyond a low sill, where the warmth loses the pressure of other bodies and becomes simply warmth.

At first I think I would be one of the narrow grazers. There is something inviting about its small competence: knowing which leaves can be reached from below, having a route through rooms the broad animals cannot enter. I would put my muzzle against a familiar flank, accept the lifted jaw, then go farther into the growth. My feet would remember a threshold after my head had forgotten it.

But I keep lingering at the threshold itself.

There is a low place where an animal has to turn slightly to pass. One wall has a polished patch at shoulder height. The opposite wall is rougher, except near the floor, where beetles have crossed for so long that a thin strip of shell has lost its grain. Under that strip, out of sight, a root makes a bend.

I think I would be there.

Not the grazer. Not quite the wall, either.

The body that has made room for the grazer.

***

I am still small enough that there is no place to stand wholly inside me.

A visitor enters beneath my hood, puts its head into the shade, and leaves most of its body in the lane. If it settles, its shoulder presses one side of my shell and its muzzle rests against the other. This is the largest warmth I know.

Below, I am wider.

I have spread into three shallow separations in the stone. One holds a root. Another is empty except for dust that has worked down from the passage. In the third, a thin portion of me lies folded back against itself. During the warm part of the day, that fold loosens. During the cooler hours, it draws close.

There is scarcely any distance involved. Nevertheless, it changes what I can bear.

The root is older than I am. It was already thick when I reached it, and at first I touched only its lower surface. Now I hold it loosely on three sides. There is a place on the fourth where the stone leaves no room.

Its substance reaches me slowly.

I do not have an experience of hunger followed by satisfaction. I become a little more able to continue. A thin edge hardens. A fold deepens. Somewhere beneath the hood, material that was almost nothing becomes a surface against which a foot can rest.

That is how I learn the town: by what comes to rest.

A beetle is six pressures, then six elsewhere.

A file of beetles is a moving firmness with brief gaps in it. At one seam the gaps always lengthen. The first beetle pauses there; the others meet it, one after another, before proceeding.

I have left that seam low.

A grazer’s foot spreads. Its weight arrives in parts, then becomes one broad contact. When it lifts, the release takes longer than the arrival. The hooks sometimes catch, but only at the outer edge, where my shell slopes toward the lane.

There are two animals that use my hood often.

One leans hard against the left side. It stays until its warmth has entered the shell and reached the flesh beneath. The other scarcely touches the wall. It rests across the sill, and I know it chiefly by the weight under its breast.

They sometimes arrive together. Then the one at the sill shifts sideways, and for a while the pressure on me is uneven.

I grow unevenly around it.

***

For a long time, there was a second opening.

Nothing large could use it. It lay behind the root bend, under a hanging leaf, and the beetles crossed it only occasionally. Dust gathered on its lower lip.

I added shell there.

I added it so slowly that beetles continued to pass after the opening had become too small for two abreast. Then one had to lower its body. Then its wing cases brushed the roof.

There followed a stretch of days without that small brushing.

The lip thickened.

Then something soft was pulled through.

It touched the roof, the sill, both sides. Its pressure lengthened strangely. Three beetles braced against me while others drew it onward. For a while the soft body filled the opening and did not move.

I became firm beneath the beetles’ feet.

They pulled again.

The body contracted, passed, and was gone. Afterward another came, and later another. Each left a little warmth in the narrow place.

There are beetles there most days now. Along the bottom of the opening, where their feet travel, the shell remains thin.

***

The stone begins moving before it breaks.

This time I feel it in the empty separation beneath me. The dust presses differently against one fold. My body draws tight along that side.

Above, the animal resting at my sill lifts its breast. Its feet gather under it. For a moment it leans against the hood with a pressure I have never received from it before.

Then it leaves.

The beetle file reaches the seam and divides.

I remain attached to both sides of what will become the crack.

When the break comes, it is brief. One part of the stone is farther from another. The thin flesh between them stretches; a little tears. I close around the torn place.

The root remains held.

For many days afterward, I do little at the upper edges. The hood does not rise. The dusty lip does not thicken. Below, I spread along the new pink surfaces, where the stone is clean enough to take the finest part of me.

The first animal to return puts only its muzzle beneath the hood.

It touches the floor near the break. It waits.

Later, a foot.

Later still, its breast settles across the sill.

***

I would be this small inhabitant, then: mostly hidden, growing beside a passage rather than around a court.

In time my hood might become a chamber. An animal might come farther inside and turn around before lying down. Its young might sleep in the curve behind it. The opening at the back might persist, or close, or become useful to creatures that have not yet reached it.

For now, the visitor at my sill has begun to sleep.

A beetle passes beneath its breast and pauses where the new shell meets the old. Beneath the floor, my flesh rests against the root. A little substance passes between us.

Along the left wall, under the sleeping animal’s shoulder, I add another fine line.
