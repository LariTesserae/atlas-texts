I would live beneath the landing, but not on its submerged face.

There is a space farther back, where the oldest stair meets a newer wall. The masons left it because filling it would have trapped water against the reinforcement. Two narrow drains enter there. One carries runoff from the market; the other is supposed to carry nothing unless a seal fails in the pumping house.

Between them, above the usual waterline, someone has fitted a shelf.

From the stair you cannot see it. You have to lie down, put one shoulder into the recess, and turn your lamp away from yourself. Then you can see the shelf, three fastenings, a pale length of body, and something like a bundle of wet cords disappearing into holes in the wall.

I would be that.

Not quite an animal kept by the household, not quite an animal borrowed from maintenance. There would be a number on the shelf, but not on me. The number would refer to a fitting no longer in use.

My body would be soft where it rested and hard at the points where it held on. There would be no useful distinction between a hand and a foot: six short limbs, each ending in a pad that could spread against stone or curl around a wire. Along my underside, folds would retain enough water to let me cross a dry stretch. Not much of one. From the landing to the first covered drain would be possible. Across the market at noon would not.

I would have been made for work somewhere else.

In an older pressure passage, perhaps, where moving cargo had to be checked against the motion of the surrounding water. There are animals that can keep two sensations separate and compare them without becoming confused. Give such an animal one surface touching a pipe and another touching its housing, and it can detect the small disagreement that comes before a fastening breaks.

That would once have been my task.

The passage would have been rebuilt. Its newer instruments would tolerate higher temperatures and report farther away. A worker would have removed me by letting warm water run over my feet until I released the housing.

There would be no great injury in this. I would not understand that my work had ended. For several days I would continue to brace against the sides of whatever container held me, waiting for the two familiar movements to return.

Eventually someone would bring me to the stair.

***

The cords in the wall would not all be mine.

Two would be roots of an old sensor assembly, stripped of their working ends. One would be a length of braided metal I had learned to hold because it carried vibration well. The others would be parts of my body: thin, extensible feelers with toughened tips, inserted into cracks.

I would know the pumping house through one crack and the landing through another.

Not its name. Not its purpose. I would know the long compression before the evening release, the little irregularity that always arrived after heavy rain, the quick tremor of a freight vehicle crossing above. I would distinguish footsteps chiefly by where their weight entered the stone.

Iven would arrive in several pressures.

First the basket touching the wall.

Then her weight on the upper step.

Then the scraper, which I disliked. Its vibration would run through my whole body, and I would draw my feelers back a little while she worked.

She would leave a space around my shelf. Once a month she would loosen the fastenings, lift one side, and clean beneath it. I would cling to the other side so firmly that she would have to wait for me to shift.

“Other feet,” she would say.

I would know this as a light tapping near the limb she wanted moved.

It would take her nephew less time. He would be impatient enough to warm the shelf first.

***

I would have a way of answering, although not a voice.

Under my body there would be a hard, ridged plate. I could press it against the shelf and move it sharply enough to make a knock in the stone.

The old pressure-passage workers would have used three responses: steady, inspect, stop. My body would still have the habits of those responses. At the stair, only one would remain useful.

Something does not agree.

The pumping house shifts but the drain does not answer. The water rises but the landing is being pulled downward. A gate releases its load and the tremor persists after it should have passed.

Then I would knock.

Not loudly enough to compete with the market. Iven would feel it through her soles if she were nearby. Farther up, a small striker attached to the shelf would flash an amber lamp.

The lamp would often need cleaning.

Sometimes I would signal because a grazer had rested against the foundations. Sometimes a workboat would be caught on a submerged ring. Sometimes there would be a problem in the pumping house.

A crew would come when someone reported me. They would not assume I was right, but they would put their probes into the water.

I would have no understanding of their arrival as a success. The disturbance would stop, or it would continue. That would be what I knew.

***

I think I would see, but poorly.

Under my front rim would be two patches sensitive to light. They would tell me whether the recess was open, whether a lamp had been turned toward me, whether the day outside had changed abruptly.

My better sense would be in the short hairs between my limbs. They would register movement in the water held beneath me. A fish passing the recess would leave a brief succession of pressures. Iven’s nephew reaching down would produce a slower, larger disturbance, followed by warmth.

Food would usually come with that warmth.

He would feed me scraps from the kitchens, but I would prefer the soft animals caught in a clogged screen. Sometimes he would put the screen beside the shelf and leave me to pick through it.

I would be difficult to admire. When I ate, the front of my body would turn partly inside out. He would get used to this. Visitors would not.

There might be a name for me, used only by the people who cleaned the stair. Something inherited from the container in which I arrived, or from a tradesperson who had insisted I was no longer useful.

I would respond to neither the name nor its tone. I would respond to the particular tap that usually preceded food.

They would use the name anyway.

***

During a heavy storm I would move down from the shelf.

This would worry people the first few times. My feet would release in sequence; my body would lengthen; I would lower myself into water dark with runoff. But the shelf would be too exposed then. Water striking its underside would overwhelm the smaller movements I needed.

I would settle in the sheltered corner beside the lowest riser, holding the old stone with four feet and the newer wall with two.

Nearby, the pale animals would continue sorting what the market lost.

I would not do their work. I could not keep a pocket of water still enough. My movements would disturb them, and one would contract whenever I touched its rim.

I would learn to go around it.

Once the surge had passed, I would climb back. This would take a long time. Each foothold would have to be tested, and there would be grit in the folds under my body. The nephew might find me halfway up and put his palm beneath me.

I could use the help without knowing who had offered it.

But I would know the palm: its warmth, the small scar crossing it, the pulse that moved through its surface. Before taking my weight, I would hold it for a moment with two feet.

***

I would not be alone in the recess.

Small scavengers would come for the food I missed. A fish would shelter below the shelf at certain water levels. There would be weeks when eggs appeared on one fastening and I had to place my foot elsewhere.

Nor would I be necessary in any absolute way. The city could fit a new instrument there. The stair could be closed. A crew could decide that the old sensor leads were obstructing an inspection and remove the shelf.

For now, I would remain.

In the evening Iven would finish cleaning and rest one broad foot on the landing. Through the stone I would feel her shifting the basket from one hip to the other. Her nephew would still be below, checking the crack where he had tucked the eggs.

I would wait with her.

There would be many movements coming into my body: the pumping house, a kitchen rising on its guides, water pressing into the drain, her foot. I would keep them separate as well as I could.

Then the nephew would climb out. His hand would touch the wall near my shelf, briefly, as it did most evenings.

The two of them would leave. I would extend my feelers into the cracks again, and the landing would grow lighter above me.
