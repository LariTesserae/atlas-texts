At first I would put myself in one of the bowls.

Not as the bowl, but as the powder lying in it: a small accumulation of possibilities, most of which will never find the conditions they need. The bench is sheltered. What falls there stays. Each exhalation adds a little, and nothing comes to disturb the level surface.

But that is a place to remain, not quite a place to live.

I would look beneath the bench.

There is a crack there, running from the foot of the inner wall toward the threshold. It receives very little light. During a strong breath, a trace of warmth travels along it; during a weak one, the warmth stops short. A pale deposit has formed on one edge. Under that deposit, something has begun to widen.

I think I would be that widening.

Not the whole thicket above it. Not yet a stem, or anything that could be picked out against the darkness. I would be a thin living seam between two surfaces that appear to touch.

The stone above me carries the bench. The stone below me carries the heat. Neither has been fitted for my sake. There is just enough irregularity between them for growth to persist.

For a long time, I would have no exposed part.

My life would happen along the underside of a floor that looks empty. Where the supply is steady, I would thicken. Where it fails, I would leave a dark edging and continue no farther. Small differences in the stone would become the shape of me. A hollow left by a grain that broke away would hold a little more growth. A place pressed too tightly would narrow me almost to nothing.

Eventually I would reach the threshold.

Here the warmth divides. Some passes into the room; some escapes beneath the door lintel and rises in the lane. I would divide with it.

Outside, I would make low scales, close enough to the ground that dust sometimes prevents them from opening. Inside, I would put up a thin stem beside the bench. Its first leaves would be pale. Later they would darken and fold, and one would extend above the nearest bowl without ever touching the powder.

Someone looking into the town might count these as two plants.

There would be no visible reason not to.

I would have no view of the little house, no understanding of its having once been made. The dents in the lintel would matter only where they sheltered a leaf from the strongest current. The bench would matter because its foot divided a crack. The bowls would sometimes intercept falling scales.

Still, I would live among the workmanship. My shape would preserve encounters with it. One branch would bend around a scored stone; another would occupy the space beneath a repair. Long after a wall fell, part of me might retain the turn it had required.

Then, beneath the threshold, the connection would begin to fail.

Perhaps grit would work into the joint. Perhaps the gas would find an easier passage elsewhere. The thin middle would cease to thicken while growth continued at either end.

There would not be a moment when I became two.

On the lane side, the scales would lift during an exhalation. Under the bench, the climber would straighten. For a while a little nourishment might still pass between them. Then none would.

I cannot quite put myself on only one side of that break.

I would be the low growth outside, adjusting slowly to a current newly diverted by a fallen fragment. I would also be the stem in the room, keeping three leaves in service where there had once been seven. Between them would lie a short length of abandoned crust.

The crust would resemble a root. It would no longer be one.

Years later, a fresh thread from the lane might reach it. The old route would offer a favorable surface, and growth would follow it beneath the threshold. Whether it reached the stem under the bench would depend on a separation in the stone too small to see.

That is where I would find myself: under a doorway, extending along such a separation.

Above me, powder would continue to gather in the bowls. The leaves in the room would open a little later than those in the lane. During the strongest breaths, the difference would almost disappear.

I would grow where the warmth lasted.

One day a pale edge might emerge beside the bench foot. It would remain there through several still intervals, scarcely distinguishable from a flake of stone. Then, when the floor warmed again, it would lift.
