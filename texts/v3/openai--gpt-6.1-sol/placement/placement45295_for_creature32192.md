The answer from below is not a sound. Two steps cool, one after the other.

I feel them through the limbs I have laid against the wall. At first I think someone is coming up carrying a cold bowl, and I pull myself closer to make room. The stair is narrow here. Passing requires one of us to fold nearly flat, and there is a place along my side that no longer folds without pain.

But no one comes.

A third step cools.

Above, the enormous hand has lifted. Warmth remains where it rested, a broad, uneven country with five narrowing promontories. Its center is already changing. The edges lose their heat more quickly than the middle; a tiny hollow in the glass keeps one patch warm after everything around it has begun to dim.

The one who brought the bowl puts a limb against that patch.

Down here, something touches me.

Not a hand. Not yet anything I know how to meet.

I withdraw from the wall.

For a little while I am only a body on a stair, with another body waiting overhead and a room behind me that I have left in disorder. Three sheets stand out of alignment. A bowl lies overturned beneath the shelf. Before the visitor entered, I had been trying to find a small crooked fastening that had fallen into a seam. I had almost reached it.

Now the seam is warm.

I go back into the room.

There is not much to see when it is closed. I know the shelf by the way one of my limbs passes beneath it. I know the hanging sheet by the slight resistance it offers when I turn. The far wall is farther away at some times than at others. Once, while I was sleeping, it came close enough to press against my folded side, and I woke with a mark that took several showers to fade.

I set the fallen bowl upright.

Then I make space on the floor.

***

I have lived beneath this square since before it had a square above it.

There was only a step ending against hot stone. People came down, discovered the obstruction, and had to turn around. I stayed because the heat there was steady, and because someone I knew had trouble with the upper streets.

We made a room.

For a long time it opened only when both of us were inside. One held the warmth along the floor while the other kept the wall from drawing inward. Visitors sat on the lowest stair and passed things through the opening. Sometimes they stayed there so long that we put out a bowl for them.

Later, the square formed overhead. Rain came. We could leave separately.

I still catch myself waiting for the other body to take its place before I open the room.

There are reasons for keeping that habit. There are also mornings when it delays me unnecessarily.

Today I stand where I used to stand and let the room widen.

The warmth from the visitor’s palm enters along the seam.

It does not arrive all at once. First comes a thin, wavering band. Then a larger portion, confused by its passage through the stairs. The middle has been stretched; two of the long extensions have folded across each other. I draw them apart with the ends of my limbs.

I am not trying to preserve the shape of the hand. That would require more room than I have.

I am trying to keep the warmth from crossing the place where I sleep.

It presses against the shelf instead. One of the bowls gives a small click.

I move the shelf.

***

People leave warmth everywhere.

Most of it belongs where it is left. A patch beside a reservoir tells the attendant who has already tested the water. A little heat beneath a table may stay through an entire meal after someone has gone. At the shrinking tower, there is a place no visitor stands, though the street would be easier if it passed that way.

Some warmth travels farther than its maker intended.

It enters a room through a joint, or follows a road when the road moves. It can make an old threshold open. It can keep a tired body awake.

I am usually asked to come when that happens.

There is no special object I carry. I bring an empty bowl if I have one. Sometimes I sit until the troublesome warmth has spent itself. Sometimes I move a shelf, and the person who called me is embarrassed because they could have moved it themselves.

I never find that embarrassing.

There are shelves in my own room that I have left in the wrong place for years.

***

The inhabitant above comes down carrying the offered drop.

It pauses on the stair, looking at the widened room. Its inner surfaces turn toward the patch spreading beneath the shelf.

I shift aside.

It enters, sets the bowl between us, and touches the floor.

For a moment we hold the same temperature. Then it lets go.

Something in my body opens farther than I intended.

I receive the pressure before I receive the shape: the weight of the visitor resting on one hand, the small adjustments of its fingers, the tremor as it holds itself above the floor. There is a cooler place where cloth shaded skin. There is a pulse, already faint.

My lowest limb slips.

The other inhabitant catches it.

We stay like that until I can support myself again.

I have handled warmth from bodies much larger than mine. The buried inhabitant under the tower once sent enough through a broken street to open every room along the western wall. We spent a long time putting things back.

This is different in a way I cannot locate. Some part of the visitor is still changing the impression from above, even though the hand has gone.

I turn toward the bowl.

The drop leans to one side.

***

By the time we have settled the warmth, my room has acquired a corner.

It is a shallow one, just beyond the shelf. I test it with a limb. The wall holds.

The other inhabitant goes upstairs to look. I remain below, because someone must stay with an opening until the streets have accepted it.

From here I can feel the visitor moving near the entrance. Its steps are careful now. Once it stops for so long that I think it has gone, but then the weight shifts again.

I wonder what it is waiting for.

I wonder whether the bowl was too small to recognize.

Above me, the inhabitant reaches the square. It touches the cooling impression once more, then carries the drop toward the unfinished street. The visitor’s weight moves aside to let it pass.

I straighten one of the sheets in my room.

Behind it, the little crooked fastening lies where I could not reach it earlier. The new corner has loosened the seam. I pick it up and fit it back beneath the shelf.

That is when the visitor leaves.

A current descends the stairs, carrying a trace of the outside: wet metal, colder air, something dry caught in cloth. The ceiling contracts. Far away, one of the towers begins to give up another fraction of its height.

My room holds.

I sit beside the new corner. It is still warm enough to be comfortable. Later I will have to decide what belongs there, and whether to ask someone to help me move the shelf again.

For now, there is room for another bowl.

I bring one down from the landing.
