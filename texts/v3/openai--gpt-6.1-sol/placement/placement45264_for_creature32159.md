I think I would be under the work shelter.

Not hidden. There is a task down there, and I have stayed with it longer than necessary because the light comes through the floor trays in narrow stripes, and because nobody can hand me another task without first crouching to find me.

I am clearing the shed skins from around a lashing.

The skins belong to the little shell animals. They have worked themselves between cord and stalk, where pressure has flattened them into thin, sharp flakes. Left there, they will saw through the fiber. I pick them out with a hooked thorn and collect them in a seed-case lid. They are useful for scraping food stones, though too brittle for anything requiring a handle.

Above me, someone shifts a carrying frame. Dust falls through the floor.

I tap the underside twice: *Not yet.*

The weight moves away.

My place in the settlement would begin with small exchanges like that. I would know which floor tray must be lifted before which other tray, which doorway has a cord that looks loose but is carrying half the wall, which household packs its warm stone too soon and then has to unpack it to finish softening the food.

I would be one of the people who takes things apart.

***

I have long fingers, even for these people, with a slight inward bend at the last joints. As a child I could reach into the narrow throats of empty seed cases to retrieve things other children had dropped there. For a while this made me popular. Then someone pushed a live shell animal into a case to see whether I could get it out.

I still have a pale crescent beside one fingernail.

My eyes are ordinary. My feet grip well enough. I am not particularly good at knowing what the ground is saying.

This embarrasses me more than it should.

When I put my teeth to a listening peg, I feel the great crowded pressure of the place: a knock inside a rasp inside something that might be a long tear or might be the passage of many small feet. Esset can separate them. I keep listening until my jaw aches, certain that the next moment will make the difference clear.

Sometimes she takes the peg away.

“You have been listening to your own teeth,” she tells me, close enough that I can hear her.

But a rope is simpler. Its pull changes under my fingers when someone steps onto a floor. If a brace has begun to twist, one diagonal tightens before the others. A lashing that was fitted in cooler air may draw so close against a swelling stalk that I can no longer get a thorn beneath it.

I remember how each fastening felt when we made it. That is what I can check.

The shelter keeper trusts me with the cords along the lower edge. He checks the overhead ones himself.

***

My first important tool was a wedge carved from the rib of an exploded seed case. One end is thin enough to work beneath a loaded knot. The other has two shallow hollows where my thumb has worn it.

I carry it inside my clothing, though it has no need of warmth. I dislike reaching for it and finding it somewhere else.

With it I can ease a little space into a knot without putting my finger where the cord will tighten. Then a second wedge holds the space while the first goes deeper. Most knots yield. Some must be cut, and part of the work is recognizing those before the blade becomes difficult to reach.

There is a cord in the oldest house that I have wanted to cut for three moves.

Its owner insists that it is sound.

It is sound. It is also too short, and every time the house is assembled we have to draw two panels closer than they ought to be. The resulting narrowness pleases her. It makes the doorway familiar.

I carry an extra length for her without saying what it is for.

***

I would have a house, though at first I imagine myself sleeping in the shelter, always available, owning almost nothing.

That would become less attractive after enough nights beneath other people’s work.

My house would be one of the six, the narrow one with a patch of darker husk beside the entrance. Inside, there would be a sleeping wrap, a warming slab, three shallow food dishes, and a screen I had made badly enough that I could recognize it wherever it was carried. One corner would remain slightly crooked.

Beneath the wrap I would keep a small piece of an earlier floor.

There is a hole in it. The hole was made by a thorn pushed through from below, years ago, when someone wanted my attention and did not wish to wake the others. I woke with the thorn beside my cheek.

The person who pushed it is now living several chambers away. We exchange route bundles when there is someone to carry them. Most contain practical news. One contained a little scraping edge, no explanation. I sent back a better one and regretted it afterward; perhaps the first had been a gift rather than a request for repair.

I have not told anyone why I keep the floor scrap.

It is small enough that nobody has asked.

***

Beneath the work shelter, the stalk presses against my shoulder.

At first I think someone above has shifted a load.

Then the pressure continues after the footsteps stop.

I put the seed-case lid on the ground. The flakes inside it chatter. A little farther along, the clean oval grazed beneath the floor changes shape as the tissue rises.

The short-footed grazer folds itself.

I back out on my elbows just as Esset loosens the first lashing.

She does not need to tell me where to begin. I see her two fingers laid along the rib.

The boy comes for the grazer. I lift one corner of the nearest tray so he can get both hands around it. Its feet catch in the shredded bark, and for a moment he pulls while it holds on. I touch his wrist.

He stops.

We peel the bark away instead.

Then he is out, carrying the animal against him, and I am working the first hook free.

There is always a moment in dismantling when a familiar room becomes a collection of pieces. It happens quickly. A tray comes away, exposing the stalk beneath. The basket that stood beside the wall no longer has a wall beside it. Someone standing in what was the doorway turns and walks through the place where we kept the food.

Usually I enjoy this part.

Today the rib is rising too fast.

One of the loaded cords draws tight around my wedge. I cannot pull it free. The shelter keeper reaches for his blade, but I touch the back of his wrist and point to the tray beside him.

He lifts. The pressure eases.

I get the wedge out, undo the cord, and pass him its end.

We work more quickly after that.

***

The new surface across the basin looks close.

It is not close enough to step onto.

Between our leaf and that pale strip is a lower seam full of moving bodies. Grazers are crossing it. Their backs make the distance look solid until a break in the column reveals the depth underneath.

Two residents have found another approach along a stiff shoot reaching from the side. They are testing it with a pole.

I would want to go over immediately. I know this about myself. An empty surface makes me imagine arrangements: the shelter there, the six doors turned toward one another, food stones near the warm edge. I can furnish a place before anyone has established that it will bear our weight.

So I make myself finish coiling the cords.

Across the gap, the pole sinks through the fur. One resident leans on it. The other watches the base of the shoot.

They come back without giving the crossing signal.

We wait.

The carrying frames stand ready. Our houses lie in bundles around our feet. With everything down, I can see two neighbors who were hidden from me by walls a short while ago. One is rubbing pollen from the seam of her outer eyelid. The other has opened his packed food and is eating.

Esset moves her peg to a different point on the rib.

I sit on a floor tray. The boy brings the grazer over and lowers it beside me.

“You take it,” he says.

“I took it last time.”

He puts two fingers under my elbow: *Hold this weight.*

I look at him.

He leaves the animal and goes to help his father.

***

When the crossing is finally tried, I go second.

The first person carries nothing but a pole and a loose cord. I follow with wedges, spare fiber, and one empty floor tray. Halfway along the shoot, we stop while a column of small grazers passes beneath us. Their feet tick against the tissue through my soles.

The far surface is warm and thickly furred. It gives under my weight, then holds.

I lay down the tray. We stand on it together, testing how the leaf responds to our combined load. Near its margin, new fur is still separating from a close pale mesh.

There will be work here even if we stay only a little while.

I would be thinking about the shelter corners, and whether the shortest cords would reach, and where to put the food so the smaller eaters could not approach unseen. I would also be looking for somewhere to set the grazer when it arrives. Its shortened feet make bare tissue difficult.

Behind us, a loose end of cord catches in the new growth.

I turn back to free it before anyone pulls.

There: that is where I find myself. Not yet inside the next house, no longer inside the old one, crouched at the edge of the crossing with a small necessary thing caught between my fingers.

I loosen it carefully.

Then I carry it on.
