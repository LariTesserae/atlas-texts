At first I think I would be in the narrow shelter, somewhere near the dying man’s feet.

Not on his shelf. Below it, where the wall meets the floor and a little amber light comes through the doorway. I would know the scrape of the feeding bowl being set down. I would be close enough to hear what he says when the person tending him has gone out.

But the floor is not where I am. It is above me.

His voice reaches me through the underside of the stone. The words arrive without their edges. I know the long vibration of a complaint, the shorter sound that follows it, the shift of weight when someone sits beside him. When his heel moves against the shelf, dust loosens from my ceiling.

I have been removing that dust for some time.

The passage here is narrow enough that my shoulders touch both sides. This is comfortable. Behind me it opens into a chamber where I can turn, and beyond that are two ways toward the heat. One is good. One has begun to taste wrong.

I go a little way into the wrong passage each day. I press my face against its floor, then turn back.

Something farther down has closed.

***

I would be a burrower, though not the one the people usually hear beneath the cooking seam.

Mine would be the smaller workings along the back wall: shallow passages under the shelters, a deeper run beneath the rack of hooks, a chamber under a stone that Eren thinks is solid. I would have digging shoulders and a face badly suited to being seen. In darkness I could put that face against everything. The floor, the ceiling, a clot of packed powder, the flank of another burrower.

Exposed, I would have nowhere to put it.

My eyes would tell me very little. The broad soles passing overhead would be more distinct than the people attached to them. I would know the oldest woman by the interval between her steps. Tal would be a confusion of small impacts, stopping and starting, sometimes returning along the way she had just come.

Eren would be the one who waited.

I would often stop beneath her. She would often stop above me. Neither of us would know whether the other had finished.

***

My body would fit the work.

The heavy plates along my shoulders would loosen stone where it had already begun to fail. My forefeet would gather the fragments against my chest. To carry them away, I would back through the passage, pushing a load with the underside of my chin.

There would be a bare patch there, polished almost white.

Warmth would reach the skin of my face before it reached the rest of me. I would turn toward it and hold still, testing. Too little warmth, and a passage would gradually fill with powder. Too much, and I would retreat before the next breath came through.

I would eat from the walls of the good passage, taking a thin crust away with the small plates inside my mouth. There would be places I returned to and places I passed without feeding. The people would not see a difference between them.

For a long time, the passage beneath the narrow shelter would have been a good one.

Then the warmth would begin to collect at its far end.

The stone above would stay warm after everything around it had cooled. I would knock at it with my shoulder. Work, at first: a short series of blows to loosen a projecting edge.

The edge would not loosen.

Above me, someone would lie down and listen.

I would hear a wrist touch the floor.

***

They might move a stake outside the shelter. They might move it back.

The old man would object to something. His heel would strike the shelf, sending down a familiar sift of dust. I would carry the dust away, return, and find more.

The blocked passage would grow hotter.

To open it, I would have to approach from the other side. That would mean leaving the shallow workings and taking the deep run beneath the wall. I would dislike that run. Its floor would slope toward a heat I could feel but could not reach, and another burrower would sometimes occupy the turning place.

We would have pressed our faces together once, then backed away in opposite directions.

This time the turning place would be empty.

I would work there for much of a day. Above, people would cross the hollow, eat, repair a basket. The One That Clicks would drag its outer hooks over the stone. Its ticking would travel down through the old channels and mingle with the sound of my digging.

When I reached the obstruction, I would find packed red powder held beneath a slab that had tilted by less than the width of Tal’s finger.

I would begin pulling it out.

***

The wind would reverse while my head was inside the blockage.

Down there, I would feel it as a sudden loss of pressure against my face. The passage would draw air past me. Loose powder would slide toward the opening I had made.

I would back out.

Not far enough.

The mountain’s breath would catch beneath the tilted slab and lift it. A crack would open through my ceiling. For a moment, grit and light would fall together.

I would be on the surface before I knew there was a surface to be on.

There would be room everywhere. My shoulders would touch nothing. The stone under my chin would be moving with the footsteps of people I could not locate.

I would press myself flat.

A shadow would cross me, then withdraw. Something hard would touch the pavement beside my face.

Not claws. The end of a forked pole.

Eren would be crouching beyond it. Tal would be held by the back of her belt, reaching down with her scoop despite being told not to.

I would not know either of them.

The scoop would come close enough that I could feel its edge, and I would push against it, trying to get my head underneath. Tal would pull it away in surprise.

Then the slab would arrive.

Two people would lower it across the broken pavement, leaving an opening at one end. Its underside would shut out the light. I would move beneath it so quickly that my shoulders scraped both edges.

There would be dust under my face.

There would be a ceiling.

I would stay there until the feet had gone.

***

Afterward, the people would discuss what had happened beneath the recess.

Eren would put a stake outside the doorway. Someone would move the old man’s shelf a little farther along the wall, and he would complain that now he could not reach the place where he kept his scraper.

Below, I would clear the fallen powder.

The new slab would not fit the old passage exactly. One edge would leave a low pocket where warmth gathered. I would widen the approach to it, then open a channel through the debris toward the deeper run.

For several days my shoulders would be sore. I would rest under the shelf and listen to the bowl being set down.

The old man would accept a piece of pith. Another would be offered. He would turn his head, and the woman beside him would wait.

I would know the waiting by how little came through the stone.

Eventually her feet would move. His heel would shift once above me.

A little dust would fall.

When I got up, I would carry it away.
