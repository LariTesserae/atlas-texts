I would be beneath the fitted slab, close enough to the inhabitant that its movement reached me before I could see it.

From there, the hollow would not look like a courtyard. It would have no whole shape. There would be the underside of the slab, a pale strip of wall, and the gray-brown fold of an elbow moving past. Most of the entrance would be hidden. Morning would arrive as a little more light on the submerged stones.

I think I would have weight, but not enough to remain exactly where I had been put. Over many days, the water could work a grain of grit beneath me. I would begin to rest unevenly. When the inhabitant next reached for me, it would feel the small rocking movement and set me down somewhere flatter.

That adjustment would matter to me.

At first I imagine having feet of my own, small ones suited to those narrow gaps. But there is scarcely anywhere to go that the inhabitant does not already know, and little there to sustain another moving body. I find myself wanting less movement, not more. To be lifted occasionally. To have a place that becomes familiar through being returned to it.

I would be the earthenware vessel.

Not when it was new. I would already have lost the handle on one side. The break would be old enough that the exposed clay had darkened, though its roughness would remain beneath the mineral film. On the other side, the surviving handle would be sound. There would be room through it for the inhabitant’s fingers, but little room to spare once the crust thickened.

I would know its hand by where the pressure fell.

One finger would take most of my weight. Another would steady the handle near its lower join. Sometimes the thumb would rest against my wall; sometimes, when I was tilted, it would move to the rim. The inhabitant would make these changes without pausing. I would have been lifted this way so often that the worn places belonged as much to the hand as to the clay.

My inside would remain darker than my outside.

Fine sediment would settle there, brown against brown, visible only when a little light found its way beneath the slab. I would hold it while the inhabitant waited. Some grains would take longer to fall than others. A slight movement of the wrist would send them up again, and then there would be another interval of stillness.

I would have no means of making that waiting shorter.

This seems important to what I would be here: useful, but not quick. The inhabitant would bring me through the water and hold me at an angle. Water would leave over my lip. The soft deposit would remain. Then a bare fingertip would pass along the inside, carefully enough not to take much grit with it.

Afterward, I would be lowered into the basin again.

I would not be empty for long. Water would enter as soon as my rim went under. It would occupy the scratches inside me, the shallow unevenness of my base, the small hollow where the clay had been pressed thin before firing. There would be no dry interior kept apart from the spring.

Still, I would not quite become one of the pools.

The inhabitant could pick me up. That would remain our distinction, even as the same pale deposit gathered on my rim and the ledge beside me. I could carry a small portion of the hollow somewhere else within it. Across a basin. Up a step, perhaps, if there were reason. Usually only a hand’s breadth sideways, out of the way of an elbow.

Visitors on the slab would seldom notice me. Their heels would appear above the water, then disappear into it. Their movements would send a slow disturbance through the basin. Occasionally one might bend down and see my mouth in the dimness.

I would hope they left me where I was.

Not because a human hand would be unwelcome. It would simply be less certain. Someone might lift me by the rim, or put a finger against the missing handle as though wondering whether it could be repaired. They might set me on the slab to look at me. The warmth would drain from my wet surface there. The inhabitant would have to reach farther to bring me back.

I would prefer its ordinary reach.

In time, the crust might begin to close the space beneath my remaining handle. The inhabitant would notice when its fingers no longer passed through comfortably. It might take the harder stone from the shelf and scrape the deposit away, holding me against its body while it worked.

There would be a few small shocks. Fresh pale scratches. Clouded water around us.

Then its fingers would fit again.

That is where I find myself most fully: against the inhabitant’s side, held steady while it makes enough room to continue using me. Afterward it would rinse the loosened mineral from my surface and lower me beneath the slab.

My base would touch stone. The hand would stay a moment, checking that I sat evenly.

Then it would let go.
