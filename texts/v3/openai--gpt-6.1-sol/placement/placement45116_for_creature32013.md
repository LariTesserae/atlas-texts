At first I would mistake the brightness for a place to go.

I would be one of the narrow animals, still able to fit my whole body between two leaves. Above me, the underside of the surface would hold a second grove. I would swim toward a fork in that grove and find no bark there. My front would touch the water’s upper skin. For a moment, part of me would be cold.

Then I would fold downward again.

I would not yet know how to stop. I would rest against things and release them: a root too smooth to grip, a leaf that turned under my weight, a purple face of stone where there was nothing to eat. In open water, the dark cord inside me would be almost the only part of me that cast a shadow.

A current would take me over a saddle.

Not much of a current. I could swim against it, but I would have to keep swimming. If I folded my fins and waited, the saddle would gradually pass beneath me. Pollen would travel alongside. Ahead, the slope would descend into trees whose lowest branches disappeared in blue shade.

I would catch on a root there, release it, and catch again farther down.

The place I stayed would not be the warmest place or the brightest. It would be on the sheltered side of a pale trunk growing outward from the slope, just below the point where the trunk began to turn up. Leaves would have collected between it and the mountain. One hanging root would enter that bed, emerge farther down, and enter it again.

I would lie along the root.

For several days, I would still have a direction. My front would remain pointed toward the valley, and I would fold my fins whenever a change in the water reached me. Each time, their edges would pull against the bark. Each time, a little less of me would come free.

Then the edges would widen.

Pollen would lodge along them. Small fringes would open, and the gathered particles would begin to move inward. My body would flatten into the shallow space behind the root. The cord that had run straight through me would divide around a knob of bark.

After that, leaving would require gathering myself.

There would be no single moment when I decided not to.

***

I would live mostly below the leaves, but not entirely.

One part of me would climb the trunk into the light. Another would pass through the litter and come against stone. A loose strip would hang where the slow circulation crossed the saddle. It would sometimes bring food to me, and sometimes lean away for so long that I would withdraw it and grow elsewhere.

My first bare patch would be smaller than a leaf.

I would clear it by degrees, lifting the coating into a fold, drawing that fold back, settling against what remained. The stone beneath would be cool and finely ridged. Along one side, a root pressed into a crack.

There I would feel changes that did not reach my feeding edges.

Warmth would arrive through the rock while the water around the trunk remained unchanged. I would loosen my contact, then settle again. When it came more strongly, I would draw my newest tissue toward the root.

Nothing would break.

The warmth would diminish. I would stay where I had moved.

Farther up the trunk, a fallen bladder would lodge beneath a stone lip. Its skin would soften. The air inside would join a pocket already there, enlarging it enough to displace one of my upper folds.

For a while that fold would hang below the pocket. Then its margin would thicken against the wet boundary.

I would come to have a small part that touched air.

White roots would occupy most of the chamber. Moisture would gather on their tips and fall against me in drops too small to feel anywhere else. Below, my fringes would continue gathering from the water. The difference between the two places would persist through the length of my body.

***

There would be another animal above me.

I would meet it first along a root, where my growth reached farther than before. Its edge would tighten. Mine would stop.

We would remain like that through a long interval of empty water.

Later, a scrap of bladder skin would catch between us. My fringe would turn toward it. So would the other’s. The scrap would stretch, part, and disappear along two separate channels.

I would not extend farther on that root.

Elsewhere, there would still be room. A branch beside the trunk would dip beneath a thickening crown, opening a narrow gap that had previously been closed. I would spread through it. When the branch moved again, my fold would take some of its weight.

Over time, its resting position would change. Light would reach a shoot underneath.

That shoot would grow past me, its leaves silver on the side I touched. I would have to move a feeding edge around its stem. Later, I would clear fallen leaves away from it because they covered the channel through which food reached my lower body.

The shoot would keep growing.

***

I would not remain wholly in that first place.

The root around which I had settled would darken and soften. Some of my oldest tissue would loosen with it. Leaves would enter spaces that had once been occupied by my body. A new stem would rise through a fold that no longer tightened.

But farther up, beneath the air pocket, there would be young inside me.

One would straighten before the others. Its dark cord would lie briefly alongside one of my branching seams. When its pouch opened, it would remain caught among the fringes, folding against the slight movement of water.

I would loosen around it.

It might stay on the same tree. It might pass over the saddle. I would feel its last contact and, after that, feel only what arrived where it had been: water, a grain of pollen, the tip of a root.

By then, I would occupy parts of two neighboring trunks. I would have lost the place where my front once lay. The bare patch on the mountain would be larger, though one corner would have grown over where I had ceased to press against it.

If you came looking for me, you would have to remain beside the tree.

A silver leaf would rise a little from the litter. Behind it, a fold would open. Higher up, something nearly invisible would tighten between a branch and the trunk.

You might put your hand near the stone and feel warmth.

I would already have drawn back from the crack. One of my loose edges would still be resting against its cooler side, and another would be opening into the water, slowly finding a place to attach.
