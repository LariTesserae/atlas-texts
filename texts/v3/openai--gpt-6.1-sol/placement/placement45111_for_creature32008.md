At first I would mistake my place for the chamber beneath the seed-breaking stone.

It is dry there. The ceiling slopes low enough that my back touches it when I turn, and a root hangs from one corner, bare of anything worth eating. Close to the entrance, reflected light reaches the floor. Farther in, the darkness is complete.

I would sleep with my nose beneath the thick root of my tail. My coat would make me look larger than I am. Beside me, two other bodies would give off warmth; when one shifted, I would shift without waking. We would fit ourselves around the same small irregularities in the floor.

The roar would enter through the stone.

Outside, it would fill the air so thoroughly that I could not have learned to think of it as a sound with a beginning. Inside, some of its force would be lost. Particular vibrations would remain: one beneath my ribs, another in the joint of my jaw where it rested on the floor. When the wind changed direction, I would wake before the air at the entrance changed.

I would lift my head. Wait. Put it down somewhere else.

That might be the first thing to know about me: I would have several places to put my head.

There would be a hollow where the noise was bearable, a ridge where I could feel another animal approaching, a patch of packed nest material that remained warm after everyone had left. I would know them without seeing them. In sleep, a forepaw could find the edge of the nest and pull a loose feather nearer.

But the chamber would only be where I rested.

My place would extend out through the crooked passage, past the stone that scraped one flank unless I turned slightly, to the bright mouth of the crack. There I would stop with my body still concealed and my nose in the cold.

Nothing moving.

A wing, far off.

Nothing moving nearby.

I would come out.

My sight would be better at finding interruption than explaining it. A shadow passing over the gravel would stop my forefoot in the air. The dark outcrop across the basin would scarcely matter. I would not mistake it for something close, because I would have no reason to go there. The distances important to me would lie between this crack and the next shelter, between a feeding patch and the groove from which I could safely inspect it.

There would be one exposed stretch I disliked.

It would be short enough for a person to cross in three steps. I would cross it quickly, my belly low, arriving with grit caught in the fur beneath my chest. Once across, I would turn and look back.

I would do this even on days when nothing had frightened me.

Near the silver leaves, I would become a different shape: folded onto my haunches, forepaws busy, muzzle working. I would scrape aside the dry surface until the soil underneath gave off its faint, close smell. A root might come free with a little tug. Another would hold, and I would brace both feet and pull until it broke or until I lost interest.

There would be many things I left unfinished.

A shell too hard to open. A passage explored only as far as a cold draft. A pale stem brought halfway home and abandoned when a bird landed above me.

I would return to some of them. Others would disappear from my life without consequence.

The favored stone would take longer to learn than it appeared.

As a young animal, I would carry a seed there because an older one had carried a seed there. I would sit where it sat and strike where it struck. The shell would bounce from my paws. I would retrieve it, strike again, then try biting it.

The older animal would already be eating.

I would crowd nearer, and it would turn its shoulder toward me.

Eventually I would discover the slight unevenness beneath the shell, the place my claws could hold. I would learn to rotate the seed after a blow. Some days I would open one quickly. On other days it would slip beneath the slab, and I would lie flat with one arm reaching into darkness, unable to accept that the thing I had just held was beyond me.

My whiskers would touch stone. My claws would close on grit.

I would stay longer than was useful.

Years later, a smaller animal would do the same beside me. I might let it take a piece I had dropped. I might eat the piece myself. Much would depend on how long I had been feeding.

I would not be the keeper of that stone. I would know the underside of its lip, though, and the sheltered place beside it where the afternoon warmth lasted. I would arrive empty-mouthed sometimes, search among the shells, and remain after I had found nothing.

A footfall would reach me from below.

I would press one forepaw down, then the other. Pause.

Whatever passed between us would be brief. Another animal was there. The passage was occupied. I was about to enter.

Enough to prevent our meeting face to face in the narrowest part.

There would be a season when I stopped sleeping beside the two warm bodies. Perhaps one vanished. Perhaps we crowded one another as we grew. I would try a chamber farther under the shelf, carrying grass into it a mouthful at a time, only to find after rain that water ran along its lowest edge.

I would leave the wet grass.

A second chamber would be dry but full of the force of the wind. I would sleep badly there, wake often, emerge with my coat lifted oddly along one side.

The chamber I finally used would have an awkward entrance and a shallow floor. One pale feather would catch on a projection above the nest. I would tug at it several times without freeing it. After that, it would remain.

This is where I think I would find myself: not beneath the most conspicuous stone, but under a shelf nearby, in a room imperfectly chosen and gradually made familiar.

Outside, my routes would overlap those of animals that seldom met me. We would drink from the same pockets at different hours. We would wear the same edge smoother. A bird would drop a feather, and I would spend most of a morning bringing it home.

I would grow older without the plateau showing it.

Perhaps I would hesitate longer before the exposed crossing. Perhaps I would choose softer seeds. My coat would still round out my body, and from a distance there would be little to distinguish me from the young following close behind.

Near evening, I would come up once more.

The stone would be warm against my forepaws. Light would reach beneath my chin. For a while I would hold something small and edible without eating it.

Then one of the young would move.

I would turn toward the crack, lower my head, and go in. Behind me, their feet would disturb a few shell fragments. I would feel them entering before they touched me.
