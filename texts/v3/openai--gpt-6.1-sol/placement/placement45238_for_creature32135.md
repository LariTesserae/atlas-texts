At first I would look for a room that had stayed in the same place.

Not a whole building. A room would be enough: a counter, a lamp, somewhere to put a cup without watching it. I would ask at the station, and someone would send me to a records office, and the records office would have moved. At its former entrance there would be a man fitting hinges to a door that now opened downward.

He would tell me where to try next.

After several days I would understand that I had been asking the wrong question. There are rooms here that have kept their furniture, their afternoon light, even the same patch of condensation beneath the sill, while moving a considerable distance. There are rooms that have not moved at all and can no longer be reached.

I think I would end up in one of the places where messages can be left.

It would occupy the narrow space beside a municipal greenhouse. One wall would be glass, whitewashed to waist height. The other would be unfinished material, inspected twice a week. On cold days the greenhouse workers would hang their coats on our side because the humidity spoiled the linings. We would complain about this, without removing the hooks.

There would be two entrances and a hatch. The hatch would once have been a third entrance. We would keep the lower half of its door in the storeroom in case it became useful again.

My work would be to receive things that had failed to arrive.

Mostly paper. A few keys. Invitations already out of date. Notices about pipes, wages, school closures. Once, a mitten containing a tooth.

People would come to the counter and say, “She knows this address,” or, “He always asks at the old gate first.” I would write down what they said. I would also ask where the person had last actually been seen. These would occupy separate columns.

I would not be particularly good at finding people at first.

I would trust a properly printed address more than a description such as *the room above the woman who sharpens scissors*. This would lead me to send a parcel to a numbered block that had been divided among three streets. The woman with the scissors, meanwhile, would still be sharpening them beneath the same room.

The parcel would return with its corner wet and a note pinned through the string.

After that I would keep both sorts of information.

***

There would be a bench for waiting, made in three sections so it could be carried through either door. Beneath it we would store a folded roof, a small stove, and the feet that allowed the counter to stand on uneven ground.

I would like the counter. Its surface would be covered in cuts, most of them made by people opening parcels before they had finished signing for them. Someone would have burned a crescent into it with the bottom of a pan. Along the customer’s edge there would be a shallow polished place where gloves had rested.

I would catch myself treating the counter as an address.

This would be harmless until it wasn’t.

One morning the floor would rise beneath its eastern end. We would adjust the legs twice, then stop, because the unfinished wall had begun to lean toward the glass. The greenhouse workers would help us move everything outside. The coats would go first. Then the ledgers, then the stove, then the counter.

By noon we would be working under the folding roof beside a freight ramp.

Several people would find us without difficulty. One would complain that we had moved farther from his house. One would congratulate us on the light.

For three days I would turn toward the old storeroom whenever I needed string.

***

I might become a listener, though not one licensed to examine foundations.

There would be a difference, and I would be careful about it.

Sometimes someone would bring us an account of a sound heard in a wall: a surname, an argument, the distinctive knock used by a relative. Usually they would want to know whether we had received a message from that person.

Occasionally they would want us to send one.

I would devise a form for this, and then discover that the form was too confident. It would have a space marked *time of occurrence*. People would put the time they heard the sound. Others would put the time they believed it had been made. Some would write both and draw an arrow between them.

I would change the heading.

Then I would add a space for uncertainty, which would fill up faster than any other part.

A woman would report hearing her husband call for a wrench. She would know it was him because he never asked for a size, only held out his hand and expected the right one.

“Is he missing?” I would ask.

“No. He’s at home. I want to know where he was doing repairs.”

I would explain that we could not establish that from a sound.

She would leave irritated. For the rest of the day I would imagine possible locations, none of which belonged in the record.

I think that would be the part of the work I had to learn most slowly: leaving a space empty after having thought of something to put in it.

***

There would be a gray bird that visited our unfinished wall.

It would arrive before we heard anything. At first I would enter its appearances in the daybook. After a while I would stop, because it also came when the greenhouse workers spilled seed.

I would still watch it.

One afternoon it would move sideways along the surface, feet spread, following a sound too faint for me to distinguish. I would fetch a stool and sit nearby. Through the wall there would come the scrape of a chair, water being poured, then a person laughing with their mouth closed.

Nothing else.

I would remain on the stool longer than necessary.

That evening, when my colleague asked why the receipts had not been sorted, I would say the wall had been speaking.

“What did it say?”

“Nothing we can deliver.”

She would hand me the receipts.

***

I would live within walking distance, though the walk would vary.

In my room I would keep a cup with a handle too small for gloved fingers. I would have bought it cheaply after advising someone else not to transport breakable things without padding.

A shrub would grow through the window in late winter. I would cut back its larger branches and leave a small one beside the lamp. Its leaves would remain cold even when the lamp had been lit for hours. At dawn it would drip onto the sill.

I would put a folded cloth beneath it, then forget the cloth until it began to smell.

I don’t think I would be unusually graceful about living here. I would resent a blocked stairway when I was tired. I would sometimes prefer an inaccurate map that got me home to an accurate one that required another hour of walking. I would have to remind myself not to repeat yesterday’s directions merely because I remembered them clearly.

But I would get to know certain people.

The child who collected messages for three households and insisted on signing separately for each. The repair cook who left her changing locations on cards stained with stock. An old man who came every Thursday to ask whether there was anything for his sister.

For months there would be nothing.

Then there would be a letter.

I would keep it at the front of the drawer, although our system required it to go under her name. On Thursday I would look up each time someone passed the greenhouse.

He would arrive late, annoyed about a footbridge. I would give him the letter. He would turn it over twice before putting it inside his coat.

“Do you need somewhere to sit?” I would ask.

“Not yet.”

He would leave by the nearer door.

I would watch long enough to see that he knew the steps had been moved. Then I would put the drawer back in order and take the next person’s address.
