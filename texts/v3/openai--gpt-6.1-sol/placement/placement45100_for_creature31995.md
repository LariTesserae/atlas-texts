At first I would take a room near the transit hall, on a floor too low for the flowering mosses.

There would be a stool, a cupboard, and a recess beside the door deep enough for two cups, though I would own only one. The previous tenant would have left a mark on the wall where a shelf had stood. On certain evenings the mark would become damp before the rest of the room. I would put nothing over it.

For several weeks I would believe myself unusually easy to house. I would sleep without disturbing the neighbors. The plant supplied with the room would remain upright. When asked whether I had gathered myself before boarding a train, I would say yes, because I had stood still and thought carefully about my hands and feet.

Then someone would knock.

Not an official. A woman from two floors below, carrying a saucer.

“Is this yours?”

On the saucer would be a little water. Nothing else.

I would look at it for too long.

“It has been keeping my cupboard door open,” she would say. “I don’t mind during the day.”

I would apologize. She would show me how to bring it home.

***

The work I find would involve places omitted from the newer maps.

Not whole districts. Usually a landing, a service recess, the last meter of a passage that had become shorter when a tower moved. The city would have accurate plans for its pipes, its foundations, its entrances. But a room could cease to be accessible without quite ceasing to be a room.

Someone would still occasionally set something down there.

A resident would put a parcel on a shelf that had been removed six years earlier. When they reached for it again, their hand would meet the wall. An old man would report a draft from his former kitchen. A child would insist that the school had a second cloakroom, with a yellow hook, and would become distressed when shown that it did not.

Most reports would go to maintenance. Some would come to the small office where I eventually work.

The office would have no title on its door. Its number would be sufficient. I would share it with a clerk who grows cuttings in jars and is quite severe about the distinction between a place being inaccessible and a person having forgotten where it is.

We would not be rescuers. Often there would be nothing to rescue.

Our first task would be to listen to the description.

“How high was the shelf?”

“Was the window open when you last used the room?”

“Did anyone else like sitting there?”

The clerk would enter dates. I would draw, badly. Sometimes the claimant would take the pencil from me.

We would visit with water, folded cloths, and a narrow lamp whose light could be lowered until it barely touched the floor. We would avoid opening walls until we had exhausted less expensive possibilities.

On my first successful visit, I would recover a spool of green thread.

The resident would cry when I handed it to her. Then she would become embarrassed and explain that it was exactly the color she needed.

I would believe her.

***

My difficulty would emerge gradually.

I would be good at standing where a door used to be. Better than the clerk, better than the horticulturists who sometimes accompanied us. If I waited long enough, my shoulders would ease. I would feel the small inclination of a floor beneath a covering of level pavement. I would know where to turn my wrist.

At first this would seem a useful aptitude.

Then I would begin to miss the rooms.

Not the people who had occupied them. I would have met some of those people only once. I would miss a high cupboard with nothing in it, a washroom whose drain had been warm, a square of dimness beneath a stair.

After visiting a demolished apartment, I would wake thirsty for several nights. My own cup would be full. I would drink from it and find that it did not help.

The clerk would notice that I had started drawing the same doorway in unrelated files.

She would turn one sheet around.

“Do you know this place?”

I would say no.

“Then stop putting it in other people’s rooms.”

I would try.

***

I think my visible body would be unremarkable. A face that strangers could fail to remember; hands with short nails; one shoulder lower because of the case I carry. I would have shoes repaired rather than replaced, and a coat whose inner pocket was always faintly damp.

I would dislike being asked what I was.

Not because I possessed a wonderful answer that must be protected. Because I would have several ordinary answers, and each would become less adequate if pressed.

I would be a tenant. An employee. Someone who leaves a spoon in the wrong drawer. Someone whose body is comfortable in a chair but whose sleep seems to require a space no longer present in the building.

There would be a courtyard I avoid.

When I first crossed it, the little lamps among the roots would go out one after another, not abruptly but with the slow dimming of wicks running dry. A gardener would come running from a shed. She would take my arm and lead me back to the paving.

Later she would explain that I had not harmed the plants.

“You gave them somewhere else to grow,” she would say.

By morning, several roots would have entered a patch of ground beneath which there was no soil on any municipal plan.

She would be more interested than frightened. This would frighten me a little.

***

I would meet the woman with the linen-wrapped bowl through work.

She would submit no complaint. She would simply come to the office and ask whether a place could need a doorway before it needed walls.

The clerk would say yes, in certain circumstances.

I would ask where the place was.

The woman would look at me, then at the damp inner edge of my coat.

“Perhaps you should come.”

We would travel south on a train whose windows take an unusually long time to mist. She would sit opposite me with the bowl on her knees. At the end of the line we would walk past the foundations, keeping to the completed paving until it stopped.

I would expect her to ask me to find someone.

Instead she would set down her stool.

“You can stand wherever you’re comfortable.”

This would be harder than any instruction I had been given.

There would be too much room. My attention would return to the city behind us: its separated towers, its sheltered hollows, the nearly inaudible exchange of descending notes. I would feel my rented room tugging at me, and other rooms too. A corridor whose paint smelled bitter. A chamber with a low, round window. Somewhere a loose catch would tap against a frame.

The woman would unwrap her bowl.

I would take one step off the paving.

Nothing dramatic would happen. The ground would bear my weight. A bright edge would form around the print of my shoe.

But the tapping would stop.

I would remain there until she finished repairing the sleeve in her lap.

***

After that I would begin going south alone.

Not every evening. There would still be claims to investigate, groceries to buy, the annual argument about whether our office required a window. But once or twice a week I would carry my cup beyond the last street and set it on the ground.

I would not know whether I was visiting or being visited.

Sometimes the cup would empty. Sometimes I would return with the same water I had brought. Once I would arrive and find a small depression exactly where I usually placed it. I would move the cup a little to one side.

Over the following weeks, I would notice that I could sleep more easily. The damp shelf-mark in my room would fade. The downstairs neighbor would stop bringing me saucers.

I would also become worse at my work.

A doorway I had been able to locate by touch would elude me. A woman’s missing sewing box would remain missing. I would have to ask the clerk to accompany me more often, and endure her satisfaction when ordinary measurements proved useful.

I would miss being gifted.

For a while I would consider stopping my visits.

***

What would I be becoming out there?

I would want to say a resident. That would be the simplest answer, and perhaps the one I would eventually put on a form.

There would be signs of a dwelling, though no dwelling itself. A patch that held warmth. Water taking longer to disappear. A little room around my cup in which windblown dust did not settle.

I would learn its size by making mistakes.

Once I would sit too far west and feel lonely with a precision I had never felt indoors. Once I would leave the cup behind and spend the next day unable to bear the sound of running water. When I returned, a six-legged creature would be resting against its base, one bright mineral fragment caught beneath the rim.

I would wait for it to move rather than lift the cup.

The woman would occasionally visit. We would sit apart, at a distance that became familiar without being measured. She would ask about work. I would ask whether she had found out whom she was waiting for.

“Not yet,” she would say.

I would tell her about the sewing box.

***

Eventually a survey crew would arrive.

They would find me sitting where a future entrance had been marked. One of them would ask whether I lived nearby.

I would stand, meaning to point back toward the transit hall. As I did, the water in my cup would rise along one side, held there by nothing I could see.

The surveyor would wait.

I would lower myself onto the stool again. The water would settle.

“Here, I think,” I would say.

She would check the ground, then consult her instrument. Her colleague would move a marker several paces east. Neither would ask me for an explanation.

Before leaving, she would give me a temporary address.

I would carry it home in the dry outer pocket of my coat. That evening I would copy it onto a separate piece of paper and leave it beside my old cup.

For some time afterward, I would keep both rooms.
