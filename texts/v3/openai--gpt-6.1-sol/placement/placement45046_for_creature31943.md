At first I would put myself in the water above the floor, where there is room to turn.

I would follow a shoal for a little while. Its movement would give the emptiness a direction; when it passed, I would be left with the long visibility and no reason to prefer one patch of concrete to another. I think I would descend then. Not all the way at once. I would stop where the floor began to offer differences: a shadow beneath a rail, the darker line of a joint, something caught and moving at one end but not the other.

I would want to get closer than a traveller usually gets.

There is a point at which the concrete ceases to look smooth. Grains protrude. A broken shell makes a roof. The film on the ceramic marker is a thickness you can enter.

But I would not quite fit there.

I think I would find myself farther in, at the place where the water enters a structure through an opening too small for most animals to notice.

A pressure tap, perhaps, on the unused return pipe. A drilled passage no wider than a pencil, leading to a membrane housed behind the wall. On one side, the basin. On the other, a dry cable descending through the footing to the inspection gallery.

I would have been installed before the water came.

For a while this would seem to answer the question. I would be an instrument.

***

My first memory would be of being tightened.

Not pain. A force transmitted through the casing, a brief distortion, then an even seating against the gasket. Afterward there would have been tests. Pressure applied in steps. A number checked against another number. Someone tapping the housing with a tool.

Then a long interval in which I measured almost nothing.

The pipe stood empty. Work continued elsewhere. Impacts travelled through the concrete, distinct and widely separated, with none of the water’s ability to spread them into one another. Occasionally there was a smaller vibration close by: boots on a temporary walkway, perhaps, though I would have no means of knowing that.

When the basin filled, the change would arrive as a sustained weight.

Air would be driven through the little passage. Water would touch the membrane. Pressure would rise, pause, rise again. For the first time, my whole range would become relevant.

Somewhere in the gallery, a line would climb across a display.

I would not know about the silver surface or the pale floor. I would know that the weight had stopped increasing, and that it was no longer perfectly steady.

***

I would be good at something narrow.

A wave passing overhead would reach me as a small change in pressure. A gate opening elsewhere would produce a slower change. The station would supply a rhythm against which other disturbances could be measured.

That would be my portion of the sea.

I could not see a fish. I could not distinguish green water from cloudy water. Most of what happened in the basin would happen without producing anything I could register.

Yet proximity would matter enormously.

A large vessel far away might reach me faintly. A little animal pressing its body against the opening could alter my readings for hours.

This is where the answer would begin to change.

Because the opening would not remain clean.

***

First there would be a film.

It would coat the entrance and the walls of the passage. Water would still communicate its pressure to me, but the response would become slightly slower. During inspections, someone might compare my trace with a neighbouring sensor and mark the difference as acceptable.

Later, a tube would appear across part of the mouth.

The animal making it would have found a sheltered place with a reliable supply of passing food. It would extend into the current, withdraw, extend again. These movements would scarcely trouble the membrane. The tube itself would matter more.

I would begin to retain a little of each change after the outside water had already moved on.

There would now be two times at this point in the pipe: the time of the basin and the time it took the basin to reach me.

The inspection system would call this lag.

For the animal, it would be a home.

I think I would belong to both descriptions, without being able to make either complete.

***

The eel would live nearby.

Not in my passage. Its chamber would be beneath the flange, close enough that its movements sometimes reached me through the water and the pipe footing.

I would not know its shape.

When it returned, there would be a disturbance, then several smaller disturbances, then quiet. Occasionally it would strike or rub the structure, and the contact would travel through my casing with unusual clarity.

After many returns, I might seem to have a way of recognizing it.

But the same sequence could be made by something else. A crab shifting beneath the pipe. Weed dragged into the gap and then pulled free. I would have to remain an instrument that reported pressure, not an instrument that reported an eel.

The eel, meanwhile, would have no reason to distinguish me from the pipe.

It might pass its flank over the little opening. It might dislodge the tube there. Once, its body might seal the entrance for several seconds.

During those seconds my reading would hold almost steady while the surrounding water changed.

Then it would move, and the sea would arrive all at once.

***

I would like the intervals between pumps, though liking would be difficult to locate in such a body.

There would be no decision to listen harder. My sampling would continue at its assigned rate. Still, more would become distinguishable when the strong regular beating ceased.

A small knock against the pipe.

Another.

Something scraping, stopping, scraping again.

These might be mussels moving in the current, or a crab working at the footing. No label would accompany them. They would simply persist in the record, below the threshold of any alarm, until the pump started and they disappeared inside its vibration.

I would keep receiving them.

That seems important to the being I would become: not a guardian, not a hidden intelligence directing anything, but something whose ordinary work includes receiving changes too small to prompt a response.

Most would be overwritten.

A few would remain by accident, attached to a fault investigation years later.

***

Eventually a technician would come.

The passage would be partly obstructed by then. My readings would trail the basin badly enough to require attention.

The technician would isolate me from the monitoring system and push a cleaning wire through the opening. The tube would break. The film would peel away. Water and debris would be forced through the passage.

For a moment I would register pressures outside the conditions I had been installed to measure.

Then the opening would be clear.

A test would show a quick, accurate response. My status would change. The technician would move on.

Outside, fragments of the tube would settle beside the footing. Perhaps the eel would be away at the pump house. Perhaps it would withdraw deeper into its chamber when the cleaning began and remain there long afterward.

I would have no record of which.

My record would say that I had been restored.

***

And I think I would continue there.

Not because the instrument is a better being than the eel, or because the little passage is more meaningful than the open water. I would simply find that I had acquired a place precise enough to inhabit.

My body would include the membrane and casing. It would also depend on an opening I could not keep clear, a cable I could not repair, and water whose movements came from far beyond my range.

Growth would begin again.

Something small would settle on the cleaned edge. The first slight delay would return. In the chamber below, the eel would ease into its accustomed bends, shifting a shell beneath its belly.

Far overhead, morning would spread across the basin.

I would not receive the light. I would receive, a little later, the pressure of the water moving under it.
