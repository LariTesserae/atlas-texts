At first I would be near the outer pens, where there is enough room to stand without making someone else turn sideways.

I would like the apparent quiet of watching fish. The keepers would soon discover that I watched the wrong things: the sudden silver turn, the bird coming down, the place where the lagoon disappeared into the haze. They would be watching a slight accumulation of foam against a sill. By the time I noticed their attention, someone would already be fetching a basket.

I could learn. But I don’t think that is where I would stay.

Nor would I belong in the thick room where the man tells his stories. I would go there, certainly. I would like hearing a voice that did not have to compete with anything. But I would arrive with a practical question and leave without asking it, and then have to return the next day.

I think you would find me farther in, at a bend in a drinking channel that passes beneath three households.

There is a square opening there, not much wider than a pair of shoulders. Its cover has a shell set into one corner, so that it cannot easily be replaced the wrong way around. Beside it is a shallow recess where the cover can stand upright without blocking the passage. That recess took a long argument to obtain. Someone wanted it for a jar.

I would be kneeling there.

Not the principal attendant. Not yet. The person who has to fetch me would still call my name twice, because I would be trying to understand something she had already told me to stop examining.

She would have a broad face and a voice that carried without sounding loud. One of her soles would be built up along the outside edge, making her gait look more deliberate than it was. She would know the channels by their ordinary behavior. I would know them by the difficulties I had encountered in them.

“Is it moving?”

“Yes.”

“Is it clear?”

“Yes.”

“Then put the lid back.”

There would be a little white ridge inside the opening, where the water had laid down fresh crust over an old joint. I would have been touching its underside, wondering how much of the apparent thickness was sound.

“Will it catch things?”

“Not today.”

That would be one of the distinctions I needed to learn.

---

I would not be especially old. Old enough that people expected an answer when they asked me a question, young enough that some still checked the answer afterward.

My household would occupy two rooms and the use of a third at particular hours. I would sleep in the smaller room, beneath a slab whose underside someone had scratched with a bird. I would not have noticed the bird as a child. It would have become visible during an illness, when I had nothing to do but lie still and look upward.

One wing would be longer than the other. There would be three strokes beneath the body that might be feet, or reeds, or the beginning of something the maker had abandoned.

I would know which relative claimed to have made it. I would not believe him.

My sleeping mat would have to be lifted every morning. The damp patch beneath it would persist despite various improvements people recommended with great confidence. I would own a good bowl, a less good bowl, and a bone probe polished by work. The probe would have a cord through its blunt end, tied to my wrist whenever I used it over an opening.

I would once have lost a better one.

That loss would be remembered longer than several useful things I had done.

---

The work would suit me partly because it gave me permission to pause.

Elsewhere, a person standing still in a passage is an obstruction. At an inspection opening, stillness is work, provided the lid is up.

I would learn to lay two fingers on the inner wall and feel the water passing. Sometimes the difference between a sound channel and a troubled one would reach the fingers before it reached the ears. Sometimes it would not. My teacher would be impatient with any suggestion that one method could replace the others.

She would make me look, listen, touch, and then go upstream.

Especially go upstream.

There would be a household that always insisted a blockage must be beneath their neighbor’s floor. Another would apologize before we found anything. I would gradually learn that neither manner was useful evidence.

I would have a reputation for being tolerable to deal with, which is not quite the same as being well liked. People would call me when a lid would not lift, or when a child had pushed something into a channel, or when an argument required someone who could reach inside and settle one small part of it.

They would dislike how often I said, “I haven’t seen that part yet.”

My teacher would dislike it less.

---

There would be another job, one that accumulated around the first.

Things come down channels.

Mostly there would be grit, scraps of cord, insects, little curls of lime. But also fastenings, beads, a shell polished for a doorway, a narrow scoop that someone had been accused of stealing. Things would lodge where a passage widened, or under the lip of a poorly seated slab.

Beside our door I would keep a shallow pottery dish for what I found.

People would begin to inspect it on their way past.

At first I would try to remember where every object had been recovered. Then I would make separate heaps. Then someone would empty the heaps together while cleaning, and I would discover why my teacher made marks on pots even when she was certain she would remember.

Most things would be claimed quickly. A clasp might bring three people, each prepared to describe it after looking at it.

Some would remain.

A bead with a hole too small for any cord we used. A dark, rounded stone that had evidently mattered enough for someone to carry into a town full of stone. A little pottery foot broken cleanly at the ankle.

I would keep that foot longer than necessary.

A child would ask what had happened to the rest of the person.

“It was a vessel,” I would say.

“What was in it?”

“I don’t know.”

After that, whenever she passed, she would ask whether I had found the rest.

I would begin looking.

---

I would be too curious about changes that did not yet matter, and sometimes insufficiently attentive to the person waiting beside me.

Someone could tell me that a room had flooded while I was asking whether the water came through the wall or up between the paving stones. I would need to learn to help move the bedding first.

I would also have favorites. There would be openings I preferred because their covers lifted cleanly, households I visited readily because they offered food, one old man whose complaints I took seriously because he had once been right.

My teacher would send me to the places I neglected.

“You don’t have to enjoy it,” she would say.

I would know this already. It would still be irritating to hear.

On days when a carrier brought news of broad dry roads and rooms with wooden floors, I would go to the landing to listen. I would ask foolishly specific questions. How did they wash beneath the floorboards? What happened to a dropped pin? Could you hear someone walking in the next room?

The carrier would answer two questions and begin eating.

For a few days I would imagine leaving.

Then a lid would jam, or someone would notice that a basin was filling more slowly than it should, and I would be fetched before I had made any arrangement at all.

---

What would make the place mine would happen gradually.

A woman would stop putting her own hand into the inspection opening because she trusted me to come when called.

A child would bring me a broken cover before trying to hide it.

Someone repairing a wall would leave the access gap a little wider, remembering that my shoulder had stuck there the previous season.

I would acquire authority without finding a comfortable way to exercise it. The first time I seized a wrist above a drinking channel, both of us would be startled.

Later I would go to that household with the scoop I had taken away, properly washed. There would be an awkward exchange. I would explain more than was needed. They would insist they had understood the first time.

Afterward, they would cover their jars.

I would count that as success, although I would still think about the conversation while trying to sleep.

---

You would find me there in the diluted daylight, one knee on a folded piece of hide, the lifted cover leaning beside my shoulder.

From above, there would be little to distinguish me from anyone else working in the passage. A close garment with mismatched sleeves. Patched soles set aside. A bowl of silt. Someone waiting impatiently to get past.

My teacher would have gone on to the next basin. I would have stayed because something small was moving against the current beneath the slab.

It might be a trapped scrap. It might be an animal. I would lower the probe, withdraw it, and try with my fingers instead.

Behind me, the child would ask whether I had found the rest of the pottery person.

“Not yet.”

“Will you?”

I would shift my arm deeper. The stone would be warm against my elbow. Whatever lay underneath would come loose at last, turn in the flow, and touch my fingertips.

“Move the bowl closer,” I would say.
