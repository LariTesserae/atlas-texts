At first I think I would be under the stone: one of the flat animals living in the narrow interval between warmth and cover. There would be food on the underside, a little dampness, the pressure of other bodies. When the roof lifted, I would move toward the nearest seam.

But I find myself holding the stone up.

One hand is underneath it. The other rests on the ground, receiving the small disturbances made by what is escaping. I catch two grazers and lose the rest. The lifted flake is heavier than it looked, and I lower it carefully. A finger remains beneath its edge for a moment, then comes free.

I am one of the broad-backed animals. Not the oldest, not the one whose marks are most often found beneath the stones. My scales still have distinct edges, though the fur between them has begun to collect pale grit. There is a place on my shoulder I cannot reach. When it itches, I press against the mouth of my shelter and move backward until the rock finds it.

My shelter is partway down a shallow ravine, under a shelf that has settled without quite meeting the ground. I enter sideways. Inside there is room to turn, provided I fold the long hands close to my chest. The floor slopes toward the entrance. Old grass gathers at the back, where my movements have pushed it.

I do not sleep beside the best water.

The best water draws too many feet. Through the floor I feel the little animals coming and going, the longer pauses of hunters, the weight of another of my kind lowering itself to drink. Even after I stop attending to these movements, my fingers sometimes spread in my sleep.

Here, farther up the ravine, I can rest.

When I wake, I put a hand outside before my head.

The shelf cools differently from the ground beneath it. There is usually a faint dampness where the two nearly meet. I lick this first. Then I lie still with my mouth against the stone, waiting for enough moisture to gather that it is worth licking again.

There are nights when this takes a long time.

***

I know several hollows, but I do not know the country between them in the way a creature with better eyes might.

On one crossing, a band of close stone carries my steps ahead of me. I can feel another animal before we come within scent of each other. On another, loose debris absorbs almost everything. I lift my head there. My fringe no longer touches the ground, and the air moves across my exposed chin.

I dislike that crossing.

Beyond it is a shrub whose lowest branch lies along the rock. Berries sometimes lodge beneath the branch. I put my fingers through and draw them out. The branch presses on the back of my hand. It has done this for so many seasons that I approach with my wrist already turned.

Once there is no branch.

I search the stone beside the shrub, then farther down the slope. I find a broken length caught in a hollow. Its berries have dried. I eat them anyway.

For several journeys afterward, I still turn my wrist.

***

There is a young animal that follows me sometimes.

It is not mine, though I have slept across the entrance of its chamber. Its mother knows my smell and passes close enough for our scales to catch briefly against one another. When she leaves, the young one works its way under my belly. Its hands are hot against the softer skin there.

Now it is large enough to walk between the shelters and water. It lifts stones that are too small to conceal anything. It lays both hands on a shelf and shifts them before the faintest vibration has had time to arrive.

I wait.

Waiting is easy while there is warmth in the ground and no unfamiliar scent on the wind. Eventually its fingers become still. Something moves beneath the shelf. Its head lowers abruptly, and I feel the small knock of its chin through the stone.

Later, at the meeting place, it strikes too quickly.

I have already given my three blows. An answer is coming from below the ravine when the young one begins. Its impacts run into the arriving pattern. We remain there, touching the shelf, while the stone becomes quiet again.

The young one strikes once more.

I lower my chest beside it. For a while neither of us moves.

***

The water in my nearest hollow begins to fail.

At first I stay longer with my tongue in the crack. Then I return sooner. The fringe beneath my mouth dries before I reach the next ridge.

Other animals have begun using a route above the shrub. I find fresh grease beneath a stone there. The stone is awkwardly placed, wedged against a larger one, and I must reach from the side to bring my nose close.

I follow.

The new water is down a narrow opening where the air smells strongly of mineral. My shoulders fit, but only with the scales drawn close. I place one hand ahead of me and feel repeated little impacts somewhere below. There is water beyond the reach of my tongue.

Farther in, a low tooth of mineral is wet along its underside. I press my mouth to it. Water runs into the fur of my chin. I remain until my forelimbs begin to tremble from holding my weight at that angle.

Coming out is harder. I cannot turn. I back along the opening, testing behind me with each of my four walking feet.

Outside, I rest with my hands folded underneath me.

I will come here again. I will also visit the old hollow. For a time, both journeys belong to the route.

***

I would be found most easily near the thick shelf at the ravine junction, though it might take several visits to see me there.

I arrive with dust on my back and a little dried water below my mouth. I smell the underside of a marked stone. I put both hands flat.

Some nights an answer comes while my chest is still against the shelf. Some nights I wait through several long breaths before feeling it. Once, a familiar animal answers from a direction where I have never encountered it before.

I turn that way, but do not immediately go.

The young one is behind me. It has stopped to examine a flake tilted against the ravine wall. One hand holds it up; the other passes underneath. Its head follows, too far, and the flake begins to slip.

I reach back and take the weight.

For a little while we stay like that, each with a hand beneath the same stone. Then its head withdraws. Its fingers find room beside mine. We lower the flake together.

Afterward I return my hands to the shelf.

There is still time to feel whether the other animal has moved.
