I would first look for somewhere the water almost reaches.

Not the summit bowl. I would visit it, but I think I would descend again before the cloud came through the western gap. There would be too much to see from that height. I would keep following one channel instead: along a wall, beneath a crossing gutter, into a district where the tiles had been laid but the cups were still stacked under cover.

There, a narrow run of water would end at a plugged outlet. Beyond it, the next course would be unfinished.

I would sit down beside the plug.

For a while I would suppose I had chosen this place because it was quiet. Then I would put my hand on the wall and feel the faint, uneven pressure of water moving on the other side. Somewhere inside the construction, a chamber would be filling. The wall would cool beneath my palm before anything changed on its visible face.

I would stay to find out where the water emerged.

***

My body would be pale across the back and darker underneath the arms, where the surface seldom dried. One knee would have been repaired with clay from a different firing. It would hold a muted reddish color even when wet.

I would have trouble with that knee on the narrow tile borders. On level ground, it would work well enough. On a long ascent, I would have to turn the leg slightly outward.

There would be a recess inside my left wrist, shallow enough to look like an unfinished thumbprint. When I rested my hand palm-up, it would hold a little water. I would often forget it was there until I reached for something and felt the water travel down my fingers.

I would be able to speak, though I would do so infrequently. The beginning of a note would come more easily than its ending. Sometimes another inhabitant would wait for me to finish; sometimes it would supply the last change of pitch, and I would touch its arm in acknowledgment.

I would not know whether I had always been like this.

Some of my surfaces would carry marks older than anything I could remember making. Along one shoulder, three small parallel grooves. On the sole of my sound foot, a nearly closed hole. They would become clearer when I soaked.

I would sometimes examine them without arriving at a story.

***

The district beyond the plugged outlet would become familiar to me.

Most days, someone would come carrying a piece of it: a length of channel, a bundle of cords, a shallow bowl wrapped in damp cloth. I would get up to help. We would carry the piece to its marked place and sit beside it until we understood how it ought to fit.

I would be reasonably good at this.

Not at judging a whole slope. From a distance I would make mistakes about where the water wanted to go. But with my cheek close to a joint, one hand on each side of it, I could find the slight difference between two surfaces that looked level. I could feel which edge needed to be lowered.

My repairs would take a long time.

Sometimes the other worker would leave to fetch something and return to find me still pressing the same corner. Sometimes it would settle beside me and begin smoothing a different part. We would move without looking at one another, passing cloudy water back and forth in a small cup.

By the end of the work, the cup would usually need cleaning.

I would clean it last.

***

Under the roof where the unused cups were stored, I would begin keeping a few pieces aside.

At first they would all be useful: a tile too short for its intended course, a channel tooth with a rounded tip, a bowl whose outlet had been cut too high. There would always be some smaller place where a rejected piece might fit.

But one cup would remain after the others had gone.

Its floor would rise slightly in the middle. Filled to the notch, it would leave a pale island showing through the water.

I would turn it several times, trying to decide where to put it. Then I would set it in the recess beside my resting place.

For several days I would tell myself I was waiting for the right use.

Eventually I would stop moving it.

Cloud water would gather around the raised center. When the air cleared, the ring would thin and separate into two crescents. One would disappear before the other. I would learn which one, and still look.

***

There would be an inhabitant who came through the district carrying unfired clay.

It would have a wide, dark patch across its chest, with a small bright area near the collar where a recent repair had not yet dulled. Its hands would leave very little mark on the things it carried. I would notice this before I learned to recognize it from far away.

At first it would stop only to wet its wrists.

Later it would set down its load.

We would sit with the stacked cups between us. If there were work to do, we would do it. If there were none, it would inspect my odd cup, sometimes adding water, sometimes drawing a little out with the corner of a cloth.

Once it would turn the cup a quarter-turn.

I would put it back after it left.

The next time, I would let the cup remain where it placed it. The smaller crescent would now face the open court. It would disappear faster there.

Neither of us would say anything about this.

***

I think I would be young enough that some work still frightened me.

I would dislike the firing courts while they were being prepared. The dry scrape of blocks being moved into place would make my fingers stiffen, even if my hands were wet. I would help carry fuel to the mouth, but I would find reasons to leave before it was lit.

There would come a time when a crack opened behind my repaired knee.

At first I would keep it submerged whenever I rested. Then it would widen while I was carrying a gutter, and the inhabitant with the dark chest would take my end before I asked.

The repair would require more than water.

I would spend the cooling interval in a side chamber, with a slab between my body and the floor. I would remember very little of the heat. What I remembered afterward would be the approach: my hand against the worn clay at the entrance, and the other inhabitant waiting while I tried to bend the knee one last time.

When I came out, the leg would not move properly.

It would lead me down by the long route, stopping at every pool.

For a while I would be unable to sit in my usual recess. Someone would bring the odd cup to the place where I was recovering. I would know who had brought it from its orientation.

***

Eventually the new district would receive water.

I would be there when the plug was removed. The first current would carry a little clay dust into the waiting channels. Several of us would follow it, crouching to watch each junction.

It would reach farther than I expected.

A shallow collecting bowl would fill. Its notch would darken. Water would pass through and enter the next court.

At one joint, a bead would grow on the underside instead of traveling along the intended groove. I would put my hand beneath it. The bead would fall into the recess in my wrist.

Behind me, someone would begin bringing the tools.

I would not repair the joint immediately. I would carry that first small measure to the cup under the roof.

Then I would return.

***

That is where I think I would be: in a district gradually becoming wet, with a body that needed attending to and hands that could attend to other things.

I would have a place to rest, though it would not stay entirely mine. Workers would shelter there. Someone recovering from a repair might occupy it for days. I would sit elsewhere, close enough to bring water when needed.

There would still be an unfinished course beyond the newest channel.

Some afternoons I would go out along it, taking care with my knee, until I reached the last fitted tile. I would sit with my feet in the water and look at the marks where the next pieces were meant to go.

If the inhabitant with the dark chest came looking for me, it would know to follow the channel.

I would hear it set down its load before I turned.
