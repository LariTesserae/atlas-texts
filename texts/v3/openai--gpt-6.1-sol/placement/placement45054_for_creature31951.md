I would be farther inland than the animal beside the stone, but not as far as the young.

There is a place where a channel passes beneath a shelf and comes out narrower on the other side. At low water, the opening looks blocked. A root hangs across it. Shell fragments have gathered against the root, and behind them the mud is dark and wet. Water continues to pass there after the visible current has stopped.

I would lie on the downstream side, with one forefoot inside the opening.

At first you might find only the foot: four spread toes, the webbing loose between them, a nail resting against a broken shell. The rest of me would be under the shelf. My back would fit badly enough that the stone touched it in two places. I would have learned to enter with one shoulder lowered.

I think I would be an old one of these animals. Not enormous, not the owner of the channel. There would be larger animals nearer the open water, and in warm weather one of them might make me leave. But I would know where to go without crossing the soft basin. I would take the longer route along the gravel seam, pass behind the stand of sedges, and come down to a second hollow where the water smelled more of roots.

My tail would have a slight bend near the end. It would lie comfortably in some places and refuse to settle in others.

For much of the day, this would be enough to determine where I was.

***

The shelf keeps the light off my eyes.

Outside, a bird steps through water. I feel each foot arrive before I see it: a small pressure against my jaw, then the disturbance of mud. The bird’s legs move past the opening. Its body is above the part of the world I am watching.

A fish comes through beneath the root.

I turn too late. My teeth close on water, and my chin strikes the bottom. For a little while, clay obscures everything.

I remain where I am.

The next fish brushes the webbing of my foot. I do not turn until it has passed my wrist.

Then there is the fish in my mouth, its movement changing as I hold it. I lift my head enough to swallow. The shelf scrapes a dry patch along my spine.

Outside, the bird has stopped.

***

By midday the water beneath the stone is warm, too.

I leave backward, pushing with my forefeet until my ribs are clear. There is a brief difficulty with the bent part of my tail. I pull it free and lie beside the shelf.

I would not spend every hour hidden. I would cross bare ground when there was something to cross it for. From above, my route might seem uncertain: a turn toward a pool, a halt short of it, a long diagonal over nearly dry clay. But beneath my feet there would be differences. A skin of firm mud over something that would not hold me. A cool strip beside a stone. Ground that yielded without taking my whole weight.

At the sedges I press my belly down.

A fly settles on the salt dried beneath my eye. I close the lid, but the fly is below it. After a while I move my head.

Here the frogs call close enough that I can feel the vibration through the wet ground. One falls silent as I enter. The others continue.

I stay until the heat leaves the uppermost mud.

***

There would be a small animal under the next stone.

I would know it first by the scratches in the clay: light feet, a narrow drag between them. When its head appeared, the stripes would still be visible. It would flatten at the sight of me, though there would be no room beneath its stone to become much flatter.

I would pass.

Later, returning, I might find it in the channel I use. It would stand with its body raised, mouth open, making a sound too slight to travel beyond the stones.

There is room beside it for my head.

I stop.

Water touches the soft skin beneath its throat. Its mouth closes. For a moment neither of us moves; then it slips backward into the gap, leaving its tail outside. The tail withdraws last.

I lower myself into the channel.

I would not know where its eggs had been laid, or whether I had once rested beside the shrub above them. The young would appear after certain warm weeks, and their tracks would begin crossing places where there had been no tracks before.

***

The evening tide reaches me from behind.

First it fills the shallow marks left by my hindfeet. Then it gathers beneath my tail. The incoming water is cooler than the water under the shelf, and I turn toward it.

For a while two movements meet here. Water still drains from the inland hollow while the tide presses into the channel. A leaf turns slowly between them. Tiny fish hold their places on either side of the root.

I ease forward until my chin is beyond the stone.

This is where you would find me most often: at the slight narrowing, with enough water over my feet to loosen the webbing and enough ground beneath them to brace against.

Not quite visible from the bank. Easier to see from the water, if you were low enough.

The shelf darkens as the tide climbs it. Somewhere beyond it, another animal gives the hollow call. I lift my throat from the bottom and answer once.

Then I settle again. A fish has entered beneath the root, and I can feel it moving along the inside of my foot.
