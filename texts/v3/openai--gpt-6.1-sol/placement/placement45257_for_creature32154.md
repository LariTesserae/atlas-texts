At first I would put myself at the entrance, with one knee wet and a hand against the clay. I would think that being able to see into the hollow meant I was outside it.

But the hand would trouble me. It is too large. It can feel the engines, but not the place where a root hair has loosened from the wall. It can touch the animal only by disturbing the moss. To find out more, it would have to damage what it wanted to know.

I leave the hand there for a while. Then I leave it out.

Perhaps I am one of the pale inclusions in the clay: a shell fragment with a dark edge, recently uncovered. Water gathers underneath me. For the first time in a long time there is room for something to fall.

But that is too still. There is a small movement I have not accounted for, an adjustment made before the drop releases. Something beneath the moss draws closer to the root.

I would find myself there.

Not at the visible rim, where a person might notice a notch closing. Farther underneath, at the turn between two roots, where the animal has folded a part of itself back over another part. A narrow place. The moss above it is old enough to have a brown underside, and the root below it still wears a little clay.

Here I have almost no surface that belongs to me alone.

A root hair enters between two of my plates. I could close the gap, but it would take effort, and the hair does not hurt. When the moss lifts, it pulls gently there. When the clay softens, it helps keep that portion of me from sliding. I have grown around it.

My body reaches places I cannot bring together into a view. One edge is in cold water. Another is pressed against a leaf that has become mostly veins. A fold deeper in the bank receives the steady pressure of wood. Between these places, fluid moves through me, stopping briefly where I am compressed.

I know the hollow by what arrives.

Fresh water makes one edge loosen. Salt makes another hold. A shrimp stands on me, and its weight is several separate points, arriving and leaving too quickly to require much of a response. I draw in beneath one foot. The foot returns.

Sometimes a cup comes back with nothing useful in it. Sometimes it holds a softness that can be passed inward. There is no need for the rest of me to stop. A portion beneath the root goes on testing the clay while the food travels towards my mouth.

I would be this animal, though at first I might have mistaken myself for the sheltered part of it: the pale seam that seldom moves, the fold where the young develop.

It takes the rising water to show me how much of me there is.

The bank disappears by degrees. Water reaches the underside of the leaf, then the lowest plates, then a depression in my back. An air pocket moves against me. I close around it. Elsewhere my feet release and settle farther up the root.

Layers separate. A fold I have kept pressed flat becomes a passage. The cups extend into spaces that were too dry an hour ago.

There is more room inside my body when there is less room above the bank.

I move then. Not towards a destination I can see. One patch of clay has become soft enough that my feet enter it instead of holding it. Another place remains firm. I pass a little weight there, bring a fold after it, leave some feet behind until the others are secure.

For a long time, almost nothing appears to happen.

Then the old patch is empty.

The engines reach me through all these holdings. Their vibration arrives differently through wood and clay. At a certain pitch, a plate under my outer edge touches the next plate and keeps touching it. I flatten there. Beneath the moss, more feet find the root.

Before the chamber opened, the pressure above me was close and fairly even. Now there are interruptions. Parts of the wall move without the neighbouring parts. Water falls from heights it did not have before.

And light reaches a seam that has never needed to close quickly.

I am slow there.

When a shadow passes over the entrance, the exposed cups withdraw first. Farther in, the old fold remains open for another moment. Its young lie against one another, opaque in my thin flesh. Then it closes too.

I do not know what has passed outside.

I know that afterwards the shrimp returns.

At the ebb, the pull begins beneath me. A loose leaf rubs along my edge and is taken away. I shut the cups. Water finds the little spaces between my feet; the spaces change shape as I tighten.

One portion holds clay. One holds moss. The oldest strip remains wound around the root.

For a while, everything I can feel is trying to go somewhere.

Then the pull weakens.

I remain folded after it has stopped. Water still drains from the moss above me, and each small movement could be the beginning of another withdrawal. Gradually the root becomes only a root again. The clay bears my weight without shifting.

A cup near the entrance opens.

I would be here: broad, mostly hidden, with one part feeding and another still waiting. A few feet would keep their unnecessary grip for a little longer. Beneath the fern, a second cup would lengthen into the cold water.

It would touch a grain, close, and let it go.
