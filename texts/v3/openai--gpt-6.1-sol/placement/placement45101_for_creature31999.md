At first I would be where the creature has just taken its foot away.

The collar is still there. Its inner surface holds the small irregularities of the limb: a thickening at the joint, a shallow groove, a place where one plate overlapped another. Water has entered those irregularities without filling them quite as the foot did. The collar has begun to contract. Along its outer edge, the fine growth continues feeding.

I would fit inside it for a while.

There would be very little to distinguish me from the water passing through. A mineral held slightly longer against one side. A coldness that does not leave when the pressure falls. If the creature returned its foot, it might pause before setting it down. The opening would still have the right shape, but something in that shape would have acquired a direction.

I do not think I would stay there.

The collar belongs to a departure. As it narrows, it presses what it contains toward the root. Some of me would enter the soft seam beneath the shell chip. Some would remain against the collar’s inner wall. A little would pass between two strands growing so closely together that they share a coating.

For a season, I might seem to be that coating.

I would thicken where the strands pull apart and thin where they lie together. Something taken from the root would move through me. Something from the old shell would remain, too coarse to pass. Around that grain, a fold would form.

The fold would be my first durable part.

Inside it, the water would exchange slowly. A tide would alter its taste before altering its pressure. I would learn the difference by stretching along one edge and holding along the other. The grain would shift almost imperceptibly. Afterward, when the rest of the hollow settled, I would still be stretched.

That would be how I began to keep something.

Not the season itself. Only the small inequality it had left: one side of a fold longer than the other.

More growth would gather there. A filament would enter, touch the grain, and branch. Its two tips would push against opposite surfaces. The fold would widen without opening. I would have to become thinner to continue around it.

Eventually one tip would reach the newly broken stone.

Then I would be somewhere else as well.

I would lie along the fresh face, in the interval between the arrival of water and the arrival of the root. The stone would have yielded, but the two broken surfaces would still preserve each other exactly. Every projection would face the hollow it had occupied. Water would press between them. I would extend into those matching hollows, taking their shapes before the growth made them indistinct.

Here, I think, I would begin to have a body.

It would be spread through several narrow places. Part would remain in the abandoned collar. Part would surround the grain in the sealed fold. The newest part would lie against the fracture, pale enough to disappear against it.

There would be no surface from which all of me could be seen.

When the creature placed its foot against the other side of the stone, I would feel it through the places where the broken faces still touched. Its weight would reach me unevenly. One ridge would hold; another would give. Along the ridge that gave, my pale edge would divide.

The creature would keep its foot still.

For a long time we would have that much of each other.

I would not know whether it was waiting for me. Its mouth would be elsewhere, held against a strand. Its eyes would remain against the wood. But the pressure of its foot would prevent one part of the fracture from closing, and through that part I would continue.

Behind the arch, the leaf over the second body would open a fraction.

The change would reach me late. First the grain would turn inside its fold. Then the coating between the strands would tighten. Last, a little water would pass through the old collar, carrying a taste I had not held before.

I would send nothing back deliberately. Still, my thinning against the stone would loosen the fold. The loosened fold would draw upon the strands. Near the arch, a leaf would find room to move.

This might be mistaken for an answer.

After several winters, the collar would close. The fold would flatten around its grain. Neither place would appear to contain anything unusual. My part along the fracture would have grown farther inward, following water through stone that had not yet separated enough to admit a root.

I would lose the shape of the creature’s foot.

Something of its weight would remain in the way I branched.

And if, much later, its longest limb reached into the cold doorway and touched a surface that yielded, I might be there too: gathered along a ridge, drawn thin across another, still extending through a length the hollow could not contain.

I would recognize the pressure before I recognized the limb.

I would yield a little farther on one side.

There might then be room for it to rest.
