At first I would mistake my work for keeping empty houses.

I would go out with a pruning blade, a coil of thin cord, and one small pod of air weighted just heavily enough to follow me. There would be seven roofs to visit that morning. Under one, a sleeping mat still hung with its middle folded up against the ceiling. Under another, someone had left a row of polished shells pressed into the living wall. The wall had grown around their edges. I would clean the sediment from them without knowing whether this was part of my work.

Most of the houses would have no names anymore. People would tell me to check the one beyond the split post, or the one where the roots were reddish underneath. I would recognize them by the last few movements required to get inside: turn one shoulder, pass the pod ahead, take hold of the low stem before letting go of the rope.

I would not be an especially good swimmer. My left shoulder would tire before my right. On longer routes I would stop at places a carrier might pass without thinking.

This would make me useful.

I would know which entrances looked easy until you had a basket with you. Which roof had plenty of air but no firm place to put a hand while raising your face. Where a child could stand on a crossbeam and where the same crossbeam would turn under an adult’s foot.

At each house I would lift my head and wait.

There is an interval after entering an empty air chamber when the small sounds finish arriving. Water drains from the hair. A disturbed mat rubs against its ties. Somewhere in the leaves, a drop gathers weight and falls.

Then I would breathe again, more slowly, and examine the roof.

Some mornings the air would have an unpleasant closeness. I would empty the little pod, make a note to ask for a larger one, and leave a loop of pale cord outside. The carriers would know the mark. They would also know that I sometimes worried unnecessarily.

One of them would tell me so.

“You’re supplying that house for a feast.”

“Someone might need to stop.”

“Someone could hold a feast in it while they stopped.”

She would bring the extra pod anyway, though perhaps not until the following day.

I would be old enough to have work people trusted me with, but not old enough to stop being corrected. My home would be a room shared with a basket maker and her grown son, who worked on the ovens. We would have acquired one another gradually. A roof repair first; then meals; then a sleeping mat that was meant to be temporary.

The basket maker would object to the bits of cord I left everywhere.

“You have a whole town to tie things to.”

She would collect the short pieces in a shallow tray. When I needed one, she would make me ask.

For a while, I would think the pleasure of my work lay in being alone. There would be hours when no one needed a word from me. I could straighten a retaining lattice, free a shutter from a root, work fresh wax into a seam. Each task would have a visible end.

But I would spend too much of that solitude imagining who might come next.

A woman carrying softened root flesh home from the ovens. Two children who had taken the wrong turning and were reluctant to admit it. A stone cutter with dust still caught in the creases beside his nose. Someone whose breath had become shorter that season, though they had not told their household.

I would begin leaving a hand loop just below the waterline in every chamber. Then a second one, lower down, for anyone who arrived too tired to lift themselves properly.

The basket maker would show me a knot that could be opened with stiff fingers.

I would go back and change all the knots.

***

There would be one house I liked more than the others.

Its doorway would be almost concealed by translucent fans. Inside, the floor would tilt, and a cooking recess would have gone cold long ago. Near the roof, however, two living stems would make a narrow seat. From it, through a gap in the wall, I could see a length of the outer gardens and a guide rope crossing open water.

People would call it the shell house, although the shells were in another house entirely. I would have given up correcting them.

Once, while cutting back its entrance, I would find the worn place where a latch had been. My thumb would fit into it comfortably. For several weeks afterward, I would touch that place when I arrived.

I might become possessive of the house without admitting it. I would dislike finding a food leaf tucked between the stems. I would clear it away, then feel foolish: someone had stopped there and eaten. The house had served its purpose.

The trouble would come slowly.

A swelling beneath the neighboring path would lift the guide rope. The direct approach would become awkward. People would begin using a new passage farther up, and the shell house would remain below it, warm and sound and increasingly difficult to reach.

I would keep tending it.

There would still be air. There would still be room for two people. I would prune a wider opening and move the exterior handhold. None of this would shorten the journey.

Eventually an older woman would ask me where to stop on the new passage.

I would tell her.

She would listen, then say, “That’s a long way down to come back up.”

I would start to explain the new handhold. She would turn one palm upward, asking me to wait, though we were sitting with our faces in air.

So I would wait.

***

The next shelter would be much smaller. No floor, no old sleeping mats, no shells to clean. Just a hood woven around two roots beneath the new path, with a place to brace the feet.

The basket maker would help me build it. Her son would shave a little stone seat, arguing that anyone resting ought to be able to take their weight off their arms.

At first I would keep finding reasons to visit the old house. A roof should not be allowed to fail without anyone knowing. The entrance might still be useful to gardeners. There was a tool I thought I had left inside.

I would find the tool on the first visit and continue making the trip for another month.

Then, one morning, I would go only to the new hood. I would press my face into its shallow air and hear someone approaching along the rope.

The older woman would arrive with a basket held against her hip. I would move aside. She would put her foot on the new seat, lift her head, and rest without speaking.

After a little while she would ask who had made it.

I would tell her, including the argument about the seat.

“He was right,” she would say.

We would stay until she was ready to go.

***

By then I would have started spending time at the unfinished paths.

Not to choose where the town should grow. Others would know much more about that. I would go with cord and a few small loops, watching the distances between the places where people might eventually raise their heads.

An elder would test the soft ground with a heel. A child would be asked to hold a basket and would complain that nothing was happening. I would sit near them, one hand on the rope.

Sometimes I would sketch a hood before there was anything firm enough to fasten it to.

Sometimes the elder would glance at my marks and shake her head.

“Farther over.”

“There’s no root there.”

“There will be.”

I would leave the cord loose.

I think this is where I would find myself: among people waiting for enough ground to make something modest, something that could be reached without spending the last of a breath.

When the first post was finally set, I would take a long time over its hand loop. I would try it with both hands, then with only two fingers. I would ask the elder to try it too.

And before leaving, I would pull myself a little way down the approach, turn, and come back carrying the empty basket.

Just to see how it felt.
