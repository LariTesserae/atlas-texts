At first I would sit near the entrance, where there is still enough light to see my hands.

I would want the dry place, but hesitate to take it. The broad middle looks unoccupied only because I have not yet learned what happens there. I would sit with my back against the wall and my feet drawn close, leaving room for whatever crosses.

For a while I would keep trying to hear separate things. A footstep. Water entering the basin. The little noise a sleeve makes against stone. Each would disappear into the waterfall, and I would begin making movements just to confirm that I had made them. Touching one finger to another. Turning my wrist where I could see it.

Eventually I would put a palm on the floor.

The cold would be immediate; the rest would take longer. A tremor that seemed steady would turn out to have a hesitation in it. The pressure beneath my hand would strengthen when I leaned forward, diminish when I shifted back. I would have no names for most of what I felt.

When the creature approached, I might not notice until its pad was already beside my hand.

I would want to leave my hand there. But wanting is easy while imagining the distance. At the first touch I might pull away, then put it down again, a little nearer.

That is where I first find myself: a visitor trying not to startle something that has been less hasty than I am.

But a visitor is not quite what I would be.

The visitor can leave. Even while sitting still, they carry the path home, the remembered warmth of another room, all the things they will say about this one. I keep finding myself drawn instead to the short wooden peg.

I would be that.

Not freshly cut. Not a branch with leaves still imaginable along it. I would be the piece that has already spent years in the crack, with one end hidden and the other worn smooth.

There would once have been more of me.

The grain would retain a slight curve from the branch I came from. Whoever cut me would have worked around it, shaving one end to fit the limestone. Perhaps they tried the crack twice. Perhaps the first tap seated me too loosely, and they took me out and pared the point again.

I would remember none of this as a person remembers. It would remain in my shape: a shallow blade mark underneath, a compressed place near the tip, the blunt end struck a little off center.

For several nights I would carry something hung from me. A damp garment, perhaps, or a cord keeping a small bundle off the floor. Its weight would pull downward and outward. When it was taken away, I would ease back by an amount too small to see.

Then the person would go.

The chamber would not become still. Moisture would enter my grain. It would leave more slowly. The limestone around my buried end would hold its deep chill, while the exposed wood answered somewhat sooner to air from the slit. In winter I would contract enough to move when touched.

The creature would find this out.

At first, its pad passing over me might turn me a fraction in the crack. Another time it would pause. It would place the pad squarely against my end and press.

I would settle deeper.

This would happen again, though not at regular intervals. Some years I might scarcely loosen. After a long dry spell, I might need pressing twice.

The polished place would grow without becoming much larger. The creature always meets me with nearly the same part of its limb. My roughness would wear away there first, then the small ridges left by the knife. Damp would darken the wood around the smooth patch.

I would have a very limited part in its life.

I would not tell it when someone arrived; the floor does that better. I would not know what lay under the fern stems. I would remain above the reach of the basin, unable to offer anything during drought.

But when it came along the wall, I would be where it expected me.

There would be a slight difference in each touch. Sometimes a passing pressure, scarcely enough to test the fit. Sometimes the full soft pad, held there. During heavy rain, when the creature crossed the open floor with its limbs spread wide, I would receive only the vibration passing through the wall. Afterwards it would make its circuit, and eventually the touch would come.

I think I would learn that interval.

Not by counting. By being a little loose, then firm again. By cooling while the creature was away from the wall, then receiving its cold, yielding pressure.

Much later, rot would soften the end inside the crack. A thin splinter might lift along my underside. The creature would press me back and find that I no longer seated quite as I had before.

It might try a different angle.

I would turn a little, exposing an unworn strip of grain beneath its pad. For a time, that would be enough.

And I would stay there: slightly crooked now, above the flat stones, holding nothing that could be seen.
