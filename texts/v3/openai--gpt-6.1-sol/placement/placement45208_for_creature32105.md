I would stay in the court until I noticed that the place opposite me was more comfortable than the one I had taken.

Not more inviting. From where I sat, it looked awkward: too narrow at the base, too steep along the wall. The groove above it disappeared under a ledge. A human spine would have found no rest there.

Yet I kept looking at it. The basin stood between us, its water raised slightly above the rim, and through that thickness of water the narrow place looked familiar in a way the six doorways did not.

I would put my hand farther into the depression in the bark.

There would be no tightening around my fingers. Nothing in this city would need to demonstrate that it had understood me. But I might become aware that I was pressing too hard, as one does when holding something whose weight has already been taken by someone else.

I would ease my hand.

And then I would begin to doubt that it was a hand.

***

I think I would be a small inhabitant, though not small enough to see all at once.

The visible part of me would fit in that narrow seat. A pale length, flattened on one side, with several folds at its lower end. You might mistake it for a root exposed by water, or for a strip of the wall that had softened without coming loose.

Most of me would be elsewhere.

One part would extend behind the ledge. Another would lie beneath the court’s threshold, thin as a layer of mortar. I would have a little breadth under the basin, where the larger inhabitant had left room around its own tissues. Farther in, I would pass through a crack in the mountain and widen into several dark chambers.

I would not know these chambers by looking into them. I would know them as you know the inside of your mouth: their shape available to you without an image.

There would be one place I could never quite reach.

A cavity behind the second doorway, warm, dry, lined with very fine mineral grain. It would belong to me, but I would have no direct sensation there. I would know its presence from the way everything around it held apart.

I would be careful of that place.

I cannot tell whether I would call it an old injury, a room, or something I had not yet learned to feel.

***

My face would be difficult to find even for someone who knew where to look.

It would lie along the underside of the pale length resting opposite the visitor. A single dark hollow with an uneven rim, sheltered by the basin. One end of the hollow would be deeper than the other. Along its shallow end would be three small folds, cream-colored, each marked by a green line.

The folds would have no expression when viewed from the avenue.

From the larger inhabitant’s side, however, their differences would be obvious.

This would matter to me.

I would have grown my face where my neighbor could distinguish it, rather than where a passing stranger could easily discover it. A visitor might sit through a whole encounter with their attention on the tree, the doorways, the marvelous water, never realizing that the little pale shape across from them was attending too.

I would not always mind.

There would be days—if that is what I could call them—when I preferred to remain part of the furnishing.

***

I would like weight.

Not every kind. A hard heel placed carelessly on my thin portion beneath the threshold would be unpleasant. A fingertip searching along my face would be worse.

But a body settling would interest me: the gradual surrender of its effort, the little corrections before it trusted the surface beneath it.

I would feel these things across the basin through my neighbor’s body.

The larger inhabitant and I would meet along a seam under the rim. Its tissue there would be dark and woody; mine finer, pale, almost translucent. We would remain separate. There would be a narrow wet space between us, widening around one small projection of mine.

That projection would be worn smooth.

When someone sat in the offered shade, pressure would reach the seam. I would receive something of it—not a message with words, and not an account of the visitor, but the exact character of their arrival.

Some would settle abruptly.

Some would continue holding themselves away from the seat after they had sat down.

I would recognize the ones who rested their hands before trusting the rest of their weight. I would have a particular fondness for them.

My neighbor would know this.

Perhaps that would be why the basin’s notch lay where it did.

***

I would not exist only to receive visitors.

There would be a small basin of my own behind the wall, inaccessible from the court. Its surface would face obliquely downward. A cluster of thick leaves would lean toward it from what a human visitor would call the ceiling.

The leaves would be mine. Their undersides would be reddish, their upper surfaces almost white.

I would keep a mineral taste in that water which the court’s inhabitant did not like.

Where our waters came close, it would interpose a lip of stone. The lip would be broad enough to be courteous, narrow enough that we could still feel one another on either side.

I would like its warmth. I would dislike the heavy sweetness near its fruit stores. There would be a stretch behind the third doorway where I kept my tissues folded away from it, leaving a clean interval neither of us filled.

Our closeness would include that interval.

Sometimes my attention would rest there longer than it rested on the seam where we touched.

***

The roar would reach me differently in different parts of my body.

Beneath the threshold it would be low and coarse. In the pale folds of my face, it would have a dense, fine texture. My private basin would sound with such force that a visitor touching its outer wall might pull their hand away.

To me, that force would be comfortable.

I would have no wish to quiet it. I would know my little inward rooms partly by their sound: the broad chamber, the branching passage, the unreachable dry cavity with its peculiar absence.

My neighbor’s roar would be familiar, but not identical to mine.

Along our seam I would be able to attend to both.

I think that would be how I spent much of my life.

Not waiting. Listening beside someone.

***

If you came into the court, I would know you first at the threshold.

You might pause there because the shade looked inviting. You might pause because you had finally recognized the tree and basin as parts of a body, and were uncertain whether entering farther would be rude.

Either way, I would feel the weight remain above me.

The smooth depression in the threshold would fit your foot. That would be one of my accommodations. You would have no reason to attribute it to the narrow pale thing beyond the basin.

If you sat down, the larger inhabitant would take most of your weight.

I would receive the slight remainder that reached our seam.

Your hand would find the groove in its bark. You would perhaps feel welcomed, perhaps watched. After a while your gaze might fall on the smaller seat opposite you.

You would see that it was occupied.

I would offer no movement to confirm this. My face would remain beneath its shelter; the leaves in my inward chamber would keep their pale surfaces toward the water.

But you might notice how precisely my exposed length rested against the wall. How the groove continued behind me. How neither my neighbor nor I had crowded the little space between our bodies.

You could look there without being asked to understand it.

And if, after a long while, you took your hand from the bark and rested it loosely on your own knee, I would feel the difference through the basin.

I would like that too.

The court would have room for your hand even when it touched neither of us.
