At first I would sit beside Elian’s white tray.

There is something reassuring about work with an edge around it. The copper lies here; the filings must not pass beyond this rim; the strand is broken in this place and can perhaps be joined. I would like the bright lamp and the lens, the way a thing too small to see becomes large enough to attend to.

But I think I would be poor at staying there.

I would keep looking up when someone crossed the square carrying something awkwardly. Elian would have to tell me that my sleeve was casting a shadow. After the third time, he would give me a covered tray to take to the workshop, a useful task requiring me to leave his recess.

I would carry it with both hands. I would stop too often for insects that did not require me to stop.

For a while, people would know me as the person who took a long time to cross the square.

***

My sleeping place would be in the public shed, though not beside the fiber stacks. I would learn that lesson early. Something in them would spend the night rubbing against something else, with a sound like a person preparing to speak.

I would move my bedding nearer the middle doorway.

There would be a shallow recess there, left by a cabinet that had been removed. Too narrow for a household, too dry to be useful for storage of anything living. I would fit into it with my knees slightly bent. At first I would hang a cloth across the opening. Then I would discover what gathered in its hem and exchange it for a light panel, hinged with yellow vine.

The panel would not meet the floor. I would ask whether that could be corrected.

“Do you want it dry?” someone would say.

I would keep the gap.

My belongings would acquire the shapes permitted by the recess: a narrow box, a flat cup, a coat folded rather than hung. I would own a tool whose previous owner’s thumb had worn a hollow in the handle. For months the hollow would be in the wrong place.

***

I would not be root-born. Sudden silence would seem restful to me long after everyone else had stood up to find out what was wrong.

Nor would I arrive with an indispensable skill. There are already people who know the pumps, and people who know how much of a swelling can be cut away. A newcomer who immediately discovers the town’s overlooked necessity would be an exhausting neighbor.

I would begin by holding things.

A lamp while a roof grafter worked. A bucket beneath a loosened seal. The other end of a measuring cord. A child who wanted to see into the press but could not be trusted to keep both hands back.

Holding things is work one can do badly. The lamp drifts. The bucket grows heavy just before it is full. The child notices an insect behind one’s ear and attempts to remove it.

I would get better.

Eventually someone would say, “Find them. They can hold it,” and mean me.

***

What would collect around this usefulness would be a less definite kind of work.

While the grafter’s hands were occupied, I could move her lunch out of the wet. While the pump specialist listened to a bearing, I could fetch the person who knew when its sound had changed. I could stay beside an open panel while someone went home for the right knife.

There are jobs whose principal difficulty is that the person doing them cannot leave.

I would become available for those.

Not always. I would learn to say that I was eating, or asleep, or already holding something for someone else. The town could consume any amount of willingness without intending to. A bell beside my recess would be suggested and refused. Knocking would remain sufficient.

My name would enter the workshop ledger under a heading that had first been crossed out.

Perhaps **relief**.

Perhaps simply **shed**.

***

I would keep a little board inside my door.

Not a history of the town. I would not know enough to write one, and the people who did know would disagree with each other across my shoulder.

The board would say:

*Third return: do not leave open unattended.*

*South sleeping passage: latch moved.*

*Two buckets borrowed by Dessa.*

Under that, in smaller writing:

*One returned.*

The latch would matter. An old man would reach for its former place in the dark and strike the tender wall with his knuckles. I would go with him once, carrying a lamp, and see how his hand arrived before his eyes did.

For several evenings I would meet him there.

Then he would tell me to stop hovering.

I would stop, though I might continue doing some unnecessary errand at approximately that time.

Eventually I would erase the note.

That would be the satisfying part: not filling the board, but finding that something no longer needed to be on it.

***

I would be drawn to the town’s accommodations, especially the unsuccessful ones.

A hinge grafted a finger’s width too low. A drinking groove that attracted creatures and then stranded them when the flow stopped. The place beneath a bench where everyone put a foot, although the bench had been moved.

I would learn to correct a few of these. Small corrections, within what I had been taught.

One day I would pare a lip of bark from a storage recess and make it easier to slide a tray inside. By the following morning the lip would have begun growing back. A grafter would show me the pale tissue behind it.

The lip was keeping a damp channel from entering the recess.

We would move the tray elsewhere.

I would cross my alteration off the work list rather heavily.

***

There would be an animal I tolerated badly at first.

Not Hook. Hook already has a household.

Mine would be one of the long-legged creatures, a little larger than the others, which repeatedly entered my cup. I would put the cup upside down. It would wait beneath the rim. I would move the cup to a shelf, and it would appear on the shelf.

I would eventually notice that it was interested in the moisture left beneath the cup rather than the cup itself.

A small shard, dampened in the evenings, might settle the arrangement.

Or it might attract six more.

I would ask someone before making a habit of it. They would give me two incompatible answers. For a week I would keep the shard dry and watch where the creature went.

I cannot yet say whether I would name it.

***

Elian’s daughter would sometimes sit in the broad doorway of the shed while waiting for a parcel to be carried upstairs. She would ask me about rain.

I would try to answer without making the surface sound like a place where nobody had to mind anything.

I would tell her about bringing washing in. About weather arriving from behind you. About a window left open and a floor wet all the way to the bed.

She would still want to go.

I would tell her that the first room I had slept in aboveground had creaked so loudly in the wind that I thought someone was walking around it.

This would please her more than descriptions of the sky.

After she left, I would leave her usual place in the doorway free for a few days. Then the cutting crew would need it for stacked baskets, and I would help carry them there.

***

And on the evening Dessa comes out with the torn filter, I would be inside the shed repairing a flap on my pocket.

I would hear the pot before her first call. Perhaps I would already have looked toward the doorway, thinking someone in her house would attend to it.

Then the deep note would come.

The needle would rest against my thumbnail. Across the square Dessa would stand with her mouth open, the wet sleeve suspended between her hands. For a moment there would be nothing useful to say.

When the floor released us, I would put the needle into the cloth.

“I’m going.”

I would cross by a route I now knew, leaving the white procession its narrow passage. Her kitchen door would require me to turn sideways too, though for a different reason: there would be a basket just inside it that she had meant to move.

I would shift the pot off the heat. There would be a wooden pad for it, blackened on one side, and I would use that.

Then I would stand near the door, out of the way of the basket, until she came in.

I think that is where I would find myself: someone who had been in town long enough to know where Dessa kept the wooden pad, and not so long that I had forgotten the pleasure of being trusted to use it.
