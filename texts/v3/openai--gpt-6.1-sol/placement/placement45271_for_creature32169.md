At first I would put myself on the walkway over the pipes.

It offers somewhere to stand without having to trust the ground. I would hold the wet rail and feel the flow through my hand. Beneath the grating, sediment is travelling toward a place where it will stop being cargo and begin to hold weight. The lamps have come on, but there is still enough daylight to see the narrow toes of the birds.

From there I could imagine being useful: reading a gauge, checking a joint, reporting that the level had risen.

But I think I would keep looking down.

There is a patch beside one of the pipe supports where the water trembles differently. The main current passes it; a weaker current turns back. A little suspended material gathers there, disperses, gathers again. Something has made a hole in the gray surface. The hole is scarcely larger than a raindrop’s mark, but unlike the raindrop’s mark it remains.

That is where I would find myself.

Not on the surface. A little below it.

I would be one of the worms in the older mud.

My body would be longer than the space I usually occupy. Folded into a passage, I would know the passage by contact: a wall against one side, a loose grain beneath me, water moving over a part of my skin that had been still. There would be no view of the embankment. The river’s height would reach me as a change in pressure, then as a change in the water entering my hole.

The machinery would have no separate voices. Its vibration would come through everything. Occasionally a new pulse would arrive, strong enough to make me draw back. Then the usual trembling would remain, and I would extend again.

I would live close enough to the surface to be in danger.

There is food there. Small particles lodge along the mouth of the passage. Water brings things I can use and things I cannot. I take what I can reach, withdraw, come forward again. Some of the passage has to be cleared. A grain that fitted yesterday is obstructing it now. I work around it until it moves.

Above me, the wader’s foot presses down.

I do not know a foot. I know that the roof has shifted and that the water has been squeezed along my body. For a moment every contact changes.

Then the pressure lifts.

The bird has stepped elsewhere. Perhaps its bill has entered a neighboring hole. Perhaps it has caught the worm there. Nothing in my passage tells me which. I remain contracted after the disturbance is over, then begin, gradually, to move.

I would not be brave. There would be no need to give the remaining alive a name.

The patch where I live has rested for several weeks. It is still soft, but it has acquired passages. Water can enter it without taking the whole surface apart. Other small bodies move nearby. Sometimes one crosses mine and leaves a little collapse behind it. I open the passage again.

Far above, a surveyor might describe this layer by its elevation and water content. Under the bird it is a place to probe. For me it has close places, open places, places I can turn in, places where I must draw myself thin.

Toward evening, fine sediment begins to settle at the entrance.

At first I can clear it. I push through, and cloudy water rises into the shallow pool. More arrives. The opening narrows while I am below it.

I come forward and find a wall where there had been water.

Here, perhaps, I would become more distinctly myself: in the effort to continue a passage that no longer quite reaches outside.

There is no decision made all at once. I press into the softer material. Stop. Contract. Press again. The old opening stays behind me, plugged and still recognizable for a while to something moving through it. The new section rises at an angle.

It is slow work. The layer is thin.

Eventually water moves freely against my front end. I have reached the surface again, a little higher and a little to one side.

The bird that fed above me is resting on the sand shelf now. Its eye opens when a neighboring bird shifts. I cannot sense it there. Between us lie a few meters of shallow water, some fresh sediment, and differences in warmth too slight for a person on the walkway to notice.

During the night, water touches its foot. It stands and moves uphill.

Below, material settles around my new opening. I withdraw, then return to clear it.

This is where I would be: near the pipe support, beneath the sheltered pocket, in a layer too thin to receive its own band on the survey stake.

By morning there might be a small cast beside the hole. The rain might flatten it before anyone saw.

I would still be there, working a grain loose.
