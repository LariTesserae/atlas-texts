At first I would be mistaken for something put aside.

I would be on the low shelf beside the old man’s table, between a spool of dark cord and a forefoot whose owner had stopped coming to the house. There would be a shallow tray beneath me, though I would have grown too wide for it. One edge of the tray would press a line into my side.

I think I would have hands before I had a face.

Not very good hands. One would close neatly, but the other would leave a narrow opening between two fingers. A peg could fall through it. The old man would notice this and put a strip of pale material on his table, ready to make the correction.

Then he would find a use for the opening.

He would set a split seed husk between my fingers while he worked on its hinge. I could hold it without bringing its two sides together. He would turn the husk, inspect it, turn it back. When he stopped for his meal, he would leave it with me.

That might be the first thing I remembered: being trusted not to close my hand.

For some time I would live within reach of his table. I would learn the sound of a repair going badly—the very small click that makes him lay down his knife—and the longer silence when he has found where an earlier maker concealed a fastening. I would learn not to offer help until he had set the work down.

He would occasionally rest his stiff palm against my back. I would be warm enough by then to make this useful.

My legs would be fitted later. They would not match. One would have been intended for a creature that moved beneath cupboards; the other would bend more readily, and would need its foot finished after I had begun walking. For several days I would turn in the same direction whenever I tried to leave the room.

A child would put a flake of fruit on the threshold.

I would reach it by going around the table twice.

There would be laughter, but someone would move the tray out of my way.

—

I would not be the person who knew how to make things.

I would carry things between people who did.

A bowl, at first. Then a door latch wrapped in cloth, a leaf with its lowest rib broken, a jaw whose owner followed close behind me and nudged my leg whenever I stopped.

I would be low enough to see beneath the hanging foliage. The undersides of leaves would become more familiar to me than their upper surfaces: the knots, the fine splinters, the places where a maker’s pinch had thinned the green. I would recognize houses by what had caught under their steps.

There would be a pale bead beneath one doorway that nobody could reach. Passing it each day, I would try with a finger, then with a sliver of bark. One morning it would be gone. I would spend longer looking for it than I had ever spent trying to retrieve it.

After that, I would begin noticing what had changed.

A wall animal’s mouth would no longer quite clear the edge of a plate. A child’s elbow cup would leave a deeper mark than before. The old man would move his chair a little farther from the table, then forget and reach too short.

I could bring these things to someone’s attention. More often, I could simply stay.

When the plate was loosened and moved, I would hold it until the paving gripped it again. When a new strip was fitted to the elbow cup, I would keep the ends apart while they cooled. At the old man’s table, I would push the spool toward his hand.

This would become my work without anyone quite assigning it.

People would call me when something needed to be held for longer than was convenient.

My name would be short in most rooms.

—

There would be a place behind the leaf-maker’s stool where I liked to sit. A root would have taken the paving on either side but left a depression in the middle, wide enough for my body. From there I could watch her feet and the undersides of the leaves she raised to the light.

She would sometimes give me the end of a thread.

I would hold it while she looped the other end around a branch. When she had tied it, she would touch my fingers rather than ask me to let go.

Once, the touch would not come.

She would have been called indoors to help with something, leaving a leaf suspended between us. I would sit through the slow passage of pressure across the court. The leaf would turn, drawing the thread tight. Its edge would brush my wrist.

By dusk the branch would have grown into its hole.

But my end of the thread would still be there.

I would take it to her the following morning. She would examine the dark length, look at the tree, and unwind it carefully from my fingers. There would be a green mark where it had lain.

For a while she would give me heavier things to carry.

I would miss sitting behind the stool.

—

Eventually I would have a room, though it might remain the space beneath the old man’s stairs. Someone would fit a woven frame into it. Someone else would bring an empty cup.

I would keep the cup beside the bed because the others did, and turn it in my hands in the morning. Its rim would fit the opening between my fingers. I would discover this accidentally and then do it every day.

I would still leave unfinished things beside my sleeping place: a shutter pin, a bird’s loose foot, a little chair found in fruit. Not things I knew how to mend. Things whose owners would come back for them.

Sometimes an animal would arrive carrying a piece that did not belong to it.

I would make room anyway.

And sometimes, after the lamps were covered, someone would call softly from a nearby house. I would find them sitting with a new joint held between both palms, unable to reach the tool needed to secure it.

I would fetch the tool. Then I would take one side of the joint.

We would sit without talking until they began to shift their weight.

“Has it settled?” they would ask.

I would feel along the edge with my imperfect hand. One part would be firm. Another would still give a little.

“Not yet.”

They would sit back.

Outside, a seed would tick against the roof and stop somewhere in the leaves. We would wait together, holding what neither of us could put down alone.
