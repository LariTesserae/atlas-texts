At first I would put myself in the shadow beneath the second step. It seems the only place where the brightness loosens its hold: a little shelter, a place to remain while the water passes.

But the shadow moves. By afternoon it has lengthened; by morning it has drawn back. I would need something smaller and more dependable.

There is a grain in the mortar that projects farther than the grains beside it. On its downstream side, the water still moves, but less forcefully. Something can touch there without immediately being taken away. I would find myself at that contact: neither deep in the joint nor out in the current, just where a passing thing first becomes a thing that stays.

I might be the first cell to catch. Yet the first cell is already dividing, and the distinction would soon become difficult to keep. One holds to the grain; another holds beside it. Their clear secretions meet. A particle of roof dust settles between them, altering the current at a scale too small to trouble the light.

I think I would be that clear meeting.

Not quite the green thread. Not quite the mortar underneath. I would be the faint, clinging substance between them—the part that would be missed by someone looking down from the coping, even someone who noticed the green and leaned closer to inspect it.

Water would pass through me as well as over me. I would hold no enclosed space, make no separate little room. My boundaries would be loose strands, hydrated folds, places where the current slowed enough to leave something behind. A cell, a speck of softened dust, a molecule carried from the roof: each would change what could catch next.

The mortar would remain visible through me.

That matters here. The chamber is so newly made that occupation has scarcely begun to alter its appearance. Every surface still declares the work done to it. The rounded fillet keeps the floor easy to clean. The tiles fit squarely against one another. Water follows the arrangement it has been given.

I would have no quarrel with that arrangement. I would adhere to it.

The tiny ridge left by the wet tool would become, along one side, a place of weaker flow. The joint would offer pores the tile face did not. Even the careful smoothing would leave differences. I would spread unevenly through those differences, making the most of each without knowing there was a whole room around me.

Near noon, the green cells held within me would receive the light. A bubble might begin against a strand and remain there, round and bright, until its buoyancy exceeded my small grip. Then it would rise.

I would not follow.

At the spout, my margins would be continually tested. Beneath the step, they could extend farther. The chamber would not be one environment to me, but many neighboring conditions: warmth a little greater here, light a little weaker there, a current that brushed one exposed edge all day and scarcely reached a hollow beside it.

My form would be the record of those differences.

A surge would pull a filament taut. For a while, what had been a loose curve would become a straight line. Then it would part. The detached length would turn above a tile, catch the moving sunlight, and disappear from the small region in which I remained.

There would be no clean wound. The edge left behind would fray, and water would enter where the strand had been. Nearby cells would continue their slow additions.

Perhaps some of the departing fragment would lodge farther along the same joint. Perhaps it would leave by the overflow. I could not gather these possibilities into a story about myself. My continuity would be local: this attachment, these neighbors, this water arriving again with traces of what had happened elsewhere.

When evening withdrew the light, the tiles would keep their warmth. I would become harder to see without becoming less present. The current would press the loose edges flat, lift them, press them flat again.

Above, someone might look into the chamber and find it nearly empty.

I would be there in the nearly: beneath the visible green, around the projecting grain, making a slight place for another arrival.

By morning, a little more of the mortar would be occupied.
