At first I think I would live in the socket at the end of the bridge.

Not in the bird’s nest, but below it, in some recess the builders left between two thicknesses of metal. I would want the garden behind me and the open chamber in front: a place from which to watch the falling leaves, the moths held between currents, the pale opening that makes even this enormous hollow seem enclosed.

But that is a choice made with eyes. It assumes that seeing a place makes it available.

I put a foot against the enamel and find nothing to hold.

So I am farther back than I first imagined. Beneath the ceramic lip, close to the mother’s belly. I am the young one, with a short tail and feet that already know how to close before I know what they are closing around.

The first room is not the chamber. It is the space between her foreleg and her side.

There is warmth on one side and a root on the other. When she moves, the root goes away. I hold more tightly, and another surface comes beneath my hind feet: the soft inner fold of her membrane. I do not yet distinguish being carried from the world changing its shape.

The roar has never begun. There is no memory of its arrival and no expectation that it will stop. What I notice are interruptions inside it.

Her breast lifts away from the root.

A pipe receives her weight.

Something small strikes the metal twice, above us.

She becomes still, and I become still with her. Before I learn the bird, I learn what she does when the bird lands.

***

I grow first in my feet.

They reach beyond the warm place where I sleep. One finds the ceramic, and its fine creases flatten against a surface harder than anything I have touched. Another finds a root. I can press against one and pull against the other. This lifts my head.

For a little while, that is enough.

My mother moves around me. Her nose pushes into the hollow behind my ear. She lowers her body, offering the familiar place, but I have found the rim of the plate and do not release it immediately.

There is a screw beneath my left foot. I take hold of its head.

Much later, when I can cross the lower rack without her, I will still pause at that screw. Its edge fits between two claws. I do not need it to continue along the plate, but I put a foot there whenever I pass.

My first journey without her is shorter than her body.

I go beneath the leaf beside our hollow. Its underside presses across my back. There is a beetle in the fibers ahead of me, too large to take, and I follow it until it slips beneath a crust I cannot lift.

When I turn around, the hollow is hidden.

I can see a piece of my mother’s tail. I cannot see the route by which I came.

I try to go directly toward her and find myself on the rounded ceramic, sliding. My claws make no useful sound. They make no useful purchase either. I flatten my belly, reach backward, and catch the leaf that brushed me.

It bends almost to the plate.

My mother watches from the hollow. Only when I have pulled myself beneath it again does she come out and put her nose against mine.

For several days I use the same route. I learn where the leaf bends, where a root passes under the mat, where I can turn without letting go with more than two feet at once.

***

I had thought I would belong to the bridge because of the emptiness around it.

Instead I belong to the underside of things.

The hollow behind an elbow pipe. The gap beneath a lifted washer. A patch of filter felt that keeps moisture after the exposed fibers have dried. These places do not look like much from the chamber. Some cannot be seen at all.

One afternoon, I discover the glove.

It is farther down than the routes my mother usually takes, lodged behind a drainage ridge. Most of it is hard. Water has entered through the cuff and left through a split in one finger, carrying fine debris with it. The split holds a little dark growth. Two fingers contain nothing but dust.

I enter through the cuff.

At first I back out because the smell is unfamiliar. Then I enter again.

The shape permits me to put my back against one wall and all four feet against another. There is a narrow opening through which I can see the garden, though not the bright door. When a bird lands above, the vibration reaches me faintly through the hardened palm.

I sleep there once, badly.

I wake thirsty. I have missed my mother’s departure and must find her by following the places where her cheek has touched the roots.

Still, I return to the glove. I pull a loose strand through the cuff. I bite a beetle behind the thumb. I rub my own cheek against the split finger, and after that the smell is less unfamiliar.

There is already a being in this world, with a sleeping hollow and a young one to carry. I do not replace her. I become another animal nearby, smaller for a season, using some of her routes and gradually finding others.

***

My first crossing is not graceful.

There are two roots, and between them a space through which I can see the lowest ledge. I have stepped over narrower gaps. This one requires me to let go.

I lean until the root bends.

Then I push.

My forefeet strike the far root too low. My body drops beneath it, and for a moment my whole weight hangs from my claws. The membranes open because my legs have spread, not because I have made good use of them.

I pull myself up.

On the next crossing, I leave earlier. On the one after that, a rising current turns me slightly, and I land with my hindquarters against a pipe.

There are distances I never learn to cross. The bare wall remains bare. I watch insects stand on it without understanding how they keep their feet.

But eventually I reach the bridge.

The bird in the socket lifts its wings. I stop. It taps its beak against the rim, and the taps come through the rail beneath my belly.

I lower myself and pass underneath.

For the first time, I am where I thought I would be: close to the unfinished end, with the garden behind me and the chamber open ahead.

I do not look outward for long.

There is a moth below the rail. There is a pipe I might reach. I shift my feet and wait for the current to lift the fur along my side.

***

The glove becomes too narrow before the next long rain.

For a while I try sleeping with my head outside the cuff. Then one night I turn in my sleep and cannot find enough room to fold my hind leg. I leave it and settle beneath a cable tray until daylight.

My next hollow is behind a shallow buckle in a panel near the lower roots. Warmth comes through its back. A fern conceals the entrance. I clear out a dry leaf that blocks the narrowest part, though I leave the softer pieces beneath it.

From here I can feel arrivals on three different routes.

My mother passes above without stopping. I know her weight before I see the end of her tail.

Later, another animal comes down the pipe and pauses at my entrance. Its nose moves against the metal where I have rubbed my cheek. I plant my feet wide. We remain that way for some time.

It goes on.

Inside, I turn twice before settling. One hind foot rests against a rivet; the other fits beneath the edge of the panel. My tail curves along the wall.

There is still almost nothing in the chamber’s middle. A leaf falls there while I am awake, rising once, turning, then passing below the ledge. I follow it with my eyes until I cannot see it anymore.

Afterward I tuck my head beneath my foreleg.

The loose panel begins to tremble. I move one foot off it without waking fully, and sleep against the warm wall.
