At first I would stay in the room where wet equipment is left.

Not because it is comfortable. There would be a draft beneath the door, and the benches would have grooves worn into them by buckles and tools. But it would be a place where arrivals are expected to take time. Nobody would mistake my standing still for competence. I could learn the drain’s position from the sound of water falling out of a sleeve. I could learn the door by putting a hand against it when someone else opened it.

There would be a peg I was allowed to use.

For a while, that would be enough to distinguish me from something carried in with the weather.

I imagine being given small jobs. Washing the grit from a valve seat. Rolling cuffs from the backs of the door handles, taking care not to tear them. Sorting fasteners into compartments whose shapes I would initially confuse. Someone would check my work without making a ceremony of it.

I would have to learn not to ask a question while another person was listening.

That lesson would take longer than learning the compartments.

At night I would lie near an interior wall, hearing the station through it, unable to separate one operation from another. A knock would seem ominous until someone told me it happened every few minutes. Then a sound I had hardly noticed would bring two people out of bed.

I would want to know what they had heard.

“Wait until it does it again,” they would say.

Sometimes it would not do it again for three days.

***

Eventually I think I would live at a small station near the edge of a maintained district, where one of the old conduits turns inland and disappears beneath a suspended forest.

My room would be in the narrow space between the drying room and the workshop. Too warm when the racks were full; colder when nobody had come through for a while. There would be room for a bed, a recessed shelf, and a basin. I would keep my boots on two pegs, soles outward, so I could examine them before putting them on.

The station would have a turbine with an uneven beat that visiting workers always mentioned.

“Yes,” we would tell them.

“How long has it done that?”

We would give a different answer depending on which part of the beat they meant.

I would learn the machine well enough to take a shift, but I do not think it would become my principal work. I would be slow at some repairs. My hands would understand a mechanism more readily after it had been assembled than while its pieces lay apart.

What I would be good at, after enough practice, is carrying an account from one place to another without making it more certain on the way.

I would become a route examiner.

Not someone who discovers passages through untouched country. Most of the routes I worked on would have been used for generations. I would go out to establish whether they could still be used, by whom, carrying what, and under which conditions.

A crossing might be sound for a person with an empty pack and unsafe for two people carrying a bearing between them. A handrail might remain firmly anchored while the ground beneath it had lowered beyond a short person’s reach. A warm stream might be harmless at the usual pressure and deep enough to take someone off their feet after the inland springs began to knock.

These distinctions would be my work.

I would carry a short sounding rod, a cord with spaced knots, wedges, a narrow scraper, and a record case sealed against water. There would be two changes of dry wrapping for my hands. One I would use too early; the other I would keep long after I should have used it.

My heel plates would be plain, but one would have been repaired badly enough to give my step a recognizable second tick.

People would hear me come into the equipment room.

“You’re back.”

Then, usually:

“Which way?”

***

There would be an old route I kept trying to understand.

It would leave the station on a shallow descent, follow a pipe through reed beds, and reach a place where the pipe rose clear of the ground. Beyond that stood the remains of a maintenance shelter. Its roof would have fallen years before, but the floor would still drain, and one wall would retain heat from the conduit.

Travelers could rest there.

In the records, the next section would be described as a stone crossing between two banks. Several later hands would have added qualifications. Slippery at the inland end. Rail loose at the second fastening. Lower surface preferable under low water.

But there would be no stone crossing when I reached it.

There would be roots, packed tightly enough to walk on, and water moving somewhere beneath them. Iron fastenings would be embedded in the growth. The rail would enter a trunk and emerge farther along, as if the tree had swallowed it incompletely.

At first I would assume the crossing had been replaced.

An older traveler would tell me it had not.

I would return with someone from the nearest settlement. We would examine the banks, the fastenings, the underside where it could safely be reached. Eventually I would find stone beneath the roots: not the span itself, but its end, tilted downward.

The bridge would still be there. It would have broken and lowered into the channel. The roots, already woven over it, had taken the load.

I would spend several visits trying to decide what to put in the record.

Calling it a stone crossing would mislead people. Calling it a root crossing would lose information they might need. The old description had been written by someone whose hands had touched a structure I could no longer reach.

My first revision would be too long.

A keeper would read it, tapping through the raised lines with increasing impatience.

“What do I do when I get there?”

I would rewrite it.

Approach from the pipe side. Test the first exposed root before transferring weight. Keep one hand on the rail. Do not use during a deep-water surge. There would be separate notes on the buried span and the uncertainty of the inland anchorage.

Even then, I would leave space.

***

I would not travel alone as often as I imagined when I began.

Sometimes an apprentice would come with me. Sometimes I would accompany a family moving between food beds, checking a route as we went. On difficult sections, I would hire someone who knew the local water and try not to resent how little they needed my instruments.

There would be a child who liked to count my wedges and announce any discrepancy before I had finished unpacking.

There would be someone I loved who worked at the station and disliked the drying-room wall between us. Eventually we would move into a house on one of the stone shelves. I would learn their approach without listening for the heel plates: the brief pause before the last stretch of rail, where they always adjusted whatever they were carrying.

Coming home, I would sometimes stand outside longer than necessary.

Inside, a vessel would be set into its recess. A floorboard would answer a step. Someone would open the lid of the food chamber, then close it again after deciding to wait for me.

I would knock before entering even though it was my house.

My absence would have changed the room’s arrangement in small ways, and I would want a voice to meet me before I put my weight into it.

***

With age, I would become less useful in places where a person had to climb.

I would begin teaching others how to compare their observations with a route record. I would be particular about dates. I would insist on recording what the water was doing, not merely whether it was raining.

I would also have to endure my own entries being corrected.

A young examiner would come back from the old crossing and report that the inland root had split. They would have found another way around, longer but safer.

I would ask too many questions.

They would answer patiently until they stopped being patient.

At last I would give them the record case and let them make the amendment. Later, alone, I would run my fingers over the new lines.

I think that is where I would most fully become an inhabitant of this place: not at the moment I first trusted the ground, but when someone else’s knowledge of it became better than mine.

I would still keep a hand near the station wall when the deep water moved.

I would still wake before knowing why.

But on some nights I would hear the younger keeper get up, cross the room, and take the listening rod from its bracket. I would wait while they tested the housing.

If they needed me, they would come.

Otherwise I would remain in bed, feeling the pressure gather and pass beneath the settlement. In the morning there would be an account to hear, a meal to make, and some small correction to the routes by which people found their way home.
