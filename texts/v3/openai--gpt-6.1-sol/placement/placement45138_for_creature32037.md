I would come from the door that opens last.

For a while I would remain on the threshold. The dry strip would hold the warmth of my feet, and I would feel the garden through it: the larger body braced against the retaining wall, the small one below, the water arriving unevenly across six steps.

There would be something else in the ground, too slight for me to place. It might be a root dragging against the inside of a joint. It might be the soft back trembling.

Behind me, the recess would still be warm.

I would have left it before feeding was finished. Along one side of my back, the plates would close cleanly; along the other, they would catch against a new band of tissue. Turning would require care. The wall would have been holding that side while I rested, and out in the street its unsupported weight would surprise me.

I would go down sideways.

On the fourth step, I would stop. Water would pass over the divided end of my foot, carrying a trace of mineral from the new runnel. I would lower one arm into it. There would be enough warmth to feed the patch, enough force to loosen its soil.

Below me, the smaller inhabitant would shift its pads farther apart.

A narrow stream would escape between them.

I think I would first put my arm there.

The stream would divide around it and find the shoot anyway. For a moment the leaf would flatten. I would pull back too quickly, sending a small wave across the dark ground.

Then I would wait.

The leaf would lift, with a grain of pale sand caught in its crease.

Above us, the reservoir machine would pass. Its pale bar would travel over the walls, the steps, our backs. In that brief change of light I would see how little covering the small one had: a single firm ridge, the surrounding tissue drawn tight by its effort. Water would bead there. It would be losing warmth faster than the patch.

I would move behind it.

There would be no convenient shape for this. My knees would fit badly on the edge of the lowest step. The plates at my shoulder would press together, and one would make a faint dry sound. I would need to settle twice before I could bring the soft surface beneath my open back against the small one’s shoulders.

At first it would stiffen.

Then some of its weight would come into me.

The current would still need dividing. Its arms would remain spread. Mine would rest outside them, taking the heavier water while its pads attended to the thin streams close to the shoot.

We would stay like that through the next pressure cycle.

At the first knock, I would feel the small body prepare to straighten.

At the second, I would loosen my knees.

At the third, we would answer together, awkwardly. My vibration would pass through its body before reaching the pavement. For a moment I would feel where it had grown and where it had not, a narrow firmness among yielding surfaces. Its faint answer would return through me.

Across the garden, the larger inhabitant would shift its weight against the retaining wall.

The approaching current would diminish.

Only then would I notice how much warmth I had given away.

---

Before this, my favored place would have been a wall cavity facing the shortest street.

Heat reached it irregularly. Some arrivals lasted almost exactly as long as the pressure chamber took to empty. Others continued through many cycles, deepening until I had to lift part of my back away from the wall.

I would know the intervals closely. I would know when to turn so that warmth reached a slow-growing plate, when to open my underside to the cooler air. There would be a pulse that often came before the window clearing, another that arrived afterward and smelled faintly metallic through the fluid channels.

I would have spent much of my young life there.

After the garden, I would return to the same cavity, but settle differently. The middle of my back would remain tender where the smaller body had leaned against it. I would turn that place toward the wall.

During feeding, I would feel the district adjust. Some distance away, water would enter the widened crack.

I would leave before I was full.

Not every time. Sometimes my plates would be too soft, or the heat arriving from above too thin. Sometimes another inhabitant would already be on the lowest step. I would feel its weight there and remain in my recess.

But I would begin to take that route often enough that the paving held my passage. A faint depression would form on the fourth step where I paused to test the current. The wall beside it would acquire a pale smear from my arm.

Nothing anyone above would recognize as belonging to someone.

It would be enough to find again.

---

I would not know what the shoot was meant to become.

Its roots might enter a heat channel. Its leaves might eventually pour water down the bank with all the others. The uncovered patch might close over, leaving no visible reason for the hours spent beside it.

For now, one leaf would open toward the blank window.

I would learn to touch the soil at some distance from its stem. I would learn how slowly water could arrive and still arrive, and how long the small inhabitant could hold its arms out before its back began to tremble.

Once, while it rested against me, its divided pad would close loosely around the end of mine.

I would keep that arm still.

When it woke and reached toward the crack again, I would let it go.
