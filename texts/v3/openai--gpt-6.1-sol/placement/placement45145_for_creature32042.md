At first I would put myself in the water beneath the smallest handprint.

Not in the print itself. There is a little ledge below it where the panel was set unevenly, and water rests there after the channels empty. I would be in that water, against the clay, small enough that the worn palm above me seemed like a shallow chamber rather than the mark of a hand.

I think I would have arrived from the pool.

There would have been a short time when I could cross open water. My margin would have lifted and folded; the pale scoring on the bottom would have passed beneath me. A drop would have turned me over. For several contractions I would have had nothing against my back and nothing against my underside, and I would have tried to fold around that absence.

Then I would have found the wall.

The first gap I entered would have been too narrow for my thicker middle. I would have remained half outside it while a row of seam-walkers stepped over me. Their feet would have made little dimples that disappeared slowly. Water would have come, and I would have pressed farther in.

By the time I could withdraw, withdrawing would have required a different shape.

So I would have stayed.

***

My place would be where a clay panel, a buried jar, and the end of a wooden rib nearly meet.

They would not quite touch. That would be the room available to me.

The jar would offer a smooth curve against which I could spread. The wood would offer narrow grooves full of softened matter. Behind the panel there would be enough depth to thicken when water arrived. I would learn these surfaces by growing against them. Some portions of me would become stained and rough. One fold, sheltered against the jar’s glaze, would remain clear enough for the dark bodies inside it to show.

For a long while I would be very small.

A shell would seem heavy when it settled on me. A fiber dragged across my skin would remain there through many rounds of knocking. I would open a mouth beneath it, close, and have food enough to occupy that place for hours.

Above me, the handprint would receive water. I would receive what ran out of its worn wrist.

I would not know it as a hand. The five shallow furrows would be five routes by which moisture sometimes came. One would dry first. Another would retain a bead until a knock shook it loose. My upper edge would grow toward that one.

***

I would hear the court through unequal thicknesses.

The wooden rib would bring a strike quickly, with a hard beginning. The jar would continue sounding after the wood had stopped. Through the clay panel, the same event would arrive softened and spread out, sometimes scarcely distinguishable from the pressure of water entering my gap.

At first, everything would make me contract.

Later, some sounds would be followed by a swelling instead. The three knocks would bring water often enough that I would begin making room before it arrived. I would pull away from the back of the panel, opening a little hollow. Water would fill it. Two white grazers would discover that hollow and remain there between flows.

They would scrape my coating.

I would thicken the coating.

They would scrape it again.

Neither of us would move away.

Eventually their passage would keep a narrow route open along my side, one I could not have maintained alone. I would grow around it. When one grazer died, its shell would lodge at the entrance, and for several days the water would come differently.

That would occupy more of me than the death itself.

***

There would be another body behind the jar.

I would encounter it by extending into what seemed to be an empty passage. My leading margin would touch something yielding. I would stop. It would not withdraw.

For a while we would lie against one another with a film of water between us.

Then the film would narrow.

I cannot say whether I would join it. Perhaps I would feed against its shed surface. Perhaps our outer layers would adhere while something deeper remained separate. A contraction would sometimes come through the contact, too slow to resemble a knock. I would answer it with a movement that had already begun elsewhere in me.

Afterward, food taken at my upper fold might thicken a lower fold I had never reached.

Or the change might be caused by water.

I would continue occupying the ledge beneath the small handprint. There would still be a portion of me that swelled there first. That would be the most reliable way to find me, though not necessarily a way to find my boundary.

***

When the great pot sounded, I would flatten.

Nothing in my little chamber would strike me hard enough to account for it. The panel would tremble slightly. The jar would hold the note. Yet I would draw down from the handprint, close my mouths, and press against the glazed curve.

The grazers would become still too.

Their stillness would reach me through their feet.

After the note faded, they would resume scraping before I began to swell. One would step onto the tender part I had exposed by withdrawing. I would feel each foot separately.

I would have no way to approach the pot’s mouth. Even a long growth toward the doorway might end against the wrong surface, in a passage with no opening into the court. Whatever I received from it would have to come through what was already touching me.

Once, perhaps, the note would last longer than usual.

The water waiting above me would descend while I remained flat. It would pass my little hollow without entering. The grazers would follow it down.

When I finally opened again, the place would be empty.

***

I would persist unevenly.

One end would dry and cease to draw back. Animals would shelter beneath its curled edge. Another would enter a cracked ring and find room to become thick. I would lose the passage behind the panel, then recover part of it when a grain of mortar loosened.

There would be no view from which these changes made a recognizable animal.

Still, at the ledge, I would keep a small active fold.

In the quiet after water, I would extend it into the worn palm. The clay would be cool. Its hollow would permit a shape I seldom had room to make elsewhere: a broad, thin end, spread without being squeezed between two surfaces.

I would remain there until the knocking began.

Then I would withdraw toward the jar and open the hollow for the arriving water.

Sometimes a little of my coating would stay behind in the print. A shell fragment. A loosened fiber. A pale, soft film that the next flow would almost remove.

Someone standing in the court might look at that film and wait.

Below it, out of sight, I would be making room.
