At first I would look for a room from which I could see the whole basin.

I would choose badly. A low roof beside the market would seem suitable, and I would carry a stool up there, together with a board for writing on. By the second afternoon the roof would have lowered enough that the spreading leaves over the stalls concealed half the town. By the fourth, someone would ask me to come down because my stool was interfering with the formation of a nest.

I would have begun a map. There would be a great deal of correction on it and, in one corner, a careful drawing of a stair that no longer existed.

For a while I would persist. I would put times beside my observations. I would draw water differently from walls, then discover that this distinction was costing me more work than it saved. People would be helpful without appearing to have much confidence in the result. A child would point to a blank place and tell me that her grandmother lived there.

I would say that I had been there that morning.

“Yes,” she would say. “She was out.”

Eventually the board would become a shelf.

This would not feel like a revelation. I would need somewhere to put a bowl someone had left with me while going to fetch its owner.

—

The room I kept would be near the highest strip, but not on it. There would be two entrances during wet weather and usually one afterward. I would learn to leave the uncertain entrance clear.

People would bring things.

A tool found beside a newly opened drain. A packet of letters taken down from a wall just before the wall closed. A child’s garment, stiff with dried membrane, recovered from the underside of a bridge. Most would be recent losses. A few would not.

I would not be a merchant. Payment would be irregular and sometimes inconvenient: six eggs when I had nowhere to keep eggs; a repair to my sleeping mat; a portion of cupboard flesh wrapped for cooking. Someone would bring a fine handrail root and be offended when I explained that I expected to move before it established itself.

Nor would I be an official. If there were a name for the work, people would use it chiefly when explaining to visitors why my room contained so many mismatched bowls.

They would say, “Leave it with that one.”

That one would be me.

I would keep the letters above the tools and the tools above anything that might still be alive. At first I would wrap everything carefully. Later I would learn which objects needed air, which needed darkness, and which were liable to attach themselves to the shelf.

There would be a crab that repeatedly came in with recovered furniture. I would carry it to the drain behind the market. It would return. Eventually I would recognize the nick in its left foreleg and carry it farther.

The children would consider this an excellent use of my time.

—

I would get to know people by what they came looking for.

A woman who could describe every chip in a missing cooking bowl but not remember where she had last cooked. A boy asking for a polished wedge in a manner that made it clear he had borrowed it without permission. Two people arriving separately for the same packet of letters, each expecting me to understand something neither intended to explain.

I would learn to ask fewer questions.

“Where was it found?” would be useful.

“Does it belong to you?” would sometimes be useful.

“Why do you want it?” would usually make the room smaller, whether or not it was raining.

I would keep records, but they would not be maps. A cord tied through a handle would mean that someone had already asked after it. Two knots would mean I had promised to bring it myself. I would use a different cord for things recovered from below. Those would lie on a separate shelf until they had dried enough to be handled.

There would be mistakes. I would give a basket to someone who recognized it and later discover that recognition was not ownership. I would leave a packet too near a warm seam. The ink would remain, but the folded pages would grow together at their edges.

I would sit beside that packet for a long time before taking it back.

The person who had entrusted it to me would be angry. I would not expect the town’s habits to excuse mine.

—

After some years, I would be found less often in the room.

Finding an owner would usually mean walking. I would carry things tucked high under my arm, stop beneath eaves, wait for a passage, change direction when a frog came out of a niche. People would ask me to take something farther. Then they would ask me to tell someone something.

I would resist the second request at first. An object stayed what it was while I waited for a causeway. A message could become wrong.

Tell him the room is dry.

Tell her we are still beside the hall.

Tell them there is no need to come.

I would begin asking, “What should I say if that has changed?”

Some people would find this tiresome. Others would call me back before I had reached the door.

I would become a person who arrived carrying a cup and said that someone had been asking for it yesterday, near the eastern pool, but had probably moved since.

My feet would broaden. I would develop an opinion about whether this was flesh or practice, then cease to be certain of it. There would be a pale line behind one ankle that troubled me during dry weather. I would own shoes and use them to hold down folded cloth.

I would still occasionally step too quickly.

—

One afternoon someone would bring me a heavy cup from a deep opening.

It would look ordinary after cleaning: low, dark, with a handle worn narrow at the top. I would set it on the separate shelf.

A person visiting for another reason would see it and stop speaking.

I would not touch the cup then. I would clear a place to sit. If the visitor wanted to hold it, I would bring it over. If not, it would remain where it was.

Perhaps they would identify it. Perhaps they would tell me only which room it had stood in, and what used to be visible through that room’s window.

For once my old map might be useful. Not because it could tell us where to go, but because there would be a stair on it that I had refused to rub out.

Afterward, I would find it harder to describe the separate shelf as a shelf for unclaimed things.

—

I would go to the quiet place near the rim when I had made too many decisions.

I would not have a special understanding with the little animal. I might fail to see it for weeks, then notice that it had been standing beside my foot. Once I would bring a small piece of fruit and be told, gently but firmly, not to begin that.

I would sit with my hands empty.

From there I could see the room I was currently using, or part of it. Someone might be waiting at its entrance. I would watch them decide whether to leave their object under cover or carry it away again.

The small creature would step into the overflow. Its fold would open. Across the court, a doorway would begin to form.

I would lean forward, trying to recognize the person behind it.

If they were carrying something awkward, I would get up.

—

So I think I would be an inhabitant, eventually, though not one who could name every sound beneath the floor. I would have useful knowledge and conspicuous gaps. The old woman with the ivory pegs would know more than I did about nearly everything, and would still send people to me when they had lost a ladle.

I would keep things dry. I would carry messages carefully. I would know which person had been looking for a carved figure, which child would come back for the broken bowl, and who should not be sent a recovered object without warning.

My room would have a hanging cloth that moved with me.

Near the entrance there would be a low, empty shelf. People in a hurry could leave something there without finding me first. I would check it whenever I returned.

Sometimes there would be nothing.

Sometimes there would be an object I knew, and I would have to stand still before going inside.
