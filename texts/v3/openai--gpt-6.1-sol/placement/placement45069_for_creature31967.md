At first I would be found among the boards.

Not on the newest ones. Those are handled too often, lifted to the window, set against the man’s knees. I would live behind the boards that have been turned to face the wall: a stack raised on two pieces of antler, with a little space beneath it in case water comes through the room.

There is wool caught on one of the antler tips. There are three shed beetle cases. There is me.

For some time I would be taken for a binding cord.

I am narrow, jointed, the colour of wood where wood has been wet and dried many times. My feet fold so closely beneath me that they look like fraying. When disturbed, I stiffen. Someone picking me up would find what seemed to be a short, unhelpful length of cord, too brittle to tie.

If they laid me down and looked away, I would be elsewhere when they looked back.

The man knows about me. He has known for years. Once, trying to lift a board, he put his remaining fingers on my middle, and I folded around them before either of us understood what was happening. He waited. I let go.

Now he taps the lower edge of a board before moving it.

I do not always move in time.

***

The grooves are comfortable.

A groove protects my sides and lets me travel with most of my feet touching something. The man cuts them well. Even a shallow one has a clean bottom, without splinters to catch beneath my joints.

For this reason, I have spent much of my life following streams that are no longer there.

I have crossed the dye house in winter and gone beneath a staircase demolished before the child’s grandmother was born. I have curled in a basin recorded as dry, on a board whose other face shows the same basin overflowing. Once I passed through the man’s room while the man was in it. He was eating. A crumb fell near the doorway marked in the wood, and I left the channel to investigate.

I eat crumbs, the soft edges of old cord, and something that grows in the deepest cuts. I would not be indispensable. No channel would fail because I had neglected it. The man could put all his boards in a cupboard and I would have to find somewhere else.

He does not, partly because he dislikes carrying them across the room.

***

I would hear best through my feet.

Outside, the birds’ wings are mostly a disturbance in the light. A mallet striking the stone beneath me is an event that passes through my whole body. I flatten before I know to flatten.

The older sounds are harder.

Sometimes a patch of wall begins giving back footsteps. I follow them along a joint, expecting the pressure of a sole, the useful fall of something edible. But the footsteps stay in the stone. They may go on while I am standing directly over them.

I learn certain places.

Below the man’s window, someone has been dragging a stool for several winters. Nothing ever arrives there. Near the door is one small impact that comes back at long intervals. That one still makes me curl.

When the man speaks to children, I feel his voice through the boards resting on his knees. I cannot make words out of it. I can tell when he has asked something and is waiting: the wood becomes still, except for the movement of his left thumb along an edge.

If a child puts a finger on the board, I withdraw.

Sometimes the child notices. Usually the man notices the finger and tells them not to rub away the marks.

***

Eventually I would leave the room.

Not bravely. Water would come up beneath the antler supports one night, touching places that had always been dry. I would climb the stack. By morning I would be on the newest board, exposed beside a fresh groove.

The man would carry that board outdoors.

There would be immense light, even through my closed outer skin. Wind would get beneath the edges of my joints. He would set the board on a low wall to free his hand, and I would follow its groove to the end.

Beyond it, the actual wall would continue.

For a while I would keep one pair of feet on the wood.

Then I would go down.

The stone would be rougher than I expected. No cut would run cleanly for long. A comfortable seam would narrow until I could not enter it; another would open beneath me without warning. There would be birds close enough to pick me up. In the first hour I would lose two feet to one of them.

I can grow feet again. It takes time. For several days I would walk badly, turning toward the injured side.

***

I would come to live beneath a threshold near the public square.

Not because it is particularly safe. Because I could get there, and because the underside has a hollow into which my body fits. Above me, boots are removed. Their cord ends trail down. Small quantities of food fall within reach.

There is a narrow sleeping place just inside the door.

When someone occupies it, I feel them arrive through the threshold: several uncertain footfalls, a bundle lowered, then the long redistribution of weight as a body lies down. People sleep differently in a room that is not theirs. They turn more often. Sometimes they get up, stand at the door, and do not go out.

I would stay beneath them.

If water began touching the back of my hollow, I would emerge. The woman of the house would eventually learn to notice this. She might lift the bedding when she saw me, though sometimes I would have come out for a dropped crust. She would make mistakes about me, and I would make mistakes about the water.

Once she would catch me in a cup and carry me to the drain.

I would come back over two nights.

After that she would leave me alone.

And when the child passed the door on her way home, her empty tray held beneath her coat, I would be just inside the threshold, eating the wet end of a cord.

She would stop with one boot lifted.

She has learned to set things down slowly. She would wait for me to finish crossing before she put her foot there.
