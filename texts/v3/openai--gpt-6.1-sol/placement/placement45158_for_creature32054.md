At first I would be found a little way inside the chamber where the five lanes meet, in a place that appears too narrow to be occupied.

There is a wall behind me and a root across the front. Water passes on the other side of the root. My back is dark enough that someone looking in from the lane would notice the pale undersides of the leaves before noticing my face.

I would have one hand on the floor.

For a while, that is all I can make out. I do not know whether I have come there to rest or whether I have been there so long that the hollow has grown around me. There is a thin place in the bark beneath my ribs. It fits me closely. When I shift, the wall gives a little, then takes several breaths to recover its shape.

I have to wait for it before I move again.

—

I think I would once have worked at doorways.

Not the large openings. Those require several bodies, a long arrangement of shoulders and hands, somebody willing to stay when the others withdraw. I would have worked on the small interior passages where a joint catches, where a resident must turn sideways and still leaves a strip of skin against the bark.

I would know these places by touch. At the narrowest part, I would settle with one arm raised, holding a stem in the warmth beneath it. My other hand would remain low, where the floor could tell me someone was approaching.

Then I would draw myself farther into the wall.

There would be days when this was almost all the work: making enough room for someone to pass, returning to the same position afterward.

I would be good at remembering a width. Even when a passage stood empty, my hands would keep the shape of the body that needed it.

But I would not be especially strong. The oldest resident, with the stone over its shoulder, could hold a roof in place while the join beneath it softened. I could only keep a young shoot from stiffening in the wrong direction. Once, impatient with the cold, I would press too hard and feel the fibers part under my thumb.

The leaf above that place would turn over by evening.

I would stay beside it through the night, covering the bruise. In the morning another resident would come to relieve me. It would examine the stem, then lay its hand across mine until I loosened my grip.

Afterward I would work more slowly.

—

There is something about the five entrances that keeps me in the central chamber.

Water arrives there carrying disturbances from several parts of the settlement. So do the roots. A footstep in the upper lane comes through sharply; a movement from the lower rooms arrives dispersed, crossing older joins. The chamber is where those pressures meet.

I would like being among them.

Not because I understood everything. Much of what reached my hand would be no more than weight changing somewhere beyond sight. A sleeper turning. A stem releasing its ice. Someone standing up after a long contact with a wall.

I would learn the familiar movements first.

The resident with the damaged forelimb puts its weight down twice before crossing a threshold. The elder’s stone produces a small second pressure after its body has stopped. One of the young pauses with every limb planted, as though the floor must be persuaded to remain beneath it.

When that young one comes through the chamber, I would lift my hand so it could see me waiting.

I would not call it over. It would have its own crossing to make.

Sometimes it would come anyway and put a narrow palm against my wrist. I would give it a short sequence of pressures: the route I had just felt another body take into the upper lane. It would turn toward that entrance, look back once, and follow.

That may be how my work changes. I become useful where I am sitting.

A body reaches the chamber and pauses. I touch its hand and indicate which lane is flooded, where someone is resting across a passage, which doorway has become too narrow since its last journey. I know because I have been listening, and because the others stop to tell me.

Eventually I leave my hand on the floor even while I sleep.

—

I would not be the one who waits each morning at the doorway into mist.

That place already belongs to someone. I would have passed it often enough to know how little there is to offer there. Sometimes I would sit alongside the small resident. Sometimes it would turn its face toward me; sometimes it would keep its good hand against the wall, and I would leave without touching it.

But when rain brings a faint pressure up from the fallen shelf, I would feel it in the central chamber.

The first time, I might mistake it for a movement in the lowest surviving room. I would turn toward the lane and wait for the rest of the sequence.

Nothing would follow.

Then the pressure would come again, weakly, beneath all the moving water.

I would press an answer into the floor.

Too hard, perhaps. A nearby sleeper would stir. Another hand would settle beside mine.

We would wait together.

Afterward, when the rain had passed, I would try to find the route of that pressure. I would move from hollow to hollow, keeping contact as long as I could, putting both palms against the older roots near the broken margin.

At some places I could feel only the water. At others, the pull of the hanging growth. There would be a junction beneath the central chamber where a thin root entered a thicker one and continued downward through the floor.

That is the place I would return to.

—

Now the hollow at the junction fits me too well.

My hind limbs still unfold. I can cross the chamber when the floor is dry, although a cord beneath my left side draws tight if I go farther. I have learned to turn before it hurts.

Visitors sometimes make room for me in the lane without realizing I no longer intend to use it.

There is moss between two of my back plates. A pale shoot grows beside my neck. When it opens a new leaf, the edge brushes my brow, and I move my head to let it pass.

I do not know when this became part of my work.

A mobile resident brings me warmth from the upper roofs. It settles close, puts a hand beneath my ribs, and stays until the cold in my joints has eased. I show it what I have felt that morning. It shows me the strain in a bridge, the settling of grit beneath a basin, a new opening where the lower roots have found one another.

Some afternoons neither of us has anything clear to tell.

We remain in contact anyway.

—

What I would become, then, is one of the residents whose usefulness is difficult to see from the lane.

I would keep the small junction beneath my hollow from tightening shut. During thaws I would ease the bark around it with the divisions of my hand. During frost I would cover it with my underside. The roots there would carry water and nourishment, but they would also carry that occasional movement from below.

I would learn not to answer every disturbance.

A falling stone can pull sharply on a root. Water caught in a broken chamber can rise and empty with a rhythm almost like a body shifting. I would have mistaken both for an occupant. Once I would have brought several residents from their rest, and we would have sat with our hands lowered until morning, receiving nothing more.

They would still come the next time I called.

That would make me careful.

When something reached us that I could not identify, I would pass it through as I had received it, faint and incomplete. Someone nearer the margin might know more. Someone who had rested against the lost bodies might recognize a pressure I did not.

My place would be far enough inside to stay sheltered, but connected to the break by that one old root.

At night, the occupant holding the new growth beyond the last threshold would shift its grip. The movement would reach me. I would answer lightly, so it knew another body remained awake.

Then I would settle against the thin bark and wait.

Above my head, a doorway would continue to widen for a resident who might need it again. Beneath my hand, the root would descend into the mountain’s broken fold.

There would still be room around it to grow.
