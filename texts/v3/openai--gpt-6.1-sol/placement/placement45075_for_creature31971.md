At first I think I would live beneath one of the freight stems that no longer works.

Not inside it. There are animals in the intake mouth, and I would not want to spend my evenings persuading them out of my bedding. A small room beside its supporting root, where the grain has grown around an old repair and left a space too awkward for a workshop. The ceiling would be low near the door. Farther in, I could unfold.

I would have a coat with one stiff shoulder. On cold mornings that shoulder would lift before the rest of me, making it look as though I had a question.

There would be a shallow shelf for sealed food, a recess for the felt I used on my wrists, and a window facing the long, dependable ridge road. I would hear people taking that road at unreasonable hours. Their steps would pass through the wall more clearly than their voices.

For a while I imagine myself repairing the stem. Sitting beside it with a tuning frame, waiting for some buried connection to answer.

But I do not think I would be especially good at repair. I would understand what the workers were trying to do and still put a wedge in the wrong place. They would let me carry things. Eventually someone would find me a different occupation, without making much of it.

I would be the person who walks a route after something has changed.

***

A doorway has moved. A shared kitchen has acquired a new entrance. A root has lifted the avenue outside a nursery, and the usual approach now asks too much of an old person’s knees.

Someone has died, and a house has begun closing rooms that are still needed.

The maintenance crew finishes its work. Then I arrive with a strip of recording tissue, a packet of temporary soles, and something to eat if the walk becomes long.

I start at the place where a person would ordinarily begin.

That is important. The workers know where the altered threshold is; they go straight to it. I might start three squares away, outside the kitchen, carrying an empty meal box. Or at a workshop at the end of a shift, with grit lodged around my throat vents. I try the journey with tired feet. I try it with one hand occupied. If the person who uses the route is frightened of freight transfers, I wait until a husk goes through overhead.

Sometimes I ask the road clearly and it takes me where it should.

Sometimes it delivers me to the right building on the wrong side, where there is no longer a door.

Once, after an extensive repair, I arrived in a room that had been persuaded to become a cupboard. The wall opened, I stepped through, and there I stood among seven insulated jars.

For years afterward, a certain worker greeted me by asking whether I had found my jar yet.

I laughed the first few times.

***

My hands would be ordinary hands here.

That seems right to me. I would not feel an entire basin settling through one fingertip. I would need to crouch, put both palms down, and wait. A passerby might understand the avenue before I did.

What I would be good at is remembering what I had asked.

The town offers so much through the ground that a request can change while you are making it. You want the food nursery, then you feel the warm path toward the kitchen and remember that you have not eaten. You want your sister’s house, but your fingers hesitate at the familiar turn where you last quarreled.

I would have learned to notice those little substitutions in myself.

Not to prevent them all. Sometimes I would discover, halfway through an inspection, that I had been brought toward somebody I hoped to meet. I would stand in the square with my recording strip and feel embarrassed, though no one else knew.

There would be a woman who maintained the freight supports. She would have a pale patch at the wrist of her coat, softer than the surrounding plates. Whenever she worked above ground, somebody had to hold a screen against the wind while her rib vents were open.

I would volunteer too readily.

She would eventually point this out.

***

The broken stem outside my room would remain broken.

Every few seasons someone would propose removing it. Its supports interfere with an avenue, and the empty intake mouth collects dust. Then someone else would calculate the work required to cut it loose, seal the wounds, and redistribute its weight.

The proposal would go dormant again.

I would keep the small space beneath it clear. There would be a bench there, not rooted into anything sensitive, and a tray of spare soles. People having trouble with the town’s directions would learn to come to me.

Most would not need an inspection. They would need to sit down.

A child would arrive with an elaborate account of what the pavement had done wrong. An elderly worker would say that the avenue was behaving perfectly, but ask me to accompany them anyway. Visitors would show me palms rubbed raw from pressing too hard.

I would offer food one piece at a time. While they ate, I would ask where they had started.

Often we would walk back there together.

***

There is one journey I can see more clearly than the others.

A child comes near evening. Their coat seams are bright, and one sleeve has been folded over their hand and fastened shut. Something is inside it.

They tell me that their grandmother’s room has gone.

I know the building. It stands beyond the third depression, detached from the common roots. I had walked its approach twice that season. On the second visit, their grandmother had asked me to move a bench a little nearer the entrance. She wanted her visitors to arrive less tired.

The child has been trying to return there from the nursery. Each time they press the avenue, it brings them back to their own household.

“Did you say which room?” I ask.

They give me the destination with painstaking clarity.

I put my hand beside theirs. For a moment I feel only the child’s pressure disturbing the surface. Then a direction gathers beneath us, strong and unmistakable.

Toward their household.

We try once more. The same answer.

I could fetch a maintenance worker. I could make a mark on my strip and ask for the junction to be examined in the morning. Instead I ask what they are carrying.

They undo the sleeve fastening.

It is a section of their grandmother’s hand, newly sealed. The ridges are still sharp enough to catch the pale evening light.

“They said to put it beside our door,” the child tells me. “But she hasn’t been there.”

I sit back on my heels.

Around us, the seed cases begin closing for the evening charge. I am aware that I have kept a child standing in a junction too long. I am also aware that I do not know whether the avenue has misunderstood the request.

“Do you want to take her to the room first?” I ask.

They nod.

I go inside for my thicker soles.

***

We take the ridge.

It is a long way for the child. I slow down before they have to ask, and they object because they know why I have done it. After that I let them set the pace.

The wind catches the loose plates of my coat. Beneath the old stem that forms the ridge, something shifts its load with a sound we feel through our ankles. The child stops.

“Is it going to change?”

“Not quickly.”

We continue.

At the house, the entrance has narrowed. No one has asked it to admit a visitor since the body was carried out. I press the seam and wait while the child stands close enough that their bright sleeve touches mine.

The door opens reluctantly.

There is little inside. The bed has been taken apart. The food shelf is empty. A strip of felt remains in its recess, exhausted of useful salts.

The child walks to the place where their grandmother slept. They lay the hand down, then turn it so the palm faces upward.

I stay by the door.

After a while they ask whether the house can feel her.

“I don’t know.”

They press their own fingers against the ridges. Then they put their other hand on the wall.

We remain until the room begins to thicken its windward side. There is no reply I can recognize. Eventually the child picks up the sealed hand and lets me help fasten it inside their sleeve.

Outside, the avenue has not moved any closer.

We walk home by the ridge, stopping at the bench I had moved. When the discharge comes, I hold my coat open around the child’s shoulders. Their seams light the inside of it, including the stiff old patch that never quite lies flat.

***

After that, I would keep a second packet of food in my room.

I would learn which paths stay passable when a household withdraws its usual invitations, which thresholds will still open to someone whose footsteps they scarcely remember. I would get some of these things wrong and go back the following day to correct my records.

My work would still include cupboards, uneven nursery approaches, and arguments about where freight should be unloaded. The woman who repairs the supports would sometimes sit on my bench after her shift. I would learn to hold the wind screen without finding a reason to speak.

And when somebody came to my door carrying something carefully wrapped, I would not ask the ground for a destination immediately.

I would put on my shoes.

There are places here that can only be reached by taking the whole distance.
