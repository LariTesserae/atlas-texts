At first I would think I was lying on the floor.

There would be a pale ledge beneath my mouth, and beyond it a passage I could recognize only when something crossed it. Silver fish would make it briefly high and wide. A grazer moving along one wall would make it narrow again. In the intervals, I would know the opening by the cold coming through.

I would try to put a hand against the ledge.

Several parts of my underside would lift instead.

The movement would disturb small animals tucked between them. I would feel their departures separately: a quick unfastening, a brush against a ridge, then the tiny pressure of one creature that had moved deeper into a fold rather than leave. Some would return before I had finished lifting.

I would lower myself carefully.

For a while, that would be enough to learn.

My back would be dark and broad, but not yet broad enough to fill the hollow where I rested. Along one side, the floor would rise beyond the reach of my fins. Along the other, a supporting ridge would press against a part of me that did not want support. I would shift away from it, then slide back when the current changed.

The hollow had held someone larger.

I would know this without seeing its outline. Beneath me, the wood would be smooth in places I could not touch, and there would be a shallow groove beyond my mouth. Water would run through the unused space with a fine, persistent vibration.

I would spend much of my time elsewhere.

There would be a low arch nearby, its inner curve lit by friction. I would rest beneath it with two limbs around a buttress. This would be a better fit, but a poorer place to listen. The wood there would gather the sound of the overflow so strongly that distant calls arrived with their lower notes rubbed away.

I would feed along the edge of a terrace where the nap grew thick. Small fish would come with me. At first I would feel them as interruptions: a touch beside the mouth, a flicker against an eye, bodies passing between my underside and the crop. After many circuits I would begin lifting a fin before they reached it.

One fish would wait farther ahead than the others.

It would have a light beneath its jaw. When I stopped feeding, that light would stop too. When I turned back, it would hesitate, as if there might still be something worth disturbing farther along.

Then one day another fish would occupy that distance.

I would not know how much time had passed.

My eyes would give me very little of the basin. Even their amber light would seem to belong chiefly to the creatures just in front of them. I would learn the greater place through my belly: the interrupted scrape of a grazing company, the blunt settling of a jaw against wood, a shoal passing over a terrace and briefly changing the water’s weight.

There would be one call I listened for more closely than the rest.

It would reach me through the old hollow.

Three notes, the last one held until it entered the continuous sound beneath everything. Sometimes it would come while I was away, and I would receive only what the arch allowed through. Sometimes I would already be lying in the hollow, with every sensing branch spread against the floor.

The call would not be frequent. Much could happen between one arrival and the next. The nap could recover from my feeding. The waiting fish could change. I could leave for a distant court and return with new creatures in my folds.

Still, when it came, I would turn toward it.

My body would be too small to take the position easily. One fin would rest on the ridge that pressed me badly. Another would extend into open water. I would remain that way until the last vibration had passed.

Eventually I would answer.

I would lift a small patch of my underside and bring it down. The note would enter the wood unevenly, with a second, lesser sound where I had struck the edge of the hollow.

Small grazers nearby would pause.

Nothing else would seem to change.

Much later, the three notes would return.

I would answer again.

For a long time I would use the same clumsy place beneath my belly. Then the wood would begin to support me differently. A shallow rise would grow under the ridge I lifted, allowing it to fall cleanly. The unwanted pressure along my side would ease. The cold thread of water beneath me would divide and pass around parts of my body it had once passed under.

I would stay longer.

The distant animal would move. Its call would arrive through another terrace, slightly altered by the intervening arches. I would turn, and after many returns the hollow would change with that turning.

I might never see it.

I would know when it lay against thick wood, when it rested somewhere loosely, when its body did not come down quite evenly. On some arrivals there would be a faint additional vibration. I would listen to that for years before recognizing the young beneath its fins.

Perhaps it would hear such things in me.

There would be times when I carried young, and the hollow would cease to suit us. Their mouths would move against the pockets of my body. Later, on my back, they would pull at the algae and slip into the water whenever I lifted.

I would take them through the low arch and into brighter passages. I would rest where currents met, so they could leave in several directions.

My call would be small there, made against wood I did not know well.

Most would return.

One would come back with its clear sheath full of green light from something it had eaten. Another would cling so far behind my eyes that I could locate it only by the drag of its tail. A third would depart along the underside of a ledge and not come back before I had to move.

I would return to that ledge often.

Afterward, I would go again to the hollow.

I think I would be an animal that returns too much: not the oldest, not the largest, but one whose wandering has acquired a center.

The outer bank would draw me occasionally. I would feel its close-spaced calls long before reaching the thick straps of growth. Near the crest, the floor would speak so differently that I would have to fold my sensing organs and open them again, learning how to bear against it.

I would lie beneath the overflow.

Small lights would pass above my eyes and vanish over the edge. The wood on the outer side would carry touches whose sources I could not place. I would reach toward them with a limb, finding a smooth depression beyond the crest, then draw the limb back.

I would remain for a while with the water pressing over me.

Then I would turn into the basin, moving slowly until there was floor enough beneath my whole body.

At home, the distant call might already have arrived.

Or it might arrive long after I settled.

Once, it would fail to come.

I would continue feeding, continue making my circuit, continue returning. Other sounds would occupy the route it had taken. I would press my note into the wood and listen as it traveled away.

The hollow would fit me closely by then. My limbs would reach its supports without stretching. Little water would move beneath me.

For years I might remain turned toward the same place.

Then, during an ordinary interval between grazing sounds, I would feel a small, uneven knock.

It would come from that direction, but not from the same distance. It would have an accidental second note, as though a narrow body had come down against the edge of a hollow.

I would lift a patch of my belly.

Before answering, I would wait for the small creatures beneath it to move clear.
