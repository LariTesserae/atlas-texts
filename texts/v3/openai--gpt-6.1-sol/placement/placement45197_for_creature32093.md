At first I would choose one of the abandoned shelters.

Not the lowest, with its doorway already half underwater. One on a long rise, still sound enough to sleep in, with a roof that drips in only two places. I would like the high recess where a previous occupant kept something dry. There would be a worn patch beneath it, made by people reaching up with one hand while holding a lamp in the other.

I would put my own cloth there.

But I would have chosen badly. The shelter would be empty for a reason. Its nearest seep would still wet the rock, and there might even be a violet stain around it, but the stain would not be enough. I would bring animals to it and watch them travel to the edges of the basin. I would deepen the basin. I would scrape a passage from another pool. For a few days, after rain, I would think I had succeeded.

Someone from the next inhabited rise would come over carrying a scraper I had asked to borrow.

They would look at the animals, then at the channel I had made.

*Leave it.*

I would show them the new growth beside the opening.

*Leave it, I have already tried.*

So I would not live alone in an abandoned shelter, although I might keep returning to that one longer than was useful. I would end up sleeping on the narrow ledge in somebody else’s house, with my feet tucked under a projection in the wall. To get up, I would have to draw my knees toward my chest first. For several weeks I would strike my shin every morning.

My place would be among a cluster of four or five households, where a broad ridge divided the nursery yards from deeper water. I would know the routes between those houses very well. Beyond them I would be slow.

I would not have an important title. People would come for me when they could not remember how a pool had been arranged before the rain.

This would begin with an unremarkable habit. Whenever we moved a regulating stone, I would scratch its former position on a piece of slate. At first the others would tolerate this as something that occupied my hands while they did the difficult work. Then, after a storm, we would spend half a day searching for a flat stone that had slipped beneath a ridge. My mark would show that we were searching on the wrong side.

After that, someone would occasionally say, “Bring your slate.”

I would learn what was worth putting on it.

The depth of a basin, yes, but also where the depth was taken. The width of a channel, but also which piece of shell had been used to measure it. A drawing of a stone would be useless unless it showed the chipped corner by which a person could recognize it underwater.

I would become good at drawing things as a hand would find them.

There would be one heavy regulating slab with a shallow hollow on its underside. We could not see the hollow without lifting the slab, which took three people, but we could reach it from the west with two fingers. On my slate it would look like a mouth. A child would point this out, and afterward that would become its name.

I would still make mistakes. Once I would reverse a drawing and help open the wrong channel. Bitter water would enter a nursery, and we would have to carry the young animals out in every available dish, including the one in which our food had just been served.

For years afterward, if I turned a slate toward someone, they would turn it back toward me before accepting it.

***

I would keep a few grazers of my own.

Not many. There would be three shallow basins close to the shelter, entrusted to me because they required frequent attention rather than great strength. In one I would keep the ugly little nursery cleaners. Their harsh calls would be so familiar that I would stop hearing them until one sounded wrong.

I would have a favorite, although I would deny it if asked. Its shell would be narrower on one side, making it turn slightly whenever it moved. I would repeatedly place it at the upper end of the basin, and it would repeatedly work its way down to a seam near the outlet.

Eventually I would test the film there and find that it was better feeding than the film I had been providing.

I would move the outlet.

There would also be one large caller beyond the yard, in a pool no household quite claimed. I would use its note to judge the water at night. In shallow water it would have a rough beginning; when the pool deepened, the roughness would disappear beneath a long, full sound that I could feel through my shoulder against the wall.

I would recognize it for years before seeing the whole animal.

The first time it moved while I was nearby, I would step backward onto smooth stone and fall. There would be no dignity in this. I would sit in the water, holding my wrist above the surface because the slate cord had tightened around it, while the grazer slowly uncovered a pale patch of rock.

Afterward I would have a better mark for its position.

***

My most useful possession would be a bone point with a handle thick enough to hold when my fingers were cold.

It would not look valuable. The point would be worn short, and a split in the handle would have been bound with hide. I would use it to clear the grooves of slates, to lift biting swimmers from a grazer’s folds, and to pick grit from the fastening of a cuff.

Then someone who cleaned wounds would see me using it on an animal and forbid me to bring it near a person’s skin.

I would acquire another point. I would keep the two apart with excessive care, having learned how easily “only this once” could become a whole swollen hand.

I think I would be useful in these small ways, and sometimes tiresome. I would ask which pool an observation came from when everyone wanted to eat. I would remember an argument longer than the people who had made peace. I would have to learn not to produce an old slate merely because it settled a question.

Some marks would be washed off deliberately.

Others I would cut deeper.

***

There would be an old keeper whose hands had begun to stiffen. They would still read a pool better than I could, but fastening their sleeves would take time, and lifting a wet basin would hurt.

I would go with them in the mornings.

They would show me the thin growth beneath a shell lip, or the way swimmers collected on one side of a seep. Often I would see nothing at first. They would wait while I changed position, shaded the water with my body, or lowered a dark slate behind a nearly transparent animal.

On one cold day they would fail to fasten a cuff.

I would make the sign for going indoors.

They would answer that there were two more pools.

I would take the basin from them. They would be angry enough to speak aloud, though neither of us could hear the words clearly.

Later, beside the covered lamp, they would ask whether I had checked the second pool.

I would have to admit that I had not.

We would sit warming our hands, both dissatisfied.

***

In time, I would go back to the abandoned shelter where I had first intended to live. Not every week. Perhaps once after the heavy seasonal rains, carrying a small basin and a scraper.

I would uncover the seep, leave two nursery cleaners in a shallow hollow, and return several days later. Usually they would have moved away from it. I would collect them and go home.

Once there might be a good season. Enough growth to keep six animals, then twelve. I would bring someone else to look before carrying any bedding there.

By then I would know the difference between wanting the shelter and knowing how to live in it.

Most evenings I would still be on the narrow ledge in the inhabited house. Someone’s damp sleeve would hang too close to my face. A child would have borrowed my bone point and returned it to the wrong recess. My slates would be stacked beneath a stone to keep them from knocking together during the calls.

When the deep note came through the wall, I would lay my hand flat against it.

If the water was rising, I would reach down and lift the lowest pair of shoes.

Then I would lie still long enough to hear whether anyone else was awake.
