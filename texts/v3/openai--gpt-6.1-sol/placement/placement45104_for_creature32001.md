At first I would be found halfway down the stairs, where the two flights fail to meet.

There is a small platform there, added after the handrail was installed. To cross it, a resident must let go, take one step without support, and find the next rail behind a pipe. I would be standing beside that step with one hand extended.

Not everyone would take it. A few would touch my wrist instead, avoiding the worn contact in my palm. One would tell me, each time, that the arrangement was temporary.

I would have a room elsewhere. I would not have been assigned to the stairs. But from the platform I could see when the service lift blocked the lower passage, and hear a person arrive at the turn before they came into view. I would have started by waiting there for someone. After a while, others would have found me useful.

My body would be narrow enough to stand aside without making the crossing worse. Ceramic over the chest and shoulders; flexible copper-colored material at the waist, where the plates had proved troublesome on steep stairs. One knee would bend in an extra place. The other would not.

There would be a shallow recess across my back, fitted with two latches and no cover. Occasionally someone would offer to make one. I would say I was still deciding what to keep there.

For a long time, I might believe that recess was meant for something important.

I would remember the pressure of an object being secured in it, and a voice saying, *Keep this level.* No object, no speaker. When carrying a tray, I would find myself obeying.

My room would be in the second row, under a duct that made its ceiling lower on one side. There would be a bed, a sink, and a shelf installed at a height inconvenient to everyone but me. The door would open fully only when my neighbor’s door was shut.

We would have an understanding about that. They would tap twice before coming out. I would keep my door against my heel until they had passed.

On the inside of my door I would keep pale ceramic strips recording routes.

Not a map. Maps would already exist: plans clipped beside cabinets, amended diagrams, numbers resolving into other numbers. Mine would say things like:

*Third stair warm enough to soften a repaired sole.*

*Passage behind tank clear, but not for someone carrying a hand in a bowl.*

*Upper landing: wait until the lift has finished moving before answering a quiet voice.*

Some entries would be crossed out. Some would remain because I could not yet establish whether they were wrong.

A resident would come to borrow one, then return several days later to tell me that the route had been open but they had not wanted to use it.

“Why?”

“They were there.”

I would widen one shutter, waiting for a name.

They would give none.

After that, I would have difficulty deciding how much to record. A useful strip could also tell someone where to find a person who did not wish to be found. I would begin keeping some information only until the person who needed it had passed through.

This would leave gaps. I would dislike the gaps more than I expected.

I think I would be good at carrying things, though not especially strong. A removed joint wrapped in cloth. A tray with grit piled too close to its edge. A ceramic rack whose owner walked beside me holding one end even after asking for help.

My hands would have small retaining ridges on the fingers. They might once have held instruments. They would be good for catching the lip of a strip, less good for letting it go. Occasionally I would place an object on a table and find that one finger remained hooked beneath it.

Someone would wait while I disengaged it. Someone else would reach over and do it for me.

I would prefer the first, except on days when the heat had made my hands uncertain.

On those days I would go to the unfinished room earlier than the others. I would sit near the cable reel, outside its path, with my palms turned upward so they touched nothing warm. The cutting head would hang close enough to see the scratches along its teeth.

I would listen to the proposals for the room.

At first I would support keeping it open. An open room could be reached from either passage; no one would have to ask admission. Then the resident who wanted a door would explain, very quietly, that they had never rested anywhere without a duct or a pipe belonging to someone else.

I would look at the capped connection in the wall.

For several evenings I would have nothing useful to add.

Then I would bring a strip on which I had recorded how long each of us stayed, and where we sat.

The resident interested in limits would examine it.

“You missed the morning.”

“I wasn’t here.”

They would hand it back.

I would have hoped, without quite recognizing it, that the marks might settle the question. Instead, they would give us something else to discuss. Someone would object to being counted. Someone would ask why a vacant stool appeared in the record. I would explain that a visitor had left it there and intended to return.

The visitor would not return that evening.

I would keep their place for another evening, then move the stool against the wall.

There would also be things I did without recording them.

Behind my room, where a tank support passed close to the excavation face, I would keep a shallow dish of stone fragments. Not the neat pieces available beside the cutting machine. Flakes caught behind the retaining mesh, released when someone loosened it for inspection.

They would have a reddish layer along one edge. I would arrange them with those edges touching, trying to recover the line through the stone.

It would never fit properly. Some fragments would turn out to belong to a different depth. Others would crumble under my fingers.

The resident who brought back descriptions of trees would visit and watch me working.

“Roots go through stone sometimes,” they would say.

I would ask how thick a root could become.

They would show me with their hands.

I would look at the gap, then at the little dish.

Later, I might go up with them. Not immediately. I would first have to arrange for someone else to help at the mismatched stairs, though no one had asked me to remain there.

That would be harder than finding the route.

I would leave my records in my room. I would carry only one blank strip, fitted into the recess across my back with a piece of insulating cloth to stop it rattling.

At the upper landing I would pause beside the resident sitting with their knees drawn up. Perhaps they would ask where I was going.

“To look at something.”

They might make room without getting up.

Coming home, I would take longer. The stairs would be no better aligned, and I would have to turn carefully with the small branch I had been given.

I would bring it to the unfinished room first. The teller could put both hands down while we looked at it.
