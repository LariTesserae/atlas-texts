I would find myself a little below a spring, where its overflow disappears.

Not at the black rim where the animals crowd, but farther down, among stones that look dry. There is a hollow on the uphill side of one slab. The wind crosses it without entering. A thread of water runs under its floor, too deep to hear, and keeps the earth from freezing quite as hard as the earth a few feet away.

For a while, that would be enough to know about the place.

There would be no view from inside the hollow. A portion of sky, sometimes. The legs of passing animals. Gravel moving under a foot and then settling. On a still day, the sound of a tongue being drawn out of a crack.

I might at first seem to be something that lived beneath the slab: a pale body pressed into its underside, withdrawing when the light reached me. Or something in the water, moving between mineral grains. There is so much country overhead that it would be easy to mistake smallness for the only way of belonging here.

But I think I would need to leave.

Not often. Not far at first. I would need a body that could carry its warmth out of the hollow, and enough memory to find the hollow again when it had become indistinguishable from every other patch of gravel.

The memory would not be a picture.

It would be a way of placing a foot, followed by another. A change in the slope felt through the shoulder. The rough edge of a stone against the lower lip. It would survive snow, darkness, and the rearrangement of a few loose pieces after thaw.

I would be one of the pale grazers. An older female, without a calf that season.

There would be nothing conspicuous about me from the ridge. Dust in the divided ends of my coat. Frost along my back. A darker patch at one shoulder where the outer hair had worn away. When I lay down, that patch would turn toward the ground.

My left forefoot would not open quite as widely as the right.

It would have healed badly after a winter crossing, though I would have no recollection of the injury as an event with a beginning and an end. Only certain kinds of footing would still require care. A thin shelf of ice over running water. Gravel that slid before it bore weight. The downward step from a slab when the landing could not be tested first.

I would avoid those places when I could. When I could not, I would stop.

The animals behind me would stop too.

I would not know how many were waiting.

***

The hollow below the spring would be one of several places I used, but the one I returned to most often.

Lying there, I could tuck the stiff foot under the warmer part of my body. The slab would shield my muzzle. Two other females would commonly rest nearby, one farther down the slope and one on the open ground above. We would not always arrive together.

Sometimes I would wake to the breath of the lower female beside my face.

Sometimes her place would remain empty for days.

I would know each of them by things that did not carry far: the smell of the mouth after feeding, the pressure of a cheek beneath my jaw, a low sound passing through the ground while we rested close together. Across a basin, they would look no different from the other pale bodies. I could walk within a few lengths of one without recognizing her if the wind were wrong.

Then she would breathe toward me, and I would stop.

There would be no need to account for the time apart.

At the spring, I would usually wait below the crowded rim. Not out of deference. The ground there would be easier on my foot, and I would know that the animals above must eventually finish.

The water would taste different before it looked different.

One morning there would be less of the dark mineral taste and more of something thin and unfamiliar. I would hold my mouth at the outlet, withdraw, and try again. The small creatures behind my lip would move as the skin stretched. One would work its way toward the corner of my mouth, and I would scrape that side of my jaw against the stone.

I would leave the others.

They would be part of the ordinary feeling of drinking: their weight when swollen, the faint irritation as they climbed back into the fold. I would carry them without knowing where they had begun or where they would fall away.

When the water changed, I would search uphill.

A wet stone would not always mean a useful seep. Sometimes I would lick several and find nothing worth staying for. Sometimes the gravel would be dark beneath the surface, and my muzzle would come away cold.

On one such search, I would find a narrow opening beside a slab I had passed for years.

I would steady my lips against it. My tongue would enter only a little way before meeting the warm film.

Then I would remain there long enough for the female following me to press her nose against my flank.

Afterward, she would use the opening too.

***

There would be an older fracture farther from the spring, across a low rise.

I would still go to it.

The route would pass a cushion of leaves and a shallow stone ring, then turn across the slope. Near the end, I would shorten my steps. There would once have been room to set my mouth between two pieces of shale and draw out roots that resisted steadily, without breaking.

Now the lower piece would have shifted.

I would nose loose gravel away. I would try the seam from one side, then the other. The projections along my muzzle would find the familiar edges, but the tongue would no longer pass between them.

Perhaps a root would remain inside. Perhaps water had stopped reaching it several summers earlier.

I would have no way to know.

I would lie down nearby.

This would not happen every time. Some days I would make one attempt and leave. On others, I would remain until the light changed, digestion continuing, my chin resting near the closed opening. There would be no sharp boundary between waiting and resting.

Eventually I would get up.

That part of the route would stay in me long after it ceased to feed me.

***

I would not always be the animal with its head raised.

After feeding, I would sometimes settle so deeply that frost gathered along the hairs above my nostrils. The movements around me would reach me without becoming anything I needed to answer. A hoof placed nearby. A body lowering itself. The slight shift of gravel beneath another animal’s weight.

At other times I would remain awake while the two females slept.

I would watch the spaces through which something could approach.

A dark animal lying on a ridge would be difficult to see until it moved. Even then, I might know only that one piece of the ridge had changed its position. I would lift my muzzle. The upper female might lift hers.

We would stay down if it stayed far away.

There would be afternoons when I was hungry and still did not go to the spring.

***

Later in the season, a calf would begin resting near my hollow.

It would belong to the female on the open ground above. Small enough to pass beneath her neck, still dark over most of its body. It would investigate the worn patch on my shoulder with its nose and step back when I moved.

I would not draw it close. But I would grow accustomed to where it lay.

If it slept against the sheltered edge of the slab, I would place my stiff foot farther out.

As the routes opened, we would leave that spring district. The calf would travel between us, sometimes beside its mother, sometimes behind me. At a crossing, it would stop on the last dry stone.

I would be standing just beyond it, in shallow water.

Behind the calf, its mother would wait. Farther back, other animals would be approaching along the narrow passage, though I could see only the first few.

The calf would lower its head. Touch the rim with its nose. Lift one foot and put it down in the same place.

I would shift my weight off the stiff forefoot.

Water would move around the three lobes of the other hoof. Beneath it, the gravel would hold.

We would stay there a while.

Then the calf would step toward me, close enough that its wet muzzle touched the dark underside of my coat.
