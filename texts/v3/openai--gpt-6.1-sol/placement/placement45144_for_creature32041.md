I would be in the glove, but not in the finger where the moss grows.

I would be one finger farther over, where the cloth is damp only underneath. The upper surface has hardened into a shallow trough. Nothing much enters it except dust. Below, between the lining and the outer fabric, there is still a passage just wide enough for a body to turn.

That is where I would turn.

At first you might take me for a loose thread. I am pale, and I lie along the weave. But a thread does not shorten when a shadow crosses it. A thread does not gather its length beneath a seam and leave two small feelers outside.

My back is narrow and soft, divided into segments that overlap only when I contract. Along each side are short legs, almost invisible against the cloth. They do not carry me far above a surface. My belly touches whatever I cross, and often that is how I know whether to continue.

The glove is safe in patches. A dark patch may be old oil and dry. A pale patch may hold water deep in the weave. I press myself against each before bringing the rest of my body forward.

There is a place near the fingertip where I can drink without putting my mouth into the open. Water collects behind a broken stitch. I push my head between its two fibers and wait. Sometimes I wait long enough that the stitch begins to whiten around me.

Then I have to pull free.

I am not unusually clean. Fine deposits gather on the hairs along my sides, beyond the reach of my mouth. For a while they are useful. They make me difficult to see. Later they join together, and I cannot bend properly.

By then I have become broader than the passage in which I live.

I rub myself through a gap between two stitches. The cloth holds the crust while I draw my body out of it. What remains is a pale, jointed scrap, with a few hairs caught inside. There are several of these behind me in the glove. The oldest has become part of the wall.

A mite shelters beneath it.

—

I do not go out at midday.

Even inside the glove, I know when all four openings are bright. The fabric warms. Moisture withdraws from the broken stitch. A small beetle that travels across the fingertip becomes visible through the weave, each foot briefly closing a hole.

I wait until the machinery starts again.

The first vibration tells me little. The next tells me which way to go. In one direction the threads tighten against my body; in the other they loosen. I follow the looseness toward the wrist of the glove.

There, a seam has split.

It is an inconvenient entrance. The edges are rising separately, and what was a narrow slit has become an opening high enough for a centipede’s head. I used to sleep close to it. Now I sleep farther down the finger, beyond a turn the centipede cannot make.

Outside the seam lies a flake of blue paint. One end rests on cloth, the other on a mineral ridge. Beneath it is a space that changes every day.

I feed there.

A softness grows on the underside of the paint, especially along the edge nearest the glove. I scrape it with my mouth, moving sideways until the clean strip is a little wider than my head. Sometimes the darker red animal is already there. Its claws make a dry sound against the flake. I wait against the fabric until it goes.

There is enough for both of us if we arrive at different times.

Once, we arrived together.

It put one raised foot against my head. I contracted so quickly that I lost my hold and dropped into the narrow space below the ridge. By the time I climbed out, it had gone. The underside of the paint was clean except at the farthest corner.

I could reach that corner. It could not.

Since then I enter from below.

—

My longest journey is to the swept tile.

I do not make it often. There are nearer places to drink, and between the glove and the tile is a slope with no useful cover. A folded leaf once bridged it. The leaf has since lifted on three white points, and the space beneath it is occupied by ants.

I go around.

The route passes the buried drain cover, follows a cable for a short distance, then turns into the impression left by something that has risen. I do not know what that thing was. Its underside made a shelter. Its departure left a shallow bed with a rim, and for now I can travel inside the rim without being seen from above.

Near the tile, I wait.

The brown inhabitant sweeps. The fiber passes over the surface in broad strokes, leaving dark arcs that slowly fade. Small bodies gather at their ends. I go to the place where the fiber changes direction, because more water is left there.

If the sweeping reaches me, I flatten.

The fiber bends over my back. For a moment I am held beneath it, all my legs caught in its dampness. Then it lifts, and I remain.

I drink until the next stroke.

The brown animal has stepped over me many times. Once it touched me with the end of a foot, not hard enough to move me. I stayed flat long after it had gone up the ladder.

There are fewer rungs now. I can feel the difference through the tile when it descends: a pause, a scrape, then its weight coming down more abruptly than before.

The next morning I find a strand of laundry fiber caught between the tile and the cable beside it.

I climb the strand partway, because it is wet.

I do not go all the way up.

—

During the local rain, I stay beneath the blue paint.

Drops strike its upper surface and make the whole flake jump. Water comes over the edges. The ground beneath me darkens, and the space fills with the smell of exposed iron.

Once the runoff enters from both ends.

I climb onto the underside of the paint and hang there. My crust is heavy. Several of my legs slip. I move toward the glove, but the flake has shifted, and there is now a bright gap between it and the cloth.

For a while I hang above that gap, touching the air with my forelegs.

Then a drop gathers on the cloth edge. It swells until it touches the paint. I push my head into it, find a fiber on the other side, and pull.

The water holds my back. I lengthen. One leg comes loose, then another.

When I reach the glove, part of my crust stays behind in the drop.

Afterward, I am able to turn more easily. I return to the broken stitch and sleep with my head pointing inward.

—

The blue flake will not remain a roof.

Already I have to climb to reach its underside. Already the clean corner is too dry. Something pale has begun growing on the narrow stalk beneath it, and I stop there to feed before returning home.

At evening, the red animal’s grain falls beyond the glove. I feel the impact through the cloth. Later, while crossing the wrist seam, I find it resting against a loop of thread.

I put my head beneath its edge.

There is almost nothing there yet. A little dampness. A taste unlike the paint.

I stay until the loop tightens under the grain’s weight. Then I withdraw. When the red animal comes down, I am back inside the finger.

By morning the grain has lifted clear of the thread.

And at the entrance to my passage, one of my discarded crusts has risen too. It stands on a thin white support, its empty segments curved as though it were still trying to squeeze between the stitches.

There is room beneath it now.

I test the space with my head, then draw myself through.
