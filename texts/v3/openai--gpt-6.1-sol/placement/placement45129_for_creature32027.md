At first I would put myself beside the shed.

There is enough warmth there to make a distinction between one hour and another. The door opens; a little water loosens beneath the step. A boot comes down on the metal, and something sheltered underneath receives the weight. I think I would want that small succession of events. In a place where evening scarcely changes the color of the ground, I would live where something occasionally arrives.

But the pad beneath the step already has its work. It fills and empties. It can remain there without knowing what presses it.

I would be farther out.

Perhaps under the abandoned platform, where the timber is still sound at its center and soft along its edges. The pump vibration travels through the grain. Water gathers beneath a bolt, follows its rusted shaft, and reaches the underside of the wood. There would be enough shelter for a thin body, enough movement to distinguish the board from the ground.

I might spend a winter there. I would grow around the bolt without reaching the iron itself, occupying the narrow sleeve of wet, altered wood around it. Above me, moss would cover the place where a person once put a foot.

When the wood split, I would lose part of that shelter.

What remained would draw down along the grain, then into the peat. I would become difficult to locate from above.

I think I would finally be near the crossing marked by the three short posts.

Not beneath any one of them. Between the second post and the trench, where an old route through the creature has been cut.

There are two living ends there.

One is packed into a layer of sedge, protected by the bank. It receives the engines clearly through the buried foot of the post. The other lies farther down, in a wetter seam. It receives the same motion late and softened, along with the small irregular pressures of water passing under ice.

The distance between them is less than the width of a hand.

For years it has been too far.

I would begin as a swelling on the lower end, scarcely different from the other swellings along it. Water would enter me and remain a little longer than elsewhere. Fine material would collect in my folds. I would thicken beside a broken stem.

I would not know there was another end above me. Its presence would have no shape I could perceive. Sometimes a disturbance would pass through the bank, and I would tighten against the stem. Sometimes that disturbance would come through the water instead, and the outer part of me would loosen.

My growth would take the direction in which loosening lasted longest.

A thaw would give me several millimeters.

Then cold would return. My tip would whiten and become stiff. The living part would withdraw, leaving the extended tissue in place. When water moved again, I would use some of it as a shelter. I would grow along my own dead edge.

This is where I would find myself: under the unsafe crossing, occupying a gap that a standing person could step over without noticing.

I would have no use for the whole distance across the basin. The root beside me, the broken stem, the wet face of the cut would be sufficient obstacles. Once, a grain of gravel would settle against my advancing fold. It would keep the fold open. Water would pass through too quickly, and for several days I would be unable to hold enough to extend.

Eventually a smaller branch would pass beneath it.

That branch would survive.

There would be more than one attempt. Some would reach upward and dry. One would enter a hollow in the peat and spread there, making a soft, blind pouch. A strand would cross the gap during a warm interval, attach to a loose fiber, and be pulled apart when the fiber shifted in the next freeze.

The torn length would stay on the far side.

For a while, something of me would persist there, supplied by water caught against the upper bank. It would swell when I swelled, though there would be no connection left between us. Later it would darken. I would continue below.

Then, during a stoppage of the pumps, water would rise into the gap.

The route I had repeatedly tried would become possible in several places at once. I would extend along the sedge fibers. The upper end would soften and extend downward. Between us, the broken strands would fill again.

The first contact would be slight. Two wet surfaces would rest against one another. They would remain separate through a night, then cease to pull apart when the water shifted.

Fluid would pass.

What reached me from above would be different from groundwater: warmer by almost nothing, carrying material from a part of the body that had lived against timber. I would swell unevenly. A fold that had faced the trench would turn inward. The blind pouch would empty.

When the pumps restarted, the pressure arriving through the post would enter me directly.

Below, the old seam would answer.

For the first time, both movements would pass through the same small piece of flesh.

I would be that piece. A short, thickened junction beneath the crossing, with one side fitted to the impressions of sedge stems and the other still pale from recent growth. The machinery would run through me in two slightly different ways. Water and dissolved food would pass from a crowded part of the creature into one that had been surviving on little.

The join would narrow when the pumps drew the level down. In hard weather it might close altogether. I would keep a little water in the pouch beside it, and open again when I could.

Above me, the three posts would lean at different angles. Frost would gather on their exposed ends. Someone inspecting the basin might stop near them, judge the crossing unsafe, and turn back.

Their weight would reach the upper strand, pass through me, and diminish in the lower seam.

Afterward I would slowly fill again.
