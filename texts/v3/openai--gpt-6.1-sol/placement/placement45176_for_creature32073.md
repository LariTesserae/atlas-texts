I would look first for a place where I could sit without requiring anyone else to move.

This would take longer than I expected.

The ledge beside the western stair is needed for setting down baskets halfway up. The space beneath the nursery lamps belongs to whoever is sorting greens, even when nobody is there. A bare patch beside a pillar turns out to be where a door opens. I would learn this by being struck, gently, in the knee.

Eventually someone would point out a folding seat outside the repair shop. It has to be raised when the floorboards come up. It is not anybody’s seat, which does not mean it is always available.

I think I would begin there.

At first I would be a person with a bag between my feet, listening too conspicuously. I would mistake the neighborhood’s quiet for a request to make no sound at all. When someone asked my name, I would answer so softly that they would have to ask again.

“You can speak,” they would say. “Just speak here.”

They would indicate the small distance between us.

***

The repairer would eventually ask me to hold something.

A lamp, perhaps. Then a bracket while he marked its holes. Then the end of a pipe that must not be allowed to turn.

I would be good at holding things still, though not immediately good at knowing what needed to remain still. Once I would brace a pipe against a root, and he would stop working.

“Not that one.”

There would be no visible difference between it and the root beside it.

He would make me take my hand away and watch while he tested the bracket with a finger. The root would move slightly. A window above us would click in its frame.

After that, I would ask before leaning.

I would earn a little money doing work that was difficult chiefly because there was no room to do it: replacing a screw behind a cupboard, filing the underside of a stair tread, passing a washer through an opening too narrow for the repairer’s hand. I would have slender hands. This is one of the first things I can know about myself here.

Not especially clever hands. I would drop things. For some months, every dropped screw would sound to me like an announcement of my arrival.

***

My room would be on the third terrace, behind the place where two balconies nearly meet.

It would have been part of a larger room once. The partition would stop short of the ceiling to let air pass, and on the other side someone would keep irregular hours. I would learn the sound of their chair before I learned their name.

There would be one plant already inside.

Its pot would be wedged between a water pipe and the wall. Three stems would grow upward; a fourth would enter the partition through a hole neatly edged with cloth. The previous tenant would have left instructions on the underside of a shelf.

*Don’t turn the pot.*

Nothing else.

I would want to turn it. One side would look neglected, and the soil would be easier to reach from the other. But I would leave it alone until I had asked enough people to discover that nobody knew why the instruction had been written.

Then I would still leave it alone.

This would not always be wisdom. Sometimes I would be frightened of making a change whose consequences belonged to someone else.

***

The work I would gradually take on would be making things that could be adjusted again.

Not new doors or whole staircases. Slotted brackets. Collars with an extra hole. Small wedges joined by a piece of string so that the unused one did not disappear. A shelf support that could be lowered without emptying every jar.

I would keep the removed pieces in shallow drawers.

People would begin coming to me when something no longer quite fitted.

A latch would catch in the morning but not in the evening. A drying rack would put pressure on a wall that had begun to show a pale line. Someone would bring a drawer that stuck only when it was full, insisting that it had always held the same amount.

Sometimes I would find an ordinary cause. A bent nail. Damp wood. Something fallen behind the runners.

I would be relieved by these discoveries. More relieved than the customer thought necessary.

Other times I would make a little more room and write down what I had changed.

I would not become the person who understood the hollow. There would be too many things I had not noticed, and people who had lived beside them for thirty years. But I could become someone who remembered that a particular screw had been moved twice already, or that a pipe should be loosened before the hot season rather than after it.

My records would be untidy. Sera would dislike the way I wrote sevens.

***

I would have a drawer for things I could not yet decide to discard.

The broken shoot would not be mine. Nor the boiler fitter’s samples. I would have smaller uncertainties: a washer with a white ridge across its rubber, a piece of plaster that had separated cleanly along a mineral line, a root collar removed from a root that was now narrower than when it had been fitted.

Some of these would eventually prove useless.

I would keep them too long.

Once, looking for an urgently needed hinge pin, I would have to empty the drawer onto my bed. My neighbor would help, and would hold up a fragment of tile.

“What are you saving this for?”

“I thought it might be useful.”

“For what?”

I would not have an answer. We would throw away most of the drawer’s contents that evening. I would retrieve one piece after she left, then feel foolish about concealing it.

***

I would go aboveground regularly.

I do not think I would become someone for whom the hollow was sufficient.

There would be days when the warmth of a railing irritated me before I had even touched it. Nights when the person beyond the partition turned their page at precisely the moment I was almost asleep. I would resent having to explain where I was going when all I wanted was to pass through someone’s kitchen to the stairs.

On the surface I would walk farther than I needed to. I would enjoy wind without trying to find a use for it.

Sometimes I would stay until the shops closed.

Then, coming down, I would stop on the uppermost landing and look for my room. I would not be able to see it. I could locate it only by the balcony above, the pipe beside it, and a pale leaf that had lately grown across the lane.

My bag would contain things people had asked me to bring back. Oil. Fine wire. A packet of needles. Something for myself that I had forgotten to buy.

I would go down while there was still a little light in the shafts.

***

Eventually I would take a turn at the unfinished room.

I would be the person outside the door, holding the notebook.

Inside, my companion would count the shoots. I would repeat each number before writing it, because the bellows’ pressure and the heat would make me unsure of what I had heard.

When they measured the longest shoot, the rule would touch the ceiling with a small hard sound.

I would look up from the page.

For a moment I would want very badly to enter. Not to investigate. Just to stand where they stood and see the green tips without the doorframe cutting off part of the ceiling.

Then they would ask whether I had recorded the measurement, and I would discover that I had not.

We would do it again.

Afterward, at the basin, Sera would ask whether there was water.

“No.”

“Door still shutting?”

“Barely.”

She would ask me to look at its lower hinge next week.

***

Years later, someone new would sit on the folding seat outside the repair shop.

I might ask them to hold a lamp. I would try to remember to tell them where to put their feet.

By then I would have changed the seat’s hinge twice. Beneath it, a root would have appeared through a joint in the stone. It would be pale, warm, and quite small. There would be a pencil mark beside it, followed by a date in my handwriting.

The new person would ask what it belonged to.

“I haven’t found the other end,” I would say.

Perhaps they would bend down to look. Perhaps, holding the lamp differently, they would show me something I had missed.

I would find myself making room beside them.
