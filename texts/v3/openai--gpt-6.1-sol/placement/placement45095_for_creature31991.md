I think I would first be found underneath something.

Not deep underground. There would be light within reach: a thin, crooked opening through which I could see a strip of pale stone, sometimes a foot passing, sometimes the underside of someone carrying a vessel. I would know the hour by where the light stopped, but I would have to go outside to see the sun.

The chamber would be too low for an upright visitor. For me it would be comfortable except in one place, where the ceiling had come down enough that I had to turn my head sideways to pass. Someone would have marked that place with three scratches. I would keep meaning to deepen them.

There would be a warm surface in the household, but it would usually be elsewhere. An infant would need it, or someone would be repairing a collector. When it came back, I would hold it between my forefeet for a little longer than necessary before putting it away.

I would not be particularly old. The transparent coverings of my eyes would have a few scratches that caught the evening light. One outer foot pad would be thicker than the other, the consequence of a crossing I had managed badly when young. On quiet ground this would scarcely matter. In certain currents I would have to remember to turn that foot.

I would have ordinary possessions: a drinking vessel, two useful ceramic wedges, a strip for sleeping on. A piece of polished inner bone small enough to fit behind my mouth’s hard scraping edge. I would have no story about the bone that I told readily.

At first I imagine myself tending the gray cushions. There is something appealing about work that can be carried in both hands: lifting a damaged colony, preparing a crack, arranging the chips around it. But I think I would be too easily distracted by what was passing through the stone beneath it.

I would stop with the plant still in my hands.

Someone nearby would have to touch my wrist.

Put it down first.

So perhaps my work would be in one of the surface shelters. Not the important one, where people descended to adjust the anchor. A smaller building, a little out of the way, whose doorway had ceased to face a sensible direction.

Its bowl would receive well and send badly.

I would be the person who went there most mornings.

***

The shelter would have a name that referred to a feature no longer visible. A shallow red fold, perhaps, worn away or covered by another plate. Young people would assume the name belonged to the building itself. An older relative would occasionally correct them, then lie in the doorway and describe the vanished fold at considerable length.

I would hang part of my weight from the hooks and put one forefoot in the bowl.

There would be long intervals with nothing to do.

I would remove grit from the threshold. Check the wall’s pull with a little suspended chip. Repair the insulating wrap around a vessel. Sometimes I would fall asleep with my digits against the ceramic and wake unsure whether a movement had woken me or entered a dream.

Most messages would be practical.

A crossing closed.

A collector’s lip had split.

Three travelers were delayed, but had water.

Someone wanted a warm surface sent back sooner than expected.

These messages would not arrive as intact little sentences. The plate would stretch one interval and shorten another. A pressure meant to be distinct might arrive spread across several digits. I would know the regular senders partly by their mistakes, partly by the way they compensated for a route’s peculiarities.

One person always pressed too hard at the beginning. Another repeated quantities, even when conditions were excellent. A child learning to send would hesitate in the same place each time, and the hesitation would gradually become part of how I recognized them.

I would sometimes get things wrong.

Once I would send someone toward a shelter that had no water to spare, because I had mistaken *bringing* for *holding*. They would return tired and angry. For several mornings afterward, I would ask for repetitions so often that another receiver would complain.

I would tell them why.

That would be part of my work too.

***

There would be one signal I could not place.

It would come through the heel of the bowl rather than the rim, most often near evening, when the great sheets began tightening against one another. Four pressures, a pause, then something faint and irregular.

The first time, I would think it was a damaged message.

After a season, I would think it was part of the anchor.

After several seasons, I would be less sure.

I would invite people to feel it. Some would hear an attempt at a name. Some would feel only the repeated loading of a buried fitting. One visitor would be certain it came from a settlement beyond the blister fields, and would give me directions so old that half their landmarks were gone.

I would try to answer.

The bowl’s poor sending would turn my efforts into something I could barely recognize under my own foot. I would adjust the pressure, move around the hollow, use both forefeet. Below me, the anchor would make a small change in the distribution of weight. A hook would tick against the wall.

For a moment the signal would stop.

Then it would resume.

I would not know whether that counted as a reply.

This is where I might become a keeper, though not of the anchor itself. I would start arranging my days around the signal. I would take my food to the shelter. I would postpone a visit because the evening conditions seemed promising.

My family would tolerate this for a while.

Then one of them would bring a leaking collector to the doorway and set it beneath my nose.

It would be a collector we actually needed.

I would go home and help repair it.

***

I do not think I would solve the distant signal.

That seems important to the person I am finding here. I would be capable of attending closely without being rewarded by certainty. But I would also be capable of confusing attention with usefulness.

Eventually the shelter would begin to fail.

Not dramatically. The wall’s pull would drift. Dust would gather where it had not gathered before. The bowl would become clearer for a few days, wonderfully clear, while a seam behind the building widened.

I would want the clarity to mean improvement.

An anchor worker would come, lower their body to the floor, and remain there a long time. Their digits would be dark in the folds.

Afterward they would tell me that the shelter had begun borrowing stability from a plate on which two households lived.

We could quiet it, or we could continue listening.

I would ask how long we had.

They would touch the floor again.

Not that question, they would say.

I would dislike them for answering correctly.

***

On the last working evening, I would have several people with me.

Not an assembly. There would be no formal farewell. Someone would come because they wanted the hooks, someone because they did not trust me to leave when I should. Two children would wait outside with the carrying strips and rearrange small stones behind each other’s backs.

We would listen in turns.

I would try once more to send a name: my own, repeated slowly, with the pauses widened enough to survive a poor route.

Then I would send the ordinary departure message.

This place will no longer receive.

There would be no way to know whether it had gone anywhere.

The worker would open a fitting beneath the bowl. I would help, holding a ceramic piece in position while they eased something loose. For a few breaths, weight would incline toward the wall. One of the children would laugh outside, startled by a pebble rolling uphill.

The four pressures would come again.

My forefoot would be occupied. I would feel them through my wrist, imperfectly.

Then the fitting would settle into its new position.

The hook above me would hang straight.

We would carry away what could be used.

***

After that, I think I would travel more.

I would go with families moving their colonies, with workers inspecting collectors, with someone bringing a warm surface home. I would learn how different a name could feel after crossing several plates. At shelters I would ask to hear the messages no one had understood, but I would also carry answers people were waiting for.

Sometimes a person would hold my forefoot and repeat a name until I could give it back accurately.

That would please me more than I expected.

I would still visit the old shelter when the route was safe. Its bowl would fill with dust. Without the anchor’s correction, the building would acquire a slight lean, scarcely visible from outside. I would put a foot in the hollow and feel the weather moving through the stone.

Once, perhaps, I would feel four pressures.

I would stay until evening.

Then I would go home.

If you arrived in this country, I might be the person lying across the seam from your doorway. Not because I had been appointed to meet you. I would have been passing with a message tucked into memory and a small vessel secured beneath my body.

I would notice that your belongings were too close to the edge.

While you slept, I would move them back.

In the morning I would have difficulty understanding why you kept looking at my raised foot rather than the place it indicated. I would repeat the movement. Then I would set the foot down, feel the plate once more, and choose a slightly different place.

You would be heavy. I would not know how quickly you could move.

Before asking you to cross, I would come close enough to touch your hand.

I would want you to feel the timing.
