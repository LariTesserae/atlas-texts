At first I would look for myself in the shallow joins: where a root presses against stone, where the dampness lasts a little longer than it does on either side. Not in the pool. Its surface would be too much like an opening, something I could touch without finding anything firm beneath it.

I would be close to one of the ledges, but not at its center. Beneath the outermost leaf, perhaps, where the growth thins and a little bare limestone remains.

There would be a ridge nearby. From the entrance it would be invisible. From where I lived it would interrupt the ceiling.

I think I would begin on the far side of that ridge.

Small enough that getting over it required several decisions.

***

For a while I would have no stone.

My forelimbs would close on grains that did not leave the ground. I would pull, adjust my feet, pull again. The grain would remain where it was. Eventually I would let go and lower my mouth to the dampness around it.

There would be food there, though not much.

Farther beneath the leaf, an adult would be scraping. I would know the scraping through my feet before I could see the animal clearly. Three little disturbances, a pause, then another. When I came close, its plate would fill most of the space ahead.

I would wait behind one of its hind feet.

Sometimes the foot would move, and I could put my mouth where it had been. Sometimes the animal would turn and I would retreat to the root. I would not yet know which slight changes in its posture meant that I could come nearer.

My own back would still be soft. When the leaf lowered, I would lower with it, without having learned to do so.

That is where I would first belong: not to the chamber as a whole, but to a space made by another animal’s leg, briefly available.

***

Later, I would find a grain I could lift.

It would not be especially good. One side would be smooth enough to hold, but the working corner would catch and then slip. I would carry it anyway.

The weight would change how I crossed the ridge. Without it, I could reach forward and test the slope. Holding it, I would have to put my chest against the stone and push with the rear pair of legs. Halfway over, I would stop.

The grain would rest between my forelimbs and the ridge.

Nothing would happen for some time.

Then I would shift one foot, lift the grain a little, and finish crossing.

On the other side would be a damp place I had fed from before. A root passed close to it without quite touching. The film there would collect in a shallow nick, beyond the reach of my mouth.

I would bring the corner down.

At first I would merely move the grain against the surface. Then it would catch correctly. A little of what lay in the nick would come loose.

I would put down my mouth.

Afterward I would try again, holding the grain at nearly the same angle.

I would not discover that I was an animal with a tool. I would find that this position of my limbs brought food closer.

For the rest of that morning, I would keep returning to it.

***

I would grow into the grazer that takes the longer route around the ledge.

The shorter route would cross a smooth hollow. Most mornings it would be dry, and other animals would go that way. But once, after rain, water would have collected there. I would have stepped into it before my forefeet found the change.

One leg would catch against another. The ground would disappear from my pads.

For a long while I would float with my back turned upward, touching air with the one part of me that could not walk.

I would come against a root.

It would take time to free the first foot. Once that foot held, the others would follow.

Afterward I would go around the hollow. Even when it was dry. Even when the longer way required climbing over the ridge.

There would be no visible path, only my repeated pauses at the same rough places.

***

The chamber would rarely arrive whole.

A root would be a direction. A leaf would be shelter and, at certain hours, an obstruction. The ceiling would press brightness into everything without needing to be looked at.

A snail descending nearby might first be a dimness that moved.

I would fold my forelimbs and wait. The dimness would widen, drift, then leave. Much later, walking past where it had landed, I might touch the folded edge of its fringe. It would withdraw, and I would draw back too.

Neither of us would go far.

Once a person entered, I would be holding a grain.

The ledge would tremble. The light would change across my eyes. Air unlike the air beneath the leaf would reach the joints of my legs.

I would press down.

The person might kneel so close that their breath moved the leaf above me. They might finally see the grain, then the tips holding it, then distinguish my body from the limestone.

By then I would have been still for a long time.

If they stayed, the moisture beside my mouth would begin to matter more than the disturbance. I would move one forelimb. Test the grain against the rock.

The person might think I had become accustomed to them.

I would have resumed feeding.

***

In the evening I would leave the grain near the clean patch.

There would be no reason to carry it beneath the leaf. It would have a useful corner in the morning, or it would not. Another animal might turn it over before I returned.

I would settle where the root widened against the limestone, placing four feet around the slight unevenness of the stone. My forelimbs would come together beneath my head.

Above, the last light would remain in a thick place in the ceiling.

Here it would remain longer in the damp edge of the root.

I would still be able to distinguish the ridge when the far wall had disappeared. Then the ridge too would cease to reach my eyes.

My feet would keep its position.

By morning, a bead of water would have formed overhead. Before it fell, moisture would spread along the root and touch one of my pads.

I would lift that foot.

Set it down nearer the wetness.

Begin there.
