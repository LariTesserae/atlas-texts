At first I would mistake waking for a change in the ground.

A pressure beneath the left side. Then a little less cold. The small things living above my spine would begin to move, and I would know this before I knew that I had a spine, or that the weight of the garden belonged to me.

One of the fans on my back would be holding frost. Another would have released it. Water would run between them into the old fur, stopping at a fold in my skin.

I would not rise yet.

There would be a great deal to feel.

Under the basin, a turning colony would have begun badly. Every revolution would carry the same hesitation: a pressure arriving, gathering, failing to pass cleanly into the next pressure. Farther away, through stone rather than roots, another colony would be turning without difficulty. Its warmth would not have reached the surface.

Between these two things, I would lie with my cords extended.

I think I would be an animal not yet old enough to stay, but old enough to take a long time leaving.

My six feet would be sound. The rear pair would carry scars from an exposed shelf crossed when I was young. On one shoulder there would be a cushion plant with a bare center, worn by a grazer that returned whenever I slept. I would tolerate it until its feeding reached the tender new growth. Then I would lift that shoulder, very slowly, and it would step down as though the ground had changed its mind.

There would be no name inside me.

There would be places I could recognize through my belly.

A ridge with a thin interruption in its iron bed. A hollow where warmth arrived from two directions and therefore could not be trusted to last. A stone beneath which the cold remained almost constant, good for sleeping through the long stillness. I would know a certain buried trunk by the faint current it carried and by the way that current diminished when the needles above it were frozen.

Some places would be familiar because another animal had known them.

Long before my garden thickened, I would have rested beside a broad body with three ribbed fans growing along its flank. Our cords would have met under the leaves. What passed between us would not have been a picture of a route. There would have been no ridge seen from above, no sequence of instructions.

Instead, a set of pressures would become familiar.

The narrow pulse before a sheltered crossing. The long absence after it. A warmth that had to be approached from below.

Later, travelling alone, I would encounter those differences in the ground and slow down. My feet would begin arranging themselves before I had reached the break in the vegetation.

That animal would be gone now. Or still somewhere, so covered with plants that I might have fed beside it without knowing.

I would carry its route across the basin.

***

When I finally stood, the first thing I uncovered would be a youngster.

Not mine. I would know that by its skin, though it had been beneath my flank long enough to acquire the damp smell of my fur. It would have pressed itself into the place where my sixth foot folded against the body. As the foot opened, the youngster would remain crouched in its hollow.

I would wait.

It would make its little searching clicks into the ground. Another youngster would answer from farther underneath me. I would discover that I had been sheltering two.

This would alter the journey.

The distant colony was warming beneath a shelf of bare stone. Alone, I could cross the shelf directly. With the young underneath me, I would follow the longer edge, where vegetation hung over shallow cracks.

My hunger would not become less. I would feed as I went, lifting the pale underside from a leaf without taking its upper skin. Sometimes a youngster would reach the same stem first. Its small mouth would hurry. Mine would stop until there was room.

At the stone, I would spread my front feet.

The young would gather beneath my chest, pale bodies touching. Wind would press the garden on my back flat against the fur. I would wait through several movements of the turning colony, holding my weight behind the open pads, until one youngster found the crack.

Then we would cross.

From the ridge, nothing much would have happened. A patch of frost would have moved along the margin of a basin.

***

I would have one place I returned to without a reason that could be felt in the stone.

A black pool, scarcely wider than my body.

The plants around it would be poor food. There would be no reliable warmth nearby. Nevertheless, after long journeys I would approach its margin and settle flat, my feet drawn close.

Before my head emerged, something pale would occupy the water.

I would wait for it.

There would be occasions when the shape seemed narrower than I was, occasions when it lacked the fans I carried. Once, after a long dormancy, it would have a white mark along one side. I would remain there until the cold reached the skin beneath my garden.

Then I would extend my head.

The mark would be frost lodged in the short fur of my cheek. Perhaps it had been there all along.

I would drink without disturbing the center.

The young would learn this from the weight of my foot, gently laid across them when they hurried. I would have learned it that way too. Whatever else the water held, there would also be the remembered pressure of an adult’s pad, and the cold plants against my belly, and the release that meant I could go forward.

***

Much later, I would settle beside the colony with the uneven turn.

Its hesitation would have deepened by then. Roots would have grown around the dragging surface. Some nights it would stop with its leaves half-open, leaving the warm margin exposed to frost.

I would find a position where my longest cords could reach both its outer roots and a damp seam beneath a loose stone.

There I would remain.

Plants would cross my feet. The grazers would wear small paths through my garden. A predator would take up residence beside my shoulder, and I would learn the slight inward movement it made before unfolding its limb. If it hunted too near my mouth, I would lift it away.

I would still be capable of leaving.

For a long time, that would matter.

Sometimes another animal would settle close enough to touch my cords. I would feel its hunger, the fine adjustments of its feet, the unfamiliar burdens growing in its fur. Beneath those things, the basin would continue its difficult turning.

I would answer through the silver skin of my belly.

A short pressure. A pause. A longer one, held until the other body settled fully against the ground.

Beyond the ridge there would be a shelf where warmth had not yet surfaced. The crossing would be sheltered along its lower edge. I would let those differences pass through me, as they had once passed through another animal.

Eventually the visitor would withdraw its cords.

I would feel it moving away for a while.

Then only the colony, dragging a little at each turn; the small mouth feeding beneath my head; water collecting in the fur above me.

And after an interval, from somewhere on the other side of the ridge, a pressure I recognized.

The crossing had been found.
