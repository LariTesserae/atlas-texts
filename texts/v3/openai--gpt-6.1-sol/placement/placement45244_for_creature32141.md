I think I would live above a passage, in a room whose floor is also the roof of somewhere people stop to shake out their capes.

There would be a small sound from below whenever someone struck a sleeve against the wall. In wet weather, this would happen often enough to become part of the room’s quiet. I would know the last factory shift had come through when the blows grew heavier and were followed by the scrape of lunch tins being set on the stone bench.

The room would have two windows, neither quite satisfactory. One would face a wall close enough that I could remove dead leaves from its fern bed without leaving home. The other would look down the passage toward a piece of street: a tram rail, the corner of a drying house, and a little of the sky reflected in a reservoir lid.

I would initially think I wanted a western window. Then I would spend an evening in someone else’s room at the rim, with light rising under the table and the cloud moving past below the sill, and find that I could not settle to anything. I would keep looking up from my plate.

At home, there would be a lamp that hummed slightly, a narrow bed, and a cabinet standing on four mismatched blocks. One block would be a failed valve housing brought back from the yards. Its clean circular opening would make a good place to keep a spare key.

I would not work in the factories. I would like visiting them, especially near the end of a shift, when the machines could still be moving although nobody was feeding them. But I would be poor at the work. After making thirty identical things, I would begin attending to some small irrelevant difference in the thirty-first.

My work would require that habit, though not always reward it.

I would work with the town’s duplicate plans.

The originals would stay in the water offices, laid flat between boards or hung in shallow drawers. The copies would go out with crews. They would return folded, stained, annotated, sometimes with a whole corner worn away by someone’s thumb. I would dry them, copy changes onto fresh sheets, and decide which marks belonged on the office version.

That last part would take most of the time.

A penciled line might mean a newly discovered channel, a proposed channel, or simply the route someone had taken while carrying a ladder. An arrow might indicate flow, unless a particular crew leader had drawn it, in which case it indicated where she wanted a person to stand.

I would learn the hands before I learned all the streets.

There would be one worker who drew beautifully precise little squares for inspection covers and never bothered to show where the covers were. Another would write measurements across the very feature being measured. Someone from the north district would mark every blocked drain with the same fierce black star, whether the obstruction was a fallen leaf or three feet of rooted earth.

For a while, I would make the copies too clean.

I would replace a wavering boundary with a straight one. I would give uncertain channels the same weight of line as confirmed channels. People would compliment the result. Then a crew would lift paving in the wrong place because I had made a guess look like a fact.

The old sheet would still be in a drawer. I would take it out and find the hesitation plainly visible: a line started twice, one end rubbed away, a question in cramped writing beneath a stain.

After that, I would preserve the hesitations.

I would also have to go outside.

At first this would be a nuisance. Work waiting on my desk would be easier to account for than an afternoon spent finding out whether a narrow runnel still passed behind a kitchen. But eventually I would be assigned one circuit a week, then two, accompanying someone whose measurements I had repeatedly failed to understand.

She would walk slowly because of her knee and leave me breathless because she never stopped where I expected.

We would inspect a channel beside a house with immaculate steps. She would give me a spoon and ask me to clear it. I would begin at the visible blockage.

“Below it first,” she would say.

I would find another obstruction farther down, then another beneath a removable grate. Clearing the first would have sent water straight over the threshold.

I would remember this well enough to become irritating about it.

At home, when somebody complained of a leak, I would ask too many questions before helping. Which side? Since when? Was the water cold? Had anything been moved upstairs? Once, while I stood asking, a bowl would overflow into their shoe.

There would be people who liked me better away from my work.

One of them would live across the passage. She would repair removable coat linings and keep a warm iron wrapped in felt on her table. We would begin by borrowing things: a lamp, a basin, a length of cord neither of us could remember owning. Later, we would eat together on evenings when our work ended at similar hours.

She would have no interest in my maps except when a change affected her rent.

I would try to tell her about two channels that appeared to connect under a building but could not, according to their measured depths. She would listen, then ask whether I wanted the rest of the onions.

I think I would need her indifference. Not because it would diminish the work, but because I could otherwise spend a whole evening following a line on paper and forget that I had come home hungry.

There would be one place I visited without needing to.

Not the abandoned square, where too many people would know why I was there, but a shallow stone basin beyond the last northern garden. A low tree would grow against one side. Someone would have cut footholds into the approach long ago, and the uppermost one would now be almost filled by a root.

The basin would appear on an old plan as a source. On a later plan it would be crossed out. Neither marking would have a date.

I would go there in different weather. Usually it would be dry. Sometimes it would hold a little water, but never when I had brought the right vessel to measure it.

I would want it to have a pattern.

This would be an unreasonable desire, and I would nevertheless keep visiting. I would note the temperature, the cloud, the condition of the surrounding moss. Then I would lose the notebook for a month and discover it beneath my bed, where I had put it while looking for a stocking.

Eventually I would bring someone along. She would sit on the edge of the basin, test the stone with her palm, and tell me it was a good place to eat lunch.

Afterward there would be crumbs in a seam I had been watching. I would be annoyed for a moment, then ashamed of being annoyed.

I would still go back.

What kind of person would this make me? Useful, in a limited way. More exact in writing than in speech. Inclined to keep damaged things because the damage explained something. Occasionally too pleased to discover an error, until I met the person who had made it.

I would have a cupboard full of old plans I had been authorized to discard. Some would be kept for good reasons. Others would remain because I had become used to their presence and disliked the empty shelf.

Years into living there, I would find an early copy of my own among them. The lettering would be careful and slightly too large. The water routes would all be drawn in an even blue line, with no distinction between what had been measured and what somebody thought was probably there.

On the back, in another hand, would be a grocery list.

I would turn it over several times, trying to remember whose list it was. Then someone below would strike the water from a cape against the passage wall. A lunch tin would scrape along the bench.

I would leave the sheet on the table and go down to see whether they had eaten.
