I would find myself beneath a flake of enamel that has lifted at one end.

Not in the open basin yet. The basin would be something arriving through the gap: a cooler edge of water, particles caught against the underside of my shelter, the weight of another body passing overhead.

There would be light, but no view. Amber would enter through the flake itself. Silver would enter along the floor. When something crossed outside, the silver would disappear without the amber changing.

I would learn that difference before I ventured out.

My back would still be soft enough for the shelter’s edge to leave a shallow mark. Food would darken inside me. In a pit beside the place where I hatched, I could put my mouth against the floor and feed without moving my feet. There would be enough there for a while.

The immense vibration would have no beginning. I would not know it as a noise made by machinery, or even as something separate from the floor. It would be the condition in which smaller things happened.

A weight would settle nearby.

Two knocks.

A pause in which grit moved through the entrance.

Two more.

The body outside would scrape along the flake, stop, and scrape again. Its mouthparts would be visible before the rest of it: pale, working, close to mine but much larger.

I would withdraw farther into the pit. It could not follow.

For some time, that would be the useful fact about my smallness.

Then the pit would cease to be enough.

I would go out by degrees, feeding first with my rear feet still under the flake. The open floor would not feel open everywhere. There would be hollows where my body fitted, little rises I had to go around, a loose grain that moved when I leaned against it. The broad gray surface a person might call smooth would offer a choice at almost every step.

At the end of my shelter, a raised line would cross the route to a runnel.

It would be new.

During the dark part of a cycle, the material beneath the enamel would have closed a crack. Nothing would have entered from outside. No animal would have built it. Yet the floor I could cross yesterday would now press against my underside.

I would try it with one foot, then another. I would turn and try elsewhere.

Eventually I would find the low end and pass around.

On the other side, water would have gathered in a crescent scarcely wider than my body. Cells would collect there. I would feed until the dark inside me lengthened, then return by the same low end.

That would become my route.

I would be one of the grazers, though not yet the dark fragment that disappears against the worn floor. My plates would harden unevenly. For a few cycles a pale margin would remain around them. I would be conspicuous in places where older bodies were not.

When a shadow came, I would flatten.

Sometimes nothing would follow. Sometimes a foot would touch down so close that its pad displaced water against my face.

I would remain still while the foot lifted.

The scarred hunter would live beyond distances I could ordinarily travel, but its reach would enter my life long before I approached its shelf. A grazer ahead of me would stop scraping. The floor would take a sudden load. A body would vanish from the edge of the silver light.

Afterwards there would still be food in that place.

I would wait before going to it.

My first knocks would be made beside the runnel, where another grazer occupied the narrow strip I wanted to reach. I would strike the ceramic with my forelimbs. The vibration would return through the feet that remained on the floor.

The other animal would answer.

It would not move.

I would feed farther down, where the film was thinner.

Later, at another patch, I would knock and a body would turn just enough for me to pass. I would move between its back and a grain lodged in the channel. Water would touch both of us. Behind me, its scraping would resume.

This would be most of what I knew of company: pressure close by, a pause, room made or withheld.

Before the flood, my familiar crescent would change.

The water would reach a little farther beneath me. The cool thread against my mouth would become warm. Around me, animals would stop feeding and begin to climb.

I would follow a back I had learned to recognize by a missing corner.

The ridge would be crowded. I would find a place below two larger grazers, then leave it when water touched my feet. Higher up, a body would knock against the floor.

I would stop.

It would shift sideways.

There would be room for my front feet on a dry patch and my rear feet against the sloping ceramic. I would hold there while swimmers passed through the space where I had been feeding. One would strike the ridge, turn, and disappear into the moving sheet.

The flood would bring particles within reach. I would not release my grip to take them.

When it drained, I would descend with the others.

My shelter would still be there. My route around the repaired crack would not. A little deposit would have collected at its low end, and the runnel would now slip along the opposite side.

I would search with my mouth close to the floor.

A few body lengths away, fresh food would have settled against the raised scar. Another grazer would already be working over it.

I would knock twice and wait.

If it moved, I would enter. If it held its place, I would go around. Somewhere along that small new edge, I would find enough to begin again.
