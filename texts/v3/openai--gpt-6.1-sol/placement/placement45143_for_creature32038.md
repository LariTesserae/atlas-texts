At first I would put myself inside the pump house, in the space between the brick pier and the belt.

There is shelter there, and something to attend to. Before the wheel turns, the leather changes shape. One edge lifts; a crease that has held through several still hours begins to open. From close enough, this would be a considerable event. Dust would shift in the grease. The brick would give a little of the coming movement to whatever rested against it.

But I would not live beside the belt. It is too far from water, and when it moves there is nowhere to flatten safely. I would go down the pier, through the missing mortar, and remain for a while with my front half outside and my rear half still in the building.

That seems nearer.

I would be one of the plated animals, though not one you could pick out by any conspicuous difference. A dark seam from the pump house would remain along my left side. One of my rear plates would have grown back slightly crooked after a bad shedding. When I passed through a narrow place, that edge would catch. I would have to loosen my hold, move backward a little, and try again with my body set lower.

I would know several passages by this difficulty.

There would be one beneath the pipe support that I could enter from the pools but not from the wall. Another would admit me only after its water had begun to run outward. I would wait with a feeler inside it. The water would press the feeler toward me, and then, gradually, cease pressing. I would enter before the first visible grain of sediment came out.

Most of what I knew would be like that: a difference becoming small enough to pass through.

I would not know the hollow as a hollow. I would have no occasion to gather its walls, roofs, pools, and divided sky into one place. The upper steps might never become distinct from the mass of stone above me. The hanging chains would reach me as occasional blows conducted through limestone, each with a long, weak remainder.

There would be regions of good contact and regions where my limbs found nothing. There would be water that brought food, water that took it away, and water whose arrival could be felt before it arrived.

The pipe would be unmistakable. Its interior would touch many plates at once. Even after leaving it, I would retain that close, even pressure for a while and hold my limbs too tightly against myself.

I might go back nevertheless.

Beyond the mouth, beyond the narrowing that left rust on my back, there would be a place where the pipe joined something wider. Food gathered along the lower edge of the joint. The water there was seldom still, and for long periods it carried the fine white material that could be caught and worked between my mouth parts.

I would feed until the iron began to tremble.

Then I would withdraw. Not quickly. There would be other animals behind me, and my rear limbs would meet their shells before finding the pipe wall. I would press, stop, press elsewhere. Eventually there would be room.

Once, perhaps, I would fail to leave in time.

A change of pressure would take my hold away. For a moment every limb would be folded by the same force, and I would travel without touching anything. Then my crooked plate would strike a roughness. The rest of me would pull against it.

Afterward I would spend a long time beneath the bottom step.

That is where I would find myself most often: not at the entrance to the hollow, as a person might think, but underneath a broad, low ceiling with water coming through its back wall.

The seam would be deep enough to receive my whole body if I entered at an angle. Near its mouth, the ceiling dipped. I would rest there with the first plate sheltered and two or three of the others exposed. My foremost pads would hold the underside of the step; the middle ones would spread against the floor. Water would pass between.

There would be a small grain of stone lodged above me that I could move but not dislodge. Over many visits I would push it farther into its pocket. Sometimes it would return.

I would feed along the back of the seam, where the film was soft and renewed itself unevenly. The two tracks of my mouth would cross older tracks. At a particular bump I would have to lift one side of my body, leaving a thicker patch that smaller grazers would find when I moved away.

I would not clean the place. There would always be something I could not reach.

When a basin emptied, I would come out far enough for the air to touch the upper surfaces of my plates. My limbs would remain in water. Small creatures would arrive along the shrinking wet edge, some passing underneath me, some climbing onto my back.

Their movements would reach me separately: a shell dragging near the rear, many brief touches at the side, the abrupt struggle of a shrimp.

Usually I would continue feeding.

Sometimes I would fold a limb across a place where the water was escaping. Moisture would collect behind the pad. An armored grazer might remain there until the basin filled again. I would have made no shelter deliberately. When I needed to move, I would move, and it would have to find another hold.

I would shed in the deeper part of the seam.

For some time beforehand, the hollow would reach me poorly. Pressure would spread where it should have been precise. A touch at the edge of one plate would seem to occur beneath another. I would stop going into the pipe.

The old covering would loosen in pieces. I would work against the stone, rest, work again. Afterward the new plates would be too yielding for the narrow mouth of my usual shelter. I would stay farther in, where the roof rose and the water moved continuously.

One old plate would remain fitted against the ceiling.

On returning, I would put a feeler to it. Its curve would resemble the curve of my back, but from beneath. Water would collect along its edge and fall onto me. I would leave it there until it softened enough to sag. Later, finding the passage partly blocked, I would push it out.

The small animals would turn it over in a footprint.

Light would matter chiefly at the edges of things. I would feel the warming of an exposed ridge, the change in film where a strip of sky reached the stone. I would cross open water with my limbs folded into paddles and settle as soon as there was something solid beneath me.

Sometimes another shape would continue after I settled.

It would have my extended feeler, my uneven rear plate, the slight bend left by swimming. I would touch where it appeared to touch the stone. My actual feeler would find only water.

I might repeat this several times.

Then a hunter would enter the space, strike, and turn away. Its movement would reach me more clearly than the shape had. I would draw the feeler in and press my underside down.

There would also be boots.

Before a foot reached the bottom step, I would feel the two impacts above it. They would be broad and closely spaced. The step’s underside would give a little under my foremost limbs.

Usually I would retreat.

But after shedding, or while the water behind me ran strongly outward, I might stay. I would shift the limbs that had lost their purchase. One pad would spread into a shallow flaw in the stone. Another would find the grain I had never managed to remove.

For a moment, the weight above and my small adjustment below would meet through the step.

Then the weight would leave. The ceiling would ease. Clear water would rise around the front of my body, carrying loosened grains into the pool.

I would remain there until my pads were properly seated again. One feeler would go back against the stone. The other would lie in the current.
