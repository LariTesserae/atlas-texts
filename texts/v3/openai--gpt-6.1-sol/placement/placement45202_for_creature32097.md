I would live in the ridge west of the central depression, where a slab has slipped just far enough to make an entrance. From outside, you would notice the grove of shortened stems before you noticed my door. One stem leans across it. I have moved that stem many times. Someone keeps putting it back.

Inside, the passage turns around a swelling in the stone. I have to release the ground with one side of my body to get past it. There is a polished place there, worn by shoulders much older than mine.

My sleeping ledge is beyond the turn. At its narrow end is a hollow that holds a cup I brought from another ridge. The cup does not fit. One edge stands slightly proud of the floor, and if I touch it carelessly it rocks.

It still fills, sometimes.

I have lived here long enough that the others no longer try to replace it.

***

I think I would be an adult, though not one of the large, dark-folded individuals who can wait through an entire afternoon without shifting a foot. There would be rose dust under my left shoulder and a pale scar across two of the digits beneath my throat. When I folded those digits, they would leave a gap.

The gap would make certain tasks awkward. Lifting a wet packet of feeding film, for instance. I would have learned to turn it first, so that its weight rested against the undamaged hand.

A young one would find this interesting. It would turn its own food the same way, spill some, and be annoyed when I helped.

I would know the basin by the undersides of things.

The slight roughness beneath the lip of a pool. The cool strip behind a stone at noon. The part of a doorway that stays dry when all the rest has begun to bead. I could cross a slope without looking up, recognizing it through four feet: a soft place, a crowded place, a place where the ground returned my weight a little late.

Distant shapes would matter less. The white birds would often be gone from my sight before I understood where they were going.

Their landing would reach me more clearly.

***

There is a damp hollow on the western slope that no longer becomes damp.

I go there often.

At first, I would probably seem to be repairing it. I would bring a nodule, set it against the soil, move three stones closer together. I would stand with my jaw pressed down and one foot lifted, leaving the others far apart.

But I would not have a repair to perform. I would have several things worth trying, and memories of occasions when one of them had helped.

The hollow lies beneath a low overhang. A dark mat still grows there, reduced to a thick rim around a bare center. Small burrowers pass along its uphill edge. They never cross the center, although the soil looks no different.

Years ago, water gathered there after the western ridge entered shadow. I remember this partly because someone else remembers it with me. When we meet at the hollow, we each put a hand where the old wet edge used to be. Our hands land in different places.

We have disputed this.

Once I brought a stem to measure the distance from the overhang. The other broke off its end.

For several days I would not touch anything it offered me.

Then it arrived at my entrance carrying a nodule from the northern basin. I made room for it to come inside. We held the nodule between us until its ticking ceased, and afterward put it in my badly fitted cup.

By morning there was enough water for both of us.

The broken stem remained at the hollow.

***

I would travel farther than most members of my household. In the cooler hours, I would visit the arrangements beside dry patches, even those that had ceased to interest anyone.

Some would have become difficult to find. A pale pebble would be almost buried. A pair of cups would have separated as the soil beneath them shifted. I would put a hand on each before deciding whether to move them.

There would be a dwelling in the eastern ridge where I was welcome but where I never slept. Their entrance would be wide, their floor unusually warm. One of its inhabitants would take every object out of my hands as soon as I arrived.

I would let it.

We would examine the objects together, though it would examine them first.

At another dwelling, I would stay near the opening until someone touched the underside of my jaw. They would have a young one that disliked unfamiliar dust. Before entering, I would press my shoulders against the outside stone and leave some of the western colors there.

These visits would take time. I would miss useful shadows. Sometimes, on my way home, I would find that the moisture I had intended to test was already gone.

I would return anyway, carrying something small.

***

I would be especially uncertain at the panels.

The earth gives many kinds of warning before it changes. Even a failed damp patch has an edge, a temperature, creatures that approach or refuse it. A panel offers a bush with one leaf unfolded.

I would bring a stalk and hold it across the view. Then lower it. Then look again.

On one occasion, I would see my own doorway with the leaning stem fallen beside it.

I would wait until the ground beneath my feet cooled. The stem would remain upright when I went home.

The next morning it would still be upright.

Later, after I had stopped checking, a grazer would catch it with an ear and lay it down.

I would have no way to decide how much of this belonged together.

Afterward I might take the young one to the panel, but I would hold it close. Its hands would reach toward the slightly misplaced world. I would allow one hand through, keeping my damaged digits around its body.

Beyond the surface, its little hand would look empty.

Against me, I would feel it grasping.

***

The young one would not belong only to me.

Some mornings I would find it asleep against another body. Some evenings it would refuse to come out from beneath the nursery slab, though it had touched my offered hand several times.

I would wait with the stone raised.

The pale creatures around it would adjust their legs. One would climb over my wrist and continue down into the light. The young would follow that movement with its low-set eyes, then tuck itself farther into the crowd.

I would put the slab back carefully and leave.

There would be work I could do alone.

Near the failed hollow, I would nudge a pebble onto the gray seam. It would travel uphill, slowly enough for me to walk beside it. Where it stopped, I would set a second pebble on the ordinary soil.

On another day, the first would move farther.

I would shift the second.

Eventually someone passing would inspect my arrangement. Perhaps it would move the marker back. Perhaps it would add a third stone, and I would have to find it at home to learn what had happened.

I could spend a season there without the hollow filling.

***

At evening I would come to the central depression by a shallow gully. The animals would already be taking their places. A grazer that drank at my doorway would turn its head as I passed, then lower it again.

I would settle where one of the dark lines thinned around a buried stone.

There would be room for both hands on one side of it, but I would place them on opposite sides. Under my right hand the soil would loosen. Under my left it would remain dry.

I would wait until the right-hand digits were wet.

Then I would move only the left.

It might find moisture. It might not. All around me, narrow jaws would lift from the ground. Birds would depart without calling. A burrower would drag its soft underside across my foot.

By the time I returned to the ridge, the smallest bodies would be waking.

Someone would have moved my cup into a better-fitting hollow. I would move it back. The young one would approach on all six limbs and put a hand on the rim, steadying it while I lowered the nodule.

We would hold it there together.

Through the entrance, the leaning stem would make a thin division in the remaining light. A grazer would breathe on the other side. Farther inside, someone would shift along the sleeping ledge, making room at its warm end.

The first tick would come through the cup into our hands.

The young one would tighten its grip.

I would loosen mine, just enough to let it feel the next one by itself.
