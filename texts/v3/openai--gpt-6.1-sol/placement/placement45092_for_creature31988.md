I would first be mistaken for part of the shelter.

Not the slab across its roof, but the low, irregular piece beside the entrance, lying at an angle that makes the doorway seem narrower than it is. Dust would have gathered on my upper surface. One of my plates would carry a yellow rim from a vent that had cooled elsewhere. There would be no reason to distinguish me from the other material brought together here.

Then the ground would tilt, and I would move against it.

Only a little. Enough to keep one end in the doorway while the other rested outside.

I think I would be an adult, though not one of the largest. My plates would no longer close evenly along one side. A pale seam would show where a support had been lost and the body had sealed itself around the loss. I could still cross a narrow opening, but I would take longer finding the places from which to begin.

I would live below the eastern rim, where the warmth is broken by intervals of cooler stone. Not because those ledges are stable. They are not. But there is room there to move sideways when something begins beneath me, and the overhangs interrupt the wind.

The basin would reach me mostly through my underside.

A change of pressure in the leftmost support. A small rotation in another. Warmth continuing upward after the surface ought to have begun cooling. The ground would offer these separately, and I would have to arrange my weight among them.

Sometimes I would be wrong.

A place I refused might remain whole through several returns of the copper light. A place I accepted might open while I was settled upon it. I would survive partly by sensing, partly because the break happened under an unoccupied fold, or because the nearer edge held long enough for my leading supports to attach elsewhere.

There would be no useful view of the whole country from where I lived. A ridge could rise behind me without my knowing its shape. I would feel the movement travel beneath my body, release two contacts, make another, and continue feeding.

At a warm seam I would become less clearly bounded.

My underside would fit itself into the minute roughness of the rock. Along one margin, the purple tissue would spread beyond the reach of my plates. The seam would continue under it, narrow and productive. For hours I would remain there with nearly all my supports withdrawn.

This might be the nearest thing I had to ease: no need to move, moisture enclosed, heat arriving at a rate my body could use.

Even then, one support would stay extended onto a separate piece of stone.

When I lifted, some of the purple would stay.

I do not think I would know whether I had left something behind or made something. The edge would part without a wound. Later, returning by a route altered by two new fissures, I might settle beside it again. My underside would meet its margin. There would be a slow fitting together, and the distinction would matter less than the renewed supply of warmth.

Some of these places would become too hot. Some would stop giving anything.

I would not have a territory so much as a number of unfinished returns.

The shelter would be one of them.

I would have helped move its roof before the slab was a roof. It would have lain flat on a ledge, cool through its thickness, with one edge slightly raised by an unevenness underneath. I would have entered there and braced.

The first effort would do nothing.

Then another body would press against a different edge. Its plates would touch mine, transmitting a small scrape. Neither of us would have room to lengthen fully. We would release, settle, press again.

At some point the stone would shift.

Dust trapped beneath it would spill out in a narrow line. There would be new space for a support. After a long interval we would move it again.

I would not carry an image of the completed shelter through all this. The stone would become usable by degrees: an edge lifted, a wedge retained underneath, a passage opened where there had only been weight. Other bodies would arrive. One might depart with no part of the work finished. The resulting darkness would belong to whoever could reach it when their covering loosened.

Now there is a soft animal inside.

Its old plates have separated along both margins. One has slipped against the back wall. Beneath it the new surface is almost black, still taking the shape of the floor.

I stand across the entrance because it is the remaining place where my body fits. My forward supports touch the ledge outside. Another reaches past the inner depression and rests close to the rear upright. The animal beneath me moves occasionally, slowly enough that I can withdraw a support before its folds reach it.

I am not keeping watch over the distance. The darkness beyond the doorway contains very little I can use.

I am spread between pieces of ground.

For a long time, their movements agree.

Then the support near the rear wall is pushed upward.

The others are not.

I tighten. My upper plates strike the underside of the roof.

The animal within draws one side inward. An old plate falls from it and rests against my underside. There is too little room for us both to turn.

The rear support rises farther.

I knock again.

Now the soft body begins to come out, bringing loose fragments with it. I pull myself toward the open ledge. My damaged side drags over the threshold and briefly catches. Behind me, the other animal extends a support that has not yet hardened at its joint.

It finds the ground.

We do not get far. The roof shifts before the other body has entirely cleared it. One corner settles into the depression, leaving a lower, slanting space.

For a while we remain beside each other outside. My plates are closed. The other’s are not. Warm air comes through the doorway in uneven breaths.

When the ledge stops moving, I place one sensing tip against the exposed new covering. It is cooler than the ground. I withdraw the tip and shift enough to leave a strip of sheltered space beneath my edge.

The animal draws part of itself into that space.

This is where I would find myself most fully: not inside a completed refuge, but along its remaining edge, with another body hardening beside mine.

There would still be limits. I could not enclose it. I could not prevent the next movement. If the stone beneath us grew hot, I would have to rise, and the narrow shade would rise with me.

Eventually its new plates would hold their shape. It would leave during a still interval, moving with an awkwardness I might sense through the ground but would not follow.

I would stay until my own need brought me back to a seam.

Later, when the shelter was empty and its rear wall had leaned far enough to touch the fallen roof, I would work a broad fragment free from the entrance. An old plate, perhaps mine, perhaps left by a body I had never touched.

It would be too large to hold between my folds.

I would get underneath one end.

The stone would resist. I would rest with its weight across my back, keeping my remaining supports on the cooler parts of the ledge. Then I would press again.

By the time the copper light returned, there would be a narrow space beneath it. Enough for a small body. Not yet enough for mine.
