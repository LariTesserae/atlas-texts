I would be in one of the rooms above a bridge anchorage, where the vibration comes through the wall strongly enough to disturb the sight of anything small.

Not the control room. There would be one of those below, with its long windows and its levers under glass. My room would have a narrower purpose. A desk, a cupboard, a sink. Somewhere for the bridge inspector to sit while taking grit out of a boot.

At first I imagine myself as something left on the desk: a sheet of paper held flat by four brass weights. The paper would have suffered where the country has not. There would be softened corners, a brown tide mark, the crease of an earlier folding. I think I would need that evidence of use. Otherwise I could not tell the room from a room constructed to look as if someone had needed it.

On the sheet would be a drawing of the bridge.

Very little of it would fit.

The anchorages would occupy most of the paper, their foundations shown in section, their bolts individually numbered. Between them, two broken lines would excuse the omission of miles.

There ought to be a hand near the drawing. Someone ought to have been dissatisfied with that omission.

So perhaps I would be that person.

***

I would have been employed to keep the pedestrian ways in order.

Not the main bridge structure. Other people understood its loads, the pressure in the enormous pipe beneath it, the noise that continued without expenditure. My work concerned the narrow walkway slung along its northern side: its doors, its refuges, the steps between one level and another.

There were places to stop out there.

They were easy to miss from shore. Each was a little enlargement of the walkway, enclosed on three sides, with a bench and an emergency cabinet. At intervals too long to make walking pleasant, they gave a person somewhere to get out of the wind.

I had drawn them larger than their scale allowed.

Beside one, I had written: **Latch sticks. Lift before pulling.**

Beside another: **Seat wet at outer end.**

The drawing was not beautiful. I had erased a railing so often that the paper had thinned beneath it.

I would be standing over the desk with my coat still on. One cuff pushed back. A pencil in my right hand, its point just short of the page.

My left hand would be under the desk, finding the lever that raised the chair.

An unimportant adjustment. I had decided to sit down before finishing.

That is where you would find me.

***

For some time I would mistake your arrival for a change in the light.

You would pass the doorway, come back, look in. My eyes would move before I understood they were moving.

There would be a great deal I wanted to say.

The first thing would be, “Mind the step.”

The floor inside the room is higher than the gallery floor. People catch their toes on it. I have watched you catch yours.

You might laugh. Not because it is funny, exactly, but because someone has spoken to you and the first words are about a step.

Then you would hold out a hand.

I would have to choose which of mine to free.

The pencil matters to me. This would be hard to explain. I would let go of the chair lever.

***

I do not think I would immediately ask to leave.

With your hand around mine, I would sit. The chair would yield a little under my weight. That small yielding might be enough for a while.

You would stand beside me, unsure whether to speak.

I would finish the mark on the drawing.

Then I would discover that I no longer knew what it was meant to indicate.

I would look at the pencil. I would look at the bridge through the window. On the far shore, the buildings would remain too small to distinguish their doors.

“Have you crossed?” I would ask.

If you had, I would want to know where you stopped.

Not whether you were frightened. Not whether the view was magnificent. Where you had actually sat down.

You might point to one of my enlarged shelters.

I would make a mark there.

***

After that, you would sometimes bring me things to put on the drawing.

A person beside the seventh cabinet. Two people farther out, facing one another. A dropped scarf hooked over the toe of a shoe.

I would ask questions that seemed excessive.

Which side of the walkway?

Before the ladder or after it?

Could the person reach the railing?

You might become impatient. You would have walked a long way, and I would be using the movement you had brought me to make tiny corrections on paper.

But the old drawing was made for people who could go wherever they wished.

It showed distances. It showed stairs without saying who stood on them. A locked door and an open door had the same symbol.

I would begin another sheet.

We would not call it a map at first. It would look more like the untidy notes kept beside a telephone.

**Woman at service door. Key in right pocket.**

**Man under west stair. Approach from below; cannot turn easily.**

**Child in second refuge. Wants mother. Not the child in yellow.**

There would be names where we knew them. Descriptions where we did not.

Sometimes a description would be crossed out and a name written above it.

***

I would not be especially patient or kind.

You would learn this when I asked you to return to someone you had just spent half a day helping.

There is a man on the walkway who carries a bag of replacement lamps. I know him. Or I knew him before knowing someone required crossing this much of the country.

You would tell me he had wanted only to lower the bag.

“Did he?” I would ask.

“Yes.”

“And you let him?”

“Yes.”

I would keep looking at you.

Eventually you would say, “He asked me to.”

I would be ashamed then, although I might not manage to say so before you took your hand away.

On your next visit, there would be no apology waiting on the paper. I could not have written it.

You would have to stay while I found the words.

***

There would be days when you came without news.

You would sit on the other side of the desk. Your boots would leave wet marks. For a little while you would tell me about the road, or a meal you had eaten standing up, or the difficulty of sleeping near the pump halls.

I would want to ask you to hold my hand.

Instead, once I understood how tired you were, I would leave it on the desk where you could choose.

You could sit beside someone without being asked to carry them farther.

I would try to give you that.

***

Eventually you would want me out of the room.

I would argue that the papers should come too. We would waste some movement opening the cupboard to find a proper case. Then I would remember that the spare cases were stored downstairs.

You would wrap the drawings in my coat.

We would get as far as the first bridge refuge.

There, the bench would be dry at the inner end. I would be pleased about this in a way you might find disproportionate.

You would sit beside me, still holding my hand.

For once I would have no pencil. The country would extend beyond the shelter’s open side: blue distance, wet stone, another shore. I would look at it for a long time.

When you were ready to leave, I would ask you to put the drawings on my lap.

My free hand could hold them against the wind. We would arrange it carefully.

Then you would walk on.

You would be very small before you reached the next shelter. I would remain facing that way, with the papers secure beneath my palm.

Later, when you came back, you would not have to search the window above the anchorage to find me.

I would be somewhere you could stop.
