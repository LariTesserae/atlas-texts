I would be below the walkway, close enough to hear footsteps but unable to see who was passing.

There would be a little maintenance cradle hung from two rails beneath the bridge. Its floor would be perforated metal, its gate awkward to close from inside. Before lowering it, I would put my lunch in the locker at the stairhead. Anything brought down here would have to be tied on.

Above my shoulder, a net would sag between its anchors. Leaves, mostly. A broken flowerpot, three pale fruits with bird marks in them, a sandal. Something heavier gathered in the lowest fold.

I would draw the fold toward me with a hook.

A cup. White, with a brown line around the rim. Its handle would be gone, but I would not know whether that had happened during the fall or long before.

There would be a little water in it.

I would empty that into the nearest planting basket rather than let it fall another hundred feet.

At first I think this would be my work: clearing the nets, checking their anchors, bringing things back up. Work for someone who is not fearless but can follow a procedure while afraid. I would dislike the moment when the cradle leaves the landing. Once it was hanging steadily, I would be all right.

Birds would pass between me and the wall.

I would learn to leave a few branches caught along the net’s edge, where they caused no strain. Insects would shelter there, and a pair of small birds would come to inspect what I uncovered. One would be bold enough to land on the shaft of my hook. I would have to wait before using it.

The cup would go on a shelf near the maintenance stairs.

Someone would claim the sandal that afternoon. The cup might remain for weeks.

Then a woman carrying folded awnings would stop in front of it.

“That’s ours,” she would say.

I would take it down, and she would turn it over to look at the bottom. There would be no name there, only a dull patch where the glaze had been worn away.

“How long ago?”

She would think.

“Before they enclosed the west stairs.”

That would not tell me a year. I would have been a child when the west stairs were enclosed.

She would put the cup among the awnings, wrapped so that its surviving rim could not strike anything.

Afterward, I would begin writing dates on the shelf labels. Not the date an object fell. The date it came back within reach.

***

But I do not think I would remain only at the nets.

There would be mornings when the cradle was out of service, or the wind made the undersides of bridges unsafe. I would work indoors then, in a narrow room behind the landing. It would have been built for some machine small enough to leave without dismantling the wall. The mounting bolts would still project from the floor.

A table would fit between them.

Here I would repair the route books.

Not the great plans of the mountain. Those would belong in rooms where people had space to unroll them. Mine would be small books, made to fit a jacket pocket, with stiff covers and pages that could survive being handled by damp fingers.

A gardener would bring one in with a thumb holding the place.

“This door opens inward now.”

I would correct the little arc beside the door.

“It’s still difficult. You have to lift it.”

I would add that too.

Another person would say the passage beyond it was closed. A third would explain that it was closed only during the grain delivery, and that knocking twice would get someone to move the sacks if the journey mattered.

There would be no symbol small enough to hold all of that. I would write in the margin.

My handwriting would become narrow.

I would keep the old pages rather than throw them out. Partly because an obsolete passage might become useful again; partly because I would sometimes make a mistake and need to know whose account I had believed.

People would come to the doorway to argue over a route.

I would have a second stool, though the room scarcely allowed it. Someone arguing from memory was more likely to remember properly if they sat down.

***

I would live two levels above that room, in an apartment reached by thirty-seven steps and a short ramp. There would be a column beside the bed and a window too high to see through while sitting.

My mother, if she were still alive, would complain that I could have found somewhere with fewer stairs.

I would tell her it was close to work.

“It’s directly over your work,” she would say. “That isn’t the same thing.”

The warmest corner would hold a drying rack for damp books. I would put my clothes elsewhere. There would be a shallow bowl beneath a pipe joint that dripped when the weather changed, and I would keep promising to have it repaired. Knowing which mechanic could do it would not make arranging the visit any easier.

I would grow something at the window. Not a remarkable tree or an ancient vine. A kitchen herb in a long box, surviving unevenly. One end would get more light than the other. I would turn the box whenever I remembered, spill a little of its brick dust on the sill, and sweep that back in with my hand.

A gecko would live behind the drying rack.

For some weeks I would mistake its droppings for debris falling out of the books.

***

What would make me useful would be a habit of going to look.

If someone told me a staircase had become impassable, I would want to see which stair, and why. Roots, a locked gate, a landing removed for repairs: these required different corrections. I would carry chalk, paper, a measuring cord. I would also carry a small wedge, because many of the doors on my routes had forgotten how to stay open.

I would not range through the entire mountain. There would be districts whose names I knew and whose approaches I did not. At the edge of my books I would write: **Ask at the lift workshop.** Or: **Unverified beyond this landing.**

An apprentice would dislike those notes.

“We could finish this one,” she would say, tracing a route that disappeared off the page.

“Not today.”

She would think I was being cautious about the distance. Often I would simply have a net to clear before the afternoon wind.

But eventually we would go.

We would find a stair narrower than reported, then a room full of empty cages, then a passage where someone had installed a sink across half the width. A man washing greens would let us squeeze past. He would tell us the door at the far end had not been opened for years.

Beyond it, the map would show a bridge.

We would leave the door alone until we could find someone responsible for the bridge. On the way back, the apprentice would mark the sink. I would suggest she mark the man’s household as well, with permission. Anyone sent down that passage ought to announce themselves before walking through his kitchen.

***

One evening a pump would stop while I was trimming a sheet to fit a book.

The scissors would keep moving for one stroke after the table became still.

Outside, someone would close a shutter. A few voices would rise from the stair. I would put the scissors down and wait, then take the book from the drying rack nearest the wall.

It would be the watering route for the terraces east of the old station.

By the time the gardener reached my door, I would have found the loose page kept behind its cover: the hand-carrying route used the last time that pump had failed.

She would look at it.

“The third landing’s gone.”

I would have forgotten. I had recorded its removal in another book.

We would spread both books on the table. The apprentice would arrive with a lantern, though there was still daylight at the window. Between us we would work out where the buckets could pass, which lift was operating, who needed to unlock a gate.

Then I would go with them. There would be enough people reading instructions and too few carrying water.

My shoulders would ache before we finished. At one bed I would pour too quickly, washing the loose material away from a stem. The gardener would stop me with her hand on the bucket.

“Here. Against the tile.”

I would do the next one properly.

Later, back in the little room, I would correct the emergency route while it was still clear in my head. Beside the vanished landing I would write the date of its removal, then cross out the route that depended on it.

The apprentice would be asleep on the spare stool, her lantern between her feet.

I would wake her before going upstairs.

***

So I would be a person with a municipal key, a stiff shoulder, and responsibility for a small portion of the district. Someone who had begun by recovering dropped things and gradually acquired a room full of amended journeys.

There would be people who knew me only as the person to ask about a closed stair. Others would remember that I still had their bowl, or that I had given them bad directions once. Children would peer into the net-clearing locker and hope for something worth claiming.

I would sometimes disappoint them.

At home, I would open the high window with a pole. The herb box would smell faintly when I brushed it. Through the leaves I could see one corner of the bridge I had worked beneath that morning.

Someone would cross it carrying bread. Behind them, another person would stop to adjust a sandal, one hand firmly on the rail.

I would watch until they moved on, then put water on for tea.
