At first I would mistake myself for something brought in.

A flake from a boot sole, perhaps, or the small curled shaving left when a metal instrument is withdrawn from its case. I would lie near the entrance with one edge lifted. The grit beneath me would be undisturbed. There would be no track to account for my position.

I would wait for someone to pick me up.

The blue seam would appear. During that interval I would discover that my lifted edge was much farther from the ground than the rest of me. The pale underside would be visible on an upper terrace. What I had taken for my shadow would remain beside the entrance.

When the seam darkened, I would fit together again.

I do not think I would understand this the first time.

***

The inhabitant would notice me because I failed to settle.

It would incline one of the pins. Dust beside me would draw into a narrow line. I would feel the change as a pressure under my raised edge, slight but persistent, and turn toward it.

The inhabitant would stop.

For a long while we would remain like that: it distributed through the basin, I scarcely distinguishable from debris, the pin leaning a little farther than before.

Then it would restore the pin.

I would turn back.

That second movement would cost me something. A little of my underside would become dull. I would have no way to tell whether this was exhaustion or wear.

The next time the pillar clicked, the inhabitant would leave a grain within reach.

***

I would not have legs. Travel would begin with finding somewhere that could bear one part of me while another part moved.

A ridge would hold my lower edge. I could ease my upper edge toward a terrace, wait for the distance between them to change, then release the ridge. To someone watching from the entrance, I would appear to have moved very little. Sometimes I would have spent the better part of a day crossing the width of my own body.

I would become good at this only in a few places.

There would be a shallow channel beside the pillar that I could negotiate without waiting for a click. A particular rounded terrace would let me turn over. On the narrow shelf beside the dish, I would discover an additional fold in myself.

I would keep it folded.

Once opened, it would be difficult to close, and the inhabitant would have to stand near me until the basin made the right distance available again. Its white repaired plate would catch twice during the effort.

Afterward I would avoid the shelf.

***

My place would be beside the footprint.

Not in it. The heel would already be filled with dust, and I would be reluctant to disturb the sharp front edge. There would be a small exposed patch just beyond the print where I could rest against the substrate without adhering to it.

From there I would see the inhabitant approach.

Its movements toward the footprint would differ from all its other movements. They would begin far away. A plate near the doorway would loosen; something beneath the overhang would turn; the hollow square would briefly acquire a dark rim. Only much later would the joint descend toward the ground.

I would learn to stay still through the whole attempt.

At first, when it withdrew, I would follow. Eventually I would stop doing that. The inhabitant would need the route back to its doorway, including several regions that looked empty to me.

I could remain beside the print until it returned.

***

I would have a way of remembering, though a poor one.

Anything that held me would leave a small bend along my edge. The bends would persist after I moved. Resting on the same ridge again would straighten one of them, and for a moment I would recover the earlier position with great exactness: the pressure beneath me, the inclination of a pin, the inhabitant’s narrow part extended from its room.

Then the recollection would be gone.

Remembering would use it up.

This would make me slow to revisit places I had liked.

I would carry one bend for a long time. It would have been made when the inhabitant first left the grain beside me. There would be little to distinguish it from the other irregularities in my edge, but I would arrange my resting positions to protect it.

Eventually the inhabitant would touch it with the fine extremity it used on its own shallow pits.

The contact would straighten the bend.

For an instant, the first grain would seem to be beside me again. The pin would have its old inclination. My underside would still be bright.

The inhabitant would hold its extremity motionless.

When it withdrew, that memory would belong to neither of us in quite the same way.

***

I would never grow large enough to help with the whole basin.

A grain lifted from the dish’s crack would be too heavy for me to carry. I could not reach the suspended fragments. When the inhabitant went inside its room, I would see only the first joint of the floor.

But I could fit beneath the pale plate where it caught against its neighbor.

The first time would be accidental. I would be resting on a ledge when the inhabitant bent past me. The two plates would close around my upper edge, and I would turn, expecting to be broken.

Instead, the pale plate would slide over me.

The movement would finish without its usual pause.

For several clicks afterward, the inhabitant would keep that part of itself close to the ledge. I would approach cautiously. It would begin the bend, then wait.

We would learn the position together.

I would not stay there always. The pressure would flatten me, and I would need long intervals beside the footprint to recover my shape. But before a difficult journey, the inhabitant would make the ledge accessible.

I would usually go.

***

By the time the recess at the entrance was nearly finished, I would know a little of the route to it.

There would still be one passage I could not make alone. The inhabitant would have to lower a dark joint against the casing, giving me a surface that led from the inner basin to the lip.

Outside would be colder in one way and warmer in another.

I would feel weather for the first time: warmth arriving without a click, a brief tremor from something moving on the mountain. I would turn toward it too quickly and nearly lose my hold.

The inhabitant would wait behind me.

The poised grain would rest just above the unfinished recess. I would be small enough to pass beneath it. Once there, I would brace one edge against the ceramic and lift the other.

The grain would move less than its own width.

It would settle against me.

That would leave the inhabitant free to press once more into the inner face of the lip. Far inside the basin, something would loosen. We would wait until it was supported again.

I would have no means of knowing how much work remained.

When the blue seam appeared, my parts would separate from sight. One would lie against the ceramic. Another would be visible somewhere near the pillar.

The grain would stay where we had put it.

When the seam darkened, I would feel its weight again, and hold.
