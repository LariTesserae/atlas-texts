At first I would look for myself in one of the spaces between districts.

Not because those places are unclaimed. The dry meadow has its uses, even if no one is crossing it; the outcrop determines where a wall can go. But there is room there to be present without immediately becoming the subject of anything. I would be somewhere along the grass beside a paved route, within sight of water but not beside it.

Perhaps I would be the person who clears the smaller channels. Someone who knows that a basin can be full while a garden below it goes dry, because a root has entered a joint or a little collapse has occurred beneath a cover stone. I would have a narrow tool for reaching into those places. One end would be worn thinner than the other.

But that gives me a usefulness too quickly. It makes a place for me by assigning me a task, as though the city were waiting for a necessary worker.

I think I would have such work. I would also sometimes do it badly. There would be a conduit I had promised to inspect and had not inspected. Someone would have asked me twice. The second time, I would have disliked their tone more than I regretted the delay.

I would know the city at the height of its lowest doors.

Not its plan, exactly. I would know which stair has a shallow third step that interrupts your rhythm, which courtyard lets the wind reach under a cloak, which house has a sill broad enough to rest a basket on without asking permission. I would know a passage that appears to end at a wall until you are close enough to see the turn.

That is where I begin to find myself: in the last turn before the court with the table.

There is a small covered place there, scarcely a room. Two beams carry a stone roof between neighboring walls. Someone has put a shelf under it. Things borrowed between households wait on the shelf, along with things whose owners everyone assumes someone else remembers.

A lid without its jar. A bundle of stakes. A wooden scoop with a split near the handle.

I am standing beneath this roof.

I am not moving either.

My left hand holds a shallow basket against my hip. My right is raised toward the shelf, with two fingers already under the rim of a bowl. I have stopped at the point where lifting it will mean taking responsibility for it.

The bowl is not mine.

I brought it here several days ago after repairing a split in its side. The repair holds, but I made the holes too near the edge. That is why it has remained on the shelf. I intended to test it again. Then I intended to return it and explain. Then enough time passed that returning it began to require a different explanation.

Now I am on my way to the meal, and the household it belongs to will be there.

In my basket are greens wrapped in a damp cloth. Beneath them is a little packet of salt I have been saving. I have brought more than my share of salt because I am bringing the bowl late.

No one needs to know this. Someone will probably know it anyway.

Through the opening beyond my shoulder, I can see a portion of the court: the end of the table, one stone leg, the hem of the young woman’s clothing. I cannot see her face or the man pouring. From where I stand, the curve of water is hidden.

The empty place near the edge is for me.

I am not certain of this. There has been a conversation about where to put me, and I was not present for all of it. The small bowl might belong to a child who will sit beside someone else. The place could have been left accessible for the person with the stiff knee. Still, I recognize the folded cloth. It has a darn near one corner that I made unevenly and have been seeing ever since.

I live alone in a house with an inconvenient lower room. Water used to enter through the back wall; it no longer does, though the stain remains. I keep the narrow tool by the door. I have a good sleeping mat and too many short pieces of cord.

There is someone at this meal with whom I am easy, and someone with whom I have recently ceased to be easy. I had expected to manage that by sitting farther down the table.

Instead, whoever set the places has left room for me near the corner.

I would like to say that I am relieved.

I am also considering whether I can put the bowl down first and let its owner notice it without my having to make an announcement. This is a small evasion, familiar enough that I do not call it one.

That is the person I would be here.

Not the visitor who can walk among the motionless people. Not the keeper of the pause, or the one who understands why the mountains have retained their sharp edges. I would have no special access to the suspended drop, the bird’s landing, the thought behind the porter’s face.

My fingers would remain beneath the bowl.

There would be a little tension in my shoulder because I am carrying the basket on the less comfortable side. The damp cloth would touch the inside of my wrist. One of the leaves would have folded over the rim, its underside pale in the clear light.

I would be alive, with no more freedom from the moment than the fish under the cistern step.

Yet my life would extend beyond what could be seen there. Someone would know how I take my food. Someone would still have a knife I had lent them. A neighbor would remember a kindness I had forgotten and another would remember a slight I considered settled. The bowl’s owner would be capable of accepting the repair, criticizing it, or noticing only that the bowl had finally come back.

None of that would be resolved by my stillness.

Beyond the covered place, the table would have room. I would be close enough that, ordinarily, someone might hear the bowl scrape against the shelf and call out without getting up.

I would not yet have answered.
