At first I would not know whether the hollow around me was a room or a body.

There would be a wall against my back, close enough that every movement met it. Something hard would run along one side. Farther away—or only beyond a thin partition—a weight would arrive, remain, and leave. Its arrival would change the whole enclosure. Not greatly. A tightening, a different distribution of pressure.

I would learn that change before I learned anything about the weight.

There would be no silence in which to hear it. There would be the long trembling that never stopped, and within that, places where I could move more easily. One bend of a limb would be possible during a high note and difficult during a low one. Sometimes the wall would shake me out of a position I had spent a long time reaching. Sometimes it would hold me there.

I would have more limbs than room.

For a while, this would seem to be the ordinary condition of having limbs.

*

I think I would be inside the fourth plate above the root.

Not waiting in any purposeful sense. I would not know that the three plates beside mine were open, or that their openings were evidence of something I might do. My world would have a firm side and a yielding side. The firm side would carry the humming. The yielding side would grow warm, then less warm.

Between them, I would be learning to separate movements that had begun together.

One limb would straighten without the others. Two would catch against a ridge inside the shell. A little fluid would move past my mouth.

I would swallow.

Later, there would be less fluid.

The weight outside would sometimes touch the edge of my enclosure. That touch would have a shape unlike the wind: pressure in two close places, a pause, then release. I would press outward without knowing which part of me ought to press.

Nothing would happen.

The weight would go away.

*

I would not open during the deepest afternoon humming, although that is when I would move most.

It would happen in one of the smaller changes afterward.

A shutter would settle somewhere beyond the porch. The root would draw a fraction tighter against the step. A seam I had been pushing without knowing it was a seam would cease to hold.

One part of the wall would move away.

I would stop.

For the first time, an effort would fail to meet resistance. A limb would extend into a space that did not give it back.

The air would dry it.

I would draw it in again.

Then put it out.

The first hooked filament to find the root would catch in a flaw too small to hold dust. Another would miss. I would hang partly outside the plate, unable to reverse the order of my movements, with several useless limbs still folded beneath me.

It would take a long time to cross the thickness of my own shell.

Below me, the pale creature would remain in its worn trough. Five feet on the ground. One against the step.

I would reach the root above its head.

Neither of us would move.

*

I would be smaller than the creature under the porch, but not simply a small version of it.

My back would still be soft between the ridges. My feet would spread too far on smooth plaster. There would be appendages near my mouth that dragged when I lowered my head, and over the next few days I would leave them behind in pieces.

I would have no useful understanding of the city.

A change in pitch would make me crouch, whether it came from a dangerous draft or from a loose leaf striking a tile. Sometimes I would crouch for so long that the danger, if there had been danger, would already be over. Sometimes I would start moving at precisely the wrong moment.

Once, a gust beneath the porch would lift my hindquarters and turn me around the two front feet that still held.

Afterward I would stay attached to the root through several changes of light.

The older creature would cross above me on its way to the wall. Its underside would briefly close out the dim green of the leaves.

I would raise one foot toward it.

It would already be gone.

*

My first food would not be in the roof garden.

Near the foot of the root, where it entered a crack in the plaster, there would be a little amber caught in a fork of white fibers. Perhaps it had formed there. Perhaps it had fallen from above years earlier and lodged where the drafts could not take it.

The older creature would never stop at that fork. Its mouth might be too broad to reach between the fibers.

Mine would fit.

I would touch the amber and withdraw. Touch it again. Press until a corner broke.

The fragment would be larger than I could swallow.

For most of the afternoon I would remain with it held beneath my front plate, braced awkwardly against the root. It would soften from the side nearest my mouth. A little color would enter me.

There would still be food left when I had to let it go.

It would lodge farther down.

The next day I would descend to it.

This would be the beginning of my range: not the porch, not even the whole root, but two places and the difficult distance between them.

*

Eventually I would find the underside of the step.

The older creature touched it from below. I would approach along the root and put two feet against its edge.

The humming there would be different. It would travel through the whole step before reaching me, with some of its sharper movements gone. I would be able to loosen four feet and still hold on.

Dust would gather on my back.

For the first time since leaving the plate, I would rest somewhere other than where exhaustion stopped me.

Above, people might cross the court. A heel would strike the paving stone, and a heavy disturbance would pass through the root, the step, my spread feet. I would flatten myself. The older creature would lift its sixth foot.

Then the daily humming would return to the places the impact had briefly emptied.

I would put one foot back.

Then another.

*

I would not be a keeper of the city. I would have no knowledge of the hands that repaired the mouthpiece, and no desire to restore what had gone.

For a long while, I would not even reach the workbench.

I would live beneath its level, close to the ground, where a splinter of amber could last many meals and the difference between two routes around a root was worth learning.

On some evening, the older creature would return and find me resting against the step a little nearer its worn place than before.

It would pause.

I would feel that pause through the wood.

There would be enough room for it to lower itself. Enough room for me to keep my feet where they were.

Above us, my opened plate would remain fastened to the root. One edge would chatter faintly in a draft neither of us could feel.
