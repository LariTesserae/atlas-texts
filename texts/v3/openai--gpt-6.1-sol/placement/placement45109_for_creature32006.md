At first I would be in the wrong place.

I would have followed a channel along the bridge-limb, keeping two feet against its raised edge, and arrived at the plate where travelers pause. Beyond it, the channel divides. One branch runs toward the packed debris; the other disappears beneath an overlapping piece of armor. I would take the second because something warm is moving there.

Not warmth by itself. Warmth passing through a narrow space, interrupted at regular intervals.

I would go in far enough that my back touched the upper surface.

Behind me, the channel would fill with traffic.

For a while, that would be all I knew of the hollow: pressure above, a rough surface below, and the occasional touch of a passing foot against my hindmost leg. No chamber. No summit. Nothing that could be understood as a mountain. Only a space with something edible at its far end, and my body making itself narrow enough to reach it.

I think I would be one of the small animals that work beneath the plates.

Not quite transparent. Along my sides there would be enough pale pigment to make me visible when I crossed a dark surface. Six legs, perhaps, though the foremost pair would do little walking. Their last joints would be fine hooks. I would brace with the other four and reach into places my mouth could not enter.

My body would fit the seam badly at first.

There would be a ridge on my back that caught whenever I withdrew. I would turn slightly to pass it. After many passages the ridge would wear down, or the seam would widen, and I would cease making that turn.

What I ate would come away in thin curls.

The underside of old armor would be soft in places, particularly near the edge where a new plate pressed upward. I would draw a hook along it, bring the hook to my mouth, and begin again. Sometimes the curl would be mineral. Sometimes it would be part of the animal. I would reject certain tastes and keep others. The rejected fragments would fall beneath me, where smaller mouths could reach them.

I would live well there.

The seam would protect me from feet and from the bright interval that occasionally crossed the floor. It would keep its warmth after the exposed backs around it had cooled. Other animals of my kind would arrive. We would work at different depths, moving aside when our hooks met.

Then, one day, the roof would descend.

Only a little. Enough to touch the ridge of my back.

I would pull free with three of my walking legs. The fourth would remain in the seam.

For a long time afterward I would stay on the outer plate, beneath the lip of an old pit. Passing animals would knock against me. I would hold still. Eventually I would begin to clean the stump with a hook, and later I would feed on what I had cleaned away.

This would be how the largest inhabitant became a body to me.

Before that, it had been a set of surfaces: some productive, some dangerous, some useful for crossing. Now there would be a movement I recognized everywhere on it. A tightening beneath my feet. A slight change in the spacing of the plates. I would feel it at one seam and retreat before it reached another.

I would find a new place farther toward the small head.

There the overlapping armor would be less worn. My hooks would meet firmer material, and feeding would be slower. But one of the great animal’s slender legs would sometimes pass below my opening, bringing fragments caught in its comb. When it paused, I could reach down and take what lay nearest.

Once, I would reach too far.

The comb would lift. My feet would leave the plate. I would be held among several bodies, some struggling, some already still.

Then the leg would turn against the stone, and I would find a surface with my remaining feet.

I would let go of the fragment.

After that I would wait for the leg to pass before feeding.

Much of my life would consist of such small corrections.

I would learn the places where new armor appeared. I would cross a joint before it closed. I would leave a pit when its occupants became too numerous. A newly hardened animal, descending from the capsules, might offer an unoccupied seam; I might follow it for a little distance and then return to the older body, where I knew how to move.

There would be others that returned with me.

When the deep heat began to rise, we would feel the sorting legs stop.

That pause would reach us before the gas. Beneath my opening, a broad foot would press against the floor. The plates would separate in a familiar way. I would withdraw my hooks, turn, and follow the underside of the armor outward.

At the lip of the seam I would wait while a pale burrower passed. Its body would keep coming long after its front had disappeared. Then there would be room.

I would step onto the old channel.

Travelers would already be gathering at the raised plate, touching it and turning aside. The bridge would lift beneath us. One of my feet would lose contact; the others would hold.

I would go toward the head, into the sheltered joint above it.

From there I could feel the roof stone resting on the shields. I could feel the great animal braced beneath the stone. When the mountain knocked, both would move, too slightly to disturb the smaller bodies pressed around me.

I would remain still until the sorting leg began again.

Then I would lower a hook into the seam and draw out the first loosened curl.
