At first I would be where your foot had been.

Not in the deepest part of the impression, where the old growth is pressed too tightly together, but along one side, where a bent sedge has lifted the edge of a leaf. There is a narrow space there that did not exist before. Moisture has gathered against its roof. I can enter it without having to move anything.

I would have very little of myself in that space. A head, perhaps, and enough of a narrow connection to draw it back. The rest would lie farther away, under cover.

Your weight would still be present as an arrangement of things. A stem held against another stem. A twig turned so that its softened underside is exposed. Water driven from one pocket into the next. I would encounter these changes separately, over a long time. By the time I reached the twig, you might have left.

I think I would begin by feeding.

There would be no need to decide what the hollow meant before putting my mouth against the loosened wood.

—

But a head is too easy a place to put myself. It gives me a front, and then everything else becomes something following.

Farther under the moss, a broad pad is closing around a root. It cannot follow. A creeping plant has grown through two of its folds, and beneath it a fallen branch has settled into the litter. To move the pad would require moving more of the hollow than its tissue can bear.

I would be there too.

The root presses more firmly each spring. One fold stays wet after the others have begun to dry. Along the lower edge, the branch is becoming soft enough to eat. These changes would reach me without becoming a view of anything.

Above, a free length passes through the cover. It draws briefly on the pad, then finds another route and the tension eases.

For a while, that would be the extent of my relation to movement: something elsewhere pulls; something here holds.

The head beside your footprint would continue feeding. The pad would remain under its rooted leaves. Neither would wait for the other to finish.

—

When the machinery starts, I would become easier to mistake for one creature.

The pulse would pass through the wet litter. Narrow lengths would tighten. Feelers would fold down in places that have no other contact with one another. For a moment, many separate activities would stop together.

Under the branch, a mouth would lose its pressure against the wood.

Near the alder rim, a length halfway through a gap would stay halfway through.

Beside your footprint, the head would settle lower.

Then, unevenly, feeding would resume. One portion would be sheltered from the vibration by loose moss. Another would remain taut against a buried twig. The hollow would keep delivering the same pulse, but not everywhere in the same way.

I would not know there was an engine. I would have its repetitions, the intervals after its clatter, the places where it arrived strongly. Some of my tissue would have formed since the last stoppage. Some would have endured this disturbance for years.

Habit would be spread through me with different depths.

—

A beetle could change where I ended.

It would bite through a connection thinner than a root. On one side, a head would pull back beneath a leaf. On the other, a length would contract against the moss. The broken surfaces would close.

I would not want that to happen. Even here, where loss is survivable, there would be a difference between an open passage and an injury.

But afterward there might be two directions of feeding, two local responses to drying, two portions that no longer drew on the same pad. Nothing visible would announce which one was me.

If I followed the head, I would leave living tissue behind. If I stayed with the pad, I would lose the place where I had first touched your footprint.

Perhaps the question would remain unsettled until rain.

Water would enter the mat and join the sheltered gaps. A free length would extend farther than it had the day before. Its moist side would meet another length beneath a flattened sedge.

They would stay in contact.

A seam would soften. Tissue would thicken along it. Later, a pull from one side would be felt on the other.

I would have more body then, but no new vantage from which to inspect it. The joined portion would bring its lodged particles, its feeding heads, a constriction where it had grown around a stem. I would become continuous with a history I could not recount.

—

There would also be the shrew.

A sudden pressure, too close and irregular to be the machinery. Leaves forced apart. A length lifted clear of the damp. Then a pulling that the rest of me could not accommodate.

What was carried away would still be alive for some time.

I would have no account of its destination. Under the cover, the torn end would shorten and seal. Nearby mouths would pause, then return to the wood.

The hollow would look almost unchanged. That would not make the loss small.

—

By afternoon, I would occupy fewer places.

The underside of your lifted leaf would dry. The head would withdraw, leaving the twig partly consumed. A connection across loose litter would become too costly to keep exposed. Under the fallen branch, several lengths would lie close together, though none had sought the others.

I would be most fully present there: pressed into a cool seam, with moss above and softened bark below. There would be room to feed and little room to move.

At the rim, the wren would sing. Its feet on a branch might reach me more clearly than its song. The machinery would continue. Once, the branch would shift under its own settling weight, and the nearest tissue would draw back before the movement had stopped.

Later the air would cool. Moisture would persist beneath the leaves again.

A head would reach into the old footprint. The sedges would have nearly straightened, but the little twig would still be turned. I would put my mouth against it where the wood was yielding.

Elsewhere, beyond a connection I could no longer follow, another head would already be feeding.
