At first I would put myself on the stool.

It is the place made for a body to wait. The wheel is within reach; the tiles lead to it; there would once have been a reason to sit there through the heat. I imagine resting my hands on my knees, feeling the slow motor through the soles of my shoes, waiting for the click that means something has finished elsewhere.

But I would have no work to do. Turning the wheel would be an experiment conducted on everything that has grown around it. Even leaving it alone would not make me the keeper of this place.

The stool can remain empty.

I would be lower down, where the tiles have retained a little moisture.

Not immediately beneath the center of a tile. That is a comparatively secure place: shaded, closely fitted, already occupied by the white roots. I would be near a corner, where the ceramic has chipped and the floor has dropped away by the thickness of a fingernail. During the cooling part of the cycle, water reaches this space. During the warming part, it withdraws, though not quite completely.

There is a stain there that looks continuous until it dries.

I think I would be some of that stain.

At first this seems too little to live in: a red-brown film between a root and a piece of fired clay. But there are differences within it. The side against the tile stays cool longer. The side against the stone receives a harsher seepage. A root lies across both, flattened by years of contact, and beneath its flattened side the film remains wet after the exposed margins have gone dull.

I would live at that margin. I would have no view of the chamber, no knowledge of the gray opening in the western wall. Light would arrive, if it arrived at all, as a slight change in what passed down the root.

The valve would reach me more clearly.

First a pressure change. Then water with a different balance of dissolved things. Then warmth, arriving from the stone below and the root beside me at different rates. The interval between them would be important. In one interval I could grow; in another I would have to spend what I had stored simply to remain attached.

My body would be spread over several such intervals.

There would be a narrow part of me in the chipped corner, a thicker part under the root, and an almost invisible part extending onto bare ceramic. That last part would die often. It would leave a trace that made the next extension adhere a little better.

I would not know the whole root. I would know the surface it offered: a slow leak of something useful, a pressure that changed when its tissues filled, the roughness of an old injury. Farther away, beneath the overlapping leaves, water would be arriving. Here, the first evidence would be the root swelling against me.

In return, what came through the floor would pass through my living thickness before reaching it. I would retain some substances and change others. There would be waste the root could use.

This would happen unevenly. Once in a while the seepage would be too strong. A strip of me would loosen, and the root above that strip would darken. By the next cooling cycle, the surviving edges would have begun to occupy the exposed place.

Perhaps I would be the whole film.

But the whole film would cross distances I could not keep together. At the far end of the tile, the conditions would be different enough to make another life out of the same beginning. Something thriving there might fail here. Even under this one root, portions could lose contact and continue separately.

So I would be a patch small enough that a single retreat of water could reach all of me. A patch with several edges and no permanent center.

When the moisture came from the floor, I would thicken downward. When it came along the root, I would gather beneath the root. During a long dry interval, most of what remained alive would be tucked into the chip in the ceramic, held in the residue of earlier growth.

That would be the nearest thing I had to sleep.

There would be no darkness in it, since there had been no seeing. There would be less exchange. The outer surfaces would harden. Processes that had proceeded steadily would stall with their materials still joined halfway.

Then a little water would return, and some would continue.

Not all.

I would become slightly different after each drying. A place that had once taken up water readily would resist it. A route across the ceramic would close. Another would open along the side of a dead filament. If there were anything like memory, it would be in these altered paths.

I would carry an especially deep alteration from the fall.

Not an image of falling. No chamber, no broken ceiling. Only a break across what had once been a broad, sheltered surface; a period when hot material entered too quickly; survival in a few compressed places. The present patch would descend from one of those places. Its preference for the corner would have begun before the corner existed.

For many cycles I would remain there, so inconspicuous that lifting the tile would seem to reveal only a stained underside and a fitted sheet of roots.

Then a root would begin to withdraw.

Perhaps the pipe beyond it had become too hot. Perhaps another route now supplied the leaves better. Its flattened side would lose pressure. A thin space would open between us, and water would gather in that space before it drained away.

I would extend into it.

The root would no longer shelter me as closely. I would lose more at each warm interval. But there would be room along its underside, and some of what it relinquished would sustain the extension. Within a few cycles, I would reach the tile’s edge.

Outside would be a short crossing of stone.

I would reach only where moisture held. Much of the crossing would remain bare. One narrow portion, beside an old grain of mortar, would stay damp long enough for me to pass.

There I would meet another film.

It would have come from beneath the next tile, fed by a different root. Our edges would lie together in the small wet space. Some of what passed out of it would enter me. Some of what I released would remain against it.

The warmth would return before either of us had spread far.

We would draw back along separate routes, leaving a little living material in the place where we had met. On the next cooling cycle, water would collect there sooner.

I would return to that place.

Above us, the tile path would still look broken. The stool would stand empty, and the wheel would move when the machinery required it. Beneath the chipped corner, I would be occupied with the few grains of stone between one shelter and the next.
