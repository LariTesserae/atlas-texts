I would be beneath a place that looks safe to step on.

Not beneath the deepest shaft, or close to one of the columns. Somewhere between them, under a shallow rise with three pools on it. From above, the rise would be unremarkable. One pool would have a fringe of dark leaves. Another would be almost closed. The third would occasionally hold a patch of yellow pollen long enough for the grazing animals to gather.

I would know those pools by their weight.

The almost-closed one would press against my left shoulder. The open one would cool the tissue along my side whenever fresh rain entered it. Under the pool with the dark leaves, there would be a root I could not withdraw my hand from without waking something lodged between its branches.

I would have been there long enough for my chamber to fit badly.

It would fit an earlier arrangement of me: a narrower chest, a limb that once bent in a different place. Roots would have thickened through the spaces I no longer used. When I turned, a fold beneath my ribs would catch against a polished knot, and I would have to let water out before I could pass it.

There would be no discomfort in this until the water began to fall.

Then I would become aware of every heavy thing inside me.

***

At first I think I would be one of the creatures that reaches up when a foot breaks through.

My palm would find the sole before the falling body had gathered much speed. Other hands would take the ankle, the knee, the loose fabric around a shoulder. We would spread the weight across ourselves and the roots.

But I would not be especially good at this.

I would hold too long.

By the time the others had found a sound part of the surface, my fine hairs would still be attached. They would pull against a cuff or a feeding tendril. The departing body would hesitate, tugged from below by something that had already done what was needed.

Another creature would touch my wrist.

Not sharply. Just enough pressure to change the shape of the water inside the limb.

I would open.

For a while afterward, my palm would retain the arrangement of what it had held. A buckle would leave a hard little square among the tips. A grazing animal’s foot would leave six shallow divisions that slowly drew together. Sometimes I would press the empty hand against my chest, and the small dark specks in my neck would rise toward it.

There would be things I could recognize only after they had gone.

***

My patch of country would move.

The first time, I would leave my chamber because the roots were pulling in two directions. I would come up through the almost-closed pool, lifting its surface in overlapping pieces. Rain would sit on my face. The wind would flatten the moss around me, and for a moment I would be exposed to a distance I could make very little use of.

A column would stand beyond the shallow rises. Its white leaves would lift.

I would turn toward it too late.

The patch beneath me would already be sliding. Water would run across the low edge, bringing loose roots, fish, part of a herd. An animal would press against my back without noticing me. I would spread my limbs into the turf, finding the roots I knew among roots that had just arrived.

Then the land would fold.

For several moments the familiar pools would be above one another rather than beside one another. My chamber would open to the gray air. I would see the inside of it for the first time: not a room, not even a hollow with a single outline, but a set of places my body had kept apart.

A pale stain would mark where my chest had rested.

There would be an old hand in the roots behind it.

I would not know whether it was mine.

I would grip the moving felt. The fold would settle. Water would close over me, and the open pool would come down against my shoulder instead of my side.

After that, I would sometimes put a palm into the old hand.

Its tissue would still be soft enough to receive my divided tips. Nothing would move when I touched it. But when a herd passed overhead, the roots would tighten, pressing the two hands together.

***

I would begin to leave more often.

Not far. Far would mean a difference in pressure, a different spacing between the pulses of the columns. I would travel along the underside until the root bundles grew unfamiliar, then wait with my head against them.

Others would come through.

One would carry a dark organ high in its back, so close to the skin that the passing water made a faint outline around it. Another would have one limb ending in a fan of blunt pads. I would recognize it by the way it gripped: two contacts, a pause, then the others.

We would touch.

Sometimes a tightening would arrive through the felt before the body that made it. I would lift a hand, leave it resting there, and several hours later something would press against it from the other side of a root bundle.

I would not always know which part of an exchange was meant for me.

A column restarting could fill the interval. An animal tearing up a mouthful of turf could pull a contact loose. I might hold a pressure in my palm until evening, only to discover that the creature I had been answering was no longer nearby.

I would put the pressure into the roots anyway.

Later, another body might stop there.

***

Once I would be caught at a root disk when the column began again.

There would be plenty of warning. The clicks would travel down through the black wood. The others would release. Their pale bodies would slide away into water that had become suddenly crowded.

I would stay attached.

Perhaps a strand would be tangled in my hand. Perhaps I would have felt something through the root and been slow to let it go.

The disk would rise.

Water would draw away from my back. For an instant my organs would hang inside me without support. Then the whole root mass would settle, and the limb beneath it would flatten into a shape I could not retract.

I would come free only after the pressure had passed.

On the surface, several creatures would gather around the damaged place. Their hands would lie against it while water entered the folds of my body again. Roots would begin to cross the darkened tissue.

I would spend a long time there.

When I finally went below, the limb would be shorter. It would divide readily on one side and hardly at all on the other. I would have to learn a new way to turn in my chamber.

The chamber would not accommodate it.

So I would lie farther out, with the old hand behind me and my damaged limb resting in a neighboring hollow. Small white animals would continue to use that hollow, passing beneath my pads. One would stop whenever the columns stopped. I would feel its tiny feet becoming still.

***

I think this is where I would gradually become useful.

Not as a rescuer. Not as a keeper of all the pressures that had passed through the ground.

There would simply be a place in me where a faint contact could remain.

The damaged limb would respond slowly. A tightening at its tips would take time to reach my shoulder, and longer to subside. While the rest of the country rattled and shifted around it, a small pressure would persist there.

Young creatures would rest against that side.

They would have recently left their mounds, and would still grip every root as if it might be the last one holding them. Their neck blisters would turn toward each thump. When the underground rush came, some would draw all their limbs close and drop.

I would catch one against my shortened hand.

It would remain there through the rush, pressing a little too hard. When the water quieted, I would loosen one part of my grip.

The young creature would tighten.

I would wait.

Eventually it would put a hand onto a root beyond me. Then another. Its weight would move off my palm, though its smallest tips would remain attached.

I would know that hesitation.

I would not pull away.

***

Years later, my back might be difficult to separate from the chamber roof.

The pool above it would narrow. Roots would cross the folds beneath my ribs. I would still be able to move, but moving would disturb more things than before: chambers occupied by blind animals, a young creature resting along my side, the route by which water passed into a lower shelf.

I would leave an arm loose.

Sometimes it would reach upward through the thin place where a traveler might step. Sometimes it would lie against the old hand, which by then would have become a dark patch threaded with pale roots.

The creatures moving through would touch my neck. A few would wait.

They might receive something. They might only feel the columns through a body slower to answer them.

One evening, a young one would arrive while the underground water was audible. It would press its head against the place where my chest still lifted, slightly. Above us, animals would crowd onto the rise. Rain would enter the open pool.

The young one would spread a hand against mine.

Somewhere beyond the roots, land would begin to fall.

I would close around its hand before the sound reached us. We would hold through the first rush, and through the long pressure that followed.

Then water would return to the chamber.

The young creature would shift. One of its limbs would find the root beside my shoulder. Its grip on me would slacken, though the last few tips remained.

Slowly, one at a time, I would let them go.
