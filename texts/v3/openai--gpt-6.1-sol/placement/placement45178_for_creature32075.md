I would be in the storehouse beneath the overhang, where the light reaches the floor but seldom reaches the back wall.

There would be no stock of finished things. A coil hung from a peg might contain three useful lengths of cord and several lengths being allowed to dry. The wedges would be sorted in narrow boxes with gaps between the compartments. On a low shelf, pieces of porous stone would stand upright, each on its own little bed of grit.

I would be sitting among them with one sleeve turned inside out.

The work in my lap would look like a tangle until I lifted it. Then it would resolve into a brush: not the small brush used at a doorway, but a long, loose bundle of fibers tied at intervals, meant to be passed beneath a bridge. Several of its strands would have gone dark. I would be taking these out one at a time.

There would be a bowl beside my foot for the removed fibers.

It would already be half full.

My left hand would be good at this. Four fingers, broad at their ends, with shallow grooves worn into the pale surfaces by the cord. My right hand would be newer. Its fingers would not quite agree about how far to bend. When I tried to pinch something fine, two tips would meet and the other two would remain politely apart.

I would have learned to use it for holding the bowl.

Beyond the overhang, the basin would shine. I would keep my work turned slightly away from it. Glare can conceal a wet fiber as easily as shadow.

Someone would come to the storehouse for a length of cord.

I would know them by the sound of a loose scale knocking against the back of their coat. They would wait outside until I had covered the work in my lap.

“Long enough for the middle bridge,” they would say.

I would take down a coil and measure it along the dry edge of the shelf.

The visitor would watch my hands.

“Not that bridge.”

I would stop.

After a little while, I would put the cord back and choose another.

There are several things I would know without having to look at them. How to find a weak place in a knot. How much powder a sill needs before more becomes waste. Which of the storehouse roof joints clicks first when the room is beginning to widen.

There would also be things I had once known that other people no longer asked me to remember.

***

My sleeping place would be against the inner wall, raised on short stone feet. At its head would stand a shallow bowl containing three pieces that had not been fitted into me.

One would be long and yellowed, with a polished hollow at one end. One would be a small curved flake, blue on its broken edge. The third would be so unremarkable that a visitor might mistake it for something set aside during repairs.

I would examine them in the morning.

Not every morning. Sometimes I would wake and begin work immediately, leaving them untouched until the room brightened. Sometimes I would bring the bowl into my lap and sit with it while the falls moved farther away.

The yellow piece would once have belonged somewhere near my shoulder. Everyone would agree about that.

The others would be less certain.

The person who brought me out of the central pool would have collected them together. There had been more pieces then. Some were used. Some failed their tests. Some had been returned to the margin from which they came.

I would not remember being brought out.

I would remember a narrow bridge from underneath.

Not the underside of its slabs. The bridge itself, seen very far above, with sunlight around its edges and a slack line of cord hanging along one side.

This memory would cause difficulty when I worked on bridges. I would want to check the cord where it hung below the stone, even after someone had checked it. I would crouch too far toward a channel. My companion would tap twice on the slab beside my foot.

I would stand.

They would not mention the pool.

***

My work would be cord because cord can be carried dry and made to hold things without asking them to touch.

I would take old bindings apart. I would tease sound fibers from worn brushes. I would splice short lengths into longer ones, keeping the knots staggered so they could not gather a single cold bead between them.

Some of the cord would have come from outside the basin long ago. The oldest lengths would be unlike anything we could make now: tough, pale fibers with a faint brown smell when warmed in the hands. I would preserve those whenever possible.

Other fibers would be drawn from the dark skins left behind where a stream had changed course. These would need washing, separating, drying, and washing again. A whole afternoon’s work might yield enough for two wrist bindings.

Nothing I made would be entirely new.

When the readable hour came, people would bring their small failures to me.

A sleeve tie that slipped.

A bowl sling that drew its knots together when damp.

A brush worn down to the binding.

They would sit on the storehouse sill while I worked. Sometimes there would be three visitors, their feet arranged so that nobody’s coat brushed another’s knee.

They would talk about a terrace newly formed beneath the fourth fall, or about the small one in the roofless enclosure that had accepted a jaw and then refused it.

I would listen.

The new hand would hold each thing firmly while the old hand worked.

Occasionally, laughter would interrupt me. I would have to set the work down. My mouth would open along an unfamiliar join, and the sound would emerge in two parts, with a little breathless space between.

No one would hurry me through it.

***

My name would also have two parts.

I would know the first.

The second would be spoken to me by the one with the broken jaw, and for a long time I would hear it as something belonging to the room rather than to myself. A little sound among the bowls and tools.

They would come most evenings, whether or not they needed cord.

They would inspect my new hand without touching it.

“Does that one still catch?”

I would bend the smallest finger.

“Only here.”

They would bring a wedge thin enough to fit beneath its second plate.

Sometimes they would sit without speaking. When they left, they would check my outer sill, although I could do that for myself.

There would be a second peg beside the door. It would stand empty.

I would have asked once why it was there.

“For a coat,” they would have said.

Afterward, I would not ask whose.

But I would keep the peg clear. If I hung a coil on it while sorting stock, I would move the coil before evening.

***

I would not be fearless about the water.

At noon, when the pools opened deep beneath their clear surfaces, I would work where I could see my own knees. If a bowl filled too far, I would empty it before looking into it. I would dislike the cold deposits under the displaced falls, though I would go there when fresh mineral was needed.

And yet I would be drawn to the gray branching sediment.

There would be a little hollow beside the storehouse, no wider than both my hands. The roof runoff would be diverted around it. It would receive its water from a crack too fine to see.

When the sun warmed it, threads would gather along its eastern margin.

I would sit nearby while separating fibers. The movement in the hollow would continue and cease and continue again. Sometimes, without thinking, I would set a pale chip on the dry bank.

The branching lines would come close.

I would take the chip away before evening.

I would not lower a finger into that pool.

Not yet.

***

One day the small inhabitant from the enclosure would be brought to me for a coat fastening.

It would have a head by then, though the back of it would still be held with temporary bindings. Its feet would rest on a folded cloth. An adult would keep one hand nearby, not touching.

I would show it three lengths of cord.

It would choose the shortest, which was too short.

I would lay a longer piece beside it and wait.

The small hand would move from one to the other. Two of its fingers would meet correctly. Two would remain apart.

I would turn my right hand palm upward.

For a while we would sit with our hands beside each other on the dry work surface.

Then it would choose the longer cord.

I would make the fastening loose enough to open without being pulled over the head. The small one would work it repeatedly: closed, open, closed. Each time the plates of its face would shift a little.

When they left, I would discover that I had not covered the work in my lap before letting them in.

The room would still be ordinary.

***

I think I would remain there a long time: under the overhang, repairing the things that let people cross a channel or carry a bowl without joining it to themselves.

Eventually the new hand would become worn. There would be a place on one finger polished by cord. I would stop calling it new.

Some evenings I would walk with the person whose jaw was broken. We would go only as far as the central bridge, checking the notches and the bindings while the valley briefly lay within reach.

On the way back, they might say both parts of my name.

I would answer before the second part was finished.

Once, perhaps, I would correct them.

A small change. A sound they had remembered slightly differently.

They would stop on the path and turn toward me.

The falls would be far enough away for us to hear one another.

I would say it again, leaving the proper room between.

Then we would continue to the storehouse. There would be time to put away the tools, to clear the sills, to set the three unused pieces beside my sleeping place.

At the door I would turn the long brush across its supports.

I would check that every hanging fiber was separate.

Only then would I go inside.
