I would stay near the place where the child tightened the tangle.

Not inside the house, at first. There is a shallow depression beneath its threshold, where a clamp once held the young doorway open. Growth has almost erased it. Water passes on either side without entering, and the warmth of the cooking stem reaches it after the household has gone to sleep.

I would notice that place before I understood what I needed it for.

From the path it looks too small to shelter anything. From underneath, it has a ceiling, a dry back wall, and two ways out.

I think I would approach along the underside of the root.

***

For some time, I would be mistaken for something caught there.

A length of discarded binding. One of the filaments shaken loose by a winged animal. Something worth removing before it worked its way into a seam.

Then I would move, though not enough to clarify matters.

My body would be narrow when extended, with more places to bend than a person expects. Folded, I could fit into the clamp mark. Six small feet would grip the nap without puncturing the living layer beneath it. They would release in pairs, so that there was never a moment when I depended on one hold.

There would be pale patches along my back.

Not luminous. Just pale enough to remain visible in the green light, like the exposed edges of old bindings. When I rested on a railing, people would sometimes look at me without realizing that they had chosen a nearby thing to look at.

I would have eyes, but they would not be the most useful part of me.

The path would reach me through my feet: footsteps, rain arriving, the slow adjustment of a limb after a binding had been loosened. A household would have a recognizable weight. I would know when one of its members was absent before I knew where they had gone.

The child would be easy to distinguish. They would take three quick steps, stop, and do something that made the adults call from indoors.

***

I would eat the soft growth around old wounds.

Not the wound itself. The damp fringe that develops where healthy bark has closed over a cut but left a sheltered lip. There would be plenty along neglected routes, less near the settlements, where people kept the joins clean.

This would give me a reason to travel.

I would go outward until the paths thinned, inspect a seam with the sensitive underside of my head, feed, and return. Sometimes I would carry a little of the growth back in a fold beneath my throat. It would keep moist there for several days.

I would also eat cooked fruit, if offered.

The first piece would be placed near me as a test. I would wait until the hand withdrew, take it, and retreat beneath the threshold.

After that, the child would leave things in the wrong place: too close to a drip, or beside an opening through which a swimmer could reach. I would move the offerings before eating them.

The child would conclude that I was particular.

This would be true.

***

I would not understand every word spoken in the house.

Certain sounds would become familiar through what followed them. A name would mean that one person was about to come through the shutter. A warning would mean hands leaving a surface quickly. The sound used for my presence would begin as a question and eventually cease to rise at the end.

The older person would call me *that one underneath* for years.

The child would try several names. One would be the name of a food. Another would belong to someone they disliked. Neither would last.

I would respond most reliably to a fingernail drawn twice across the threshold.

That would not become my name. It would be how they asked whether I was there.

***

The first useful thing I would do would be accidental.

The child would drop the small shell knife.

It would slip between the path and the lower rail, strike the root beside my shelter, and lodge in the fleece underneath. I would feel the impact and go to examine it.

By then the child would be lying on their stomach, reaching through the gap.

I would dislike the reaching hand. It would disturb the fibers and put too much weight on one edge of my ceiling. I would take the knife by its case and drag it away.

The child would make a distressed sound.

I would continue around the root, emerge above the path several feet away, and abandon the object where I usually crossed into the warmer growth.

This would be understood as returning it.

For a while, I would find unwanted things outside my shelter: a spoon, an empty knife case, a ball of cord. Once, a shoe.

I would return none of them.

The adults would laugh quietly. The child would be offended with all of us.

***

My own routes would include places the household had stopped considering accessible.

Behind the sleeping hollows, a stair would be closing. People could still see its upper entrance, but the lower one had become a seam no wider than a wrist. Inside, the steps would persist as blunt folds.

I would use it to reach a warm joint beneath the cistern.

There would be shell instructions there.

I could not read them. I would know them as cool surfaces hung against warm ones, with edges that caught loose skin during a molt. One plate would have a crack through its hanging hole. Each time I passed, it would shift.

Eventually it would fall.

I would pull it into the open because it was obstructing my stair.

The older person would find it beside the cistern and spend the afternoon examining a bulge in the floor.

A water leaf would be turned. A binding nobody remembered would be loosened. The next rain would go somewhere else.

Afterward, that person would leave me fruit.

I would have no reason to connect these events. I would eat it while it was still warm.

***

There would be a season when I did not return.

A route beneath the settlement would lead to a bowed bridge whose upper surface had become difficult to cross. Its underside would be rich with loosened fibers. Other creatures like me would gather there.

I would not be alone in the world, nor the last of something.

Among my own kind, I would be ordinary in ways the household could not observe. Perhaps slow to approach a feeding place. Perhaps unusually willing to rest near the pressure of human footsteps. My pale markings would distinguish me no more than the shape of a grazer’s back distinguished it from another.

I would molt beneath the bowed bridge.

For several days my feet would not hold properly. I would stay in a fold with the others, moving only to drink. My old covering would remain caught above us, empty feet still gripping.

When I came back, I would be longer.

The depression beneath the threshold would no longer fit me comfortably.

I would spend two nights trying to arrange myself there before moving to the lower side of the railing.

The child would find the empty shelter and put a finger inside. Then they would see me, touch the pale mark nearest my head, and withdraw when I shifted away.

They would have grown too.

***

I would never become responsible for the bridge.

I would cross the tip-tender’s working place often. I would feel the pressure of the cord through the stem and avoid the freshest cuts. If a binding developed a damp fringe, I might clean one edge of it. If the shoot changed direction, I would alter my route.

These would be things I did for reasons of my own.

But over time the tender would learn to notice where I stopped.

Once, they would find me feeding beneath a wedge that should have remained dry. They would remove it, inspect the tissue, and begin the work again farther along.

Another time I would pause at a perfectly healthy join.

They would spend a long while examining it, then tell me, with some irritation, to move.

I would move.

There would be no reliable method of consulting me.

***

Years later, the child would be able to untie the first knot.

They would come out with the lever and a coil of cord, although this would not mean they had agreed to spend their life tending tips. Someone would be ill. Or away. The work would need doing that evening.

I would be resting on the railing.

They would set the lever down beside me and examine the binding. The folded instruction would be difficult to free. They would pull, stop, and look back toward the house.

No one would come out.

At last they would work it loose without tearing it.

While they read, I would cross onto their sleeve. Not to offer guidance. The air would have cooled, and the cloth over their wrist would be warm.

They would wait until I had passed before lifting the lever.

Beyond us, the corresponding shoot would still be too distant to distinguish from the growth around it. Rain would occupy part of the gap. The person tending the other end might already have finished for the day.

On our side, the cord would need shortening by less than the width of my body.

The child, no longer a child, would make the adjustment. Then they would rest a hand on the rail and check the knot once more.

I would settle beside that hand, with all six feet attached to something living.
