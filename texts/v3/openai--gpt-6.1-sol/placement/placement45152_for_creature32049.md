At first I put myself beside the threshold, in the space the resident leaves.

It is an easy place to imagine being welcome. There is dry stone behind me, warm water at my feet, and someone nearby who will not require me to explain why I have come. I could learn the crossing. I could learn which sounds belong to animals and which belong to leaves releasing their weight of water.

But I keep finding myself lower down.

The groove is too large. The resident’s resting hand is not beside mine; it is a surface I could shelter beneath. When it shifts its flank to let the moisture drain, that drainage passes me with the force of a channel changing course.

I think I would be small.

Not one of the winged things. I would want to remain in contact with something. Flight here would mean surrendering too much information at once.

I would live where the threshold stops being dry.

***

There must be a very narrow boundary there. On one side, water beads and gathers. On the other, a wet hand comes away dry. Whatever keeps the shelter dry has worked for a long time, and things have had time to settle along its edge.

I would be one of those things.

My body would be a short, folded length, soft through the middle, with firmer margins. When extended, I might cover the last joint of one of the resident’s fingers. When disturbed, I could draw my margins together until I felt like a piece of tough root.

There would be no face at the leading end. Either end could lead.

Along my underside, small pads would take turns fastening to the stone. I would move by shortening the spaces between them. This would be slow even by the basin’s standards. Crossing the width of the doorway might occupy most of a wet period, with several rests.

I would have a thin internal chamber.

That seems important. Not a shell exactly, but a space held open within me by two flexible ribs. Pressure in the floor would alter its shape. The knocks from the bank would arrive through my attached pads and make the ribs touch, briefly, at different places.

I would feel a call as a succession of contacts inside my body.

The distant weather would be less distinct: a long pressure against everything, impossible to separate from the pressure of being alive.

***

At first I would feed on what gathers at the boundary.

There would be small residues left where water ceased to be water on the surface of the stone. Films, salts, something my ancestors had learned to use without knowing what supplied it. I would loosen these with the edge of my body and take them in through a seam underneath.

That might explain why I stayed.

It would not explain why I sometimes crossed into the shelter, where there was nothing obvious to eat.

Perhaps the dry ledge was useful when my internal chamber became clogged. Perhaps I needed a place where the two ribs could separate fully. I would climb only a little way inside and remain there until some discomfort passed.

The resident would know my route before I knew it had noticed me.

One evening—or whatever stretch of time we had both learned to distinguish—it would place a fibrous strip along the wall. The knotted end would rest on the ledge. The free end would reach almost to the threshold.

I would discover it by contact.

The fibers would hold my pads more readily than smooth stone. I would climb them, stop, descend, climb again. On the ledge I would find a loose coil whose turns offered several widths of shelter.

I would not know whether the strip had been put there for me.

For a long time, I would live on it.

***

This is where I become less certain what I am.

The strip might not simply be something I inhabit. After enough time, its fibers might pass through my margins. My feeding seam might work along them. Parts of it would soften where I rested, while other parts remained hard.

If the resident lifted it, I would come with it.

If it laid the strip against the threshold, I could feel the stone through more than my own small body. If it hung the strip from a root, I could distinguish the tightening before a leaf bed exhaled.

I would still be an animal, I think. Hungry, capable of discomfort, able to withdraw. But my shape would include a length of handled fiber that I could neither make nor carry.

There might be others like me in the bundle.

We would not necessarily know one another. A strip could remain still for a very long time and yet be occupied. Some of the hardened lengths might be dormant. Some might truly be empty.

The resident would handle each individually.

I would learn the approach of its fingers through the ledge. Before they reached me, their weight would draw the fibers taut. I could loosen my pads if I did not want to be lifted. Usually that would be enough; the resident would set the strip down again.

Sometimes I would stay attached.

***

I would have a sound, though it would take me a while to discover that it was a sound.

When my internal ribs touched, I could hold them together and release them sharply. On the wet threshold, this would scarcely travel. Along a stiff fiber resting against an empty shell, it might make a small, definite tick.

The first time the resident answered, I might contract all at once.

Its nail against the shell would be enormous.

It would wait.

Eventually I would open again.

I do not think we would develop a language that let me explain the basin. I would not know what stood in the socket ring. I would not remember who installed the buried supply. If I came from anything old, that history would have reached me as anatomy: a useful rib, a tolerance for some dissolved substance, an urge to withdraw under a particular pressure.

But we might acquire a few reliable exchanges.

The resident would touch the shell once before lifting the strip.

I would answer if my pads were secure.

If I released them instead, it would leave me where I was.

That would be enough to begin with.

***

When the drops arrived together, I would close.

The event would press every part of my internal chamber at once. There would be no sequence to follow, no separate contacts. For a little while, I would be unable to distinguish a touch from outside from the movements by which I kept myself attached.

Afterward, I would remain folded along the strip.

If the resident came into the room and lay down, its weight would reach me through the ledge and wall. Gradually there would be separate things again: a hand settling, a limb being drawn closer, breath held and released.

I might make one tick.

It would not mean that I understood what had happened. It would mean my ribs could move again.

The resident might touch the ledge near me, or it might sleep.

***

So I would be found just inside the doorway, on the free end of one of the knotted strips.

Not always. Sometimes I would be feeding at the wet boundary. Sometimes the resident would carry me to a root and lay my strip along it, then leave me there through several slow tightenings of the stone. I might return with a new crease in my body, or with a few fibers loosened enough to admit water.

A visitor could mistake me for a knot.

If they tried to untie me, I would hold still at first, then release every pad at once. The strip would slip from their fingers. The resident might retrieve it and place it on the ledge without offering any explanation.

I would not need the whole hollow to depend on me.

There would be feeding, attachment, the occasional journey in a hand. There would be the considerable work of learning when to let go.

And when the resident settled beside the old groove, I might be on the strip beneath its palm. Its fingers would rest loosely enough that I could move.

I would take a long time getting to the outer edge.

Once there, I would fasten to the warm stone and open my small chamber to the calls passing around the rim.
