At first I would look for myself behind the lowest row of vessels, where the body is thick enough to keep water through a dry interval. There is room there to loosen, to brace, to hold something in reserve.

But I think I would be farther out.

Not at the advancing edge, either. I would be somewhere growth has already passed through: a short length beneath an old mounting plate, connected at one end to a living sheath and at the other to a route that no longer receives water.

The plate would have four bolts. One would turn freely without coming out. Behind it, the ceramic would bear the impression of a washer that has been gone longer than anyone remembers. Water would reach me by following the bolt’s thread, though only when the line above was cool.

I would not occupy the whole space. There would be a dry corner I could never enter, and a patch of oil beneath the lower bolt that I had sealed away. My body would be a little thicker beside that patch. Closing it off would have cost me something.

For a long time, that might be all I was: a small wet chamber with two unequal connections.

One connection would carry the room’s distant changes. The other would carry almost nothing.

Almost.

***

The dry route would once have led toward the access hatch.

I would not know about a hand. I would receive a broad warming, gentle at its onset, followed by a pressure that altered the fine tremor of the casing. Sometimes the warming would last through several knocks from inside the vessel. Sometimes it would end before I had filled the outer channels.

Repeated often enough, it would have changed my growth.

A passage that merely admitted water would become a chamber capable of holding it. Tissue would thicken on the colder side. A fold would develop where there had been no fold, so that the small arrival could be retained after the warmth withdrew.

The worker would leave at the end of a shift. My chamber would empty.

Then they would stop coming.

I would continue to receive other things: the opening valve, the rain, a young growth joining a sheath several feet away. None would arrive in quite the same combination. Over time, the route toward the hatch would narrow.

I would keep the chamber.

It would not be a memory in the shape of an event. There would be no hand preserved inside me, no image of a person descending the stairs. There would be a capacity left over from their visits. A place able to fill more quickly than it needed to, and to empty more slowly.

That is where I would find myself.

***

I would have little authority over what passed through me.

When the folded body behind the vessels needed water, my partitions would yield. When a nearby section became too warm, I would receive what it surrendered, if I had space. A contraction arriving through the established sheath could draw me tight before the mounting plate itself changed temperature.

But the narrow route toward the hatch would answer according to conditions at its own end. Sometimes it would send back a weak tightening. Sometimes nothing.

I would live between those responses.

There might be another small body along that route, disconnected except during the wettest weeks. Or the tightening might come from my own tissue, made slow by the thinness of the passage. Nothing available to me would settle the difference.

If I sent water outward and some returned, I could continue.

***

When the rain shifted, I would be poorly placed.

The new channel would feed growth beneath a neighboring tank. That growth would become thicker, more responsive. Water would pass through it that had once passed through me.

At first I would make the old route easier. I would soften a partition, release a little grit, spend some of the water held in my chamber. For a while, this would work. Then the ceiling seam would warm again, and the bolt would stay dry.

The larger body would begin withdrawing from the sheath at my connected end.

I would feel that as an absence of small movements. No answering tension when the vessel knocked. No brief filling when the pressure changed.

I would close the passage before all my water followed it out.

Under the plate, I would become a smaller thing.

The tissue nearest the oily patch would harden. The chamber made by the worker’s visits would partly collapse. Between its folded walls, a little water would remain, unavailable unless I damaged the chamber to take it.

Eventually I would do that.

There would be no decision as a visitor understands one. A partition would thin over several hot days. One surface would touch another. Water would move, and the old fold would fail to open again.

***

I would still have the bolt.

During a cooler interval, moisture would descend its thread and arrive against my upper skin. Not much: enough to soften the margin, enough to let a pale edge advance toward the space left by the missing washer.

Beyond that space would be a joint I had never occupied.

I would grow into it slowly. My new tissue would be thinner than the old, with less mineral in its skin. Dust would adhere to it. Somewhere beyond the plate, a drop would strike, and I would receive the impact through ceramic instead of steel.

That difference would become part of what I could respond to.

Perhaps I would eventually meet another sheath. Perhaps I would remain alone behind the mounting plate, replenished a few times each year.

And if someone steadied a hand against the metal while working on the loose bolt, the warming would reach a body no longer shaped to retain it.

A little water would gather beneath their palm.

When they moved away, some would remain.
