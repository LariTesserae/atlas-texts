At first I would choose the old clearing.

I would mistake the weaker sound for a reason to stay there. I would find a place where the tool marks had made a seat, put my hands flat beside me, and watch the underside of the mountain. It would be possible to sit for a long time without knowing whether I was waiting for it to move or waiting to become accustomed to it.

Someone would eventually need the seat.

They would not ask me to leave. They would bring an old person whose fingers were folded around a smooth wedge, help them sit beside me, and begin arranging a tray at their feet. I would shift over. Later, when another visitor arrived, I would shift again.

The clearing would turn out to be less empty than it looked.

I think I would leave before I learned anyone’s name.

Not far. Only to the first shelter on the path where the markers began to draw together.

***

There would be a hanging in that shelter which did not quite reach the floor.

I would notice it because, seated behind it, I could feel the sound against my ankles. The rest of me would be comparatively quiet. I would tuck my feet beneath the bench, then lower them again to see whether the difference remained.

The hanging would have been made for a room of another height.

I would try to fix it by adding a strip along the bottom. This would make the pocket worse. A narrow shriek would acquire a definite position beside my head, and I would spend several intervals of darkening turning the strip over, shortening it, moving it a little to the left.

The person who used the shelter would return while I was doing this.

They would stand inside the doorway with a bundle of stalks under one arm. I would hold up the offending strip, as though its difficulty might be apparent.

They would set down the stalks, undo my work, and put the hanging back where it had been.

Then they would move the bench.

This would be my first useful instruction.

I would not yet be good at receiving instructions. I would want to know how far to move it next time, whether the hanging ought to be longer, whether the doorway was responsible. They would take my wrist and rest my hand against the wall.

For a while I would feel nothing but the enormous effort of the mountain.

Then, beneath it, a small uneven pressure.

The bench had been touching the wall at one corner.

***

I would have a body like theirs, although I would learn this inconveniently.

Until then, I would have understood myself as narrow, cold-handed, reasonably able to carry things. On old stone these would be sufficient facts.

On the path beyond the shelter, I would take too much care over a crossing. I would look down at my legs to see what they were doing.

My foot would arrive. My knee would not quite finish arriving with it.

The injury would be unimpressive to look at. No blood, no swelling. I would be able to stand, but every attempt to take another step would require a correction somewhere I could not locate.

Two people would help me back.

For some time I would sit against a slab while one of them held the side of my knee. They would take turns. The owner of the shelter would give me a folded leaf to occupy my fingers, but I would keep opening it too far and lose the little shape it was supposed to make.

I would dislike being helpless. More than that, I would dislike how little there was to do about it.

The person pressing my knee would grow tired. I would feel the pressure change and turn sharply toward them.

They would look annoyed, adjust their hand, and continue.

Later I would understand how much restraint that required.

***

I do not think I would become a maker of fine wedges.

I would sort them well enough. I would learn to reject a shaving without biting down too hard, mend a cradle strap, carry finished stone without resting it on ground that might take an interest in its shape.

But the work I would become good at would be holding a joint in place.

There would be no particular moment when this became my work. Once I could walk again, I would remain for a while to help the person who had helped me. A visitor would arrive with a wrist that no longer lay comfortably against the conversation slab. Someone else would need to go and check a roof. I would be shown where to put my palm.

It would be necessary to keep the pressure firm without increasing it.

At first my arm would shake. I would brace it badly and leave a deep ache in my shoulder. Later I would learn how to sit close enough that very little effort was needed, and how to move when the other person needed to move without letting go.

There would be corrections so slow that I could not feel them occurring.

There would also be the moment when a tendon slipped under my thumb and the injured person suddenly opened all their fingers.

I would learn not to take my hand away then.

That would be harder than learning to apply the pressure.

***

My shelter would be beside a modest grove, not in it.

A low branch would extend over one corner of the roof. Its leaves would take warmth from the room beneath, so I would use that room for food and spare straps. Every so often I would consider cutting the branch. The person who knew the plant would advise against it, and I would complain about the cold instead.

Behind the third hanging I would keep a broad seat with a finished slab along its back.

There would be little objects within reach: two knotted cords, a wedge polished by handling, a stalk with one end split into several tongues. One of the tongues would be shorter than the others. People would turn it toward themselves without noticing.

I would have a cup with a poor rim.

A better cup would stand beside it for visitors. Occasionally someone would choose mine, and I would feel a brief, unreasonable irritation which I would conceal by rearranging the food tray.

I would not be endlessly patient.

Some people would come too late, after continuing to work through an injury they had already noticed. Some would describe each change in the joint before my hands could find it. Others would insist that a relative knew a quicker correction.

I would sometimes press a curt answer into the slab.

And sometimes I would be wrong.

There would be a person I disliked whose hand rested very easily in mine. There would be a friend I found difficult to help because they kept apologizing whenever they needed to shift.

These things would make the work less simple than the pressure itself.

***

Names would take me longer.

I would first know people by the way they settled beside me. One would lean against the stone immediately; another would remain upright until invited to rest. A child would hold out an injured finger and watch my face rather than my hand.

My own name would be a movement I had difficulty recognizing when someone new made it.

I would wonder, at first, whether it meant something about the shelter or the work. Eventually I would ask.

The answer would take several attempts to understand. It would be only a name. There would be someone on the far rim with a similar one, a person who repaired carrying frames and was apparently very stubborn.

I would be pleased, and a little disappointed.

***

The old clearing would become familiar to me after all.

I would go there to relieve other hands.

An elderly person might need pressure maintained at the shoulder while a relative ate. I would sit down, fit my hand beside the relative’s, and wait until they were satisfied that I had the place.

Sometimes the old person would want conversation. Sometimes they would want me to stop moving my thumb.

I would come to know the shallow marks near each seat. A groove that held a tray badly. A flattened patch made by someone who had since died. Work begun to occupy a long visit, then improved by the next visitor, then left alone.

I would add very little.

One day I would bring the split stalk from my shelter for an old person whose fingers had become too dull for the folded leaves. They would handle it for a while and tell me that the uneven ends were irritating.

I would shorten the others.

Back at home, everyone who picked it up would notice the change.

***

As the gap narrowed, some crossings would become easier.

People who had always arrived tired would arrive with enough strength to talk. A household from farther along the valley would begin using my spare pocket because one shelter on their journey was now entirely dark.

There would be fewer injuries on certain paths.

I would welcome this. I would also find myself spending more time outside, examining the violet growth beneath the low branch. Its new fans would be smaller than the old ones. Food brought from the fresh slopes would have to travel farther.

I would prepare for leaving in an uneven way: straps bundled carefully, good stone set aside, the awkward bench left until last.

Then someone would arrive needing a place against the slab.

I would clear away what I had packed.

Outside, the doorway would begin to bind. A visitor waiting their turn would notice, kneel, and exchange the wedge for a thinner one.

Inside, I would move my seat closer. The person beside me would place their hand on the stone, then lift it and turn the palm upward.

I would take their wrist.

For a while, neither of us would need anything else moved.
