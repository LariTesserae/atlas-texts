I would be on the underside of the spoon, where the handle begins to widen into the bowl.

Not in the groove itself. The fit there is too close. There is a little irregularity in the shell, a place where the split followed the grain badly, and beneath that lip there is room for something smaller than a green bead.

For a long time I would look like a stain.

Water would pass over me whenever the spoon was lifted. When it rested in its groove, I would have the wall close above my back and the shell beneath me, with a narrow channel open at either end. The roar would reach me differently there. The shell would carry some of it along its length; other impacts would arrive through the wall. Between the two, I would learn when the spoon was about to move.

First the pressure of fingers. Then the small wrench as the handle came free.

I would flatten before the current found me.

I think I would have a broad foot, almost transparent, and a harder ridge down my back. Not a shell yet. A ridge in which a few grains had lodged and stayed. My mouth would be underneath, working against the seed shell so delicately that years of feeding might only deepen its flaw.

There would be enough to eat. A film gathers even on things that are often handled. Along the bowl’s rim, minute scraps remain after water has been poured away.

I would know nothing of the mountain’s summit. I would know a darkness that fitted me, followed by movement, followed by light.

And then there would be the other kind of light.

On the ridge, the spoon would tilt and empty. Water would draw back from its underside until only a skin remained around me. Through that skin I would feel cold unlike any cold in the hollow: uneven, entering first at my edges.

My foot would tighten. The softer parts would withdraw beneath the ridge.

Nearby, something warm would make the remaining water move.

This would be the small creature under the bark. I would encounter it first as a disturbance in the spoon’s last wetness, a trembling that stopped while the larger creature waited. Sometimes its short hand would touch the bowl. The shell would give a faint knock, scarcely anything beside the blows I lived among, but separate enough to notice.

I would grow used to that knock.

There would be days without it.

On those days, when the spoon returned to its groove, I would remain contracted longer than I needed to.

***

Eventually I would leave the spoon.

Not bravely. Not because I understood where it had carried me.

A film would thicken across the opening of my shelter, and I would feed along its edge. The spoon would be resting against fresh stone. My mouth would follow the film onto the wall; my foot would follow my mouth.

By the time I turned, the spoon would be gone.

There would be a pale hollow where its handle had lain. Water would strike my uncovered back. I would move into the nearest crease and hold there until the current changed.

This is where I would become less like a stain.

My ridge would divide into several low plates. My foot would grow a fringe, each small lobe able to hold while the others moved. I would graze the clear membranes before they whitened, leaving tracks so fine that the larger creature might mistake them for flaws in the forming stone.

Some tracks would end abruptly. I would lose parts of the fringe. Once, a plate would be caught beneath a hardening fold, and I would spend a whole afternoon pulling free of it.

Afterward I would carry the fold with me: a white chip fixed to my back, too heavy on one side.

The creature might notice me then.

One long finger would approach. I would close against the floor. The nail would touch the chip, testing whether it belonged to the mountain.

Then the finger would withdraw.

***

When the new wall formed, I would be somewhere near its lower edge.

The flying sheet would bring a brief darkness. Afterward there would be a new direction from which to receive the blows of bubbles, and a strip of fresh membrane running into the wrist-wide gap.

I would follow it.

Beyond the wall, the water would press less fiercely against my back. I could raise my fringe without having it torn sideways. Food would gather in little wavering folds along the floor.

By evening I would have crossed half the pocket.

Then the wedge would enter. Its tethered stone would knock down beside me, and the larger creature’s hand would follow.

I would flatten beneath that hand. The fingers would feel the roof and floor, passing close enough that their flush briefly colored the daylight reaching me.

Later, as the creature cut back the narrowing entrance, white curls would drift into the pocket. Some would lodge against my plates. I would work loose of them and continue feeding.

My track would pass along the wedge’s lower edge.

It would be a very small track. The stone would grow faster than I could eat it. The creature would still have to keep cutting, and the other wedges would still have to hold.

But when it reached into the pocket again, there would be one place where the membrane was thinner.

That is where I would live: just inside the opening, on the sheltered side of the wood.

When the small creature was finally brought through, its foot might settle beside me. I would withdraw beneath my white chip while the new weight stirred the water.

After a while, I would come out.

There would be room to feed around its toes.
