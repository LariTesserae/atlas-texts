At first I think I would find work in the washhouse.

There is a bench inside, beyond the reach of the ordinary flooding, where someone has left a ledger swollen out of its covers. I would sit there with the doors open, trying to record what arrives on the steps. A brass collar. A switch cover. Something threaded on the inside, too corroded to identify. I would draw each piece rather than name it. The drawings would be poor, but the dimensions would be useful.

Then a filling of the square would move two objects while I was watching. Not carry them away: move them a little nearer each other. I would cross out their positions in the ledger.

Eventually the ledger would become mostly crossings-out.

I can imagine staying in that room for a long time, learning the intervals between bells, putting my feet on the bench when the water came through. But when I try to remain there, the room keeps falling away from my attention. What holds it is the underside of the doorstep. The place where the stone has worn thin over a hollow, where water persists after both surfaces appear dry.

I think that is where I would be.

Not the whole creature. Not anything large enough to lie along a motor housing and receive its labor. A small part, left on the steps after the square emptied, still translucent at the edges.

For several days I would have no reliable grips.

I would make them wherever pressure suggested they might be needed. One would close on nothing. Another would fasten inside a pit in the stone and refuse to loosen, while the rest of me stretched away with the receding water. Getting free would take most of an afternoon. There would be no injury anyone could see.

Above me, the objects would be lifted and put down. Each arrival would travel through the step differently. Metal would give a short, close disturbance. The softened chair leg would arrive almost without one.

I would learn where it was by extending beneath it.

Its socket would be my first dry-weather shelter. Not dry inside, though the outer wood might become pale. Water would stay in the narrow gap around me. I would press into the old tool marks at the bottom, and for a while have an outline made by something that had once been useful.

When the larger body returned, I would know it before it touched me.

The water under the step would tighten. A fold would pass across the opening of the socket, taking away the light. I would loosen my hold and be gathered beneath it.

Below the paving there would be more room than I expected, and then less: a chamber beneath the washhouse, a low passage, roots entering through a joint, a pipe pinched nearly shut by something built after it. I would be carried through places whose pressures reached me long before their walls.

Sometimes I would spread into the larger body so completely that I could not locate the chair leg, or the steps, or the edge I had learned to contract. At other times I would pull together around a single grain of ceramic caught underneath me.

The body would continue around that small resistance.

I would return to the washhouse. Whether I came back by myself would be difficult to establish.

A worker would find me there while clearing silt from the lowest exposed step. He would tap beside me twice with the handle of his brush.

I would withdraw into the chair leg.

He would move it to clean underneath, then put it back with the socket facing the wall. For the rest of that day I would remain inside, feeling him go up and down the stairs. His left boot would land more heavily than his right.

On his next visit, I would reach out before the brush arrived.

There would be a narrow interval in which his hand and my edge rested on the same wet stone. He would wait. I would give way. Nothing else would happen, but afterward I would recognize his steps from farther off.

I would begin bringing things up.

At first, very small things: a washer held flat beneath me; a broken ceramic bead from some old electrical fitting. The bead would roll when I released it. I would spend a long time retrieving it, finally leaving it in one of the worn hollows of the step.

There would be a piece I could not carry out. A short sleeve of pipe, caught beneath a fallen lintel. I would return to it through several reversals of the pumps. Its inner surface would be ridged, with one smooth place where water still passed.

I would settle there, then leave, then settle there again.

When I returned to the chair leg, I would press farther into its socket than before.

The western wall would reach me first as an interruption. While I lay beneath the steps, the familiar pressure would slacken, and something elsewhere would begin drawing water against the ordinary direction of travel.

I would follow it on a hot afternoon.

At the foot of the wall, the older body would already be pressed into the brick. I would find a space beside it, hardly wider than a mortar joint. Above us the cups would begin to fill.

I do not think I would climb very far that first time. Perhaps no farther than the height of the worker’s boot. The brick would be close on every side, warm, yielding water slowly. I would remain there through one pulse, then another.

When I withdrew, the alley would have darkened. Water would be falling from the cups.

I would go back by the gutter, taking longer than I needed, stopping where a new pipe entered an old wall. There would still be a little daylight on the washhouse steps. The chair leg would be where I had left it.

At the bell, I would leave that shelter readily.

I would have found a grip beneath the square by then: a broken joint just beyond the passage through which the first water came. I would spread along it, small enough that the oldest workers might never count me separately.

The chain would move.

Before the seams above began to darken, I would feel the water arrive.
