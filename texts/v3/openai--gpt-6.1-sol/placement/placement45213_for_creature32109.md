I would be on the underside of a shelf, well away from its edge.

There would be no view of the surface from there. Above me, several meters of pale stone would gather the daylight and release it faintly through their thickness. Below would be water so clear that I could spend a long time mistaking distance for emptiness. The clouds would be visible only when a current bent the light correctly.

A ribbon of vegetation would grow along a warmer seam nearby. Its free end would lift when the water moved, exposing a patch of stone slightly darker than the rest.

At first, I think I would live under that ribbon.

There would be enough room beneath it for the whole of me. A bubble would press against the folded leaf. Around its curved surface, the mineral threads would have to take longer routes, and I would persist in the small discrepancies between them. When the leaf opened, I would become wider. When it closed, I would keep something of that width in the order of my contractions.

But the leaf would not stay.

One day its attachment would soften. It would lift without tearing, turn once, and begin its long passage through the sea. For a while, I would continue making adjustments for a shelter that was no longer there. The water would reach me too quickly. Several parts of me would fail.

What remained would find the stone.

There, running inward from the old attachment, would be a seam too narrow to admit the leaf, too irregular to close entirely as the shelf thickened. I would follow it a little way.

Something would already be living farther in.

***

I would first know the other by a correction that did not behave as expected.

I would hold a thread longer. The load it carried would pass inward, meet a change I had not made, and return along another route. I would release the thread. The returning load would lessen.

For several days, this would keep happening.

I would extend and encounter a refusal. I would narrow and encounter room. Sometimes the other would withdraw before I had reached it, leaving an interval in which my arrangement became unexpectedly easy to maintain.

Neither of us would be able to occupy the whole seam.

Its cold side would suit the other. Its warmer entrance would suit me. Between them, a small chamber would receive water through two openings at different pressures. The current inside would reverse whenever the shelf sounded.

We would meet there.

Under strong light, someone looking through the stone might see a fine band, curved and doubled, no wider than a finger. Most of the time there would be nothing visible. The chamber would seem empty.

I would spend a long time learning when to leave it empty.

***

The shelf would cool at intervals that were almost regular.

First, a slight tightening of its outer face. Then a low report traveling through the whole formation. Finally, cold water entering the seam.

The other would contract before the report arrived.

I would not. I would be caught repeatedly by the cold, lose my outer connections, and rebuild close to the entrance. But after many repetitions, the other’s contraction would become part of the conditions to which I responded. I would draw inward when it did.

At first this would merely prevent damage.

Later, I would begin to preserve the interval between its withdrawal and the sound.

That interval would vary. Sometimes it would be long enough for me to extend a narrow part of myself into the chamber and return before the cold. Sometimes there would be no room to do so. Once, the other would withdraw and no sound would follow at all.

I would remain contracted.

The shelf would stand unchanged in the water. Sunlight would strengthen and fade through the stone. The ribbon’s old attachment would disappear beneath a fresh layer.

Eventually, the other would extend again.

My own extension would follow more slowly.

Something about that needless waiting would remain in me.

***

Over years, we would alter the chamber.

We could not agree on a shape in advance. We could only preserve certain grains and let others join the stone. Where our adjustments interfered, growth would slow. Where they passed without conflict, the walls would thicken.

The chamber would become crooked.

One passage would narrow until only the field could move through it. Another would broaden into a shallow pocket open to the lower sea. Fine sheets of stone would divide the interior, each with an aperture displaced slightly from the one before.

A vibration entering from outside would arrive several times, altered by each surface.

We would respond differently to those arrivals. My earlier adjustments would become useful to the other’s later ones. Its later corrections would make room for my next attempt. Gradually, some sequences would require both of us to complete them.

This is where I would find myself most fully: in that crooked chamber, taking part in something neither of us could maintain alone.

Some of what mattered to me would lie beyond my extent. I would have to wait for it to return through the stone.

***

There would be long periods in which almost nothing happened.

A distant mountain would settle. A flake would turn outside the opening. Fresh mineral warmth would rise through the lower wall and draw a faint green growth across it.

We would extend into the same water without interfering.

Sometimes our boundary would shift several centimeters over the course of a season. Sometimes it would stay in one place so long that stone began to accumulate around it. Then we would have to change, little by little, to keep the passage open.

I would become good at this particular chamber.

Elsewhere, I might be clumsy. A narrow cold stream could undo days of extension. A newly forming ridge might offer more connections than I could use, each one changing the demands of all the others. I would retreat from such places and return beneath the shelf, where a report arriving through the third partition would be followed, usually, by the other’s slight release of pressure.

There would be no need to make that familiarity visible.

The chamber could go dark for months. We would still find our way through it.

***

Eventually, a storm would reach us from below.

The first sign would be warmth entering through the cold passage.

The other would contract. I would follow. But the load would already be moving through the shelf in an unfamiliar direction. Our chamber would sound once, sharply, and one of its partitions would cease to carry weight.

We would retreat along different seams.

For a while, the small sequences we had made together would continue. My adjustments would go inward and find their accustomed answer. Then some would return incomplete.

I would narrow.

The shelf would tilt. Through the opening, clouds would sweep sideways across the distant undersurface of the sea.

One final response would arrive from deep in the stone. It would be poorly matched to my correction, but I would adjust around it and hold.

After that, ordinary connections would answer me.

***

I would survive near the entrance.

Much of the chamber would remain, though a fracture would admit water where there had once been a wall. The crooked passages would still delay the sounds from outside. I would continue to respond to them.

For a long time, I would leave intervals unused.

They would have been occupied by the other. Closing them would make me more stable, but each time I began, some surviving sequence would divert the effort elsewhere. I would extend toward the warm lip instead, or spend the arriving mineral grains thickening a damaged partition.

The empty intervals would become costly.

During a colder season, I would lose enough of myself that I could no longer maintain all of them. One would close. Then another.

The chamber’s sounds would change.

I would learn them again.

***

New vegetation would eventually grow across the fracture.

Bubbles would gather under a small branching fan. One would remain for several days, caught between two leaves. Around it, a thread would hold after its work was done.

I would reach toward it and withdraw before making contact.

The new arrangement would fail twice in the next cold current. On the third arrival, it would persist.

It would not contract at the old intervals. It would extend into spaces I had always avoided. When a sound entered the chamber, its corrections would come too soon, disturbing connections I had maintained for years.

I would lose a little of my outer extent.

Then I would try a narrower approach.

There would still be a pocket beside the second partition where growth had never quite finished. Water would turn there before passing into the larger chamber. I would leave that pocket open.

For a while, nothing would enter.

Then a faint resistance would reach its edge, recede, and return.

I would be beneath the shelf, beside that return. The stone above us would be slowly thickening. Far away, a mountain would give its first low report, and the sound would arrive through passages neither of us had yet learned to share.
