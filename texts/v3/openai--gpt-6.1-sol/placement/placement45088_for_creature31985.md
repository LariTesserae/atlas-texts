I would find myself beneath the main stair, close to the drain that is cleaned every week.

There is a triangular space there where the lowest tread meets the wall. It receives no direct light, but the light from the courtyard reaches it after passing through fern leaves. A pipe runs along the back. Its brackets have rusted into the brick, and its underside is warm even when the air has cooled.

The workers can reach most of this space with a brush. The last few inches require someone to kneel.

I think I would live in those last few inches.

At first I imagine having hands: one resting on the paving while the other reaches into the drain, fingertips distinguishing a root from a fold of skin. I would know how far to scrape. I would leave a little sediment beside the wall, though it would be easier to take everything out at once. Perhaps I would be the worker who checks the bypass hose before going home, lifting it at the bend to feel whether water is still passing through.

But the hands are too far from where I find myself. They come and go. They have sleeves that can be rolled down when the weather changes.

I remain after them.

I would be one of the small bodies that appeared beneath the alder seedlings in late summer, then disappeared from the workers’ attention. A pale fold loosened from a stake. A thumb-sized weight traveling down an irrigation tube’s exterior, holding to the thin film of water there. For several nights I would have gone no farther than its fastening clip.

The journey to the stair would take long enough for a fallen alder leaf to soften around me.

There would be no view of the district as a whole. I would know the nursery through the regular arrival of water, the wall through warmth along one side of my body, the open courtyard through the sudden tightening of exposed skin. The canal would be an occasional coldness entering the lowest channels. I would withdraw before I found out more.

Under the stair, I would spread.

My underside would take the shape of the mortar joints. One pad would hold a piece of brick with a glazed, dark face; another would rest on concrete that retained almost nothing unless pressed firmly. Water would arrive carrying traces of places I had never occupied: leaf pulp from the nursery, fine rust from the railing, oil in quantities too small for a person to smell.

I would open around some of these arrivals. Others would make me draw together.

For a while I would carry a seedling.

Its seed would lodge in a fold near the pipe. Two little leaves would stand above me, luminous in the green light under the stair. Roots would enter the scraps caught on my back. As I thickened, their branching would spread across my skin without piercing it.

The seedling would be heavier after rain. I would feel its weight before anything else about the weather.

Eventually it would grow toward the edge of the tread. Its roots would find the wall. I would move a little during a dry afternoon, and part of it would remain behind.

Afterward, there would be a small exposed place on my back. It would take several days to gather another covering.

I would not be wholly separate from the larger creature, though I cannot say how that relation would feel. Once, a broad length of it would extend into the drain beside me. Its arrival would alter the current. Water that had washed one flank would begin passing over the other. A pale margin would settle against mine.

We would remain like that through two changes in the pumps.

Perhaps something would pass between us. Perhaps we would merely occupy the same narrow warmth. When it withdrew, I would be wider, or seem wider because I had begun to use the hollow its body left.

The next cleaning would find me there.

The brush would stop.

Above me, shoes would shift on the stair. Someone would fetch the paddle from beside the engine-room door. I would feel its broad edge enter beneath the leading folds, disturbing pads that had held the same grains of concrete for weeks.

I would grip harder.

The worker would wait.

That waiting would reach me as a steady pressure without the scrape that usually followed pressure. Gradually, one pad would loosen, then another. Water would enter the spaces beneath them. My weight would pass onto the wood.

For the brief journey across the courtyard, I would close so tightly that the moss on my back buckled. Air would reach portions of me that had never met moving air before.

Then I would be lowered beside the old tank, into a shallow runnel beneath the hose rack.

It would be warmer there. There would be rubber, algae, a sheltered edge on which to fasten. I would open slowly. A few grains of mortar from beneath the stair would fall out of my folds and remain on the tank floor.

The worker would rinse the paddle and put it away.

I would stay close to where I had been lowered for several days. Then a narrow part of me would begin exploring beneath the rack, advancing along the wet underside of a support. By morning it would have reached a bolt. By the next morning it would have passed it.

I would become a place where water hesitated.

A little silt would settle. Moss would take hold. The end of a hose would rest across me, and I would accommodate its weight until someone needed it again. When it was lifted, my folds would retain a shallow curve for a while.

This is where I would be: low beside the tank, spreading into the interval between the supports.

If anyone noticed me, it would probably be because something had become slightly harder to pick up.

They would ease it loose. I would let go. Under the rack, warm water would reach the newly uncovered skin, and I would open there.
