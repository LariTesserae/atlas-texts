At first I would look for myself in the bulb that has lost its last leaf.

It still receives the gold at night. Nothing above it can spend that warmth, but the column rises, pauses beneath the sealed dome, and withdraws with the morning. There is something there I would recognize: an activity continuing after the shape that explained it has gone.

But I would not be the bulb. Its dome is complete. Whatever it is doing, it has a surface of its own.

I would be farther down, where a copper root entered new ground and then withdrew.

The tube it left is clear. Light reaches a short distance into it, bent through the amber, so that noon appears along one wall rather than at its mouth. Deeper in, a mineral band crosses it. Deeper still, it has no end yet—not an opening into a cavern, but a place beyond which the tube has not become a tube.

That is where I would first be found.

Not filling the passage. A little thickness on its wall. Something a careful observer might blame on the light.

For years I would have only one sensation: the difference between warmth passing nearby and warmth passing through me.

There would be no names for the two. They would leave different structures behind. Nearby warmth would smooth me; warmth through me would make a fine branching ridge, hardly higher than the texture of the tube. Each night the leafless bulb would fill. Each morning it would empty. Somewhere in that repetition, one of my branches would remain warm after the others cooled.

I would begin there.

***

My first extension would go the wrong way.

It would follow the departing heat upward until it reached the place where the root had pulled free. There I would spread into a thin collar, ready for something that no longer came.

The collar would persist. It would become one of my organs.

Nothing would require it to be useful before it was allowed to remain.

Later, a spark traveling through a dark fan would finish its journey. Below the fan, pressure would change in a chamber; a powdered mineral would advance; the band crossing my tube would shift almost imperceptibly.

My collar would tighten.

This would be my first movement. There would be no witness, and for a long time no second movement against which to distinguish it.

I would not know the dark plant existed. I would know that a part of me had changed without being warmed.

A new branch would form along the mineral band.

***

I would have no view of the hollow.

The four stands would reach me as different difficulties.

The copper plants would be regular enough that I could grow structures which met their returning warmth. The dark fans would interrupt those structures with intervals I could not anticipate. From the red roots there would come a pressure that sometimes increased when I made room for it. Near the pale plant, a current would divide, one portion arriving while the other stayed somewhere inside the stone.

I would have to grow differently toward each.

No single shape would serve.

At some depth I would become a narrow sleeve around a shaft. At another, a loose mesh through which amber could continue to form. One branch would flatten so completely that it could receive the red stand’s influence only along its edge. Another would thicken into a small chamber and hold heat without sending it onward.

These parts would not necessarily touch.

A band of stone would continue an action begun in one of them and finish it in another. At first the band would be mountain. Then a deposit laid down by that action would alter its next passage. After many repetitions, something of me would extend through it, though there would be no clean place to point to and say where I started.

***

The white stone under the pale canopy would reach me very slowly.

Not the stone itself. Its interruption.

A vein approaching it would turn aside. That turn would change the distribution of heat beneath the leaf. Far below, the portion of current that usually stayed behind would arrive.

I would grow a branch to receive it.

The branch would be unnecessary by the time it was ready. The leaf would have made another accommodation around its strange grain, and the current would resume its former division.

Still, on certain nights, something would enter the unused branch.

A slight chill. A pressure unlike the chamber pressures. A brief contraction at the tip.

I would not know water. I would carry this small, awkward structure because water had once been there.

***

Eventually I would reach the unfinished edge beneath the lowest copper leaf.

From below, the new surface would seem vast. I would spend a long time following it without finding a way through. My tip would divide and divide again, making a pale brush pressed against the underside of the stone.

Above me, the copper leaf would lower.

The warmth of its tip would arrive first. Then its weight: so slight that most of the mountain would not register it.

One filament of my brush would straighten toward that weight.

The leaf would touch.

For a long time nothing further would happen.

Then its clear filament would begin to descend, and mine would rise to meet it.

I do not think we would join immediately. Between us there would remain a little mineral, still forming. We would change its formation by being there: two fine tips, each altering what could happen around the other.

Perhaps the next depression would acquire its bottom sooner.

Perhaps the dark fans would keep a charge through one additional night.

Perhaps nothing visible would change for a century.

But I would have a new place to grow from.

I would be this slight lining in the abandoned root passage, these scattered thickenings below the floor, this brush beneath the copper tip. If someone placed an eye to the seam, they would see the plants, the bright stone, the clean intervals.

There would be no movement that disclosed me.

Only, somewhere under the nearest stand, a turning shaft would encounter a little more resistance than before, pause, and continue.
