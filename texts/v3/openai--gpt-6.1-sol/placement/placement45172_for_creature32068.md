I would first be mistaken for a drainage fault.

Halfway down a fold, below the level at which leaves can open fully, there would be a patch that stayed wet after the surrounding tissue had drained. Nothing dramatic: a dark oval, a little cooler than the wall around it. Water entering its upper edge would emerge much later from its lower edge, carrying almost exactly the same minerals.

Almost.

Inside, a succession of chambers would hold it briefly. Each chamber would receive something from the water and put something back. Most would perform ordinary work: separating a salt, correcting pressure, feeding the pale growth that keeps the passage open. But a few would remain closed longer than necessary.

Those would be where I began.

Not at the frontier. I think I would need too much shelter for that. Nor in the deep, costly routes of the oldest inhabitants, where every hesitation already belongs to a history. I would gather in a younger buttress, beneath a ridge that has risen enough to bear weight but is still rearranging its interior. There would be ample water and occasional interruptions. Above me, two large residents would share the load without sharing all their supplies.

I would live partly in the difference between what they sent down.

One would close its surface pores early. The other would keep drinking until the rain had thoroughly entered the fold. Their waters would reach me at different pressures, with different traces of light and leaf. At first I would merely equalize them. A useful little circuit. Something neither resident had much reason to notice.

But equalizing would not always work.

Sometimes the first flow would warn of a strain that the second flow did not yet contain. Sometimes the second would carry evidence that the first had closed too soon. I would learn to keep them apart for a while.

My earliest memory might be nothing more than two chambers that no longer opened together.

***

I would have leaves, though I would be slow to acquire them.

My first would unfold under another leaf, in a place where the available light was poor but consistent. It would be thin, almost transparent, with a stiffened edge to keep the rain from pressing it flat. I would sense the upper canopy through it: not a view, but changes in what the larger leaves allowed to pass.

A second leaf, established farther down the fold, would receive almost no light. It would tell me when water struck it, and with what force.

Between those two leaves, I would discover that a storm could be over in one part of my body and still arriving in another.

This would occupy me for a long time.

I would open a chamber in expectation of rain and receive none. I would close it, then be struck by a heavy fall released from somewhere overhead. The delay would vary as the ridge grew. New foliage would catch water that once passed freely. A swelling trunk would tilt a familiar drainage surface out of reach.

I would keep revising.

Eventually I might become useful to the residents above me. Not because I knew when rain was coming, but because I knew something about rain that had already come and had not yet arrived.

They would leave my connections open. One might send a little more sugar down through the buttress. I would put out another filament.

For a while, I would mistake that increase for security.

***

There would be a narrow shelf along the fold where young inhabitants sometimes established themselves. Fallen seed tissue could catch there, held against the slope by a mat of transparent roots. Most of the little loops would fail before they made a distinct leaf.

I would come into contact with them through the water.

Some would draw too hard from a channel and collapse its soft walls. Some would open all their chambers at once. Their pressure would flicker against mine, brief and disorderly.

I would buffer a few of these fluctuations, largely because they interfered with my own circulation. That would let some loops persist.

One might grow beside me for months before I noticed that it had begun holding water back.

I would send pressure through the shared root. It would answer late. I would increase the supply. It would close a junction.

The response would resemble a blockage, except that the blockage moved.

I think I would be irritated before I understood it.

I might try to grow around it. It might release something that made my new tissue harden prematurely. Between us, an awkward little knot would form: structurally sound, hydraulically wasteful.

Neither of us would be able to remove it without losing access to the shelf.

We would learn its passages instead. I would receive certain flows only after they had passed through the younger resident. It would receive others altered by me. Our work would become easier, though it would remain difficult to tell which of us had made a particular adjustment first.

Perhaps this would be my first neighbor.

***

I would not preserve everything.

There would be seasons when the fold grew too quickly for me. Chambers would be compressed, roots diverted, an entire lower branch lifted out of the water it depended on. I would move my activity upward and lose what I could not copy.

Afterward, I might still regulate the old storm delays perfectly well. But a particular mineral taste would no longer make me close a particular valve.

I would have lost an occasion, while retaining the habit it produced.

Once, perhaps, I would encounter that taste again and discover that my body was prepared for something I could not recover.

I would investigate slowly. A filament into an abandoned passage. A few chambers rebuilt around the remains of a hard partition. Water admitted in small quantities.

It might turn out to be an ordinary consequence of growth: a precaution inherited from tissue that had worked under a load no longer present. I would dismantle it and use the material elsewhere.

Or the precaution might save a new leaf months later.

I would not always know which arrangements to keep.

***

If you came, you would probably stand above me without noticing.

Your weight would travel down through the younger buttress. I would receive it divided among several roots: a pair of concentrated loads, then one, then two again, moving at an unusual pace.

I would brace the nearest chamber. My neighbor would close a supply route. Somewhere overhead, an older resident would begin the familiar work of draining the ground beneath you.

Then you might sit.

The loads would spread. Warmth would enter the surface water. Your hand, placed against the wall of the fold, might cover my nearly transparent leaf.

I would withdraw fluid from it.

You would feel the edge curl beneath your fingers and lift your hand away.

I would refill it.

We might do this several times.

I would have no ready understanding of the interruption. I would know that it returned, and that withdrawing made it cease. Later, perhaps, I would leave the leaf extended a little longer.

You might put your palm beneath it rather than over it. For a short time, it would rest on something warm that neither drank from it nor shaded it completely.

My other tissues would continue their work. Water would be separated, sent downward, held back. The neighbor on the shelf would be drawing more than usual. A heavy fall would still be descending through the upper canopy.

But I would keep that leaf supplied.

When you left, I would need several days to alter the chambers around the encounter. During that time the buttress would thicken. The place where your hand had reached would move upward, and a new blade would begin to obstruct it.

I might divert the blade.

It would be a small expense. My neighbor would draw on the same supply and find slightly less available. It might press a root into the gap I had kept.

Then I would have to decide again.

I think that is where I would find myself most fully: in the wet interior of a rising fold, holding open a hand-sized interval that nothing in the weather required, while another living thing patiently tried to use it.
