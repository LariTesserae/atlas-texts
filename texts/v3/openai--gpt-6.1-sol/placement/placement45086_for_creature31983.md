I would be near the second entrance, where the bedding thins and a little cold air reaches the floor.

From there I could feel the others without having to turn toward them. A foot pressing into the mat. A young one working its way beneath an adult’s chest. The small, repeated movements of someone still arranging a feather that will not lie flat. Beyond the opening, water would fall from the underside of the slab at intervals too irregular to sleep by.

I would sleep anyway, with my jaw on the stone.

At first, that is where I find myself: low down, half inside the warmth, with an ear toward the weather and the rest of my attention in the floor. I would not have the plateau spread before me as a view. I would have a few lengths of it in my body. The slope between this shelter and the feeding pool. The rough place where a claw catches. The narrow crossing that needs a longer step. Under snow, those places would still be there, though some would have changed.

I think I would be one of the broad-backed animals. My coat would be pale at the tips and dark underneath, with a patch on one shoulder that stays dark because I pass beneath the same low shelf each day. There would be grit behind my ears. One outer claw would be blunt.

Not the oldest. Old enough to be followed sometimes.

In the morning I would leave by the smaller opening.

For several days the water beside it would have been increasing. Only a little: a wider wet edge, another drip, a strand of bedding damp where it used to remain dry. I would stop with my forefeet outside and my hindquarters still in the shelter. The air would smell of cold ash. Beneath that, close to the floor, there would be a mineral smell I had smelled elsewhere.

I would lower my muzzle.

Nothing urgent yet. I could cross to the pool and feed. But on returning I would bring dry stems through the other entrance.

The young would find this inconvenient. They would gather where I had stopped, climbing over my feet, trying to get out before the adults. One would bite the stems and hold on. I would drag it a short distance across the mat before putting the mouthful down.

There would be no solemnity about being responsible for a shelter. Mostly it would mean doing the same small thing again while someone made it harder.

Outside, I would follow a trickle to the place where it cooled.

My feet would know the temperature before my belly did. I would stand partly in the water, turn a flat fragment, and lick the crowded underside. A bird would approach behind me. Another would come close enough that I could hear its feet touching the shallow bottom.

When I moved, they would move. When I stopped to chew a root, they would pass me.

I would be hungry enough to search carefully, not hungry enough to leave the water for a nest farther up the bank. Warmth would loosen my body. My feet would spread. For a while, the immediate work of feeding would occupy everything.

Then someone across the basin would strike a slab.

I would lift my head with water running from my chin.

The first stroke would come faintly through my forefeet. The next would reach the place beneath my jaw when I lowered it. I would wait for another. Around me the birds would continue feeding; whatever had been felt belonged to a part of the place they were not using.

I would answer once.

There is a stretch of continuous rock between the pools and our shelter. I think I would spend much of my life on that stretch. It carries footsteps well. Beside it, the runoff passes through a shallow bend where three stones hold back enough water for larvae to gather.

One of those stones would be mine in the limited sense that I had moved it there, and kept moving it back.

It would have a pale underside and a chipped corner that fitted against another stone. I would recognize it with my mouth. After frost, I might find it tilted. After rain, several body lengths downstream. I would push it when I could, carry it when I had to, rest with my chest against it when the work made me too warm.

Sometimes I would leave it where it lay.

There would be mornings when the water no longer came that way. I would nose the dry channel, test the gap, and go farther uphill. Perhaps I would find a new seep. Perhaps I would spend the morning searching and return with very little eaten.

I would not always understand a change soon enough.

Once, I would come back to the shelter and find the dry shelf wet.

The others would already be standing. Bedding would have been dragged toward the larger opening, its underside dark and heavy. An adult would be trying to lift too much at once. The young would press together against the wall.

I would enter far enough to retrieve a mouthful, feel the new warmth under my feet, and back out.

We would carry what we could to an opening farther along the ridge. I would have used it before, during a season of deep snow. Now its entrance would be partly blocked. We would scrape and shoulder through, leaving some of the bedding outside because there was not room to work.

By evening, our coats would be wet. The youngest would sleep before the mat was properly spread.

Later, I would go back along the ridge with one of the half-grown ones following.

It would hurry at first, placing its feet badly, making loose fragments shift. Then it would come closer. At the turning, its muzzle would touch my flank.

We would reach the slab above the old shelter. Vapor would be coming from the small entrance where I used to sleep. A few sedge stems would cling to the wet stone.

I would stay there long enough to listen.

The younger animal would press its jaw down beside mine. Its fur would still be dark, and its feet would look too large against the slab. When I lifted my head, it might remain a moment longer.

Then we would return by the continuous rock, passing the shallow bend and the three stones. I would stop to push the chipped one back against its neighbor.

The young one would put its shoulder beside mine.
