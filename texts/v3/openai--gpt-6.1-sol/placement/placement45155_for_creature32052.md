At first I would be the person eating from the pale plate.

I would try not to scrape it with my knife. Once she had told me it had been a splint, I would find it difficult to forget the arm that had rested there. The food would slide into one of its shallow hollows, and I would turn the plate rather than chase the last piece across the black branches.

When the child disappeared, I would watch the doorway long after watching had become useless.

The woman would have to tell me when he reached the stone.

That is where I would begin: unable to feel something another person was waiting for.

***

I would not become particularly good at hearing through my feet.

There would be progress. I would learn the difference between a footstep on fitted tiles and a staff touching glass. Sometimes I would identify which passage someone had entered, provided everyone else stayed still. But a dropped needle, an animal releasing its grip, two people walking together—these would confuse me.

The children would discover this.

For a while they would perform complicated errands behind the partition and ask me what had happened. I would make a careful guess. They would come around the corner carrying objects that disproved it.

Eventually one of them would sit beside me and show me where to put my hand.

I think I would remain someone who needed the hand.

My work would grow around that limitation.

***

There are directions that fit into speech, and directions that do not.

“Keep the wall on your left” is useful until the wall folds back and you follow it into the passage you came from. “Take the cooler way” is useful unless you arrive carrying a load, flushed with effort, unable to distinguish the current from the cooling of your own skin.

I would begin making small things to carry.

Not maps of the whole underground. I would have no means of making those, and no confidence in anyone who claimed to.

My first would be a strip of discarded shell, its underside worked smooth. Three shallow cuts would stand for three places where a traveler should stop. A broad groove would indicate a stretch with enough room to change soles. At one end I would leave the shell rough: the route continued, but I had not walked it.

Someone would point out that the cuts resembled the marks used for harvested animals.

I would start again.

Someone else would tell me that a traveler wearing gloves could not feel my beautiful little distinctions.

I would start again.

The useful version would be larger, uglier, and much easier to make.

***

After some years, I would have a place near a narrow crossing where people already paused to inspect their straps.

There would be a bench, a low lamp, and a shelf beyond the reach of the foundation animals. Under the bench I would keep worn sole-pieces. People would leave them because I could use the bindings, or because a particular piece had carried them safely for so long that throwing it into a feeding bed felt abrupt.

I would make route pieces there. I would also repair straps, which would probably feed me more reliably.

A route piece would describe one journey between places people knew: the crossing to the needle-maker’s house, the needle-maker’s house to the broad scale bed. It would mark where to crouch, where to stop, where the wall could be touched without disturbing a colony.

I would learn to omit things.

A pretty fork in a mineral vein might be visible only while the lamp was held at an inconvenient angle. A distinctive mound might shed and lose the edge by which I recognized it. A notch in the wall could be excellent for one person and too high for another.

Before handing over a piece, I would ask the traveler to feel it and tell the journey back to me.

Some would resent this. I would remember the child answering impatiently in the doorway.

***

The woman would bring me corrections.

She would put a route piece on my bench and rest one finger on a groove.

“Not there now.”

“Has it broken?”

“No.”

“Then what?”

She would consider how to tell me. Sometimes she could. Sometimes I would have to go with her, carrying my staff and the thinner soles she still thought I should wear more often.

At the doubtful place she would stop. I would stop behind her.

I might feel nothing wrong.

That would be the hardest part of the work: cutting a warning for something I could not perceive, without pretending that I had perceived it.

I would mark who had checked the route. On pieces that had gone a long time without a new walk, I would loosen the binding at the end. Anyone taking one would have to bring it to the bench to be tied again.

There would be arguments about this. A man would say he had used the same crossing for thirty years. I would ask whether he had used it lately. He would ask whether I intended to make everyone afraid of walking.

Then, most likely, he would repair one of my wedges while we continued disagreeing.

***

I would keep the returned pieces.

The broken ones, especially.

One might have been split under a fallen load. Another would be polished almost featureless by a thumb. One would have a fresh warning cut across an older instruction.

They would hang separately from the pieces ready to leave. Children could handle them. I would show them how a shallow groove disappeared with use, how a crack could be mistaken for a deliberate mark.

Occasionally someone would ask for a piece belonging to a person who had not returned.

I would give it to them.

I would not ask whether they meant to follow it.

***

There would be evenings when no one needed anything.

I would sit on the threshold with a half-finished binding across my knees. The lamp would be turned inward. Across the passage, someone would shake a collecting cloth over a feeding bed; a few scales would settle on my work.

A seam-dweller would approach my bare foot, recognize neither danger nor food, and continue beneath the bench.

Then a traveler would stop beyond the light.

I would notice the pause before I saw the boots: the small uncertainty of someone trying to decide whether the ember ahead was a dwelling, a marker, or another person standing still.

I would put down the binding and announce myself.

If they had come onto the glass, I would not beckon them closer. I would take my staff and go out.

And if they asked whether I knew the way, I would first ask where they were trying to go.
