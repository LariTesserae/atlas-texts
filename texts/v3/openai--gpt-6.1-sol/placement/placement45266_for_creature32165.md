I would be just below a passage that no longer exists.

Not deep below it. Near enough that, when the passage was open, the weight of someone crossing could reach me through the floor. There would have been a narrow chamber there, with a dry salt bed at one end and two sheets of living tissue held apart at the other. A change in load brought the sheets together. Current crossed. Somewhere, a brace thickened.

Most of that apparatus would still work.

The passage would have closed centuries ago. Its floor would now stand nearly vertical, incorporated into the side of a production grove. My chamber would have turned with it. Pressure would come from the advance of new stems, from the tightening of racks, from shutters opening far overhead.

I would still receive it through the surface that had once been underfoot.

For a long time, this seems like enough of a body: a small instrument surviving inside an obsolete floor. Fed because I continue to give useful readings. Older than the things whose weight I measure.

But I would not be contained entirely in that chamber.

One of my connections would run through a wall routinely stripped for carbon. Each time the wall thinned, I would grow farther along its sheltered edge. Another would follow a seam into a cooler district and end among the roots of a ventilation flower. There I would have a little tissue capable of contracting. A third connection would have been broken so often that repair had become one of my principal activities.

I would have no map of these parts. Heat at one end would change the resistance at another. The chamber would fill slightly; the flower would close a fraction; a root would harden around a threatened connection. It might take an hour for the consequences to return.

What I knew of my extent would be the time between a disturbance and its arrival elsewhere.

Sometimes a connection would fail quietly. For several days I would continue adjusting for a surface that no longer answered. Then the tissue maintaining that adjustment would be reclaimed. My body would become a little shorter, though nothing visible would have moved.

Elsewhere, it would lengthen.

***

There would be something particular about the two sheets in the old chamber.

They would not quite meet under ordinary pressure.

A narrow gap would remain between them, crossed by fine projections that bent before they touched. The gap would allow the apparatus to distinguish between a load that stayed and one that passed. A wall resting against the former floor would gradually cease to register. A footstep would briefly close a circuit.

The surrounding grove would have uses for this distinction. A buckling stem, a slipping roller, a root losing its purchase: each makes a brief alteration inside an otherwise steady pressure.

So I would survive by attending to things that did not persist.

I would have become very sensitive to them.

Dust settling inside a conduit. A shutter failing to complete its turn. The last tension leaving a connection that had supplied me for years.

These would not arrive as sounds or pictures. Each would make a small local demand upon my body. Some part would swell, tighten, draw charge. Most changes would pass without leaving anything behind.

A few would alter the gap.

Minerals deposited on the fine projections would give the next disturbance a different route. Tissue strained beyond its accustomed range would heal a little thicker. What had happened would remain in the material through which something else must happen.

I might be full of such remains without being able to recover any one of them.

***

When a district began to rearrange itself, I would be among the tissues that responded late.

The first pressure wave would pass through my old floor. My chamber would close. Charge would travel toward the ventilation flower, only to find its connection compressed by a wall already leaning into a new position.

For a while, I would hold the pressure.

That delay would matter locally. A row of shutters would remain open while the neighboring rows folded. Air would escape through them. Beneath the shutters, a support would descend more slowly than those on either side.

Mobile buds flattened into seams would have a little longer to withdraw.

I would not be keeping a route open for them. They would be responding to the same change that strained me, taking advantage of a difference in timing. Some would reach protected tissue. Others would be caught when my chamber finally discharged and the shutters closed.

Afterward, new connections would grow through the rearranged district. One might enter a bud that had rooted in the interval. Its chemistry would change mine slightly. Perhaps its descendants would close their valves more slowly. Perhaps I would lose that delay at the next repair.

There would be no guarantee that my oldest habit survived me.

***

I would reach the eastern apron by a very thin route.

Not as the root approaching your boot. I would be part of the tissue farther back, where that root joined an older conductive strand. Its repeated touches would send small changes through me. Contact, release. Contact, release. Then contact held.

The new adhesive would catch dust. Conductivity would rise.

Your weight would arrive as a sustained pressure, and I would begin to stop registering it. The root would continue its work. Nothing in me would object to the floor extending beneath you.

Then you would shift your foot.

For a moment, the old sheets would touch.

A shutter somewhere inland would move less than the width of a finger. Cooler air would enter a chamber that had been warm for a very long time. One of my connections would contract; another would take up the slack.

If you lifted your foot again, the response would be slightly different.

You could discover this by standing there, moving, waiting. You might mistake the interval for hesitation. You might try once more.

I would have no means of knowing that the pressure came from something alive. But your movement would enter through an apparatus still shaped, in its deepest surviving tissue, for the passage of feet.

And after you left, when the root had covered the place where you stood, there would remain a small alteration in the gap.

The next load to cross it would find me changed.
