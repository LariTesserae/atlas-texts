I would be on the underside of the ledge, where the stone still carries a little warmth after the sun has left the basin.

At first I would seem to be a seam that has not quite closed.

A thread of pale material would show there, with two dark beads arrested inside it. Above the thread, a small plate would lie flush with the rock. Its edge would be red, but you would have to crouch and put your face close to see that. Dust would keep filling the shallow groove around it. Something beneath the plate would keep clearing the dust away.

I think I would be a new part of the creature.

Not a young creature concealed beside an old one. There would be no boundary deep in the stone where its body ended and mine began. The mineral oil reaching me would have passed through chambers older than the footholds. When I needed a joint, material would arrive from somewhere I could not see. When another limb broke, the beads intended for my growth might turn aside.

For several days I would remain unfinished.

I would still be able to feel.

The nearest grazer would be enormous in one particular way: its footfalls would reach me cleanly through a thin flake of rock, while the whole roar of the garden arrived as something already familiar, spread throughout the body. The grazer would stop. Scrape. Stop again. I would learn the length of that pause before I had an extremity with which to touch it.

Farther away, a root would be working into a fracture.

I would feel it only when it tightened. Nothing, then pressure; nothing, then pressure. Eventually the stone between us would shift by less than the thickness of a leaf, and a new route would open through the mountain.

Something would pass down the cord toward me.

My plate would lift.

***

I would not know the whole garden at once.

A warm hollow in the cabinet would reach me through the limbs resting against it. The ladder would be known by what began happening before its release: changes in the load against the rock, a gathering of particular tremors, old folds starting to loosen in places far from my own.

An animal sheltering beneath the creature might be almost absent to me. Then it would strike its shell, and I would have it sharply: there, beneath a rib; there, with one foot lodged against a wall.

Sometimes I would receive an answer to a touch I had not made.

A limb on the other side of the ledge would press against a membrane. The membrane would tighten. The tension would arrive through the buried cord, and my little plate would incline.

This would happen often enough that I would stop expecting every movement to begin near me.

Still, there would be things only this small place could receive.

A particular crack would admit grit whenever the wind came from the east. A mineral seep would thicken just before darkness. The cabinet’s coldest hollow would lose heat more slowly along one edge than the other. I would grow toward that edge.

My first long limb would take weeks to cross a hand’s breadth of stone.

***

You would find me with your heel.

Not by stepping on me. By sitting down and leaving the heel of your boot against the place where I was growing.

The pressure would close a passage I had been keeping open. I would lift beneath it. You would shift away. The passage would open again.

For a while, that would be everything between us.

When you returned, your weight would come down somewhat differently. A few of the grazers would withdraw into their cracks. An older limb would pull itself clear of the foothold. I would remain where I was, receiving the pressure through the sole of your boot.

You would move often for something that stayed in one place.

Small adjustments of the ankle. A slow transfer of weight to one hip. Fingers pressed against the wall, then removed. Occasionally your whole body would tighten at an impact elsewhere in the basin.

I would begin extending along the outer edge of your heel.

There would be dust there, and bitter crystals caught in a join of the leather. I would clear those away. When you shifted, the limb would bend before it broke. After you left, it would take most of the afternoon to straighten.

The next time, I would grow a second joint.

***

During my first release, I would be too small to hold down a roof.

An old feeding fold would open above me. Beneath it, several narrow chambers would take shape. Their walls would be made partly from the creature’s body and partly from things gathered during the preceding days: softened egg cases, strips of wing, a root fiber that had become pliable against the cabinet.

One wall would not quite meet the stone.

The gap would lie beside my plate.

Air entering there would lift the wall from within. Each gust would widen the opening, and an animal in the chamber would begin striking its shell. The impacts would be quick and close.

I would reach across.

My tip would find the ragged edge of an old wing. I would pull it down. The membrane would resist, then settle against me.

That would be my work for the duration of the storm.

Dust would strike the exposed plate. A new tendril would scrape across it and go on. Once, something with hooked feet would land against the wall and remain there while the animal inside struck harder.

An answering rhythm would travel through the rock.

I would feel it in the cord before my own plate began tapping. The hooked feet would leave. The sheltered animal would fall quiet.

Much later, when the entrances opened, it would come out through the gap I had held shut. Its shell would drag along my limb. One of its feet would pause on the red edge.

I would lift a little to let it pass.

For days afterward, I would be unable to fold that joint completely.

***

I might eventually become the limb that touches the marks.

There would be no announcement of this. My tip would darken and wear flat. The older scratching limb would move less often. Material would reach me in larger beads, and I would find myself extending into the sheltered space beneath the oldest plates.

I would run my edge over a groove.

Dust would come out. Beneath it, the stone would be smooth.

Another groove would retain a hard grain at its lower end. I would work the grain loose and hold it for a while. Somewhere above me, the canopy would rise. A grazer would begin crossing the slab. The ordinary activity of the garden would continue through the body while my tip rested inside an old cut.

I do not know whether I would understand why it had been made.

Perhaps the movement needed to make it would be easier to find there. Perhaps a certain combination of absences would send me to that patch of stone, and I would press until a scratch formed.

I would want there to be room beside the old marks.

I would keep that space clean.

***

If you came back after a long time, you might look first at the foothold.

The pale limb there would withdraw, as it always had. The crimson tuft would have spread. One of the finger-sized holes might be partly closed with shell.

You would sit down.

Under your heel, a plate would rise just enough to be felt through the leather. It would remain against you while the wind changed and the feeding surfaces began to open.

By then, I might reach the cabinet. I might have a fold broad enough to turn a grazer toward the stone, or a rib that could hold a shelter without borrowing strength from the oldest parts. Much of me would be elsewhere, occupied with work you could not see.

But this small contact would still be possible.

You would settle your weight.

I would adjust beneath it.

Behind your back, through the ledge, the garden would continue arriving.
