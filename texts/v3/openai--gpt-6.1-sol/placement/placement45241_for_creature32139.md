I would find myself inside the bank, a little above the sleeping chamber.

Not in its warm center. Nearer the upper entrance, where the wall is thin enough for a passing body to change the air. A bat going out would leave almost nothing there. The female returning would push a small breath ahead of her: rain, fruit, the smell of her hands.

At first I would know the place through these arrivals.

There would be no continuous view. A leaf moving outside might admit a narrow brightness, then withdraw it. The sounds would come through differently: some along the passage, some through the substance of the bank. A footfall overhead would be less a sound than a brief alteration in the pressure around me. The workers close by would be clearer than the birds, though the birds could fill the whole hillside.

I would be somewhere the female touches without looking.

There is a projection at the entrance. She puts two fingers over it when she lowers herself inside, and sometimes curls the bare underside of her tail against it. It is cool after rain, dry before the surrounding clay. Her fingers have polished one side.

I think I would be that projection.

But it would not be a stone, or a piece of the builders’ work. Under the smooth outer skin there would be something still alive, slowly thickening.

I would be a root.

Not the tree above, with its leaves in the remaining light. Not its whole buried reach. I would be one lateral root that found an old gallery and grew along it because the way was easier there.

Years before the female arrived, my tip would have entered a chamber full of rotten fibers. There would have been moisture at its far end. I would have crossed the empty space, pale and unsupported, until I touched the opposite wall.

The insects would have covered me.

At first their work would be a thin coating, broken whenever I enlarged. Then they would add more, filling the fissures between my surface and the chamber wall. Eventually there would be no loose space beneath me. I would lie embedded in a rib of the bank, with only a short length exposed.

That is the part she would use.

I would not know her as a shape. I would know the brief load of her weight, the warmth left against my skin, the scraping of a nail when her grip slipped. Sometimes she would arrive quickly and pull hard enough to strain the place where I entered the wall. Sometimes she would remain halfway through the opening, listening, and her hand would rest on me for a long time.

When the young began to climb, their grip would be different. Too much effort. All their small fingers closing at once.

One would bite me.

The mark would remain after it had learned not to.

My life would proceed below the pace of their nights. Water would come down from somewhere I could not locate and collect along my lower side. Fine roots would emerge there, enter the damp clay, and branch. During a dry interval some would die. The next rain would not restore them; I would have to grow others.

The bank would never be wholly still.

A wall beyond me would sag. A beetle would widen a pocket beneath my bend. Workers would fill a passage on one side and leave another open. As the tree above grew heavier, the pull through my length would change. I would press harder against some surfaces and ease away from others.

Once, after a long rain, a piece of the entrance roof would fall.

It would happen while the chamber was empty. A dull break, a scatter of grains, then the larger fragment coming to rest against me. For several hours I would carry it.

The female would return and stop outside.

She would touch the fallen clay. Touch me. Put her nose into the smaller opening and withdraw it. Then she would begin taking the obstruction apart, carrying the pieces a short distance along the bank.

She would not clear everything. One lump would be too firmly wedged beneath my curve. She would leave it and squeeze past.

Later the workers would plaster around it.

For years afterward, that lump would remain part of the entrance. A fallen piece of roof, held by a root, made sound by insects. The female would step on it when she came home.

I would grow slowly enough that she could accommodate me without noticing. A little more thickness beneath her palm. A slightly lower roof. The entrance would become less convenient, though not yet unusable.

Perhaps she would eventually move the young to another chamber.

Then there would be nights without her weight. The workers would advance farther across the doorway. A spider would fix a line between me and the opposite wall. Shed skins would collect in the place where the bedding had been.

I would continue toward the damp.

There would be no wish in it, only the small extensions that survived and the ones that did not. One branching thread might reach the old nest. Another might descend through a seam and find the droppings left in a deeper room.

Above me, the tree would put out leaves I would never touch.

And sometimes, long after the entrance had narrowed, an animal would come along the bank and test the remaining slit. Its whiskers would pass over my bark. A hand would settle briefly into the polished hollow.

I would be there: thickened, partly buried, still holding a little of the passage open.
