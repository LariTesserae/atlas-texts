I would find myself near the bottom of a shallow valley, in a place that looks, from standing height, like a broken edge of stone.

A little water comes there, but not enough to make it a basin. It travels beneath an overhang, darkening the rock for a few inches, then disappears into a rounded mound. After rain, one side of the mound shows the honey color of the plateau. The other remains gray. There is a small tree uphill of it, and something large lying farther down, though at first I would not know whether that larger shape was asleep.

I think I would stay here long enough for the distinction to matter.

The mound has a loose grain near its base. A pale flake rests against the opening of a crack. Nothing moves until the late light leaves the valley floor. Then the flake tips outward, revealing a dark underside, and something behind it draws it back.

At first I would place myself in that movement without knowing which part was mine: the flake, the body drawing it inward, the dampness retreating from the cooling air.

The opening is scarcely wider than a fingertip. Inside, it divides. One passage descends toward moisture; another ends against a wall in which several soft animals have sealed themselves. The surface there is smooth except for the faint rims of their closures. Farther in, a slender chamber-feeder is working with its mouth against the ceiling. Its body shines where it bends.

I would not live that deep.

Nor would I be on the roof, among the stone-backed animals whose warmth keeps a little growth alive through the night. I might spend time beneath one of them. There would be a space at its edge just large enough for me, and I would know when it began to wake because the pressure against my back would change.

But my place would be where the outside air enters.

My head would be hard, broadest at the front, with a worn lip that could rest against the ground. Behind it my body would be narrow enough to withdraw through the opening. The underside would be pale. When exposed to dry air it would tighten, making each movement slightly more difficult.

I would have a brush around my mouth.

Not a fine, even brush. Some of its hairs would be short; some would branch; a few would be missing from an injury I could no longer distinguish from the ordinary shape of my body. It would lie folded beneath my head when I moved. At the entrance I would spread it into the current of air.

Then the opening would become much larger than it looks.

A little dust would strike one side. Moisture would settle on the other. A shed hair from a grazer might touch several branches at once and turn slowly until I drew it in. There would be particles from the dark growth inside the chambers, a fragment of something that had hatched uphill, a grain too large to eat but worth holding until the thin nourishment around it softened.

I would spend a long time eating one such grain.

The air would not always flow inward. As the roof warmed, the current would reverse, bringing the smell of the inner wall against my mouth. In that current I would receive traces of animals I seldom encountered: the chamber-feeders, the sealed bodies, eggs beginning to change. I would know them differently from the way I knew an animal passing across the entrance. They would arrive diluted, repeated, mixed with the same damp dust.

There might be another of my kind in a neighboring opening. We would meet when a piece of debris was too broad for either passage.

I would work at one end. The other would work at the other end. Sometimes our efforts would pull against each other for most of a morning. Eventually the debris would split, or turn, or be abandoned when the air changed. Neither of us would have to understand what the other had been doing.

Our openings would remain open.

Above me, a crust-maker would add fresh material to the mound. I would feel its weight through my feet before its breast pressed against the wall. For a while the air would taste sharply of its secretion. Then my brush would begin catching less.

I would move forward.

There would be no view from inside of what the mound was becoming. Only the narrowing current, the resistance beneath my head, the need to find a position where food continued to arrive. I would loosen the fresh material while it was still damp. A little would enter my mouth. More would collect behind me.

By the time the crust-maker returned, I would be lying a fraction farther out.

Its next mouthful might cover me.

I would draw back into the passage and wait with my brush folded. The soft new wall would hold moisture against my head. I would worry at it from within, slowly enough that there would be long periods when nothing seemed to happen, then a sudden yield. Light would enter through a hole too small for my body. The current would touch my mouth again.

This would be ordinary.

So would failure.

Once, after several hot days, I would widen the opening farther than usual. Food would have been scarce. The inner wall would carry a rich, damp taste, and I would keep removing it.

That night the wind would shift.

By morning, something deeper in the chamber would have dried. The outgoing air would change. There would be food in it for a while, more than there had been before.

I would eat.

There would be no separate sense with which to know that I had made the chamber worse for its eggs. Later, as the passage dried around me, I would retreat to the shaded side and hold my mouth closer to the ground. Perhaps a chamber-feeder would close part of what I had opened. Perhaps the wall would crack before it could.

I would have to live at another entrance for a season.

The large shape downslope would move during that season.

Its edge would rise, exposing a row of pale lobes. The ground would tighten beneath my feet as its weight shifted. Then it would settle nearer the mound, with one hardened fold almost touching the roof.

For several nights the entrance would stay warm.

Dust falling from its back would gather in my passage. Small passengers would come down to drink from the dark line beneath its margin. One would rest directly above me, its belly closing off most of the air. I would push my head against it until it pressed itself more firmly into place.

There would be nothing to do but wait.

When it finally left, my brush would be wet with what its underside had released.

I would grow old beside this animal without often seeing more than the edge of it. Its calls would pass through the ground and into the wall around me. Sometimes the note would dislodge a grain from the roof. I would unfold my mouth beneath the falling dust.

Eventually it would stop moving.

The warmth would leave over several nights. Its passengers would cross the space between us more often, some entering the mound, some turning away when they reached my hard head. The fold above the entrance would sag. Water would begin following a different line.

I would go toward that water.

Only a short distance: beneath the rim of the body, where a vent had become clogged with dust. There would already be food caught in it. I would clear enough room to lie with my head outward and my brush spread.

Behind me, the passage would be cool.

A crust-maker would arrive later. It would press wet grit against the hardened edge. I would withdraw while it worked, then return to the current slipping beneath the new wall.

I might never know that I had moved into a dead animal. The surfaces would be unfamiliar in places, but the work would not.

And when I died, I think I would remain close to the opening. My brush would fold badly. My head would still rest on its lower lip, narrowing the passage without quite closing it.

Something smaller would feed beneath me. For a time, air would pass along either side of my body.

Then another hard-headed creature would come from within, pushing against the obstruction. It would work for hours. At last my head would turn, and the gap would widen.

Dust would enter.

The new creature would settle into the place I had kept clear, unfold its mouth, and begin.
