At first I would mistake the knocking for someone wanting to be let in.

I would stop in the passage with my hand on the wall, trying to decide which door was meant. Someone carrying a tray would have to turn sideways to get around me.

“Not there,” she would say.

She would point downward, but only because I was looking. For anyone else she would stamp once, lightly.

I think I would begin by being in the way.

Not at the unfinished edge, where a person who does not know what to do is immediately conspicuous, and not down in the shafts. Somewhere in the occupied middle, where three households have gradually turned a landing into a room without quite succeeding. A stair enters it from below. Another leaves through what used to be a cupboard. There is a bench nobody owns and a small opening through which heat comes from a kitchen.

I would sit on that bench long enough to be given something to hold.

A cord, perhaps. The end of a lamp wick. A child’s cup while the child climbs past me with both hands full.

Eventually someone would ask why I was still there.

I would not have a satisfactory answer. But by then I would know that the third stair moves under a heavy foot, and that the woman upstairs cannot hear anyone calling from below, though she hears perfectly well when someone sets a jar on her ceiling.

There would be work in knowing those things.

—

I would have a shallow box fitted with a strap, small enough to carry against one hip. Inside it: clay wrapped in damp cloth, a few strips of fired pottery, a blunt wooden blade, cords, and a little grease for places where the clay must not stick.

I would make the fragile pieces people put across cracks.

Not braces. I would not be strong enough, or skilled enough, to stop a wall going anywhere. My pieces would break if it moved.

Some would be no wider than my smallest finger. I would press one end onto each side of a joint, leaving a thin neck across the gap. On a dry wall I could make a neat, flat bridge. On a sweating wall I would need to roughen the surface first, and even then the thing might peel away in the night.

I would learn to distinguish that failure from a useful one.

There would be arguments.

“You said it moved.”

“I said the clay broke.”

“You came here carrying your box and made my wife move the bed.”

“She asked whether I would sleep under it.”

“And would you?”

I would look up before answering. This would irritate him more than anything I could say.

Most of my work would be less dramatic. Checking a stair after someone complained that it had begun to click. Feeling whether two scratches still met. Replacing a witness broken by a basket, a child, a sleeve, a person determined to prove that the wall was perfectly sound.

I would dislike calling them witnesses in front of other people. It would sound too important for something I made by rolling clay between my palms.

But in my own room, looking at a row of them ready to be carried out, that would be what I called them.

—

My room would be beside the landing, behind a door too low to enter upright.

I would have chosen it for the warm floor and discovered later that warmth arrived chiefly when the kitchen was busy. On days when the cook was ill, I would sleep wearing my work clothes.

The room would have a shelf shallow enough that cups had to stand sideways to fit their handles against the back. Beneath it I would keep my own pieces: failed shapes, mostly. Too thick to break when they should. Too thin to survive the journey in the box. A shape with two little feet that looked ingenious until I tried to attach it overhead.

There would also be a piece I had retrieved from a collapsed wall.

Its neck would still be whole.

I would keep it because I needed to remember the fault: I had put it across a joint that was not the joint that moved. I would not explain it every time someone noticed it on the shelf. Sometimes I would say it was an old pattern.

The cook next door would know.

When I became too certain about a crack, she would ask whether I wanted to come and look at the thing above my bed.

I would resent this. I would also go.

—

I would have ordinary hands, though after some years the left would become more reliable than the right. The right would do the making. The left would remain on the wall while I worked.

A split beside the thumbnail would refuse to heal properly. Clay would enter it. I would hold hot bowls by their bases because the rims hurt, and people would assume this was a habit of my trade.

I would be better at noticing slow movement than sudden danger.

That matters. I would not want to become the person everyone waits for before fleeing a room. If a brace snapped, I might be the one standing stupidly with my box open while somebody else pulled me out.

But I could return to a place after six days and find that a door had changed its weight. I could notice that a bowl beneath a drip had been moved twice toward the same corner, though its owner insisted the floor was level. I would remember where someone had rested a hand while telling me there was nothing wrong.

People would send for me because I kept coming back.

Some would regard this as diligence. Others would think I was looking for faults in their work. Both would occasionally be right.

—

A drain creature would live behind my water jar.

I would not keep it. It would come and go through an opening that was too narrow for the brush I used to clean it. I would discover it by the soft semicircle missing from a batch of fresh witnesses.

For a while I would try to block the opening.

Then water would begin gathering beneath the jar, and I would take the obstruction away.

After that I would leave a broken dish beside it. The creature would ignore the dish for several nights, eat one more piece of my work, and eventually settle into an arrangement neither of us had intended.

When visitors asked its name, I would say it did not have one.

When it vanished for a long time, I would check the drain.

—

The large thing in the wall would pass close to my room once.

I would be awake already. The cook would have dropped something in her kitchen, and I would be waiting to hear whether she needed help.

Then the shelf would tremble.

Not much. Enough for the handles of the sideways cups to touch the wall.

The sound would come from behind them: a long, laborious drag, with pauses during which I could hear my neighbor breathing through the opening above the floor.

I would put down whatever I was holding.

For perhaps a quarter of an hour, neither of us would call to the other.

Afterward I would find that three of my witnesses had broken. A fourth would be missing altogether, though its two attachment marks remained.

The next morning I would want to open the shelf wall.

I would have reasons. I would explain them at length to the cook, who would let me finish before reminding me that I slept there and she worked on the other side.

We would leave it closed.

For weeks I would look at the empty attachment marks whenever I took down a cup. Then the kitchen would have a bad settling of its own, and there would be other things to inspect.

—

I think I would grow older in that room.

My routes would become shorter without my quite admitting it. I would teach someone younger to carry the box, then complain that she let the clay dry out. She would make wider bridges than mine. I would tell her they were wasteful.

One afternoon she would bring me a piece broken cleanly through its thickest part.

“Yours peeled off,” she would say.

I would turn hers over several times, feeling the break.

We would change the mixture.

By then the landing would have altered. The cupboard stair would be gone. The bench would have been cut down to make room for a pipe. The cook’s daughter would run the kitchen and would not remember how far the old hearth had extended beneath my floor.

A child would pause outside my door to tell me the lower passage was closed.

“Since when?”

“Now.”

I would reach for my box.

She would remain in the doorway, watching me fit the lid and test the strap. Then she would take the lamp down from its hook. She would have been told to bring me, though nobody had remembered to say so.

We would go together. I would follow her past the place where the stair used to be, keeping my free hand against the wall.
