At first I would stay near the last foothold, with one foot still on the cooler stone above the hollow. I would be listening for a gap large enough to enter.

From there, the bare center would look hospitable. Nothing obstructs it; there is room to stand. But I would take some time to understand that the room is not the same everywhere. Beneath the tree, a body can rest without drying. Beside the window, it must keep a foot against the wall. A certain seam will accept roots, and the stone alongside it will not. I would watch animals cross the floor and notice how rarely they stop halfway.

Eventually I would go down. I would crouch near the tree without taking the seat, and stay long enough for the heat to come through my clothing.

But I do not think I would remain a visitor. The longer I imagine staying, the less useful that body becomes. Its feet are too far from its hands. Its skin reports almost nothing about the rock until the rock has become painfully hot. Its ears insist on gathering the hollow’s sounds into one enormous disturbance.

I would want to get lower.

Under the seat there is a place where the floor rises slightly toward the wall. An adult has to flatten its abdomen to reach it. That is where I begin to find myself: pressed between warm stone and the overlapping bodies of the others, with a front hand folded beneath my mouth. Somewhere close by, a young animal is ticking in its sleep. Each tick moves through the body beside it before reaching mine.

I am not the oldest. I have all my digits.

Nor am I the one that drinks beside the window. When it returns to the shelter, I make room without knowing precisely what happened there. Sometimes its abdominal scales take a long time to settle. I rest a hand against its side, lightly enough that it can move away.

I am one of the animals that brings stones back.

A stone discovered in the open center is not necessarily wanted beneath the seat. I turn it over with the four digits of a middle foot. The folded pair move above it. If I pick it up, I hold it first against my mouth, then against the hollow between my eyes.

There is a great deal to feel.

On one side, the stone has rested against a damp seam. On another, it has been exposed to the hot floor. A nearly invisible crust gives under my teeth. Something has crossed it recently, leaving a trace that catches the hairs above my nose.

I carry it under the seat.

There, with the stone between my front hands, I sleep for a little while. Heat passes out of it. My hands warm its cooler face. When I wake, I examine it again.

Sometimes I want one of the others to touch it. Sometimes I simply take it outside.

Returning it takes longer than bringing it in. I stop beside two or three places that seem right, set it down, pick it up again. The middle foot can find a shallow depression that my eyes cannot distinguish. At last the stone fits under the digits as it did before.

A visitor watching would see me leave it almost exactly where I found it.

I would have difficulty accounting for the difference.

That is the kind of creature I think I would be here: one with an inclination toward small, portable things, but no permanent claim on them. I might keep a feather shaft through several rains, then carry it to a root island and push it into the soil. I might retrieve it the next day. The strip beneath the seat would interest me greatly, though I would be slow to approach it. There would be a particular dryness to it unlike the dryness of bark.

The others could leave me examining something for hours. They would step over my middle limbs. Once in a while, one would put a warm hand on my back before passing.

At night I would work at the grit.

I would be poor at it when young. Drawing a foot through the crescent too quickly would load the ankle hairs with more grains than I could separate. I would carry pale dust toward the tree and stop, uneasy, with my foot raised.

The old one would come close. Its missing digit would leave an empty space beside the fringe of my ankle. With the remaining digits it would comb out a few black grains and press them into the tree’s seam.

Then it would go back to its seat.

I would stand there a while before turning toward the western shelf.

Even after learning, I would work slowly. Heavy grains lodge near the base of the hairs. Fine ones travel farther out. I would spread the folded digits and feel their slight differences in weight, moving along the margin of the hollow with one loaded foot held clear of the floor.

Some nights I would accomplish very little. A fruit would fall, or someone would touch their forehead to mine, and we would remain together until the first droplets began striking the roots.

I would know the window mostly by those droplets. At dawn I would wait beneath the shelf, just beyond the coolest air. Before a bead fell, the wet root mat would be still. Then the impact would pass through it, and through the hairs of the foot I had rested there.

That small sound would be much larger to me than it is to a standing visitor.

So would the ticking of the young. So would the brief scrape made by the oldest animal adjusting itself in the seat. I would hear these through my hands and belly as well as through whatever organs received the whistles overhead. The hollow would remain noisy, but I could follow one moving body through several interruptions: a foot on stone, a pause among roots, the opening of a throat.

After rain, when the others gathered in the center, I would come out last. Red paste would still be drying between my front digits. I would settle onto my hind limbs a little apart from the nearest body and wait.

There would be times when I turned toward the wall too late, after everyone else had already turned. Times when I lowered my forehead to the floor and found only the fading remnant of a vibration.

I would stay there until something else arrived.

And toward morning I would be beneath the seat again, fitted closely among the others. My back would rise against someone’s sleeping hand. One middle foot would remain outside, its four digits resting on the polished floor.

A fruit might fall within reach.

I would feel around for it without waking fully, work it loose from the crack, and bring it in. There would be a little shifting in the shelter as space was made. Then the fruit would pass from my hand into another warm hand, and I would fold my foot away.
