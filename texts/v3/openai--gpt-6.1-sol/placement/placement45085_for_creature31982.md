At first I would look for a place behind the upright slabs. There is room there for something slight, something that can flatten itself and wait. But the geckos already know those spaces. Their bellies have warmed the stone; their skins catch in its corners. I would not fit without making them move.

Farther back, beneath the fern mat, there is room without vacancy. Every surface carries a root hair, a mouth, the fine white crossing of a fungus. Even the darkness is occupied.

I think I would keep going lower.

Not into the deep passage where the air sometimes turns wrong. Only to the underside of a leaf lying in one of the shallow limestone dishes. The leaf has softened along its central vein but still holds its shape. Its upper surface looks dry. Beneath it, the stone remains dark.

That is where I would begin to have a body.

A thin one, clear enough that the leaf’s veins show through it. You might take me for moisture until you lifted the leaf and found that part of the wetness stayed with the stone, stretching between the two surfaces before letting go.

I would be alive across that small interval.

For a while, I would have no definite edge. Where the stone was damp, I could spread. Where dust interrupted it, I would thicken and stop. There would be grains caught inside me, a broken fern hair, something red-brown left by a beetle. My body would not be clean. Keeping those things would be easier than passing around them.

The first work would be feeding.

Softness would matter more than size. I could work along the collapsed rim of a fungus, enter a fruit through a place already bruised, loosen what remained on the inside of an abandoned insect shell. Slowly, the material beneath me would change. Slowly, I would become more substantial.

Perhaps after several nights there would be a faint cream-colored line around my edge.

I would move, though not in a way that made a journey easy to recognize. By evening I might have withdrawn from one side of the dish and reached a little farther under the leaf on the other. What looked like the same patch would no longer occupy quite the same stone.

The inhabitant’s forefoot would come down beside me.

I would know the pressure before I knew the warmth. The damp leaf matter would compress; a little water would be driven outward. I would follow it into a shallow groove.

Then one broad digit would settle across part of my body.

There would be no greeting. For most of the night, I would lie beneath the fine creases of its skin. Along those creases, moisture would persist after the exposed stone had begun to dry. I could feed on what the foot had brought back from its circuit: a little fruit, a loosened scale, the soft residue of something crushed.

When the foot lifted, some of me would lift with it.

That might be how I came to occupy more than one place.

A pale trace under a root. A patch no wider than a moth’s head beneath the worn lip of the basin. A living gloss in the crease of a forelimb, surviving there until the creature rested against wet stone again.

I would not know these as separate homes. Each patch would feed or fail where it found itself. One might remain beneath a leaf for weeks. Another might dry to a brittle fleck before morning. The hollow would distribute me unevenly.

In the hot weeks I would become smaller and less clear. My edges would draw inward. The leaf above me would lift as it dried, admitting air where there had been a close, wet shelter. I would collect around the last dark grains of humus.

Sometimes that would be enough.

Sometimes a beetle would turn the leaf over.

Exposed, I would have little to defend myself with. A passing animal might eat half of me. An ant might carry away a piece that still moved too slowly to be noticed. I would close around the injury if moisture remained. If it did not, the surviving part would harden and wait.

Rain would reach me late.

Above, the shower might already have ended. The bats might have left. The hollow’s inhabitant might be making its round when water finally found the underside of my leaf.

I would soften first at the margin. A clear thread would enter the dust, hesitate at a dry grain, branch around it. By morning there might be enough of me to touch the edge of the dish again.

I think that would be my place here: low, beneath something easily disturbed, close to the water but unable to keep it.

I would have no view of the hollow. I would encounter the creature a foot at a time, know the frogs by brief pressures, receive the noon light only as warmth passing through a leaf. Yet I would cross stone the larger inhabitant could not enter, spreading through a damp seam as shallow as a scratch.

Near the end of one night, its two digits would return to the dish. One would rest just short of me.

As the leaf matter settled under their weight, a narrow dark line would appear between us. I would begin to extend along it.

Then the drop would fall beside the shelter.

The digits would tighten. Water would shift through the dish, reaching a little farther beneath the leaf.

For another day, I would have room.
