I would be inside the second dead pump, but not at the center of it.

I would be folded into the space behind a bearing rib, where a little water passes without pulling hard. One arm would reach toward the rear fracture. Another would lie along the floor of the casing, its suckers attached at intervals, leaving the flesh between them loose. The remaining arms would occupy whatever room was left. There would be no need to arrange them neatly.

At first, I think I would know the chamber through these separate points of contact.

The iron carries the working pump’s vibration. The floor carries it differently. A shell caught beneath a sucker interrupts it with a small hard edge. Water entering the fracture brings the taste of animals outside, diluted and mixed with things that have already passed through the room.

One arm knows that the shell can be moved. Another has found a surface worth holding. My eye is still behind the flange.

I would not yet need the whole chamber.

Then a fish would pass the opening, briefly cutting off the weak light, and I would move enough to see.

The fish holds beyond reach. Its tail makes the same small movement several times. Behind it, particles travel toward the other pump. I can see the direction of the water without exposing an arm to it.

I remain where I am.

That would be part of living here: letting something pass even when I have noticed it.

My shelter has more entrances than I can watch. The rear fracture admits a thin current. The shaft bore gives access to the floor. The flange opening is wider, brighter, and less useful when a large fish is outside. I use each differently. Sometimes an arm goes out through one while my eye stays at another.

The casing is large enough for me now. There are places within it I seldom touch.

I would have a short arm, too. I would find myself especially there, in the new pale tip that cannot yet do what the others do.

When I cross the floor, it reaches with them. Its suckers touch concrete and fail to hold. Another arm takes the strain, and my body moves forward without stopping. The short one lifts clear of the grit.

Later, beside a plinth, it finds a smooth piece of shell.

It attaches.

Only briefly. The shell turns, and the arm loses it. But on the next reach the tip curls differently.

There is time for such small changes between the pump’s changes of load.

I would leave the casing when the chamber’s ordinary vibration had continued long enough. Along the dead machine, my skin would rise into rough points. At its edge I would pause with most of my body flattened against the metal and an eye beyond it.

The open distance to the pier is not great. Still, I would cross it low, drawing myself from one attachment to the next. Silt would come away beneath me. Where the floor is clean, the suckers would hold more firmly.

At the pier, an arm would enter a mounting hole before I had brought my body around the corner.

There might be nothing inside.

I would reach farther.

A joint, a hole, the underside of a plate: each lets me touch more than I can see. Sometimes I would find a crab by the resistance of its shell. Sometimes only packed grit. I would work at both for a while, until the grit loosened or the crab withdrew somewhere my arm could not follow.

At the fallen panel, I would wait for the shrimp to resume moving.

One arm would travel around the corner. Its tip would narrow and slide underneath. The rest of me would stay nearly still.

The first shrimp would escape. I would feel its passage against the arm, then feel several others retreat. I would follow the underside of the panel rather than chase through open water. Somewhere beneath it, a small body would press against the metal.

I would bring that one back.

The panel would settle over the place where my arm had been.

On the return journey I would stop at the clean cable.

It has a surface unlike the floor or the casing. My suckers release from it easily. I can lift the slack, but only as far as the clamps permit. I would pull once, then change my grip and pull again.

The little red light would appear beneath my arm.

I would uncover it.

It would vanish and return.

For several flashes I would stay there, touching the smooth housing and the cable above it. Nothing would retreat into a hole. Nothing would loosen. Eventually an arm already exploring the conduit would encounter something more promising, and I would move toward that.

Then the vibration would change.

I would feel it through the arm on the pier while my eye was still turned toward the conduit. My body would gather closer to the wall. The arm in the joint would withdraw, empty this time.

I would go back by the nearer route.

By the time the chain lifted, I would have passed through the shaft bore. Two arms would find the bearing rib. Others would spread against the inner casting. The short arm would lie between them, touching but carrying little weight.

Water would push through the housing. Fine sediment would enter, pass over my skin, and leave by another opening. Outside, a shell would strike the flange.

I would hold.

When the load eased, I would not come out immediately. Water still moves after the note drops. Loose things continue across the floor.

Eventually I would put an arm through the rear fracture.

The hollow beside the plinth would be shallower now. A claw that had lain near my entrance would be gone. Farther out, something pale would have caught against a bolt.

I would reach toward it, attach to the floor, and draw myself a little way out.

Behind me, the new tip would find the edge of the fracture. This time it would hold until the body had passed.
