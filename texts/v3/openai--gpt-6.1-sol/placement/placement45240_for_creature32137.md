I would be below the fallen arch, but not directly beneath the place where it struck.

There is a narrow interval between a terrace and a piece of shell that has come to rest on three uneven points. Water enters at one end, hesitates, and leaves through two smaller openings. A little grit has collected in the hesitation. Nothing large can get inside.

At first that is all I can tell about where I am.

The light reaches me after passing through a thin part of the fallen shell. It has a different color there, less blue, faintly yellow. When something moves over the outside, that patch dims. I cannot tell whether the thing is a grazer or a hunter or simply a cloud of stirred sediment. I draw inward anyway.

Drawing inward is not quite the same as moving backward. Several parts of me shorten. A fold closes over a sensitive surface. Something that had been extended into the water becomes thick and blunt and rests against something else.

I wait.

The water continues to arrive.

There are particles in it that I can use, and particles I cannot. The useful ones adhere to a fringe. The others pass over it, sometimes catching long enough to irritate me. I move the fringe against an inner fold and extend it again.

I would not be one of the upright swimmers. There is no clear distance around me, no single direction in which my whole body faces. Nor would I be the creature with brushes walking over the newly exposed pearl. I feel its steps from underneath: a succession of faint loads, too small to threaten the roof. For a while it stops exactly above me. Its brushing makes a persistent tickle through the shell.

I have no way to ask it to move.

Eventually it does.

What I have is a body fastened to an older body’s work. My attachment is broad and soft at its center, hardening toward the edges. There are three short passages around me. I can reach through two of them. The third is still too narrow, though water moves there, and I can taste the difference between that water and what comes through the others.

I am a young builder.

Not one of the transparent swimmers that has just settled. I settled before the arch fell, in a fracture along its lower support. My first shell was a pale collar no wider than the tip of a grazer’s feeler. The fracture protected it. I added a lip, then a shallow roof.

Until recently, there was very little room.

Now one of my walls is the underside of the fallen arch. I did not build it. It presses down beyond the edge of my body, and where it broke, layers of pearl stand exposed like tightly packed folds. I extend a soft arm along them. Some ridges are sharp enough to injure the fringe at its end.

I withdraw from those.

In the smoother hollows, food gathers.

For a time, I grow chiefly toward the better water. My body follows slowly behind the shell, occupying what it can, leaving the narrowest part of the old fracture to silt and small visitors. A jointed animal comes through that fracture whenever the flow is gentle. I feel its brushes against a sealed partition. It can reach places I no longer reach.

There is no reason for me to drive it out.

Sometimes its body catches a particle that would otherwise remain against my tissue. Sometimes it takes food before the food reaches my mouth. These are not separate judgments. They are separate sensations, arriving at different times.

Above me, the fallen piece shifts.

The first shift is slight. One of its points wears through the soft crust supporting it. The roof lowers until it touches my newest shell.

I stop extending that edge.

For several intervals of feeding and withdrawal, I thicken it instead. My growing fold presses against the inside, deposits material, moves a little farther along. The edge ceases to be translucent. It carries the weight without cracking.

Another point settles. Water that used to leave on that side begins to back up, carrying particles into a slow turn. My outer fringe gathers more food. Farther inside, however, the folds receive less fresh water. I contract and expel a cloudy stream. The stream returns through the same opening.

I contract again.

For a while, I can do no more than repeat this.

My third passage, the narrow one, leads toward colder water. I have scarcely used it. Now the tissue inside it swells and extends. The tip finds a break between my shell and the borrowed roof. Beyond the break there is space, but the opening catches me on both sides.

I pull back with a tear along one fold.

Later I try again.

I do not widen the break by wanting it wider. I grow beside it, laying a curved support against the roof. When the roof settles once more, it comes to rest on that support rather than closing the passage entirely.

Water begins to draw through.

The change reaches every part of me. The inner folds lift. Fine matter moves along surfaces where it had lain still. I extend one mouth into the new outlet and then withdraw it; there is little to eat there. The better feeding remains at the opposite side.

Outside, other bodies respond.

Fans open along the edge of the fallen piece. Their feet are fastened where my new rim meets the old pearl. At first there are only a few. Then there are enough for me to feel their withdrawals as a series of minute taps.

Their collars will thicken. They will make this roof heavier.

For now, their fans remove some of the grit before it reaches me.

A grazer crosses overhead. Its scraping comes through distinctly where the shell is thin. It pauses near the outlet, touches the opening with a feeler, then continues down the slope. I remain contracted until the pressure of its foot has gone.

This is where I would find myself: inside that low, irregular shelter, taking in water beneath one edge and letting it go beneath another. My body would be larger than it was when the arch stood, but still small enough that a falling plate could cover everything I have made.

I would know the upper saddle only by what arrives from it. Cold first, sometimes. Sometimes a long pause in the current, during which my fringes hang without bending. Then pressure from an opening that had been an outlet.

At those times I close what I can.

One arm remains extended into the sheltered hollow beside the new support. Even in a reversal, a little water turns there rather than striking straight through. The fringe folds against itself. It holds a few particles.

When the pressure eases, I bring them inward.

My growing edge presses again against the roof. It moves only a short distance before withdrawing, leaving a thin wet line of shell. Outside, one of the small feeding animals has already opened its fans over the place.
