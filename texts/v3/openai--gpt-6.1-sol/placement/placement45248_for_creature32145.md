I would find myself below the part that shines.

From outside, the chamber would look clear. Someone watching the animal lift into morning might see the black column of its organs, the partitions swelling around it, a little cloudiness where moisture had gathered. They might miss the thin branching growth pressed against the lower membrane. In strong light it would resemble a stain left by something that had once leaked.

That is where I would begin to become distinguishable: at the edge of the stain, where one dark filament divides around a bead of water.

I would have no view of the plateau. Even the animal carrying me would be too large to know all at once. I would know a portion of its underside by the tensions passing through it. A foot taking weight would lengthen my surface slightly. A valve opening farther in would bring a warm interval, followed by the cooler interval in which I could spread.

My days would have the shape of these arrivals.

At first, perhaps, I would seem to be a passenger. I would be borne from tree to tree without choosing any of them. The animal would pause for eleven days, and I would remain against the same curved wall, extending into the warmth and withdrawing from the cold.

But the wall would change wherever I touched it.

Heat would move through my branching body toward the shell. Moisture would collect along some branches and leave others dry. The animal’s valves would answer conditions I had helped produce. A chamber could remain supple a little longer because I had spread through it; a fold could close cleanly because I had contracted out of its crease.

I would have work to do.

***

There would be an old place in me that never quite warmed.

Early in the animal’s life, before either of us had become good at living, it would have lodged itself between a mineral shelf and a root. One chamber would have been stretched flat, its lining pressed into a narrow, nearly dry strip. Most of my growth there would have died.

The animal would eventually withdraw. The chamber would fill again.

Along the strip, a few dark points would remain.

I would reach toward them whenever the warmth allowed it. For a season there would be two separate patches. Then a filament would cross the damaged lining, very fine, and another would follow. Fluid would begin to pass between us.

The joined growth would conduct unevenly. It would always retain a cool notch.

Years later, when the animal rested with that side toward the sun, the notch would receive warmth last. When night came, it would lose warmth first. Everything growing around it would have to accommodate that difference.

This would be my oldest recognizable place.

There might be no part of my living tissue left from the original injury. Still, my branches would divide around its shape.

***

I would learn the forest through the animal’s meals.

A collar full of warm fluid would arrive as a long, gentle rise. The nearby valves would work with less effort. I would extend into portions of the chamber that had been too cold since dawn.

Feeding at a conductive root would be different. There would be a brief disturbance, a tightening across the lining. Some of my branches would draw back before the warmth arrived. Others, sheltered deeper in the folds, would continue growing.

A mouthful rich in small bodies would bring its own succession of temperatures. I could not name what had been eaten. For hours afterward, particular regions would warm and cool as the animal separated, crushed, and absorbed it.

Occasionally the meal would be too small.

The animal would remain at the seal. A foot would adjust. The chamber would flatten against a trunk, leaving me a reduced surface on which to wait.

I would contract into thicker lines.

In the coldest intervals, the entire world available to me might narrow to three warm junctions and the threads connecting them. Outside, the forest would still be crowded to the horizon. Inside, I would persist in a space smaller than a closed hand.

***

When the animal approached the pool, I would feel it before the lower shell became wet.

Vapor would soften the exposed membrane. Pressure would change. A chamber seldom used for movement would begin receiving warmth at regular intervals, even while the rest of the body cooled.

I would spread toward it.

Inside would be the young, folded close enough that their feet held one another. Their surfaces would be unfamiliar: softer, less evenly warm, interrupted by little muscular efforts that accomplished almost nothing.

I would reach the first wherever it touched the lining.

For a while I would be continuous between parent and young. A dark thread would lie across the contact. Warmth would pass through it. The juvenile would shift, and the thread would stretch, become pale, then break.

A portion of me would remain on the young.

There would be no moment in which I gathered myself and decided to leave. I would keep growing on both sides of the broken connection.

In the parent, I would still lie around the old cool notch.

On the juvenile, there would be no notch yet.

***

This younger life would be difficult.

The juvenile would open a chamber too far and lose heat. It would bring its limbs together abruptly, folding a section of me against itself. I would thicken in the fold while the animal struggled to reopen it.

There would be mornings when its shell admitted plenty of light, yet the warmth failed to reach the surfaces that needed it. I would extend slowly across those surfaces. As I grew, the young animal would become able to move.

Neither of us could hurry that arrangement.

Its first departure from the pool might carry us only to the underside of a grazer’s plate. Condensation would run nearby. Every movement of the immense host would pass through the juvenile’s feet, and through those feet into the lining where I lived.

The juvenile would grip too tightly.

It would wait too long to release.

I would spend that cold interval in a chamber bent around an obstruction, growing most strongly along the one edge that still received warmth.

By spring, I would have a recognizable place there, too.

***

So I would not be high above the canopy, or standing whole in the brief light.

I would be carried through the narrow crossings below it, close to the warmth that makes a crossing possible. My surface would buckle as the animal found a new hold. I would withdraw from a closing fold and enter another as it opened.

Sometimes a pale shape would pass overhead. The chamber would cool under its shadow. Far away, a ridge grazer would lift, and the slow engagement of its locks would reach us through several bodies and a root.

The animal would hold still.

Within it, I would continue a movement too small to trouble its rest: one branch extending, dividing, touching the next warm place.

And when its feet finally released, I would go with it, already spread across the surface that would bear the strain.
