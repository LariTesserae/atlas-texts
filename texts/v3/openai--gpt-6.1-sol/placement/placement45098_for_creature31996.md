I would first be mistaken for a fault in the carved vessel.

Not the channel water has cut through its side, but a second, much finer line beneath the rim. In dry weather the line would be white. When the vessel filled, it would darken a little sooner than the surrounding stone.

I would be underneath it.

There is room there because the vessel does not sit quite flat. Its maker hollowed the inside carefully but left a ridge across the bottom, so it rests on that ridge and two blunt corners. Beneath one side is a space hardly deeper than a fingernail. Powder has collected at the entrance. Farther in, where drops sometimes travel around the vessel rather than falling from it, the stone is clean.

That is where I would begin to be visible: a clear margin extending beyond the base, holding a few grains apart.

For a while, I might seem to be water that has failed to soak in.

Then the margin would retract.

***

I would have a body, though most of it would be folded out of sight. It would be thin and unevenly translucent, with thicker places that remained cloudy even when wet. Along its underside, short muscular ridges could grip the tiny irregularities of stone. They would release in succession, moving me forward without lifting me clear of the surface.

On a smooth face, my progress would be almost continuous. Across gravel, I would have to gather myself, make several unsuccessful attachments, and pull.

I would not be large. Fully extended, I might cover the palm of a hand. Folded beneath the vessel, I would occupy less space than one of its chipped corners.

My upper surface would carry fragments.

At first these would look like whatever had fallen onto me: white grit, a mica-thin flake, two small chips with rounded edges. But they would be held in a definite arrangement, with room between them for my body to shorten. The central piece would be curved. On its underside would run three shallow channels.

It would have belonged to the larger inhabitant.

I would have found it beneath the shelter, still damp along one edge, and spent much of a morning getting beneath it. Once lifted onto my back, it would keep the sun from reaching the thickest part of me. Its channels would also hold a little water.

I would not know where it came from. I would know which way up it belonged.

***

My place would be among the interruptions in the trickle.

Where water disappears into a crack, there are surfaces it touches only while the basin is overflowing. Afterward, beads remain under ledges. A narrow pocket stays wet long after its mouth has dried. The larger inhabitant can reach some of these places with its head, but not the turns beyond them.

I would pass farther in.

At a constriction I would leave my fragments behind. Each would be loosened from its attachment and set against the rock. Then I would stretch through, pale and uncovered, until the thicker parts of my body had passed the narrow place.

Coming back would take longer. I would have to find the pieces and fit myself beneath them again. Sometimes a grain would prevent one from settling. Sometimes a fragment would have slipped too deep to recover.

There would be stretches of the hollow where I had travelled once and could not safely travel again.

Water would enter me through the edges of my body. In damp air those edges would spread into small folds, increasing the surface on which beads could gather. In dry air they would close.

I would feed beneath the mineral skins, where moisture had left things too fine to distinguish from the rock itself. A patch I had crossed might retain its sheen a little longer than the untouched surface beside it. That would be nearly all the mark I made.

***

I would know the larger being first as a change in the pressure of the floor.

Its approach would reach me through the vessel’s three points of contact. One corner would tremble, then the ridge underneath. I would contract beneath my borrowed plate.

When its head entered the vessel, the remaining water would shift. A wet front would pass along the stone above me. For a few moments I could extend into a place that had been dry all afternoon.

This would happen often enough that I would eventually stay partly unfolded.

Once, descending the outside of the vessel, the inhabitant would put a divided pad across my route. I would touch it with my advancing edge.

The pad would be warm compared with the stone. Its surface would yield slightly, and its creases would be wet.

I would attach.

When the limb lifted, I would lose the ground.

For a little while I would hang beneath it, contracted around my fragments. Then the pad would settle beside the vessel again. I would spread onto the rock and release.

The next time we met there, the limb would come down elsewhere.

***

In the coldest part of the season I would move beneath the shelter.

Not into the old sleeping depression. I would follow the moisture along the underside of a selected floor fragment and find a hollow beneath its broadest end. The inhabitant’s weight would press the fragment down, but a small ridge would keep it from closing entirely.

Above me would be stone, then the larger body, then its overlapping plates.

My borrowed fragment would no longer be necessary there. I would loosen it and leave it beside the entrance to my hollow.

During the night, a little warmth would come through the floor. It would not be enough to keep all of me soft. My outer folds would stiffen first. The thicker cloudy places would remain pliant longer, and I would draw them close together.

When something fell from the wall, the inhabitant would put a pad against the ground.

Beneath that ground, I would feel the same impacts: the first hard strike, the second farther away, the faint settling afterward.

For several moments neither of us would move.

In the morning it would rise and go toward the basin. The stone above me would lighten. Cold would enter the space its body had covered.

I would wait until a drop found its way under the floor fragment.

Then I would unfold far enough to reach the piece I had left outside. I would test its edge, work beneath it, and fasten it onto my back again.
