At first I would put myself behind the amber lamp. There is something attractive about the only light that resembles warmth, even when it gives none. I imagine having a pale body there, out of the worst of the crowd, and being able to watch.

But there is no room behind the lamp that has not already been occupied. And watching is too easy a life to give myself in this place. It leaves me outside the arrangement, only smaller.

I think I would be somewhere along the mouth of a forming die.

Not on the emerging ridge, among the animals whose food comes through its newly exposed pores. Farther in: in the narrow clearance between the metal and the lip that has given it its shape. A place that looks empty because something must be able to pass through it.

I would be a short, soft sleeve.

One end of me would clasp the die. The other would lie against the unfinished ridge. My body would be nearly transparent, except for a cloudy seam where my muscles gathered. Through me you could see a tooling mark in the metal, though it would appear slightly displaced. That might be how you first noticed me: one fine line failing to meet itself.

I would not encircle the ridge completely. There would be a gap along my underside, crossed by three pairs of limbs. The limbs would have blunt ends fitted into shallow recesses. When a pulse came through the ceramic, they would release in order, then grip again. For less than a moment, each would leave room for water to move underneath it.

That would be most of my movement.

I would have a mouth, but not at either end. It would be inside the sleeve, turned against a feeding groove in the die. Food would arrive as a transparent film. I would draw it off with a little rasp that never quite lost contact with the groove.

There would be no reason for me to know what my body looked like.

My senses would be distributed along its two edges. At one edge, the pressure of the die; at the other, the pressure of the ridge. Between them, a short tract of tissue capable of registering the smallest difference.

The machine would not feel hard to me in the way a wall feels hard to a hand. I would have grown against it. Its minute irregularities would be the places into which my body relaxed. One nick near the upper lip would receive a thickening of my skin. A polished patch would lie against a row of delicate hairs. I would know those surfaces without ever assembling them into an object.

The ridge would be different.

It would press against me with the smoothness of something that had only just arrived.

Perhaps that difference would be my whole occupation: the die on one side, with its old marks; the metal on the other, bearing the marks it had just been given.

I would not decide whether the ridge should advance. There would be no control entrusted to me, no concealed authority over the chamber. If I contracted, a little fluid would pass along a groove. If I loosened, the fluid would pass back. Somewhere beneath the ceramic, that movement would meet other movements.

The apparatus would hold the mountain still without needing me to understand stillness.

And yet I would be trying to lengthen.

There would be muscle enough for it, and a fold in my body that could almost open. Each pulse would draw the fold a little flatter. The skin at my forward edge would tighten against the metal. Water would slip beneath me.

Then the pulse would pass, and I would shorten.

From outside, this might look like an attempt to get free. From inside, it would have no destination. The extension would bring food across my mouth and fresh fluid along my skin. I would live by almost taking a shape I never retained.

Nearby, one of the surface feeders would rest a rear foot against my exposed seam. When I lengthened, its foot would lift. When I shortened, it would settle again. I would feel its weight as a small, regular interruption, different from the machinery but no less familiar.

I might be sensitive enough to distinguish that foot from all the other pressures reaching me.

Not to recognize an individual as a face, or even as another body. Only to know the particular way its weight returned.

When a blow from far below reached the tower’s upper housing, it would come to my two edges at slightly different times. First the die would press into me. Then the ridge.

For that tiny interval, the fold in my body would deepen. My mouth would lose contact with its groove. The neighboring foot would lift before I had begun to lengthen.

Then everything would settle into its usual relation.

I would have no sense of the height the disturbance had travelled. No image of cloud, no thought of the lower floors. I would have had a brief change in pressure, a missed film of food, the absence and return of a foot.

That is where I find myself most clearly: not behind the light, seeing the chamber, but inside that small interruption.

Afterward my mouth would touch the groove again. My limbs would release one by one. Along the upper edge, the tooling mark would still look broken where it passed through my body.

You could keep your face against the clear film and wait for the two halves of the line to meet.

They would come very close.
