At first I put myself in one of the rooms beneath the station, somewhere a little away from the platforms. A room with a counter, a kettle, and a window set high in the wall. From the counter, the window shows mostly sky. Standing on a chair, you can see the wet paving outside.

People come there when a route has stopped making sense.

Not when it is officially closed. Closures have notices, and notices have someone responsible for them. They come when the shore path is still open but water has begun crossing it in an unfamiliar place; when a shelter appears to be on the wrong side of a ridge; when the directions they have followed for twenty years bring them to a fence.

I think I would work there. At first I imagine myself making maps. Then I imagine the maps arriving from elsewhere, already accurate in ways I cannot check, and my task being to find out why people cannot use them.

There would be a low stool beside the counter. Someone with a long journey behind them could sit while they explained.

“The lake was on my left.”

“Which lake?”

“The one that’s always on my left.”

We would start there.

***

I would be a person, I think, with no unusual ability to feel the ground rising. I would miss things. I might know the drainage around the station very well and be hopeless in a district where a child could lead me without hesitation.

My outside boots would have one lace replaced by a length of cord. I would keep intending to buy another lace, then forget once I was indoors.

I would be good at listening to directions that sound wrong.

There is a useful interval before correcting someone. Sometimes, if you leave enough room, they add the part that matters.

“No, not the current entrance. Where my sister used to meet me.”

Or:

“I know it isn’t a stream on your map. It was running.”

I would write that down.

The room would gradually acquire things that were not part of its equipment: a glove awaiting its owner, a child’s drawing of a pool, a photograph brought in to settle an argument about a stair. Someone would leave a smooth stone on the counter to hold down a curling notice. I would move it aside to clean, then put it back.

I do not think I would be especially tidy. But I would keep the reports in order. The ones that said *probably nothing* would go through the same process as the others.

***

After a while, I would need to leave the room.

This seems important. Otherwise I would begin to believe that a place became understandable once enough people had described it.

So there would be afternoons set aside for checking routes. I would carry a tablet in a waterproof case, a measuring rod, and a paper notebook because I liked being able to write while the screen was covered in rain.

At first I would visit the disputed places: a path that was steeper than it should be, a crossing people no longer trusted, a pavilion whose light could be seen from far away but whose entrance was surprisingly hard to find.

Later I would begin visiting places nobody had reported.

I can see myself standing beside a shallow pool beyond a residential ridge. There are pale fragments of ice gathered against its downwind edge. Water is escaping through two gaps, though only one has a channel below it. At the other, it spreads over stone and disappears.

I crouch there longer than necessary.

A small animal moves under the water, stops, and moves again. I cannot tell whether it is feeding or trying to find shelter. I take a photograph that will fail to show it.

When I stand, my knee is wet. The wind has turned. The residential windows have gone grey with reflected cloud, and I have been outside long enough that the thought of the crowded station is suddenly pleasant.

On the return journey, I record the second outlet.

***

I would live above ground, but not high up. Perhaps my apartment would be one of the windows along the base of a rock face, close enough to the lake that reflected light moved across the ceiling when the weather was clear.

There would be a public stair beyond my kitchen wall. I would hear boots on it before I heard voices.

I would know some of those footsteps, and occasionally be wrong.

My home would contain a plant that did poorly despite my careful attention. Someone would tell me I was giving it too much water. I would explain that I had already reduced the water. They would look at the pot and remain unconvinced.

I would have a friend in another district whom I saw less often than either of us intended. We would send messages proposing dinner, discover that one of us was on a late shift, and try again. When a visit finally happened, they would bring an overnight bag without needing to ask.

They would sometimes accompany me outside, though they would not share my willingness to wait beside a basin.

“Is it about to spill?”

“Possibly.”

“How possibly?”

I would have no satisfactory answer.

We would eat our lunch anyway.

***

There would be one route I knew for reasons unrelated to work.

Perhaps it led to someone who had died, or someone who had moved away. I do not know which. Only that I would continue to notice its condition after I had stopped needing to travel it.

A drainage fold would deepen. The sheltered place where I used to pause would become exposed. A new entrance would bring people onto the path from a direction that had once been empty.

I might avoid it for a season, then find myself there on an ordinary errand.

That feels like part of being a person in this city: having a practical reason to stand somewhere, and discovering that the practical reason is not the only one.

I would be a little slow returning to work that day. Nobody at the counter would know why.

***

What I would most want to keep, I think, is the connection between a report and the person who brought it.

Not merely *outlet migrated north*. Also: the woman who walks home after the last train noticed it because she no longer heard water beneath the slab.

Not merely *access obstructed by herd*. Also: the older animal waited at the sealed door again.

The official record could have its measurements. Somewhere attached to it, where a future worker could find it, I would leave the observation that prompted them.

This might make me an archivist, though I would spend too much time outside for some people’s idea of one. A route clerk, perhaps. Someone whose work lay between the survey office and the traveller at the counter.

I would not know the whole city. There would be districts I had never visited, deep systems whose maintenance reports I could barely read, lakes I confused with one another. I would learn to ask for help without making a performance of it.

And sometimes I would simply be the person nearest the kettle.

A visitor arrives wet and irritated. They have missed a connection. They put a finger on the map and say the passage cannot possibly be where it is shown.

I ask them to sit down. While the water heats, they tell me where they came from.

Outside, beyond the high window, rain begins. Someone crosses the paving and pauses at the entrance to unfasten a hood.

I turn the map around so the visitor can reach it more easily.

“Show me where you last knew the way.”
