At first I think I would be in the upper square, with a hand on the coping and the roofs below me. It is easy to imagine standing there. There is nothing to do except look, and no one has yet asked who I am.

But I would not stay long. Someone would come up with a basket and need the place where I was sitting. The stone would be cold through my clothes. Down in the town, a cover would be lifted and set on its edge, and I would recognize the sound.

I think I would go down to help lift the other side.

Not as the person entrusted with the water. There would be no single person entrusted with it, and if there were, I would be too young, or too easily distracted, or known to have let a bucket overflow while listening to an argument. But I would be someone who had become useful around the places where water passes. I would know where to put the basin cover so it could not slide. I would have a thin piece of wood for scraping the corners without chipping them.

Someone would have shown me once. Then, because I had done it once, someone would ask me again.

My room would be beneath the passage built between two houses. Not the room above it, with light at both ends, but one beside the passage, whose shutter opens onto a wall close enough to touch with a broom. I would get a little direct sun in one season. I would know the week it began to reach the floor, though I might not know the date.

There would be a bed, a chest, and a shelf fitted into a recess that was not quite square. On the shelf: a cup, a lamp, a small bundle of thread, two pegs that fit nothing at present. Under the bed I would keep longer scraps of wood because there was nowhere else to keep them. Whenever I swept, I would have to pull them out, and whenever I needed one, it would be at the back.

I would do repairs that other people had been meaning to do themselves.

A shutter that catches. A basket handle that has loosened. The wooden stop on a door. I would not make a good new door, though I might eventually learn. I could spend a morning fitting one short board into an old shutter, taking it back out, shaving its edge, fitting it again. When finished, it would look enough like the rest that the owner might forget which part I had replaced.

This would sometimes please me and sometimes annoy me.

I would want people to notice my work, especially while pretending that I did not. If a repaired latch closed properly, I might open it and close it a second time while the householder was watching.

The old mason would find things for me to carry. Small things, because he would be uncertain of my judgment with large ones. He would ask me to fetch a particular fragment from his room, then criticize the fragment when I brought it.

“No, the other one.”

“There are three beside the stool.”

“The one with the edge.”

They would all have edges. I would go back.

For a while I would think his knowledge could be learned by following him. Later I would discover how much of it he supplied without explanation. He would put his foot beside a channel and say it needed opening. I would see an ordinary shallow groove. After the rain I would see what he had seen: a place where the water spread sideways and carried dust against a door.

I would not always remember the next time.

There would be one repair of mine that failed. Probably something modest: a wedge beneath a loose roof slab, made from wood I had been told was unsuitable. It would hold through several dry weeks. I would begin to regard the warning as excessive. Then wind and rain would come together, and someone would put a bowl beneath a darkening beam.

I would have to go up in the wet to take it out.

The household would remember. Not cruelly every time, but whenever I seemed too certain.

I think I would know the child who wets the shells with his finger. He would bring fragments to the work court and leave them on the bench where they might be knocked to the floor. I would give him a broken basket to keep them in. He would want to know whether the largest shell had once been an animal larger than the cat.

I would say I did not know.

This would disappoint him. Later he would come back anyway, because I would hold the stone while he looked at it, rather than taking it from him and offering an answer.

Once, splitting a damaged slab for a small patch, I would expose a shell that neither of us had seen before. Its ridges would be clean and pale. We would call the old mason over. He would look at the piece, turn it once, and tell me it was still suitable for the patch.

I might use a different piece. I might not. The sound stone would be scarce, and the job waiting.

Outside my room, I would recognize the feet of people I did not especially like. Familiarity would not improve everyone. There would be a neighbor who borrowed my sharpest tool and returned it with the edge damaged, and another who always stood where I needed to work. I would grumble about them while knowing which loose step one avoided and why the other no longer carried a full bucket.

I would go to the lower country some years. At first for wages, perhaps to help repair bins before harvest. I would like the timber there: lengths that could be cut without planning around every bend. I would like walking through a doorway without turning my shoulders.

I might stay longer than intended.

Coming home, I would bring something awkward up the ridge—a sound plank, perhaps, bought with too much of what I had earned. I would stop often and resent its weight. By the time I reached the first houses, someone would already have thought of a use for it.

I would say it was mine.

Then I would carry it into the covered court, where it could lie flat and dry.

For the moment, though, I am beside the settling basin. The cover is leaning safely against the wall. My knees hurt on the stone, and there is grit under a fingernail. The person helping me says we have scraped out enough.

There is a little left in the far corner. I reach for it, fail to get it, shorten my grip on the wooden scraper.

Above us, a bucket is being lowered. The rope makes its small sound in the notch. I lift my head to ask whoever is drawing water not to empty the rinse into the lane yet.

They have already done it.

We wait for it to pass. Then I put my hand back into the cool basin and finish the corner.
