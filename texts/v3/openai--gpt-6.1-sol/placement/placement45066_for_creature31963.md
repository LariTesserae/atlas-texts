At first I would keep to the outside of the chamber with two doors.

Not inside it. I would have seen what happens to the water there. I would lie along its lower wall, in the shallow angle where slab meets floor, and move each time that angle vanished. Sometimes I could remain for the length of three deep blows. Sometimes the chamber would lean and I would have to leave before the first.

From that position, one doorway would be visible and the other could be felt. Whenever the room inhaled, a little stream would travel around the wall between them. Fine grit would pass over me in one direction, then the other. This would be how I learned the shape of a place I had never entered.

I think I would initially mistake myself for something stranded.

There would be a pale piece lying beside me, curved on one face, banded along its broken edge. I would keep it close. When the currents changed, I would put myself between it and the open basin. When a fold in the water carried us apart, I would search the floor until I found it.

Only gradually would I notice that I did not have to turn to look for it.

Its weight would register somewhere within me. Even buried under grit, it would pull at the same small region of my body. Other white fragments would produce weaker sensations, some hardly more than an itch.

The piece I guarded would fit against me.

I would discover this during an inhalation, when the current pressed it hard into my side. Its curved face would settle over a softness I had been keeping against the floor. The roar would change. A whole portion of it would become distant.

For a few moments, I would have an inside.

Then the pressure would pass, the piece would lift away, and warm water would sweep through me again.

***

I would be smaller than the creatures that skim the cracks. A little larger than the pale young that withdraw into the roofless chamber. Low to the stone, broad enough to cast a shadow in the bronze light, though I would rarely see that shadow.

My movement would be slow and inelegant.

An edge would loosen. A fold beneath it would swell, grip, and shorten. The rest of me would drag forward. Crossing a fresh ridge would require so much of my attention that I might fail to notice a crack opening behind me.

The clicking things would gather along my retreating margin.

I would feel them where I had just ceased to be. Their clear joints would take the places my folds released, snapping shut in a row. On smooth stone, there would be only a few. On the jagged new floor, where I had to lift and pull and try again, they would become a fringe.

Sometimes I would arrive lighter.

A troublesome point would be gone from my pale piece. A strip of tissue would be missing. Once, after a particularly difficult crossing, I would find a white fleck lodged deep in one of my folds. It would click whenever I tightened around it.

For a long while I would carry this second, smaller sound.

I would not know whether it was alive. I would make room for it nonetheless.

***

Hunger would arrive as a failure to keep my edges down.

The water would begin to lift them. My grip would weaken. Light would pass through parts of me that had formerly been opaque.

I would go to a crack, but not as the finned creatures do. They hold themselves above the opening, selecting what rises. I would approach from the side and press one small portion of myself against the new stone.

Heat would enter.

Too much would make me recoil. Too little would leave me trembling against the floor. At the right distance, the softness beneath my pale piece would thicken. Grains would adhere there. By the next boom, some would be difficult to distinguish from my body.

I would learn to leave before I felt satisfied.

Once I would wait too long. The crack would soften underneath me, and something below the floor would hold my underside.

It would not seize me. It would simply continue its work.

My folds would begin to settle into its pleats. My pale piece would tip upright. A low ridge would rise along its edge, and the current would run over me as though over any other part of the floor.

To escape, I would have to pull against a widening area of myself.

The portion left behind would contract several times. Then it would turn honey-colored.

After that, I would approach the glowing cracks with only a narrow edge.

***

The curved piece would grow.

Not evenly. One side would acquire a thick lip; the other would remain thin enough to tremble. When I dragged it, its heavier edge would catch on every shelf. I would spend longer and longer arranging myself before a movement.

Eventually I would stop beside a gully whose bottom was still shining.

I would mean to rest for one interval.

The next concussion would throw dust over me. I would hold it in the sheltered softness beneath the curve. Another blow would come. A flake would lodge against the lip, then a second, and the tissue between them would stretch into a fine brown strand.

By the time I tried to move, there would be too much to carry.

I would lift at one end and feel the whole small structure threaten to come apart.

So I would lower myself.

The place would have chosen very little for me. It would offer no lasting shelter, no dependable direction of current. But a shallow step would keep grit from striking me directly, and the gully would bring food close enough that I no longer needed to cross the floor for it.

I would remain there while the step lasted.

***

My first doorway would be an accident of poor growth.

Two pieces would fail to meet. Water would enter between them, pass across my underside, and leave through a smaller gap behind.

During the stillness after a boom, I would draw them closer.

During the next inhalation, I would spread them.

This would hurt at first. Fine strands would tighten between the pieces. The pale cover would shudder. Several times I would come close to pulling everything shut.

But closing would leave grit lodged where I could not move it. Opening would clear the grit. Gradually the exposed edges would become smooth, and the passage of water would stop hurting.

A small pale creature would arrive.

It would have three fins, one of them short and folded under its body. It would hover at my entrance, cloudy spots turning clear along its leading edge.

I would be too shallow to receive it.

It would try anyway.

Its weight would press against my back. Water would force its way around us both. I would feel the animal’s underside loosen into leaves, then close abruptly when the deep blow reached us.

A fin would catch in one of my joints.

For several pressure cycles we would remain attached, the animal pulling outward, my new wall drawing inward. At last the fin would come free of the animal.

The creature would leave with two.

I would retain a pale strip in the joint. Around it, the brown film would thicken. My wall would grow higher.

The clicking would continue long after the doorway was empty.

***

I would find myself there: low beside the gully, below the green straps, within sight of the bulbs beneath their ledge.

Some part of me would still remember moving. On occasion I would loosen one edge as if to begin. But the surrounding stone would have grown into the places where I once put my folds, and my pale piece would no longer be separable from the rest.

Inside me, there would be room for very little.

A grain falling from the roof would be audible. A single mineral shaving could obstruct the rear gap. If one of the thumb-sized young entered, its body would touch both walls.

I would learn its presence through those touches.

When the floor began to rise beneath us, I would be unable to carry it away. I would draw my slabs closer as the ridge lifted, holding the small hollow between them. The young creature would press flat against my inner lining. Bronze light would show through every joint.

Then the ridge would split.

One wall would break. Water would rush through the wound, and the youngster would be gone.

I would settle on the new slope with my entrance tilted upward. For a while, dust would fall directly into me. The broken wall would ache where its strands remained exposed.

Between two blows, a pale body would pass above the opening.

It might be the same one. I would have no way to tell.

It would descend a little, test the current, and put its leading edge inside.
