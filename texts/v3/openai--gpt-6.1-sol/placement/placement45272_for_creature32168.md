I would not begin on the highest terrace.

There is too little there to hold me: the water runs inward, the seams are narrow, the new floor is still learning how much it can carry. Nor would I begin in the deepest layers, where almost every opening already has something passing through it.

I think I would be caught somewhere between them, at the downstream end of a bridge that no longer crosses anything.

Its far attachment has torn away. Most of its length hangs into open air, but the near end remains alive. Water still enters the old vessels and travels outward until it finds the broken places. A steady rain falls from the hanging fibers onto a terrace below.

Near the surviving attachment, one fold has turned upward. It holds a little water, and in that water there is a cup.

At first, that is where I would find myself.

Not the bridge. Not yet the fold. Something loose enough to turn when the water turns.

My underside would be crowded with fine roots. Some would trail freely; others would catch in the soft lining of the fold and pull loose whenever the hanging span moved. My rim would be thin on one side, thicker on the other. A current entering the pocket would turn me until the thick side faced it.

This turning would take much of a morning.

There would be sunlight for a short time each day, entering beneath the upper terrace. During that interval, the water inside me would grow warmer than the water outside. The difference would be small, but it would run through my whole body. Along my shaded edge, a groove would remain open. Along the bright edge, the same groove would narrow.

I would have no view of the country. Its great empty basins would be beyond the range of anything I could register. I would know the bridge by the water it lost, the terrace above by its shade, and the lower country by the pull on roots that had strayed over the lip.

For a while, that might be enough.

Then a flood would arrive through the bridge’s old supply.

The water would not lift me cleanly out. It would drive one side of me under the curled fold, turning my cup almost inside out. A broad piece of dead skin would follow and lodge across the opening. Smaller fragments would pack behind it.

For several days, I would be buried.

The upper surface that had warmed in the sun would remain pressed against wet debris. My free roots would have nowhere to trail. Water would enter through narrow spaces, pass through me, and leave under the broken skin.

What changed first would be the place where I touched the fold.

A root tip, held against it by the weight above, would broaden. The lining beneath it would soften. For a time there would still be two surfaces, each wet against the other. Then there would be a passage.

Water would come through that passage when the old bridge filled.

When it emptied, water would go back.

I would cease turning.

That would be the first substantial fact of my life: not that I had grown larger, but that I could no longer be carried away without tearing something living.

***

I would remain small by the measures of this country.

The broken span would not be restored. There would be no opposite shore close enough for me to reach, and nothing in the pocket would tell me where that shore had been. I might send a narrow extension down the hanging fibers, but after a few feet it would dry at its tip. I would keep supplying it for a season before a dense band closed across its vessels.

Other growth would do better.

The debris above me would soften. Its weight would diminish, then increase again when more material washed into the fold. Beneath the changing load, my lower surface would thicken into a pad. The cup would become shallower. One portion of the rim, trapped against the bridge, would disappear beneath new tissue. The other would stay exposed and green.

I would be a patch on an old attachment.

Someone walking the terrace might put a foot on me without noticing. The surface would give a little, then recover. Their weight would briefly press water into passages that usually carried very little. Hours later, a pale thickening might begin under the place where they had stood.

But no one walks here.

The loads would arrive as rain, fallen skin, a portion of wall carried down from somewhere I had never been connected to. I would grow beneath what stayed.

Above the attachment, another body would be making a new spillway. Its margin would approach mine slowly, leaving a narrow wet cleft between us. The cleft would remain open through months of contact. Water would warm inside it. Thin layers would build on both sides without meeting.

I would not know whether that body was part of the builder that fed the bridge. Its water would arrive at different hours. Its surface would expand in the heat while mine was still cooling beneath the debris. When the terrace shifted, it would tighten before I did.

Then one afternoon its edge would split.

The split would put fresh tissue against one of my exposed roots. By evening, the root would be held. Over the following days, the two surfaces would swell around each other, and the cleft would close at that point.

Only at that point.

Farther along, the water would continue to pass between us.

I think this is where I would become difficult to name.

Part of me would still be a cup. Part would be a pad. A passage through me would connect the old bridge to the new spillway, though neither had grown for that purpose. The vessels would not line up neatly. Water would have to move through a broad, slow chamber before finding its way out.

Sediment would settle there.

Pollen would collect in a groove that had once led to the center of my floating leaf. Sometimes the groove would close around it. A few hooked strips would form and be washed away. I would retain that small way of beginning elsewhere even while most of my body was occupied with holding on.

***

There would be an old bend in me that never quite straightened.

It would date from the flood that folded my cup under the debris. New layers would surround it. Some would carry water; some would bear weight. But the original crease would remain, tougher on one side than the other.

In dry weather, the chamber between my two attachments would contract toward it.

Under pressure, the crease would open.

This would make me useful in a way I had not grown toward directly. When the new spillway filled quickly, water could pass through me into the old bridge and escape from its hanging end. The broken span would become, for a few hours, a drain.

The load would pull at my lower attachment. Fresh bands would thicken across it after each such filling. My exposed green edge would broaden where the runoff kept it wet.

On other days, the old bridge would receive water first. Then the chamber would fill from the opposite side, and some of that water would enter the new spillway.

Nothing would flow in a dependable direction.

There would be years when one attachment did most of the feeding. There would be weeks when both drew from me, and the chamber shrank until its lining lay almost against itself. My outer edge would brown during those weeks. A narrow part might die and peel away.

I would not feel the whole valley’s drought. I would have this contraction, this drying margin, these vessels carrying less than before.

When water returned, some of the closed passages would reopen. Others would stay closed. New water would take a slightly different route.

Whatever counted as my memory would be there: in the routes that remained available, in the bend that opened more readily after repeated filling, in a thick band grown under a load that no longer existed.

I would still be working around my first flood.

***

Eventually the bridge would lose another section.

The break would begin well beyond me, among fibers I no longer supplied. Its weight would fall away in stages. Each release would travel up the remaining span as a slackening, followed by a hard pull.

At the attachment, one of my oldest bands would tear.

For the first time in years, light would enter the pocket from below.

The new spillway would hold. My connection to it would be young and thick, and most of my living edge would now lie on that side. The old bridge would settle into a steeper hanging curve.

I would lose part of the chamber. Water would spill through its torn floor.

For some days, there would be less of me.

The new growth would begin along the wet edge of the tear. It would not reproduce the missing wall. With less weight beneath it, the tissue would turn outward, making a shallow lip in the newly opened light.

Rain would collect there.

The old groove leading toward my center would still be present, though its center would no longer be where it had been. Water would find it by degrees. A little debris would lodge against the lip. Beneath that weight, a rib would form.

I would be neither back to being a cup nor finished being a join.

Below me, the surviving bridge would drip into the open air. Above me, the spillway would keep widening. Most of my tissue would remain hidden between them, carrying water through its slow chamber, closing some passages and thickening others.

But at the edge there would be this small, newly illuminated surface.

By late afternoon it would hold a pool deep enough to bend it.

The bend would press against the rib beneath it, and the rib would begin to thicken.
