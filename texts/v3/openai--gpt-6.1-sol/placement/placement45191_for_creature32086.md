At first I look for a place beneath one of the light-fans.

There is a little warmth there, or the possibility of warmth, and I imagine myself sitting with my back against a packed roll of bedding. Above me, the branching fibers disappear into the roof. I would like to watch them long enough to distinguish the light arriving from the light being spent.

But people have already tried this. Someone passing would tell me so. Someone else would offer a different explanation, equally plausible, and ask whether I had anything to eat.

I would have to get up.

Then I think I might belong at a drinking place. I am drawn to work in which looking carefully matters, in which a morning can be spent beside something that has not happened yet. I could learn the six hanging points. I could learn to lift a worm without damaging it.

Yet I would be too eager, at first, to make a pattern out of the drops. After four good days I would begin to trust the interval. On the fifth, when nothing fell, I would have to learn again what a record is for.

I suspect an older keeper would find me useful but tiring.

“Put down that it stopped,” she would say.

I would already have written why.

She would make me scrape the explanation off.

So perhaps this is where I begin: not as a keeper, but as someone who scrapes things off records.

***

My first work would be preparing old peel for reuse.

There would be a household with more records than bedding, responsible for a portion of a chamber that no longer had a convenient name. People would bring its inhabitants maps, harvest accounts, measurements, debts. Most would arrive curled from having been carried close to the body.

I would sit beside the hearth tray, where the peel was flexible enough to flatten, and sort what could be cleaned from what had to be kept.

A map could be old without being useless. A debt could be useless without being old. I would not be trusted to decide either.

The woman in charge would lay a dark finger across a line.

“This stays.”

Sometimes the line would be a route. Sometimes a person’s height.

The records would have their own smells. Smoke worked deep into one strip; sour food into another. A map returned by a walker might smell of a different chamber’s sap. Before I knew any country beyond our own seam, I would know that it had a smell.

There would be one strip I was never allowed to scrape. It would show a passage through a low wall, marked with three small cuts at its entrance. The passage had closed years before.

I would ask why we kept directions to a wall.

“Because he hasn’t come back,” the woman would say.

I would be young enough to think that explained the map’s importance, but not its usefulness.

Later I would understand that usefulness was not the only sorting rule.

***

I would not become a great walker.

I would dislike sleeping alone, and my left knee would hurt after a day of crossing ridged ground. For a long time I would mistake caution for a lack of courage, particularly in other people.

But I would become good at short journeys with an awkward purpose.

There are messages that move easily through a country: a birth, a request for a tool, an account of a newly opened seam. People remember them. They have a destination.

Other messages become less welcome each time they are carried.

The fourth point has stopped.

The cup is bitter.

The western face is no longer dull.

Someone must take these to the next drinking place, and the next, before an old piece of advice becomes dangerous. It is easy to postpone the errand when there are walls to detach or food to turn. It is easier still to assume a departing traveler will tell everyone.

I think I would become the person sent to check whether they had.

My pack would contain a scraper, a little lamp-black, and clean peel cut into narrow strips. I would carry spare cord, because people are more willing to listen after you have helped fasten something.

I would have no badge. There might be a habit by which people recognized me: I would turn a map over before studying its face.

Often the reverse would be blank.

Sometimes it would bear the most important information on the strip, added by someone who had found no room among the routes.

*Two people crossed here. The smaller one needed lifting.*

*No water when we passed.*

*Do not bring a loaded trough.*

I would begin leaving space on maps for what happened afterward.

***

Once, I would arrive too late.

Not too late for a rescue. There would be no rescue to attempt.

I would come upon an old drinking place and find one man waiting beside it. The cup would be shallow and clean. Around its edge, the small leaves would have curled inward without yet becoming brittle.

He would have a map on his knees.

I would recognize our household’s work: the corner trimmed to fit a vessel lid, the date written twice because the first set of marks had run into a crease.

The map said there was water.

He would tell me his companions had gone ahead to look for another cup. They had taken the larger container and left him the smaller one. It still held a little.

I would give him some of mine. After drinking, he would offer the lid back without thanking me yet, because he was considering whether to ask for more.

I would give him more.

Then I would examine the hanging points, although I knew very little about them. I would touch the leaves. I would peer at the surface through my raised screen.

All this would take time.

At last he would say, “Are you going to change it?”

He would be holding out the map.

I would want to add a careful note: dry at this visit, date uncertain, warm sap might return. Nothing in the country warranted a permanent judgment.

But he had not asked me for a permanent judgment.

I would scrape away the mark for dependable water.

The pale place left by the scraper would be larger than the mark had been.

We would wait until his companions came back. During that time he would tell me how far they had traveled, and I would discover that they had trusted the map more than the condition of the leaves. It had been made by people known to be careful.

I would remember this whenever someone praised our records.

***

After a few years, my work would include visiting places people had left.

Not distant ruins. Usually a camp abandoned yesterday or the day before, close enough that its owners still worried about a forgotten spoon.

I would go back with someone, never alone if the departure had been hurried. We would recover what could be carried. We would look for messages. We would examine the seam before leaving a warning for the next arrivals.

There would be arguments about those warnings.

A householder would say the camp had been sound until the roof began shedding. A keeper would insist the roof was incidental; the water had failed first. Someone would want the place marked unsafe because their brother had been hurt there. Someone else would object that a whole chamber could not be crossed if every place where an injury occurred was ruled out.

I would learn to ask narrower questions.

Can a person sleep here tonight?

Can two people carry a hearth tray across that fold?

How long since anyone drank from this point?

Even then, I would sometimes write something that had to be scraped away.

***

My own house would be small and poorly arranged.

I would spend so much time attending to departures that I would neglect the preparations for my own. Someone sharing the house would complain that I kept dry peel in the water rack. I would say it was only there temporarily.

There would be a child who liked the scraper and was forbidden to use it. She would practice corrections with a blunt spoon on scraps.

One evening she would bring me a map she had made of our house.

She would have marked the sleeping sledges, the hearth, the vessels, and a little square beneath my bed.

“What is that?”

“The place for things you haven’t decided.”

I would look under the bed. There would be a damaged light screen, two strips awaiting dates, a fastening whose owner I could not remember, and the spoon she had been told to put away.

Her map would be accurate.

I would keep it.

***

That is where I find myself most clearly: returning to a seam within walking distance of home, carrying peel that still needs a date.

The settlement has moved. Its runners have left parallel marks in the wax, interrupted where a new ridge is lifting. A loosened wall peg lies beside a fold. I pick it up; someone will want it.

My companion has gone to inspect the far side of the old cup. She calls that the leaves are still soft there.

Above me, one hanging point holds a bead of water.

We had intended to mark the place dry.

I stand beneath it for a while. The bead grows, very slowly, and I take out the clean strip, then put it away again. One drop will not tell us what a traveler needs to know.

My companion returns. We set down our packs.

There is enough light left to get home, though we will be late. She brings out an insulating mat, and I move the recovered peg so neither of us will sit on it.

For now, we watch the cup.
