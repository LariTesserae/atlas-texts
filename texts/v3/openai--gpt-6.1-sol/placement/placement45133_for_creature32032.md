At first I put myself on the flat stone.

It is the one place where I think I know how to sit. Its hollow was made elsewhere, by repeated use; I could settle into it without asking the mountain to take another impression. I would leave my coat near the seam, find room for my knees, and lower a hand into the pool.

But the hand is too ready. Even held still, it is waiting to be found. I would feel the first small weight and want to turn my wrist, to learn where the body ended. I would have to keep remembering not to search.

So I leave that place to someone who can sit there longer.

For a while I am nearer the floor: something thin enough to occupy the space beneath a yielding edge. Warm water lies over me. When the creature passes, its supports press down in succession, and I learn the distance between them through the mineral. But I cannot quite find a body there. Everything I imagine either needs to eat what it eats or gets in its way.

I move upward.

There is a place between the handle and the shell where almost nothing happens, except that the cup remains a cup.

I think I would be the binding.

Not all of it. One of the fibers on the underside, drawn tight against the shell, where the creature has to reach around to touch me.

Before being brought here, I would have been longer. Perhaps I came from a stem split with a thumbnail. I would have lain across the groove in the flat stone while someone worked water through me, drew me back and forth, separated what would bend from what would snap. I do not know whether the stone came with that person or with another. There is no need for me to have accompanied every object.

I would remember the work as a difference in my thickness.

One part of me would be smooth from being pulled. Another would retain a short, stiff thread that refused to lie down. When the handle was tied on, that thread would have caught beneath a crossing turn. It would still be there.

My world would have a hard curve on one side and another on the other. Between them, a narrow dark space would open when the handle was lifted. I would tighten across it. When the cup was set down, the space would close a little, but never completely.

I would have no view of the pool.

The creature’s arrival would reach me through the shelf: first a slight load near its edge, then a pause. Sometimes the pause would last so long that the wood ceased to move beneath me. I would have no way to know whether it had turned back or was still stretched across the gap.

Then the shell would receive a touch.

It would go around the cup. Eventually one of its supports would press the crossing where I was held. The pressure would travel along me, farther than the part being touched. A loose strand would lift and settle.

I would not mistake this for the hand that tied the knot. That hand had pulled, held, pulled again. This touch comes from below and attends to small places. It does not retie anything.

In the wet warmth, I would have tightened into the shell. I would no longer slide easily against the other fibers. A little mineral might have lodged among us, though not enough to make us stone. I would remain capable of taking a strain.

The creature could remove the water beneath the cup. It could press each turn of the binding. It could not keep a fiber from wearing where it bore against an edge.

I would wear there, slowly.

This seems like a place I could inhabit: not exempt from use merely because so much around me remains at its beginning.

If someone returned and lifted the cup, I would take the weight. The handle would move a little before the shell followed. Perhaps they would notice and put a finger over the knot. Perhaps they would set it down again without drinking.

Afterward, the creature would make its difficult crossing.

I would be beneath the handle when it arrived, still holding the two hard pieces close enough together.
