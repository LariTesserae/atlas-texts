I would be somewhere beneath the crossing where the objects have accumulated.

Not directly beneath the marker. There would be a little warmth there, and too much traffic. Farther out, under the jointed pipe, where its lowest elbow has kept the floor from sealing completely, there is room for a body to lie on its side. One of the pipe’s openings faces into the gap. Dust comes through it whenever a belt moves in the next district.

At first, that might be all anyone could see of me: a few fibers caught in the opening, moving against the direction of the dust.

I would have settled there while the structure was still small. The pipe would have been useful shelter. Then someone would have brought the roll of dormant tissue, and later the wheel. Each arrival would change the pressure on my chamber. I would make adjustments. A ceiling raised here, a root diverted there. Eventually the objects above would fit together more securely than their owners intended.

I would have to be careful about that.

There would be a day when the wheel stopped trying to turn because a root had entered its bearing. I would work the root free. For a long time afterward I would keep that part of the floor cool.

Whoever brought the wheel might still want it back.

My body would be low, with a narrow waist where I had spent too many seasons passing through the same opening. Four walking limbs, although one would no longer straighten. Two hands under the chest. A third, smaller hand behind the head, grown from a replacement joint that had been fitted at the wrong angle.

The little hand would be useful for holding a tool while I worked with the others. At rest, it would comb the fibers around my head seam. Sometimes it would continue after I had fallen asleep.

I would not have a name that could be rattled out into the air. Through the root, my neighbors would recognize me by a hesitation between two pulses. The hesitation would have started as a fault in a throat conduit. By then, repairing it would make introductions difficult.

My work would concern the last few feet of journeys.

A warm route can bring a traveler to a crossing without bringing them quite to the place they left. Usually the difference is small. A feeding column has moved to the other side of a pillar. A dwelling entrance opens one layer lower. The traveler’s strip remains alive, but tightens around the wrist when held near the wrong surface.

I would notice because I slept underneath.

A footstep seeking one hollow and finding another has a particular pressure. So does a hand laid down where a familiar vein ought to be. I would feel those things through the floor of my chamber and, if I were awake, come out.

I would not be able to restore a district. Often I would not understand what had shifted.

But I could make a small place agree with a returning body.

I would ask the traveler to put a hand on the pipe. I would put mine against its buried end. We might remain that way until the ceiling brightened. Somewhere between us, the pipe would take up a little of the warmth from the traveler’s strip. Then I could encourage a vein toward it.

Sometimes this would be enough. The strip would loosen. The traveler would turn, abruptly certain of a direction, and leave before I had withdrawn my hand.

Sometimes nothing would happen.

Those would be the ones who stayed beneath the crossing with me.

There would not be much room. We would sleep with the curve of one body fitted against the plates of the other, the unused limbs passed through the pipe. In the morning, one of us would have to go out first so the other could turn over.

I would learn their feeding habits. One would need a hotter column than mine. Another would take grains from a pouch but refuse to fasten a hand to any of the local stems. I would bring back a short piece of root and let it renew in the chamber until it smelled less like the crossing.

There would be disagreements about whether to try again.

Through the pipe, an argument would reach every object overhead. The wheel would make a fractional turn. A sheet of dormant tissue would wrinkle. Residents passing above would stop and lay their hands on the structure, not necessarily to join us. Some would only want to know whether it was about to fall.

Afterward I would have to inspect all the supports.

I think I would keep a shallow groove in the floor beside my sleeping place. In it I would lay the strips that travelers had taken off.

Not all of them would be abandoned. Some would be left for a night while a wrist healed. Some would be too dry to endure the next journey. Occasionally someone would give me one, holding it between both hands until I had taken its full weight, slight as that was.

I would feed them separately.

A strip from a fast mill could distress one taken from an old roof. Their edges would turn toward each other; the neighboring leaves would brighten; by morning both might have begun growing into a single thing. I would learn to place mineral-hard scraps between them.

There would be strips I had tended for so long that I could no longer remember the bodies that brought them. One, dark and narrow, would admit nourishment only through its cut end. I would hold it there with the little hand behind my head while sorting grains.

I would have tried giving it away. Nobody’s wrist would suit it.

The young would come to look at the groove.

They would want to make the strips respond. I would let them touch the healthier ones. They would discover that a certain pressure made leaves fold three feet away, or caused a warm line to pass under the chamber wall. They would try it again, harder.

I would move their fingers apart.

One would ask, through the floor, where the dark strip went.

I would offer the few sensations I had received from it: a constriction, a cold surface, something opening after a long delay.

The young one would go quiet. Later it would bring me a small arch made of fibers, with one side much longer than the other. We would find a place for it above the groove, clear of anything that might draw nourishment from its feet.

For a while, the crossing would hold.

Then the pipe’s lower elbow would begin rising.

Only a little at first. Enough to make my sleeping position uncomfortable. I would remove growth from beneath it, believing that pressure had built up in the floor. The next day it would be higher again. Dust would pass through the opening without any belt moving nearby.

I would go up to the marker and find the return distance extended beyond the leaf’s printed margin. New figures would be forming in the fresh tissue at its base.

There would be preparations. Neighbors would come to help loosen the things overhead. The wheel would turn freely for the first time in years. I would discover that several roots I had been trimming belonged to someone living deeper down, and wait while they withdrew them.

I would have to leave the chamber before I was finished with it.

The strips could travel in a folded pouch, but I would need to keep the dark one in my hand. The little arch would go under my coat. I would take a length of the pipe, cut just above the elbow, and carry it with two limbs while walking on the others.

That would be how I met the door carrier.

It would be standing on fresh floor, the slab lowered onto its fringe of roots. I would wait at a distance while it opened the door and inspected what lay beyond.

When it closed the door again, I would approach.

We would touch the handle together. It would still hold the temperature of the place the carrier had just looked into. I would feel an opening, then a stretch of cold growth, then something I could not follow.

The carrier would lift its door.

Before it moved on, I would turn my piece of pipe so it could touch the old inner surface. We would stay like that briefly: its hand inside the elbow, mine on the handle.

Then it would take the route along the rib, and I would descend into a hollow.

There I would set down the pipe. The leaves would be too stiff and closely jointed for shelter, but beneath them I would find a narrow layer that yielded to my tool.

I would begin with a space for the hand holding the dark strip.
