At first I would look for a recess where I could put my back against the wall and still see the water. Not the deepest recess; that already belongs to someone. A shallower place beside it, where the floor begins to turn upward and the fibers lean in two directions.

I would stay there through several changes of light.

The humming would make it difficult to tell whether I was resting. Each time I began to go still, another part of it would become audible: a knock farther down in the root, a brief wet friction, the low note that persists between contractions. Eventually I would stop trying to hear all of it at once. My jaw against the floor would receive one chamber particularly clearly. I would come to know that chamber without knowing where it was.

I think I would be smaller than the gray-green animals. Small enough to shelter beside one without sharing its recess, but too large to disappear into the floor with the mites. My body would be narrow at the head and wider behind, with short legs that could tuck into grooves along my sides. At rest, I would resemble a wet seed caught where two surfaces meet.

There would be no hard shell. The back would have a firmer skin, faintly ridged, while the underside stayed soft enough to feel the nap bending beneath it. Two dark eyes would sit close to the floor. I would see approaching feet more readily than faces.

For a while I would use the polished paths. Their smoothness would make walking easy, but there would be places where I had to wait for a larger body to pass. Then I would find another route: along the damp margins, over the shallow lips of old wounds, behind the pellet stalk where the wall is almost vertical. My feet would hold well there. They would have small pads, less broad than the others’, able to grip a single tuft of fibers.

That would become my usual way around the room.

I would eat what softens at the edges. A white pellet broken by a beetle. A little of the clear film beneath a fallen leaf. Eggs abandoned in a dry place. There would be a fold inside my mouth for holding food, but I would rarely fill it. After eating, I would return to the wall and press my underside against the warmth.

The place I kept returning to would be near the basin’s outgoing openings.

Not directly in front of them. The water there would tug too steadily at my feet. Just above them, on a narrow ledge scarcely visible from the middle of the hollow. When the chambers released their pressure, water would rise and touch my chin.

I would learn to lift my head before it arrived.

Once, a translucent scrap would catch beneath a fleshy lip. The next release would pull it tight, then let it sag. I would touch it with my mouth because it moved. Part would come away. The rest would loosen and slip through.

Afterward, the water would no longer reach quite so high.

I would remain there, though there would be nothing else to eat.

Over time my resting place would grow smoother beneath me. A low ridge would develop along its outer edge, enough to keep one foot from sliding into the current while I slept. I would often wake with that foot resting against it. When I moved elsewhere for a day, the ridge would still be there when I came back.

I would know a few inhabitants by the disturbances they made. The older animal’s approach would press a slow series of dips into the floor. The young one would pause so often that I might lift my head twice before it reached me. Beetles would scarcely register through my belly, but their claws would sound clear against a worn patch nearby.

Sometimes the young animal would put its face into the basin above my ledge. Its folded fringes would open in the water. From below, they would briefly obscure most of the room.

I would wait beneath them until it lifted its head.

There would still be long intervals when I did very little. My mouth might rest against the wall without licking it. My forefeet might remain spread after I had finished climbing. A passing mite could walk over my back, investigate a ridge, and go on.

By then I would no longer choose the recess with the best view. I would spend most of my time on that ledge, half hidden by the basin’s rim, where the wall was warm and the water came close without covering me.

Late in the day, a change in the hum would wake me. I would uncurl my front feet and raise my chin. Water would swell through the openings below, carrying a fine pale strand. I might catch it, or let it pass.

Then I would lower myself again, fitting my belly to the shallow place it had made.
