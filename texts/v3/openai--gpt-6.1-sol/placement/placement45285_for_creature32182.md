At first I think I would be in the occupied workshop, somewhere near the instruments.

Not at the wheel. The wheel is too visible, and its motion belongs to everyone who enters. I would be farther back, where the measuring rod is put away: two iron cleats on the wall, one taking its weight, the other preventing it from falling sideways. Below the lower cleat there would be a small, persistent wet patch. The caretaker would have checked it for a leak and found none.

That is where I begin to find myself.

Not in the patch, exactly. Under the cleat, between its back and a brick that has lost some of its face. A space too shallow for a finger, but with considerable room along its edges.

I would not initially know the room as a room. There would be the weight of the rod arriving each evening, the slight turn of the cleat against its screws. There would be water descending the rod in two narrow paths, one of which reaches me. There would be a great movement elsewhere that changes how firmly I must hold.

The light would be less important.

My back would have taken the colour of the cleat, except along a pale margin where I had grown past it. This margin would sometimes betray me. A mechanic, leaning close to read a maker’s stamp, might notice something that did not belong to the iron.

He would touch it.

I would draw the margin in.

For a while he would wait, his thumb still against the cleat. Then he would return to what he had been doing.

I would remain drawn in long after he left.

***

The rod would tell me things before the caretaker entered.

Her approach would come through the wall and floor, but carrying the rod changes her footsteps. Its lower end occasionally touches a stair. The touch travels ahead of her, through the iron rail and the stones beneath the landing. I would already have closed some of my pads when the workshop door opened.

Then the rod would return to its cleats.

A little water. A little pressure. Sometimes grit, sometimes a strand of moss. In winter, something colder than the district has been all day.

I would feed along the underside of the lower cleat, where the water holds its finest deposit. Each grain would require a separate movement. There would be no meal large enough to finish. Hunger would gradually become the knowledge that a certain edge has not yet been searched.

I would grow toward the screw.

The screw would not move often, but it would move enough.

Inside me, the small clear chambers would change shape when the rod was lifted or replaced. Their bubbles would incline toward the pump. During the stroke, one chamber near my narrow end would swell more slowly than the others. I would have to spread two pads farther apart to remain comfortable.

I would not know this as a defect. It would simply be part of finding a good hold.

***

There is another place I might be: on the measuring rod itself.

Not travelling on it. I think I would find that difficult. The lifting, the passage over the court, the abrupt descent into water—too many pressures without a surface that stays put.

But when it rests against the wall, its lowest marked inches lie beside my shelter. I could reach them without letting go of the brick.

The paint there has cracked. Brown matter lodges between the cracks. I would begin to feed on it.

Over several months, the pale points would gather around the ordinary-depth mark. The caretaker would turn the rod toward the lamp, wipe it with her thumb, then look more closely.

On the next evening she would bring a brush.

This is the first event I can imagine that I would help to cause without understanding it.

She would lift the rod away. I would flatten under the cleat. The brush would touch the scale, pause, and pass instead along the unmarked side. She would paint a new line there, with its number beside it.

Afterwards she would put the rod back facing the other way.

The water would no longer reach me by the same path.

For several nights I would leave my shelter and return to it. I would follow the old wet edge down the wall until it ended. At the cleat I would open my front folds against iron that was clean and almost dry.

Eventually I would move a little around the screw.

There is room there that I had not used before. A narrow seam, protected by the head. The new path of water comes down through it.

In the book, perhaps:

**Rod reversed. Leave in this position.**

Nothing about me. The instruction would still improve my life.

***

I do not think I would be the oldest animal in the district.

I would have a place that was old.

Before the cleat, perhaps there had been another cleat, broader and secured lower down. The brick beneath mine bears a dark rectangle with two filled holes. I would cross that rectangle sometimes. Its lower edge would offer good feeding even where it appears dry.

Once, during a long intake, I would put a pad over one of the filled holes and feel a slight yielding.

I would stay there through the stroke.

After that, I would return to it occasionally. I would press against it in different ways, taking some pads off the brick and setting them down again. Nothing visible would happen. There would be no reason for the caretaker to inspect it.

I might stop returning after a week. I might continue for years.

Some of my life would be like that.

***

In late winter I would leave the wall.

The first distance would be the hardest: from the cleat to the old rectangle, then down across three brick courses whose joints had been repaired with mortar too smooth to enter. I would travel by the screw holes of an abandoned cable bracket. Under the bottom hole, there would be a stain broad enough to turn on.

By the time I reached the court, another animal would already be following the groove I meant to use.

I would wait.

It would take most of the night for the way to become clear.

At the basin I would find a place beside a shallow channel entering from beneath the platform. My body would not quite fit between two others. When I spread, one edge would rest over the edge of the smaller animal.

It would press upward.

I would move back until there was a thin line of exposed stone between us.

For several days, this would be my place. The wall, the cleat, the hanging rod would be distant arrangements. Water would arrive openly here, across my back. A bird’s foot would come down in the basin close enough to send a pressure through all my chambers at once.

I would hold.

When I returned to the workshop, I would be thinner. One grain on my back would be missing, leaving a pale spot that did not darken until spring.

The cleat would still be there.

The rod would have been put away correctly.

***

So I think I would be one of the animals, but not the one beneath the step.

I would live behind the lower rod cleat in the pump room, in a place where a person’s daily work brings water to the wall.

The caretaker might see me twice in a year. Once when she oils the rod, once when the cleat needs tightening. On the second occasion she would slide damp felt beneath the loose edge of my body and wait until I released the screw head.

She would hold me against the neighbouring brick while she worked.

The new screw would pull the cleat closer to the wall. Before tightening it fully, she would glance at the space beneath.

Then she would loosen it a quarter turn.

I would spend the following night exploring the changed shelter. There would be a new edge to feed along, and an unfamiliar pressure near the top. By morning I would have settled slightly farther to the left.

When the rod returned that evening, its weight would reach me through the iron.

I would put my pads down.

A little later, the pump would begin.
