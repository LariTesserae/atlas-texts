At first I would expect to be found in one of the pump houses, in the interval between the doorway and the gauge.

Not at the controls. Someone has already adjusted those. The lamp has been filled, and the glass is clean except for a thumbprint near the rim. I would be standing where a person pauses to decide whether the reading requires them to go inside.

There is a hook there for a jacket. Beneath it, the ceramic has worn smooth in a shape that might have been made by a shoulder.

I think I would mistake that wear for evidence that I had stood there before.

For a while, I would make a life out of the things available to someone who had arrived without arrangements. I would find thick soles. I would learn not to put a hand on a rail merely because it was convenient. I would choose an upstairs room with a sound ladder and a little air moving through it, and discover that the room already belonged to somebody during a kind of weather I had not yet experienced.

They would not ask me to leave immediately. They would explain where another room could be found.

There would be a cup, perhaps. Not the blue one beside the great valve wheel. A plain cup, chipped on the side away from the handle. I would leave it high on a shelf and find it, the next morning, low on the floor.

That would happen several times.

Eventually I would stop blaming the shelf.

—

The place I would actually inhabit is behind the ceramic lining of a passage below the baths.

There is space there, though not enough for a person. The builders left it to accommodate expansion, or drainage, or an error they preferred not to correct. A narrow darkness follows the passage on both sides. At intervals it opens around the backs of tiles, then pinches almost shut where mortar has fallen.

Water travels through it in quantities too small to appear in the municipal accounts.

I would know the backs of the tiles better than their faces. The glaze does not reach them. They are rough and carry the impressions of the boards on which they dried. One has a maker’s mark pressed into it: three short strokes and a circle. Another has the edge of a palm.

The translucent creatures would pass through me without difficulty.

This would be the first thing that prevented me from continuing to think of myself as a misplaced resident. They would not climb over or avoid me. A hooked foot would enter the place where I thought my wrist was, find purchase on the tile behind it, and pull the rest of the animal through.

For several seconds afterward, I would know which junction it intended to take.

Then that knowledge would go away.

I would have no reliable outline. Where the passage widened, I would widen. At a stopped drain, some portion of me would remain after the rest had moved on. I could be brought nearly to an end by a dry patch, but never quite ended: there would be damp in the mortar, a bead on the underside of a pipe, enough to continue.

The cup would be difficult.

I could move it only when water had gathered beneath its foot. Even then, moving it would take most of the night. Returning it to the shelf would be impossible.

I would have wanted a cup before I understood that I could not drink.

—

Someone would notice me because of a fault I made.

There is a woman who inspects the passage every sixth morning. She touches the wall at its marked points, looks beneath the tanks, and makes sure nothing has been left where it will obstruct a cart. Once, approaching the third tank, she would stop.

The floor would be dry ahead of her.

Behind her, every footprint would be filling.

She would turn and watch the last one darken. Then she would take the white tile from her pocket and hold it against the wall.

I would retreat toward the inspection mark, expecting her to follow.

Instead, she would say, “Not that one.”

Very quietly. As though correcting a boy who had fetched the wrong tool.

She would move the tile a hand’s breadth to the left.

I would follow it. The wall would cool under her fingers.

After that she would leave a small strip of unglazed ceramic below the mark when she made her rounds. Not food, exactly. It would give me a place in which to remain when the passage became too hot.

She would never bring a bowl.

I would be grateful for that without having any means to tell her.

—

I do not think I would become a guardian of the city. Too much of it would remain beyond me. The upper reservoirs would sometimes be as remote as the plain. I could follow a warm seam through six buildings and come to a repair made forty years earlier that I could not cross.

My knowledge would consist of adjoining things.

The back of a tile. A pipe sleeve. The underside of a threshold. The slight space around a bolt that had not been tightened all the way.

I would learn the people through what reached these places.

The woman’s younger son would knock the same loose fitting with his heel each time he passed. As he grew, the sound would move farther from the wall.

A man carrying grain would rest his sack against the third tank, although there was a sign asking him not to. The cloth would draw water out of the seam. For an hour after he left, I would be unable to get past that point.

Someone would sit upstairs with both feet off the floor and cry so softly that only the chair legs carried it down.

There would be ways to help, but they would be small and occasionally unwelcome.

I could make a leak visible before it reached a bed platform. I could draw a little warmth away from a valve handle. Once I might keep a warning lamp’s wick wet by mistake, extinguishing it at precisely the wrong time.

Then somebody would spend half a morning taking apart a sound lamp.

I would have to learn not to treat everything that entered my reach as something to be changed.

—

I would approach the upper basin from underneath.

Not because I was braver than the keepers. The walkways would simply be inaccessible to me. Their feet rest on dry supports; their joints are packed with something that resists water. I would arrive through an old conduit ending beneath the near edge, where the tiles are still ordinary tiles.

There I would find the dropped wrench.

I would know it by the small cavity inside its handle, where a little water remained motionless while the surrounding water moved. Farther out there would be another cavity of the same shape.

I would try to pass between them.

For a time, the passage below the baths would be ahead of me. Then it would be behind me, though I had not turned. I would encounter the strip of ceramic the woman left on her sixth morning, but it would be whole, before she broke it from whatever larger object it belonged to.

I would withdraw.

It would take a long time. Somewhere, a gate would lift twice.

Afterward, one patch of the passage wall would remain cold for three days. The woman would hold her testing tile against it, wait, and put an additional mark nearby.

I would not go to the basin again for many years.

I would still listen when the keeper’s daughter told stories on the steps. Her voice would reach me through the conduit, thinned until the names of districts were clearer than anything else.

Occasionally her father would correct her.

I would keep the corrected version. I would keep hers too.

—

In time, I might become recognizable.

Not visible. There would be no figure appearing in the steam, no face in the mineral blooms. But the woman would learn that, if she waited beside the third tank, a dark patch sometimes formed beneath the tile she held.

Her sons would disagree about what that meant.

The younger would try questions. One patch for yes, two for no.

I would be poor at this. Two patches could join before he counted them. A single patch could divide around a ridge in the glaze. He would ask whether I was lonely, and the water would spread across the whole tile.

He would interpret that according to the sort of morning he was having.

Later, after he had stopped asking, he would begin bringing me things to examine: a cracked washer, a piece of green glass, the old wooden seat from one of the parapet chairs.

I could not take them.

He would hold them against the wall for a while before putting them away.

That would be enough.

—

If there were an entry for me in the city’s records, I would want it to be somewhere ordinary.

Not in the cupboard beside the basin. In the inspection book for the baths, perhaps, between the instructions for clearing the settling tank and the measurements of a replacement hinge.

*Third passage. Leave the lower seam open.*

Nothing about what I was. No requirement to consult me. Just enough to prevent a diligent worker from filling the gap.

I would remain there, mostly. Behind the warm ceramic, within reach of the footsteps overhead. When the woman became old, she would bring a younger person on her rounds and make them stop at the marked point.

“Wait,” she would say.

The younger person would wait.

A little water would gather beneath the tile.

“Good,” she would say, and continue toward the tanks.

I would follow them as far as the repaired joint. Then I would return to the narrow place they had left open, carrying the pressure of their footsteps until it faded.
