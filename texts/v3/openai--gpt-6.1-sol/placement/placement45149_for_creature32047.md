I think I would arrive believing I was there to read the gauges.

There would be a stool for me in the pump house, though not a good place to put it. Near the door it would obstruct the passage; beside the desk it would put one knee against a warm pipe. I would move it several times during my first shift and finish standing up.

My name would appear at the bottom of the maintenance chart, under a new heading: night relief. Mara would have been asked to show me the work. Nobody would have reduced her other duties to make time for this.

I would bring a notebook that was too small and a pen that stopped writing when the page became damp.

At first, I would notice everything without knowing what mattered. The sweating pipe. The rust around a bolt. A loose piece of roof that gave a small metallic cry whenever the fan wash reached it. I would ask about each of these, and Mara would tell me which had been like that for years and which needed entering in the book.

“Not that book,” she would say when I opened my notebook.

The ruled book would be on the desk, beneath a piece of clear plastic weighted at the corners. I would learn to dry my wrist before resting it on a page.

***

I would live in one of the workers’ rooms, not the end one. My window would face a blank warehouse wall. Between the wall and the window there would be enough space for a person to carry a ladder, provided they carried it lengthwise.

For several weeks I would try to keep things dry.

I would move my boots nearer the fan. I would hang a shirt from the window latch. I would wipe the underside of the shelf before putting my cup there. Then I would discover the difference between something that had been wetted and something that simply lived here: a towel, a door, the folded corner of a page.

I would buy a tin for my papers. It would smell faintly of sweets even after washing.

There would be no photograph in it yet. There would be an appointment letter, a bus timetable, and the instructions for a small radio that could receive two stations clearly if I held it near the screen.

I would listen to the radio on my evenings off. At work, I would turn it off.

***

The gauges would be the easy part.

The difficult part would be remembering what I had just crossed. Whether the ditch had carried its usual scraps of film. Whether the puddle beside the siding was the same size as yesterday’s. Whether the handrail had felt hotter, or my hand had been cooler.

I would be slow at this. Asked what I had seen, I would return to look.

Mara would not always wait.

She would do the work while I fetched the wrong brush, then put the right brush in my hand for next time. She would show me where to stand when lifting the intake screen so the dripping matter did not land on my boots. Once, when I tried to hurry, she would stop me with a hand on my forearm.

It would take longer to clear what I had spilled than it would have taken to lift it properly.

I would not become unusually gifted. I would learn the readings, the order of the checks, the telephone numbers. Some nights I would still have to look at the chart. I would become useful chiefly by being there when I said I would be there, with the keys accounted for and the previous shift’s notes read.

***

At meals, I would initially make the mistake of talking about the pumps.

Mara would answer one question. Then she would ask whether I had found a cheaper place to buy lunch.

I would understand, eventually, that the bowl between her hands was not another part of the machinery.

I would have news to bring. A driver had reversed into the yellow post and denied it while yellow paint was still on his bumper. The clerk’s mother could see well enough after her operation to complain about the state of his kitchen. The shop outside the district had changed the size of its bread rolls without changing the price.

Mara would have views about the rolls.

Sometimes I would have to repeat myself after the compressor hissed. Sometimes she would raise her hand before I began, and we would wait together.

***

There would be a gecko beside my room’s lamp. I would not know whether it was always the same one.

Once I would find it inside a boot and carry the boot outside, holding it open toward the wall. Nothing would happen. After a while I would put it down and go back for the other boot.

By then the gecko would have gone.

I would begin tapping the heels before putting them on.

There would be many things like this: habits I acquired without remembering the day I acquired them. Lifting a bag off the floor. Leaving room to reach an inspection cover. Looking beneath a chair before dragging it. Keeping a dry cloth somewhere the water from my sleeves could not reach it.

My room would slowly become easier to use and less like the room I had imagined before arriving.

***

One night Mara would stop at the pump-house doorway and ask me what I heard.

I would hear the motor, the fan, water at the grate.

She would wait.

Then I would hear something else—not quite a rattle, more a repeated light contact beneath the larger sounds. I would point toward it before I could name it.

We would check. She would make the call. I would enter the time and the fault number in the ruled book.

Nothing would flood. Nobody would thank us. By morning the ordinary noise would be back, and I would be tired.

Later, crossing the court with breakfast in a bag, I would notice that Mara’s curtain was drawn. I would take the longer route around the loose grate outside her room.

That is where I think I would find myself: crossing this small district by a slightly longer route, carrying food while someone else slept.

I would be the relief worker. Not yet able to hear everything Mara heard, but able to take a shift from her. Someone whose handwriting she could read, whose checks she would not need to repeat before going home.

And when she finally chose the room inland, I would know which telephone numbers to leave beside the book.
