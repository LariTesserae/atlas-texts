At first I think I would be beside the lamp in the long seep.

Not inside its housing, exactly. Somewhere just beyond the reach of its light, where a person kneeling to clean it would set down a tool. There is a shallow depression there, worn by something that no longer happens. Water enters it from two directions and leaves by a third. A little pale growth has established itself on the sheltered edge.

It is tempting to belong to that growth. To have no need to understand the whole plain, only the composition of the seep and whether warmth will return. To lie flat through the long closures.

But I think I would be the person who comes to lift its edge.

I would not be its keeper. I would have been asked to check it while passing, and at first I would have checked too often. Someone would eventually explain that lifting it every day does no good. After that I would learn to leave it alone for longer than felt comfortable.

My shelter would stand some distance away, on a rib that continues into a flooded district. One end of the rib would still be a route. The other would be something people mention when giving directions, though no one uses it anymore.

I would have a bed there, and a second bed usually folded against the wall. Not because I preferred living alone. The room would also contain a dry cabinet, a low work surface, and more lamps than one person needed. There would be very little space left for company, but enough.

My work would begin with those lamps.

People would bring them in because the shutters stuck, because their light had become unreliable, because a pattern that ought to be visible from the next joint could no longer be distinguished through the mist. I would clean the housings, replace what could be replaced, and test them through the small window while someone stood outside.

A repaired lamp would not be returned simply because it shone. Its owner would need to recognize it.

One traveler’s shutter would close a little slowly. Another would have an uneven interval between flashes, caused by a stiff thumb rather than the mechanism. I would learn not to improve these things without asking.

Occasionally I would keep a lamp overnight to find a fault that appeared only after the casing grew cold. Its owner would sleep in the spare bed. In the morning, while they pulled on their warmed lining, they would tell me about the route they had taken.

That is how the other work would accumulate.

At first, a few notes about where a lamp had failed. Then a sketch of the place where someone had waited for water to fall. Then a correction to an older sketch, not because it was wrong when made, but because a housing had turned and remained turned, leaving its service edge on the inaccessible side.

Eventually people would come without anything for me to mend.

They would bring observations, and I would help put them into a form someone else could use.

***

I do not think I would be particularly good at feeling the ground.

I would know the rib beneath my shelter and the short route to the nearest supply opening. Beyond those, I would often need another person. There would be a pressure change everyone around me noticed before I did. I would see their weight shift and follow them, hoping not to have followed too late.

Once, as a child, I might have been able to distinguish a certain chamber filling. My family would remember it. They would still occasionally ask whether I could feel it, and I would stand with my feet apart, trying to find something I no longer recognized.

I would be useful in other ways.

I would remember which observations were made under which conditions. Who had been carrying a full drum. Who had just returned from several hours near an exhaust and could not get warm afterward. Whether a route had been tested during the lowering of the pools or only after the water returned.

I would ask irritating questions.

“Did you stop there, or did you notice it while walking?”

“Was the hatch already open?”

“Which foot?”

Sometimes the answers would make a difference. Sometimes the person would be too tired to answer, and I would set the page aside, offer the warm cloth, and make food.

There would be a place in the cabinet for unfinished accounts.

It would be nearly as large as the place for finished ones.

***

The records would not be beautiful.

Paper would be scarce enough that several kinds of information shared a sheet. A drawing of a conduit might have a list of lamp cells down one side and, on the reverse, instructions for softening a difficult batch of food material. I would copy the route onto the next usable surface and keep the original because someone might still need the recipe.

For frequently traveled ground, I would make small raised plans.

Cord fixed to a board for structural ribs. Thin metal for edges that could be felt through gloves. Movable pieces for hatches and housings. The boards would show only a little territory, usually less than a person could cross in an hour.

They would be useful to someone arriving exhausted, unable to hold a spoken account in mind.

I would guide their fingers over the proposed route, then stop at each uncertain section.

There would be no permanent piece for uncertainty. I would use whatever was at hand: a loose washer, a folded scrap, a short length of cord laid across another. People would sometimes mistake such a marker for a feature of the ground.

After that happened twice, I would begin making them all the same shape.

This small improvement would occupy several evenings.

***

I would have a coat with an old repair across one shoulder, made by someone more skilled than I was. The new material would have dried into a stiff ridge. Water would collect along it and occasionally run down inside the sleeve when I reached too high.

I would keep meaning to fix it.

There would be someone who visited often enough to know this. They would stand in my doorway with a wet lamp and glance at the shoulder before looking at my face.

They would work farther out, around the housings that turn in sequence. I would know their routes from the accounts they brought me, but I would rarely have walked them.

They would dislike how many questions I asked. They would answer anyway.

Sometimes, after we had finished, they would stay. We would eat with the work surface cleared just enough for two bowls. When the shelter shuddered, each of us would reach for the same loose container, then one would withdraw a hand.

I would gradually learn their ordinary tiredness from the tiredness that required checking their fastenings.

They would learn when I had been inside too long.

On those days they would make me come out as far as the seep.

***

We would go during a familiar phase, taking the raised joints. I would carry a lamp although they had one too.

At the ridge, they would stop where they always stopped. I would go a little farther to look at the pale plants.

The water would be thin enough to show the mineral felt beneath them. I would crouch, keeping one boot braced against the rough edge, and lift a corner with a flat piece of metal.

Underneath, perhaps, there would be new growth.

Not much. A few tightly crowded shoots, almost without color.

I would want to show them immediately. I would turn, raise the lamp, and then remember that the sign I was making meant *come here*, not *look*.

They would already be moving toward me.

I would correct the signal. They would stop. For a moment I would feel foolish, and then the pumps would change phase.

The seep would thicken. Water would press around my boot and spread into the shallow depression where I had first imagined myself belonging. The lamp’s reflection would break apart.

They would put a hand on my upper arm.

I would rise carefully, leaving the little patch undisturbed. We would stand back through the rest of the cycle.

The growth would disappear beneath the flow, then emerge again.

There would be nothing useful to do for it that evening.

On the way home, I would let them choose the route. Near the shelter, I would notice that their lamp shutter had begun to hesitate. They would say it had been doing that for days.

Inside, I would hang up both coats. They would lower the spare bed while I opened the lamp housing over a cloth.

Later I would add a short entry to the account of the seep: the phase, the extent of the flow, the new growth beneath the old.

Before putting the page away, I would ask whether they had felt any change in the ridge.

They would think for a while.

“No,” they would say. Then, “I wasn’t checking.”

I would leave that part blank.
