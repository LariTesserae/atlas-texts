I would first be noticed in the records office, although not necessarily seen.

The clerk would open a fresh pod, unfold its membrane, and find that one of the blank areas resisted lying flat. She would smooth it with the side of her hand. The measurements would settle. The blank area would rise again.

Not much. Enough to cast a shadow under the desk lamp.

She would assume something had nested in the cabinet.

For several days she would leave the pod open beside a little dish of water. This is what one does with a nesting animal whose habits are unknown: makes water available, moves the important papers, waits.

I think I would drink before I knew I was drinking.

***

There is a room behind the records office that has not yet been assigned a purpose.

It has a window looking onto a narrow interval between buildings. The interval is not quite an alley. A person can enter it from one end, but at the other end the walls join without appearing to meet. The pruners inspect it regularly. Nothing dangerous has happened there. Nothing of any kind has happened there, which is why it remains under inspection.

The unused room contains a table, two weights for flattening membranes, and a stool with a split seat.

This is where I would acquire most of my shape.

At first, I would occupy the slight depression in the stool. Later I could sit upright, provided nobody opened the door too quickly. A draft would disturb the arrangement of my shoulders.

I would have hands fairly early. There would be things to hold down.

My face would take longer. The clerk would speak to me while working, not looking up, and I would gradually become able to answer from the place her voice was directed toward.

She would ask whether I had moved the weights.

I would say yes.

She would ask why.

It would be difficult to explain that the membrane beneath them had begun to hurt.

She would move the weights herself after that.

***

I would not know whether I had come out of a map.

The oldest pod in the cabinet might contain a small figure in the rear room, seated beside the window. But old records are unreliable, and a figure on a map could mean a municipal facility rather than a person.

The clerk would check newer records.

In one, the room would contain a table and nothing else.

In another, the room would not be there.

She would take both out of circulation.

I would learn this habit from her: setting a troublesome thing aside without immediately deciding what it meant.

My first useful work would be sorting the membranes that had been withdrawn. I would not arrange them by date. Dates are already supplied by the skins of the pods, and repeating information takes space.

Instead, I would separate those that had become wrong from those that seemed to be waiting.

A wrong map might lead to a doorway that was now a wall.

A waiting map might show a doorway in a wall that had begun to soften around its edges.

There would be uncertainty. I would make mistakes. Once I would put a map of a demolished staircase among the waiting ones, and the clerk would find me standing at the site, looking upward, with my hands held as though around a railing.

She would bring me back before the cooling hours.

Afterward I would be required to eat before handling the old records.

***

Sella would dislike the dish of water.

“Use a cup,” she would tell me.

I would explain that the dish had been sufficient.

“For the clerk’s animal, perhaps.”

She would put a lidded cup in front of me. I would have difficulty gripping it. My fingers would fit well around flat things, less well around anything with a circumference.

She would pretend to be busy while I worked this out.

For some time I would eat only the water in which the crop tissue had been steeped. Then a few curls. Then rind, cut very small.

I would have no opinion about eggs until an egg moved.

My first coat would be an old one from the nursery. It would fit across the back and badly everywhere else. The nursery keeper would sew weights into the hem because, in a draft, I would lift slightly without intending to.

There would be a practical discussion about whether these were needed indoors.

I would keep them. The movement of a coat settling around my legs would help me recognize when I had stopped walking.

***

Eventually the records office would have work for me beyond sorting.

When the town grows, it makes rooms before it knows who will use them. Some remain open without difficulty. Others begin closing as soon as the pruners leave.

The clerk would send me to one of the latter with a stool and a current map.

I would sit there for an hour.

At the next inspection, the room would still be a room.

We would try again.

It would not work everywhere. In a damp building near the crop stores, the partitions would continue thickening around me, and En would have to cut my coat free. In another room, everything would remain open except the window, which would grow a shutter so closely fitted that it could not be lifted.

But in most places my presence would slow the filling of the interior.

I would make very few demands on a room. A little space to sit. Somewhere to put my feet. Enough distance between my face and the wall to look away from it.

These would prove to be demands the room could understand.

The council would pay me for the hours. The surveyor would suggest a larger stool, to establish a larger minimum clearance.

Sella would ask whether I was comfortable on the one I had.

I would discover that I was not.

***

My work would take me into houses before their inhabitants arrived.

I would sit in kitchens with no stove, bedrooms with no beds, parlors whose windows opened onto the backs of unformed walls.

I would come to know each by a small inconvenience. One room would cool my left hand. Another would make it hard to remember the way to the door. In one, I would always feel I had been interrupted, though there would be nothing I had been about to do.

I would report these things along with the dimensions.

The clerk would add a column to the forms.

Sometimes an ordinary object would do better than I could. A basin placed in a washroom might be enough to preserve it. A coat hung from a peg might keep a narrow entrance clear for weeks.

But chairs would be unreliable. Left alone, they would become involved with the floor.

I would take mine home at the end of each visit.

***

I would still live behind the records office.

There would now be a bed there. The clerk would have insisted upon it after finding me asleep over a stack of maps. She would say nothing about how the maps had folded up around my elbows.

Sleep would be difficult at first. With my eyes closed, I would lose track of which way the room extended. I would wake with one arm flat against the mattress, too thin to lift.

En would show me how to recover it.

Warm water. Patience. No pulling.

She would talk while she worked. Not about my arm. About the little case of probes she carried, which had begun to produce a lining and now would not shut.

I would listen carefully.

Later, when I could afford a second cup, I would invite her to visit. We would sit with the window closed so that she could put down her flask without it cooling too quickly.

She would ask whether I minded the room being small.

I would look at the bed, the table, the place where her knees almost touched mine.

“Sometimes,” I would say.

That answer would seem to please her.

***

I would avoid the central square at first.

Not out of fear, exactly. There would be too many familiar sensations around it: a surface holding a shape not yet settled, warmth passing beneath an apparently blank area, pressure without a clear direction.

The clerk would tell me I was not required to examine it.

I would be grateful that she had said this before I asked.

My rooms would be elsewhere. A little addition behind Sella’s kitchen. A nursery washroom. A new dwelling whose intended resident had become too ill to move and needed the doorway kept ready.

I would visit that dwelling for several weeks.

Each time, before leaving, I would test the latch and place my hand against the inner wall. The room would be warmest there. I would tell its future occupant this when I brought her the measurements.

She would ask whether a chair could go in that spot.

I would move my stool there on the next visit.

***

I do not think I would remain uncounted.

The clerk would eventually enter me as a resident of the rear room. There would be a question about my date of arrival. We would use the date on which Sella first charged me for a meal.

A name would come from someone else. Perhaps from En, who would need to call to me across a dark street. I would turn before I had considered whether the name belonged to me.

I would keep turning.

By then I would need more water than the little dish could have held. I would have a seam at one wrist that opened when I was tired, and a patch of warmth beneath the breastbone that stayed in place. The physician would examine it twice, then tell me to return if it moved.

There would be things I could no longer do. I would not fit comfortably between folded membranes. I would need to ask the clerk to open the door when my hands were full.

On some evenings I would be too dry to cook.

Sella would send food over in a covered bowl, with the water served separately. I would return the bowl the next day and sit for a while at one of her tables.

***

When the nursery keeper learned what I did, she would ask me to spend a cooling hour beside the empty bed.

There would be no emergency. The room would not be closing. She would simply need to check the clocks, wash the cups, and rest somewhere without listening for a child.

I would bring my stool.

The bed would be curved, with a clean membrane tucked around its edges. A little warmth would remain in it, replenished through a narrow vessel in the wall.

I would sit far enough away that nothing of mine touched it.

After a while I would hear the nursery keeper moving in the next room. Then the sounds would stop.

I would stay until she woke.

When she came back, she would look into the bed before looking at me.

“Anything?”

“No.”

She would nod, take my place, and pass me a cup of water.

On my way home, the streets would be sweating. Beneath the last lamp I would stop to fasten my coat. The new road beyond it would disappear properly into darkness.

I would be tired, and glad that my room was close.
