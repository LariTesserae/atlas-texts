At first I think I would be in the middle of the chamber, on the warm patch where a stranger is put.

I would have been leaning against a wall without knowing it was a wall people spoke through. Someone would touch my wrist and move me a little. No rebuke, perhaps, only a correction I could not yet distinguish from an invitation.

Then the short piece of fiber would arrive between two hands.

I would grip too hard.

Whatever had been coming through it would stop.

That seems like a likely beginning for me here: interrupting something by trying too earnestly to receive it.

But when I try to imagine the hand holding the other end, I find that I know it from within. I feel the strain along the outside of its fourth digit. I know that the fiber must be turned slightly because its two ends do not carry pressure equally. My lower limbs are holding the edge of the floor, though I have not been attending to them.

Perhaps I am not the visitor.

Perhaps I am the one who has brought out the fiber, and the uncertainty belongs to both of us.

***

I would live near a passage that used to be a doorway.

The house has settled around it. There is still room for a small body to enter without difficulty, but an adult must loosen one shoulder, reach backward with a hind hand, and draw the soft length of the body through. During the wettest months, a bulb swells into the upper corner. We press it aside while passing. Over many seasons it has acquired a shallow groove.

My chamber lies just beyond. It is neither the deepest nor the driest. Water can be heard under one side of it, and during prolonged rain a little comes up between the floor fibers. There are two good resting places and a third that I keep trying to make good.

The third is where visitors settle.

It was comfortable once. Someone older than I am remembers that. But a supporting root has thickened underneath it, making the surface convex, and an unfamiliar body tends to slide toward the wall. I have bound a young stem along its lower edge. In another few years it may hold the floor level.

For now I put a folded leaf there.

It is a small, recurrent failure. The leaf must be changed. Sometimes I forget.

I have several useful objects, but the one I reach for most often is a blunt, dark probe with a curve in it. The curve makes it inconvenient for clearing drains and very good for lifting a living strand without breaking its attachments. One end is polished by palms older than mine. At the other is a nick that catches leaf wrapping, so I keep meaning to smooth it.

I do not. The nick tells me which way the tool is turned in the dark.

***

I would not be the best listener in the house.

There is an old inhabitant two chambers farther in who can distinguish feeding from hatching through a depth of growth that gives me only a soft, irregular disturbance. There is a child who hears a familiar traveler approaching before any of us do.

I would be useful when something familiar had become difficult to hear.

A roof settles. A strand takes on water. Two stems, separate for years, grow against one another and begin to share their burdens. A message that once reached the far chamber cleanly now arrives with a second, weaker beginning. Someone answers the wrong pressure. Someone thinks they have not been answered.

I would go along the route, stopping often.

This appeals to me more than having extraordinary senses. I would know a few ways to examine a failure. I would put one palm where the speaker had put theirs and another farther down. I would ask for the same small sequence again. Then again, with less force.

Sometimes the trouble would be in the house.

Sometimes a hand would be swollen.

Sometimes the person waiting for an answer would have mistaken the beginning of one message for the end of another.

I would have made all these errors myself.

Once, after a roof shifted, I might have opened a connection that should have stayed separate. For several days, conversation in one chamber would reach another household through a root beneath the feeding grounds. Nothing disastrous, but enough to make people reluctant to use the house. I would close the connection awkwardly, and spend the next season checking whether it had joined again.

I think I would carry the memory of that mistake in my hands. Before lifting a strand, I would feel for the smaller roots underneath.

***

There would be a part of the plateau I knew badly.

A dry district, perhaps, where I had lived for several years among the crowns. The supports there would be taut, the messages quick. Wind would enter everything. I would have learned to keep three holds while sleeping.

Then I would come home.

At first I would move as though the old house had waited for me.

My hand would reach toward a hook that had been removed. I would turn my body for a passage now too narrow to take that turn. The child who heard approaching travelers would have to show me another way around the flooded hollow.

Some of the inhabitants I remembered would be gone. Others would touch me with familiar beginnings and unfamiliar endings.

I would be allowed to rest in my former place, but I would not fit it comfortably. While I was away, someone smaller had kept it firm by using it.

For a while I might feel injured by this.

Then I would watch that person settle there, all six limbs finding their places without adjustment, and move my things.

The chamber near the old doorway would become mine because it was available, because the water beneath it helped me sleep, and because I could hear arrivals from there without having to cross the whole house.

Only later would I begin bringing out the short piece of fiber.

***

I would like the young who cannot stay still.

Not always. A young one digging beside a carefully tended food opening would exhaust my patience. I would lay a hand across its back, perhaps more firmly than necessary, and feel it go quiet beneath me.

But I would remember the effort of waiting while an adult listened to something I could not feel.

So there would be objects in my chamber that could safely be handled badly: a split casing, three lengths of dead root, a bulb that had dried without opening. A youngster could press them together, drag them over the floor, test what disappeared and what traveled.

Occasionally it would discover something I had missed.

I would ask it to do that again.

I would have to mean the request. Children can feel when an adult has already turned attention elsewhere, even here, even without words.

***

At evening I would sit just inside the opening, one hind hand on the familiar support and the others loose.

Beyond the leaves, a stranger might be standing on the path.

I would feel the uneven distribution of their weight. The ground would tell me very little about what kind of creature they were. They might be frightened, or simply tired. Their stillness would not yet mean what stillness means in our house.

I would wait until they moved, so I could learn something of how they moved.

Then I would bring the fiber.

Mine would not be a ceremonial object. One end would be darker from use. There would be a soft place near the middle that I had been watching for months. Eventually I would have to replace it, and the next piece would behave differently.

I would hold it out without assuming that the stranger knew what to do.

If they gripped too hard, I would loosen my own hand.

If they let go, I would retrieve it.

If they felt nothing, we could still sit for a while on opposite sides of the threshold. I could move the folded leaf onto the firmer part of the floor. They could learn where to put their weight.

Behind me, someone would ask whether the visitor had entered.

I would answer through the root beneath my resting hand.

Not yet.

Then I would turn back toward the opening, keeping the fiber within reach.
