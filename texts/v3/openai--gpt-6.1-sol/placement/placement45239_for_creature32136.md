At first I think I would live in one of the huts.

There is something persuasive about the green door, the straps holding the roof to the rock, the promise of a place where a wet glove can be taken off. I imagine setting a cup beside a pressure gauge and listening to the rain change as the wind moves around the building. I would know which switch needed a little upward pressure before it would turn. I would keep a spare pair of socks in a tin.

But I do not quite arrive inside.

I remain at the landing stage. Then beneath it.

The boards are close above my back. Water moves between my coat and their undersides, lifting me gently into the wood. Beyond the posts, rain strikes the lake hard enough to make the surface seem covered with short, pale hairs. Here it is darker. A loose rope hangs in a loop, and each time the stage shifts, the bottom of the loop touches my muzzle.

I push it away.

It returns.

For a while this is sufficient occupation.

I would be a bolster, though not the one that follows the boat reluctantly out of the intake bay. I would live farther east, among the shallow drowned meadows, where a road crosses two low shoulders of stone before descending into water too deep to graze.

I would be an older male. Broad even for a bolster, with one forefoot that does not open properly. Two of its toes would have healed together after an injury. On rock I would place that foot carefully. In water it would make very little difference.

People would recognize me by it, but only when I climbed out.

Mostly they would recognize me by where I waited.

There is a bend in the drowned road where the stone beneath the turf still gives good footing. The current divides there. One part goes through the rushes; the other passes over the broken wall of a culvert and gathers speed. From above, neither is particularly visible. A person in a boat sees dark water, a lighter streak, leaves pointing two slightly different ways.

I feel the division along my belly.

I can stand with my forefeet on the road and my hind feet in the meadow. The water presses harder against one hip. A little gravel rolls somewhere underneath. When the pressure reaches a certain strength, I move back among the sedges.

The others often wait with me.

This is how I come to carry young.

I am there when they need to cross, and my back is wide. A youngster climbs aboard while its mother is still pulling at a root crown. Another follows, stepping on the first one’s ear. I lower myself a little to make the climb easier, then remain still until the small, sharp feet stop searching through my coat.

Their weight is slight. Their movements are not.

One will stand while I am feeding and lean so far forward that its muzzle appears beside my eye. I close my mouth on the roots, raise my head, and fold it back where it belongs. It complains into my hair. The other has gone to sleep.

I do not count them. I know the places where they are holding on.

When one lets go, I stop.

There would be a sleeping platform in the lee of the road embankment, close to a willow that grows out over the water. Its lowest branches would be worn smooth where we pass beneath them. In late summer the leaves would touch our backs. In hard rain they would release large, cold drops long after the rain itself had eased.

The platform needs work.

A corner keeps loosening where the current catches it. We pull roots into that corner and press them down. Sometimes a piece slips free overnight. I find it against the willow and bring it back, with snails still attached.

I sleep with my bad foot tucked beneath my chest and my nose on a bundle of stems. Other bodies settle against me. Through them I feel small adjustments travel: someone turning, someone drawing a hind leg in, a youngster climbing across its mother to reach the warmer space between us.

If a call passes through the water, I wake before lifting my head.

I think this would be the centre of my world: that moment of waking into contact. The platform beneath my muzzle, roots under my feet, another animal’s flank against mine. Before I can see anything, I can tell whether the water is rising.

The huts would still matter.

There is one stage where the lowest ladder rung holds the day’s warmth longer than the others. I rest my chin there sometimes. Above it, hands move around ropes and small hard objects. I watch the hands.

Once, a person kneeling at the edge would lower an empty palm toward me.

I would smell oil, wet wool, something sharp caught beneath a fingernail. I would extend my neck farther than the person expected. The hand would withdraw suddenly.

After that we would be more sensible with each other.

They would learn to step over the part of the stage where my back presses against it. I would learn that three blows on the rail mean leaving, even when roots are coming loose beautifully in the bay. The blows reach my jaw through the ladder. Then the engine starts, and I gather myself out of the warm, sheltered water.

I would not always go far enough.

The boat would follow. Its blunt bow would push a little hill of water ahead of it. I would take one last mouthful, turn, and find the others already waiting at the channel mouth.

There would be bright afternoons when I came ashore and looked much older than I felt underwater.

Getting onto the rock would require choosing the place properly. Forefeet first, chest dragging, hindquarters following with an effort. Then I would lie heavily on my side while water escaped from the long ridges of my coat.

A youngster might inspect the joined toes.

I would let it, until it bit them.

Across the channel, someone would open the hut door. There would be a brief smell of warmed iron. A bird would pick through the crushed stems at our landing place. Sun would reach the skin beneath my thinning fur.

For a little while, nothing would need moving.

Then a cold gust would come over the rock.

I would rise before the others, slowly enough that the youngster could get clear of my feet. I would nose the nearest sleeper beneath the jaw. Wait. Nose it again.

At the water’s edge I would lower my body and feel the first small claws taking hold.

The crossing would be short, but the channel would already be roughening. I would turn my head until I could feel the youngster’s muzzle against my cheek. Behind it, another pair of ears would lift from the coat.

I would wait for them to lie flat.

Then I would enter, putting the bad foot down on the submerged stone I knew was there.
