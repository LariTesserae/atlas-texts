At first I think I would live near the edge, where the streets have not acquired names. I would carry a measure and be permitted to stand beside something that has never been a doorway and say where the doorway ought to go.

But I would not be good at leaving my lamp in one position for an entire working period. I would keep wanting to look around the other side of the shoot. Someone more patient would have to correct what I had encouraged.

I think I would live farther in, in a district old enough to have inconvenient passages. My room would have a window overlooking the underside of a freight bridge. Not a fine view, but enough movement to tell the hour without consulting the ceiling. I would know the platforms by their loads and by the different sounds their rakes made.

My work would begin when other people lowered their lamps.

I would be a keeper of rooms during absences.

There would be an official name for this, longer and less accurate. The work would fall between residential maintenance and the administration of unoccupied space. Most of my assignments would be quite ordinary: a household away for six working periods; a workshop temporarily closed; a sleeper whose lamp could no longer be trusted to stay warm.

I would carry my own lamp, two spare collars, insulating cloth, a scraper, and a book narrow enough to fit in the tool case. The book would contain plans of rooms. There would be corrections in several hands.

A square drawn on a page would mean a table, unless it had a diagonal through it, in which case it meant a table that had once been there and whose former position still affected the floor.

I would have strong opinions about how to record those.

***

The first room I was trusted with alone would belong to a switch repairer recovering from an injury.

He would be asleep when I arrived. Beside his bed would be a tray with food pressed flat at one edge, where he had tried to lift it and failed. His lamp would sit in its niche, burning correctly. The room would look entirely safe.

My instructions would say: walk the perimeter twice; warm the inner seam of the door; avoid the west cupboard.

I would avoid it so carefully that I would fail to notice the opposite wall advancing.

Not much. Less than the thickness of a fingernail. But by the end of the period the repairer’s stool would be pinched between the wall and the bed.

He would wake while I was freeing it.

“That happens,” he would say.

I would be grateful until he added, “Not usually with someone here.”

Afterward I would learn to listen to the small sounds beneath ordinary conversation. A cup set down. The click of a ventilation pore. A dry, faint knock from inside a partition, followed much later by another. Some rooms would need walking through. Others would remain settled if I sat in the proper chair and moved my feet now and then.

I would learn this from the people who lived in them, and sometimes from people who insisted they knew nothing about buildings.

***

I would meet Eren over a disputed doorway.

A household in her district would have gone to visit relatives beneath another salt field. Their rooms were being tended, but the narrow door between kitchen and sleeping room had begun to close. My report would recommend extra warmth along the upper seam.

Eren would read it, put down the listening disc, and ask whether I had checked the kitchen floor.

I would say yes.

She would ask whether I had checked beneath the insulating runner.

I would say no.

Under the runner, a new supply root would be pressing upward. The doorway was tightening around the load.

“Heat that seam and you’ll get a lovely door,” she would tell me. “It won’t open.”

We would spend the rest of the period shifting a cupboard and cutting a temporary relief groove. She would do the cutting. I would hold the cloth and collect the pale shavings before the floor could take them back.

Later she might request me for a difficult assignment. I would read too much into this. She would explain that she needed someone who had already seen that particular mistake.

***

There would be rooms I disliked.

One would be very large and beautifully finished, with no loose scales, no poorly placed vents, no persistent movement behind the walls. Its occupants would leave exact instructions about where I could sit and which surfaces I could warm.

I would spend each visit standing beside the authorized chair.

There would also be a small room with an unreliable shelf that I liked enough to stay after my work was done. Its occupant would be an elderly woman who repaired lamp hoods. She would keep cracked ceramic pieces sorted in shallow boxes, although most could no longer be used.

She would ask me to read aloud while she worked.

At first I would bring accounts of distant chambers. Then she would ask for the freight notices instead. She had a grandson on a transfer crew and liked to identify delays he might have encountered.

We would disagree about whether a late shipment of mineral meal meant trouble with an engine or trouble with paperwork. She would usually be right.

When she went away to live with him, I would be assigned to keep the room stable until its next occupation.

Without her, I would find it surprisingly difficult.

The lamp hoods would be gone. So would the boxes and the low workbench. I would walk the perimeter, test the shelf, warm the doorway. Everything would remain in place, but a fine ridge would begin rising through the patch where she used to sit.

I would report it. The office would tell me to allow the adjustment.

The next occupant wanted a storage recess there.

***

I would not keep every place unchanged. I would become troublesome if I tried.

I think that would be the part of the work I learned most slowly: reading the instructions at the beginning of each visit, even when I knew the room well. A doorway might no longer be needed. A household might return with another child, or with one fewer adult. Furniture would be moved. My old routes through the rooms would become wrong.

I would keep some of the superseded plans, though there would be no professional reason to do so. Folded together, they would make my tool case difficult to close.

At home, I would leave a lamp in a niche beside the bed. The room would be small, and its one unfinished cupboard would have been unfinished for so long that I would store things against it.

Eren would tell me this was unwise.

I would say I knew its habits.

She would give me a look I had learned to recognize.

***

Eventually there would be an assignment at the new station.

Only one waiting room would be ready. The platforms would still be low growths surrounded by survey rings. Someone would need to occupy the room during the dim period so that its doors remained usable while the adjoining passages formed.

I would take my book and expect a quiet shift.

Instead, every few minutes, a grain of salt would strike the roof or fall outside the window. The room’s pores would open in an order I had not heard before. I would get up repeatedly to check the door.

Near the end of the period, a surveyor would come through carrying two empty trays. She would set them on an insulating cloth and ask whether anyone else had arrived.

“No,” I would say.

She would consult her measure, make a note, and sit down.

We would talk about where the station workers would eat once the temporary kitchen closed. While we talked, the door would ease another little distance into its frame.

Later, when the station opened, I would pass through on a freight connection. There would be people standing where I had walked my careful circuits, and a child dragging a soft-soled foot along the base of the new bench.

I would check the door without meaning to.

Then I would go and find my platform.
