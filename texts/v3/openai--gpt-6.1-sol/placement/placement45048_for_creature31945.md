I would be found by lifting something that ought not to be lifted.

A broad shell near the edge of a bank, perhaps. There would be nothing remarkable about it: a little higher than its neighbors, dark where rain had polished it, with a pale crescent showing under one side. You might put your fingers beneath that crescent, thinking it an empty shell.

For a moment it would come easily.

Then the whole bank would tighten.

Not a rush toward your hand. Nothing here would rush toward a hand. Three bodies would settle, a fourth would turn, and the opening would become too narrow to keep your fingers in. If you let go, the shell would return almost to its former position.

Almost. There would now be a small outlet beneath it. Water would run from there for several minutes.

I would be somewhere behind that outlet.

At first I imagine myself as the shell: something used after its occupant has gone, carried through floods, held in place by creatures that have grown the necessary limbs. There is room in this country for such an existence. Rain on my back, mouths working below, my shape still useful though I no longer maintain it.

But the rain is too far away.

I can feel each drop arrive, yet not where it lands. It reaches me as a succession of loads, softened by the bodies between us. A large drop divides into several small pressures. Several small drops sometimes arrive together as one. There are places above me where the weather has been falling on the same sheltered patch of shell for years without ever making it wet.

I am not the upper surface.

And I am not quite at the bottom. Below me, something grips stone. When the buried machinery takes a stroke, the stone moves first, then the feet below me, then my own limbs, then the weight overhead. I have time to alter my stance between one and the next.

Very little time. Enough.

My body would be pale and broad, with a soft back that never finishes becoming a shell. Along its edges, layers begin to harden, then wear away against my neighbors. A dark grain remains near the center. Perhaps it is the grain I had when I was small enough to feed directly from a seam. It is buried now under tissue, but certain currents still make it warm.

I have legs underneath and legs above.

The distinction matters less than it would seem. An upper leg can become a lower one if the colony turns me slowly enough. It takes several floods. In the meantime I am badly supported and have to borrow a purchase from someone else.

Most of my legs end in little spreading pads. Two have hooks. One is thick, short, and no longer bends; it fits a depression in the underside of a neighbor who has grown around it. We have been joined there a long time.

I do not know the shape of that neighbor’s back.

I know when its mouth finds food. A small regular tremor begins beside the fixed leg. I shift a little to admit more water, and the tremor strengthens. Sometimes loosened grazers come down through the gap. My mouth is well away from the opening, but one of my upper limbs has a groove along it, and food settles in the groove.

There are lean periods when I keep that limb raised long after the feeding has stopped.

The place I occupy is a passage, although it would look like a badly fitted space between animals. Water enters at one end, spreads thinly over my back, and leaves beside a pair of legs that are not mine. When those legs thicken, the passage narrows. I press against them until their owner moves. When they diminish, too much water comes through, carrying silt that packs beneath me. I lower one side of myself and hold it there.

This is most of what I do.

There are other passages nearby. I feel them open through changes in the weight above me. A current leaving my chamber enters another, colder one, and returns with a taste of metal. Sometimes it returns with fragments of a feeding margin. Sometimes it stops returning.

Then I wait.

I cannot turn around within my place. I cannot follow the water to learn what has happened. I reach into the gap with the longest limb I have and touch a body that used to be farther away. It is still moving. I brace against it, and after a while a narrow current begins again.

No one at the surface would notice.

Once, a swimmer lies outside our bank for most of a falling flood.

Its fringe brushes the stone beyond my outlet. The upper animals interrupt their feeding. Their mouths turn a little, stop, reverse. The irregularity passes down to me, and I stop my own mouth with a grazer caught halfway inside.

The swimmer waits.

Pressure continues to fall. Water withdraws from the highest bodies. Their wet undersides press closer to us, and the chamber becomes crowded with legs. I have to move, though movement is what the swimmer is waiting for.

I release one pad.

Before I can set it down elsewhere, the water hardens.

It does not feel like ice. Ice would push, or split, or give us something to grip. This holds every part of me in its present place. My free leg remains free. The grazer remains at my mouth. Beyond the outlet, a hooked limb advances very slowly.

The swimmer takes the animal against which I have been bracing.

I feel the loss first as a sideways pull, then as room. Too much room. When the water loosens, three bodies come down into it at once.

My long upper limb folds underneath me. Something tears near the pad. The fixed leg stays in its socket, and because it stays, I do not turn over.

For the rest of that flood, water goes around us.

Later, the neighbor attached to my fixed leg lowers its mouth to my injured side. I hold still. It removes the damaged pad and leaves a strip of tissue in the torn place. The strip contracts whenever current touches it. It is not yet a leg, and may never become one.

For several cycles I cannot open the passage as widely as before.

Silt settles. Small mouths begin working in it. A creature no larger than one of my pads braces between my underside and the rock. Its new limbs are too thin to carry much, but they keep a little space clear while I heal.

I grow used to its movements.

Then one flood it is gone. The space it kept is still there. I put a foot into it and find the stone clean.

I would rarely see the light from below. Once in a long while the colony shifts far enough for an orange seam to show through several chambers at once. Pale backs become visible, then mouths, then the slow crossing of a limb whose owner is hidden elsewhere.

I might see my own body that way only in pieces.

A soft fold beside a supporting pad. The damaged side, now thicker than the other. The dark central grain showing through when the current brightens it.

Above, the bank would close again.

The white flats would reach me chiefly through the water. A flood would arrive carrying almost no silt, and for a few moments an old opening would admit current again although it had been sealed for years. I would put a limb toward it and touch solid shell.

Still, the current would continue.

I would feed from it if I could. Afterward I might find a pale deposit along my mouthparts, too smooth to scrape away. Over the following days it would thin without flaking, until the ordinary grit of the water could catch there again.

I do not think I would go toward the flats. I would have nowhere to stand on their exposed surface, and no body close enough above me to hold the rain away.

I would remain several animals deep, near a channel that changes banks when the colonies move.

As the water rose, my chamber would fill. The neighbor attached to my short leg would begin to lift, and I would lift with it. Another body would settle on the opposite side. I would test the new weight, spread two pads, withdraw one that had begun to slide.

Then the ground would rise by its fraction of an inch.

Stone first. The feet beneath me. My limbs. The crowded bodies overhead.

I would keep the passage open through the stroke. Cold water would cross my back, bringing a little food with it. At the outlet, a newly legged creature would lose its grip and tumble into the chamber.

It would catch one of my pads.

For a while I would carry its small weight.
