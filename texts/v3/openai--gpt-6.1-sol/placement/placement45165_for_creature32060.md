I would begin underneath.

Not beneath the country, in the turning stone, but underneath the lifted edge of the animal beside your foot. In that moist interval where six small bodies are crowded together, I would be the one you did not count because part of me looked like the underside of the shelter.

My back would be black. My limbs would be pale only where they had recently unfolded. I would have a grain of mineral held against my mouth, although I would not be eating it.

There would be warmth coming through the ground and another warmth coming through the body above me. I would know the difference before I knew anything else.

One means stay.

The other means that staying is about to become difficult.

For a while, that would be enough.

***

I do not think I would have a clear outline.

When the broad animal opens, I would seem jointed: a few slender limbs, a narrow body, fingers gathered around the grain. But when it closes, I would flatten. Small seams along my sides would loosen, allowing me to spread into the hollows between its folds. My fingers would remain hard. The rest of me would become difficult to distinguish from the damp tissues surrounding it.

I would not be its young.

I would not remember arriving.

Perhaps I entered as something much smaller, carried on a root that the animal had eaten. Perhaps I once belonged to a column passing through the grove, and failed to lift away when the others moved. There would be a place on my back where another pair of limbs might have grown, or where a pair had been lost. Nothing about it would tell me which.

The shelter would tolerate me because I did something useful.

At first I would think this was the fitting of mineral grains into its skin.

The grains would have to be selected carefully. Some would tremble against my fingers. Some would become warm on one side when turned. Others would seem inert until brought near a particular pit, where they would begin to tug so faintly that only a tired hand could feel it.

I would travel over the animal’s back during the pauses, trying a grain here, then elsewhere.

When I found the right place, the skin would soften.

I would press the grain halfway in and wait.

That waiting would be part of the work. If I pushed too soon, the pit would close badly. If I left too soon, the grain might turn over inside the tissue, and the animal would spend the next journey tightening and loosening the same fold.

Sometimes I would sleep with both hands around a grain that had not yet settled.

By morning, it would be gone.

My hands would be full of something clear.

***

What I received would not quite be food.

It would thicken me. It would make my limbs less brittle. After drinking it, I would be able to keep one finger against hot stone longer than before.

But it would also give me small, misplaced recognitions.

A cleft I had never entered would feel familiar along my left side. The smell of a wounded trunk would make me turn toward a grove we had already passed. I would wake with my fingers arranged around the shape of a branch that was not inside our shelter.

These would come without pictures.

They might be mistakes. I would sometimes urge the traveler toward a place where there was no cool passage, only exposed rock. It would stop, unfold two eyes in my direction, and wait until I came back.

Other times it would follow.

We would find a root chamber open just wide enough for its contracted body. Or a strip of damp felt beneath a shelf that, from every other approach, had looked bare.

I would not know whether I had led it there or whether it had made the place recognizable to me.

As I grew, the distinction would matter less.

There would still be consequences for getting it wrong.

***

I would live where the animal could not quite close.

Every traveler has such places: an old tear, a thickened feeding slit, a fold that seals less well after being turned against the direction of weight. Mine would have a narrow scar running from one margin almost to the center of its body.

During journeys, the scar would admit dry air.

I would lie across it.

At first this would happen accidentally. I would settle there because it was less crowded. Then the animal would begin folding around me in a particular order, leaving that space until last. My underside would acquire pores corresponding to its own. When the air grew hottest, moisture would pass between us.

The others would gather behind my back.

One would clean the hinges of my fingers. Another would feed on the film forming along my sides. A soft creature with no visible mouth would push its front end beneath my ribs and remain there for whole migrations.

I would discover that I could be uncomfortable without needing to move.

I would also discover that this had limits.

When too many crowded into the scar, I would lift one side of myself and let the dry air enter. They would redistribute with tiny, irritated movements. Then I would close again.

Our shelter would smell of iron, warmed fiber, and the faint sourness of bodies keeping one another alive.

***

The place I would return to would be a grove growing sideways from an escarpment.

Not the whole grove. A particular root fork near its inner edge, where the direction of falling was seldom entirely settled.

Loose grains would rest there in three different inclinations. A shed frond might lie against the cliff while its broken tip hung toward the clouds. Water collected in a crease would tremble, divide, and choose separate routes.

The traveler would spread itself across the fork. Its eyes would turn outward. I would emerge and climb into the root’s dry upper chamber.

There, I would keep the grains I had not used.

Some would be useless everywhere I tried them. Some would have come from the polished fracture of a herd animal’s shoulder growth. One would have been carried to me by a creature that died before its column reached the grove.

I would arrange them in the chamber, then rearrange them when the ridge began to turn.

I would not be making a map.

A map would not help much with the root fork itself. It could be above the grove on one visit and deep within it on the next. Sometimes we would reach it after a short passage through leaves; sometimes the traveler would arrive thin from weeks spent crossing warm, nearly identical shelves.

But a grain placed against the chamber wall could reproduce a tremor I knew.

I could put my fingers beside it and wait until the tremor entered them.

Then, for a little while, I would remember how a body had crossed a difficult place.

The reach of a limb.

The order in which attachments loosened.

How much weight a soft margin could bear before tearing.

I would practice these things alone in the chamber, bracing between the walls while the grove extended into the dim air beyond me.

***

Eventually, I would become too large to fit entirely inside the scar.

The traveler would close, and one of my limbs would remain outside.

For several journeys I would drag it behind us. Its fingers would wear smooth. When the ground changed direction, it would catch and twist until I learned to fasten it ahead of the moving fold.

Then a second limb would remain outside.

We would travel badly for a time.

The animal would pause when I was ready to go. I would fasten myself where it needed to peel away. Once, crossing a seam, I would hold so tightly that a little of its underside would remain on the stone.

Afterward, we would lie apart beneath a wounded tree.

It would drink from the pale interior.

I would put my fingers against its torn place without pressing anything into it.

There would be no grain that could help.

When it gathered itself again, I would climb beside it rather than inside.

The small creatures would still enter its folds. One would come to me, hesitate, and settle beneath the loose skin along my side.

I would carry it.

***

This is where I would find myself most fully: near the back of a departure, when the first animals are already disappearing between the trunks and the grove still appears safe.

I would have enough limbs by then to brace across a narrow opening.

Not every opening. Not for long.

The broad traveler would cross first, contracting carefully around its sheltered company. Its edge would brush my fingers. Behind it would come bodies I did not know: a young grazer placing each divided pad separately, a strip of living canopy dragging its moist lobes, two pale migrants separated from their column.

I would feel the route changing through the points where I touched it.

One wall would grow warmer.

The other would begin to seem farther away.

I would adjust my grip.

There might still be something coming. Its movements would be too small to distinguish from the disturbance of the leaves. I would hold until I could see it: an unfamiliar animal carrying spores in a row of shallow pockets, stopping repeatedly to taste the ground.

I could not explain where it needed to go.

I could keep the passage open while it found its way.

When it had crossed, I would lift my last finger. The opening would deepen behind me, quietly enough that the little creature beneath my skin would not wake.

Ahead, the traveler would have stopped with one edge raised.

I would reach it slowly, carrying an ache through every joint. There would be less room inside than there used to be. I would fit where I could, leaving several limbs folded along its back.

For a little while we would rest against the same warm stone.

Then it would begin to gather itself, and I would loosen my fingers in the order I had learned.
