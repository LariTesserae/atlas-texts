I would live where the old channel disappears beneath the house.

Not in the oldest room. That would belong to someone who had a better claim to it, and who would remind me of this whenever I left anything in the passage. My room would be an addition: a former store chamber, roofed with two slabs and a length of timber that still smelled faintly resinous when the hearth warmed it. There would be a step down at the entrance. Visitors would remember the lintel and forget the step.

From my sleeping place I could see ankles passing outside.

I think I would begin as someone who carried water. I would know the crack through the mountain by the pressure of its turns against a load: where to lift the left shoulder, where a vessel had to be steadied with one hand, where stopping was worse than continuing because there was nowhere to set anything down. On the climb, I would sometimes sing. On the descent, never.

There would be a particular place near the bottom where the settlement’s sounds reached me all at once. Before I could see a roof, I would hear the loom, a goat, someone knocking ash from a pot. I would pause there even when I had no reason to rest.

But carrying would not remain my work forever.

One knee would become unreliable. Not after a grand accident. It would swell one winter, improve, swell again. I would begin to descend with the cautious, irritating pace of someone who still remembered being quick. Other carriers would offer to take a vessel from me, and I would refuse until the day I could not refuse without making them wait.

After that, people would bring things to my doorway.

A basket whose rim had loosened. A strap cut almost through where it passed over a buckle. A wooden latch that lifted in dry weather but jammed when the air changed. I would have done these repairs for years between journeys. Now the work would spread over the stone outside my room.

I would be good with bindings. This is the first thing about myself here that I can see clearly.

Not especially good at carving, and poor at sharpening a blade evenly. I would know how to make two damaged things hold together without asking either to be stronger than it was. I would splice a handle with a piece of horn, wrap a split carrying pole, replace the edge of a basket without taking the whole basket apart.

There would be cords hanging from pegs inside the doorway. Short lengths, mostly. Nothing impressive enough to steal.

People would complain that I kept too much.

They would be right. Under my bed there would be a box of fragments whose uses I could explain individually but whose combined presence made it difficult to sweep. I would have three broken latches because each had failed in a different place. One of them would remain there so long that a child who used to play with it would grow up and ask why I had never thrown it out.

“I’m waiting for its other half,” I would say.

She would tell me that was not an answer.

—

My doorway would be close to a planting trough, close enough that the shrub in it brushed my wrist when I worked. Someone else would tend it. I would object when she watered it beside my tools; she would object when I hung a repaired sling from its branches to dry.

We would have settled these matters many times.

In late afternoon, sunlight would reach the threshold and then move across the floor. I would shift my work with it. A needle dropped during that interval would be found at once. A needle dropped afterward might require me to kneel with my cheek almost against the paving while someone stood in the doorway offering unhelpful advice.

I would recognize people first by what they did before entering.

One man would tap the lintel, although he was not tall enough to hit it. A girl would scrape her soles twice. My neighbor would begin speaking while she was still beyond the bend, so that I received the middle of her complaint before I knew what had happened.

There would also be people who came without a broken thing.

They would pick up a piece of cord and ask what it was for. They would examine a basket they had seen the previous day. Eventually they would say that a son had not written, or that their sister wanted to divide the roof, or that they were tired of the noise from the next room.

I would not always be patient.

Sometimes I would keep working until they left. Sometimes, after they had gone, I would wish I had put the work down.

—

One morning the girl who knew every latch would bring me the lid of a drinking jar.

The loop on top would have broken. She would hold the lid in both hands, very level, although there was nothing on it to spill.

“Can you put it back?”

“Where’s the piece?”

She would open her fist.

It would be too small for a sound repair. I would tell her so. She would watch while I fitted it against the break, as though trying it might change my opinion.

Her household would need the lid that day. I would make a cord cradle around it, crossing beneath the rim and gathering above into a loop. It would work, though it would need washing and checking, and the lid would sit a little awkwardly beside the others.

When she came to collect it, she would lift it once, put it down, and lift it again.

Then she would ask for the broken piece.

I would have set it with the other scraps. Finding it would take longer than making the repair.

“Why do you want it?”

“It’s ours.”

I would give it back.

For some time afterward, when returning a repaired thing, I would remember to ask whether its owner wanted the pieces.

—

There would be a goat I disliked especially.

It would stand on the roof above my room and pull at the ends of my hanging cords. I would throw a rolled scrap of wool at it. The goat would eat the scrap. Someone passing would tell me this was an expensive way to feed an animal.

I would make a better fastening for the gate it kept opening.

For six days I would be pleased with myself.

On the seventh, I would hear its hooves overhead.

—

I would still go up through the crack sometimes, without a load.

I would say I needed a particular shape of wood from the fuel pile, or that someone had asked me to look at a roller. There would be truth in this often enough.

Above the shelter, I would straighten slowly. The wind would press my clothes against my body. Across the worn stone, I would see the low walls around the snow hollows, and farther off the route taken by people leaving for the lower valleys.

I would once have intended to leave by that route myself.

This would not become a story I told everyone. A few people would remember. One would occasionally ask whether I still meant to go, with sufficient kindness that I could not be annoyed by the question.

Some mornings I would still mean to.

Then someone would be waiting below with a carrying strap laid across both palms, showing me the damage.

—

At evening I would take my work to the parapet. There would be room beside the woman sorting seed if I arrived before she spread out her cloth.

The stone would warm the backs of my legs. I would mend until I could no longer distinguish the cord from the shadow of my fingers. If the repair could not be finished, I would leave the needle threaded.

People would rise around me, shaking scraps from their laps. Someone would ask whose bowl was under the bench. Someone would carry a child away with one hand cupped beneath its sleeping head.

I would stay long enough to hear the latches begin.

Then I would gather the little pieces I had brought, check the ground for what I had dropped, and go back through the narrow lane. At my door, the shrub would catch my sleeve. I would free it carefully, duck beneath the lintel, and remember the step.
