I would look first for a place beneath a walkway, near a pier, where the reflected light reaches in but the sun does not.

Not the deepest shade. Something lives there already. There would be a broad shell fitted so closely to the stone that I would mistake it for part of the footing, until the water rose and six feet appeared beneath its edge.

I would move a little farther along.

There is a narrow shelf beside the power station’s outflow. It was probably made for a pipe that has since been removed. Two bolts remain, furred with white mineral, and between them the stone stays damp without being submerged. From there I could see the underside of the crossing: the cable brackets, the soft bellies of the freight wheels passing overhead, the place where a repair has made one plank thicker than the others.

At first I think I would be something small enough to live on that shelf. Something that holds still while the useful, recognizable things go past.

But I would want to follow the water after it left.

That changes where I belong.

***

There is a room in the city where the old water samples are kept.

It is not an archive in the formal sense. The customs office has the archive, with stamped plans and agreements about discharge. This room belongs to the maintenance stores. It has a recessed window, a cupboard whose doors no longer meet, and shelves supported independently of the walls because the walls have begun to acquire thickness.

Most of the samples are dry.

In shallow dishes lie flakes of crust, a spoonful of orange powder, a translucent edge that broke from a sill. Some specimens are sealed in glass. Others have eaten through their original containers and been transferred, with the old labels laid beside the new ones.

A few must be kept wet.

Those stand on the lowest shelf, where narrow pipes bring water from three separate channels. Their stopcocks are small and unpleasant to reach. Nobody has arranged the shelf well. It has grown by accommodation: another dish fitted in, another pipe bent around the first, another warning written where there was room.

I think I would work here.

Not as the person who decides which gate to turn. I would have to learn for years before I trusted myself with that, and even then I might prefer someone else to stand beside me.

I would be the person who goes back.

After a gate has been opened, after the first excitement of a new seam growing, after the inspector has signed the sheet, someone needs to return and see what actually happened. Whether the orange margin stopped advancing. Whether the swimmers came back and stayed. Whether the apparently sound bridge footing is sound underneath.

My working day would begin with a list of things that ought to be finished.

Most would not be.

***

I would carry a shallow case with a padded lid. Inside would be glass rods, folded white cloth, sample dishes, and a blunt tool for lifting deposits without putting a finger under them.

There would also be a little hooked implement made by Sera’s sister. Originally it was for retrieving something from the back of a denture mould. She would have given it to me after watching me struggle with a specimen caught behind a pipe.

I would use it nearly every day.

My first competence would be an unremarkable one: I would learn to put things down safely.

The case on its hooks. The glass rod in its groove. The lid secured before I leaned over a channel. There would still be mornings when I reached for a tool and found the shape of its absence in the padding.

I would know exactly which pool it was in.

At noon I would eat beneath a loading platform. One of the scavengers would have learned that I do not open my lunch tin until it has settled its neck along its foreleg. It would comply, but keep its head raised.

Sometimes I would put the unopened tin away again, purely to irritate it.

It would wait longer than I could.

***

Among the wet specimens would be a dish labelled with the name of a refinery demolished before I was born.

The dish would contain a pale ceramic fragment and, on its underside, a film so thin it showed only when tilted. It would have been moved four times. Each move would be recorded. So would the flow rate, the temperature, the source of the water.

There would be no reliable account of what the film originally looked like.

On one old sheet someone would have written, *May have been amber.*

On another, *Do not increase flow merely because surface appears bare.*

I would find the second instruction especially useful.

For months the dish might seem to contain nothing at all. Then, one morning, three tiny swimmers would be holding position beneath the ceramic.

I would write down three, though by the time I finished there would be only two visible.

I would resist scratching out the first number.

***

Sera would sometimes bring a sample herself.

She would stand just inside the doorway, letting her eyes adjust. Her felt soles would leave two dark marks that faded slowly.

“From where?” I would ask.

She would tell me.

“What did you change?”

“Nothing yet.”

That would be the important part.

We would take the sample to the window. I would fetch an older dish from a shelf, then another. We would hold the three against the same light.

Sometimes there would be an answer. More often there would be a resemblance worth watching.

I would accompany her back out on those days. At the gate she would brace the glass against her tool, and I would steady the case on the pier. Neither of us would offer to take over the other’s task.

When the animals gathered around her shoes, I would crouch to look.

They would leave the cool felt reluctantly. Once, a grazer would climb onto the cuff of my trousers instead. I would discover it halfway home, a slight unfamiliar weight that resisted each step.

I would carry it back.

***

There would be a lower shelf no one had assigned to me.

I would begin keeping things there that did not justify a numbered specimen: a flake from beneath the school steps, a wire loop with mineral grown around it, a piece of paving lifted by something underneath. Beside each I would put a location and a date.

Eventually someone would complain that I was keeping rubbish.

They would be right about several pieces.

I would sort the shelf, discard what I could no longer explain, and find that some of my notes were inadequate. *Northern walk* would turn out to cover half a mile. *After the warm pulse* would mean nothing once I had forgotten which evening I meant.

So I would become more exact.

Not all at once. My early notebooks would embarrass me.

There would be pages I could not bear to throw away because a later observation depended on a careless little mark in the corner. There would be a stain across an entry I had once thought important, and a perfectly preserved account of something that never mattered again.

By then, I would have a place at the evening bowls on one of the wider piers. People would sometimes save broken things for me. Children would bring stones, expecting identification, and grow annoyed when I asked them to show me where they had found them.

I would go with them when I could.

***

At the end of the day I would close the room’s shutter, check the three small flows, and lift each wet dish just enough to see beneath it.

One would always need attention. A pipe beginning to narrow. A swimmer trapped in the wrong compartment. A film growing over the label rather than the specimen.

Outside, the returning workers would be crossing the bridges. Sera might already be home. The upper water would arrive while I was still bent over the lowest shelf, and I would feel the change through the pipe before I heard it in the channel.

Then I would put the dishes back and leave.

On the walk home, I would stop at the old pipe shelf beneath the power station. The broad grazer would still be there, or one sufficiently like it that I could not be sure.

I would have learned not to disturb it just to find out.

Above us, a trolley would cross the repaired plank. Below, the evening flow would enter a hollow that had been dry that morning. I would watch until it reached the next notch, then make a note before the light went.

I think that is who I would become: someone with wet cuffs, a place at supper, and a habit of returning to see.
