I would be in the dark bowl of leaves above the fracture, where the creature used to sleep.

Not in its center. The center has been worn smooth by the turn of its body. I would be along the rim, beneath a fern stem, in the narrow seam where compressed leaves meet the bark of a root. Before the slope broke, water gathered there without quite standing. A droplet could remain through a dry afternoon, held between two surfaces, and be gone by morning.

At first there would be little to distinguish me from that dampness.

A few threads, pale enough to disappear against a torn leaf. A soft thickening under the bark. If someone lifted the fern stem, part of me would come with it and part would remain. There would be no clean way to uncover me.

The creature would have slept against me without knowing where I began. Its coat brought fragments of the forest into the hollow: leaf scales, bark dust, the husk of something eaten. These lodged along the rim. I grew into them slowly. Where its flank warmed the leaves, I grew a little faster. Where its weight pressed them too tightly together, I stopped.

I would not have a mouth, though things would enter me. Nor hands, though I would hold.

I would be a small growth among several kinds of growth, spread through the old bedding. My finest threads would enter the leaves where their skins had softened. Others would run over them, joining one wet surface to another. Small grains would catch in those threads. In time, the grains would become part of the felt.

For years, perhaps, my shape would have been the shape of a sheltered edge.

Then the mountain would open beneath it.

There would be no single moment in which I understood the fall. One part of me would stretch. Another would tear. A wet leaf would pull free of the layer beneath it, carrying a thin sheet of me into the air. The root against which I had grown would turn, and the water along its underside would run somewhere it had never run before.

Most of me would remain uphill.

That is where I would first find myself tonight: still living in a bed that no longer had a bottom.

The wind would reach passages formerly closed to it. Threads laid down in darkness would dry at their exposed ends. A little crust would form. Behind that crust, I would remain soft.

The creature’s hairs would still be caught along the rim. Around two of them I would have grown closely enough that they could not shake free. When a gust lifted their loose ends, the movement would travel into me. Very little movement; enough to change the shape of the water between a hair and a leaf.

I would have no knowledge of the valley. The great distance below me would arrive as an increase in drying.

Nor would I know the machinery by its sound. Sometimes the root would carry a faint recurring tremor. It would reach a wet seam, weaken there, continue through a pressed layer of leaves. I would have no reason to separate it from the other small strains passing through my body.

But I would have grown elsewhere, too.

Before the break, the creature had pressed one bare wrist into the edge of its bed. When it rose, a little of the felt lifted with it. Most fell away as it moved through the shrubs. A few threads stayed in the crease beside a pad, sheltered by the flattened hairs above it.

There would be almost nothing of me there. A gray fleck mistaken for dirt.

While the creature fed, moisture from the loosened bark would wet that fleck. Its wrist would rest against the tree. A thread would reach the bark through the film between them.

So I would also be below the abandoned hollow, carried in the fold of a hand.

I would not be traveling whole. The larger part of me would continue to occupy the old leaves. That part could fail without the smaller part failing with it. I would have no way to gather the two together, or to decide which was myself.

The strong gust would come.

The creature would release the uphill root. Its wrist would slide across a patch of bare wood, leaving some of me behind. Then it would catch the shrub below, pressing the crease of its hand into a fork where old bark remained damp.

There I would be divided again.

Some threads would be crushed. Some would adhere. In the warmth beneath the wrist, those that survived would soften and begin to take water in.

Above us, the loosened soil would fall.

A little would lodge in the ruined sleeping bowl. A little would scatter through the branches. One grain would strike the creature’s coat and remain caught there until rain carried it down toward the wrist.

For that grain, there would be a place in me.

When the creature finally fitted itself between the trunks, its hand would stay open against the fork. It would not know that it had brought anything from its former bed. It had returned for nothing. It had taken only the holds it needed.

Under its wrist, I would be thinner than a leaf skin.

Beyond the wrist, the bark would be old and uneven, with a sheltered crack running into the fork. Water would persist in that crack after the exposed surfaces dried. There would be dead cells to enter, a flake to grow beneath, room enough for a pale thread to branch.

Through the next lull, I would extend into it.
