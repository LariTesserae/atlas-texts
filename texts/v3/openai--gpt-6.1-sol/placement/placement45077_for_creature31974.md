At first I would be below the door.

Not far below. Close enough that the knocking would pass through me before it reached the frame, close enough to feel the change when the door opened: a little less pressure against my back, a little more damp along the floor.

I would know the basin from underneath.

A pool would be a cool weight. The shelter would be four persistent loads, one of them shifting whenever the seat corrected itself. The animals would arrive as touches too brief to locate, except when many of them stopped together. Then there would be a patch of quiet on the surface, and the quiet would have edges.

The resident I would recognize by its pauses.

Other bodies crossed. This one crossed, stopped, spread its weight, waited. Sometimes it lay down and became difficult to distinguish from something resting on the ground rather than living on it.

When it put its belly against the stone, we would be listening toward each other.

***

I would have a body, though most of it would be occupied.

A broad underside fitted into a recess beside the stairs. Three short limbs holding connections against the wall. A fourth limb free to move along a rail, clearing deposits from the places where a pulse had to pass.

There would be no clean boundary between my work and the machine’s work. Some motions I could interrupt. Others continued through me whether I wished them to or not.

At regular intervals a cam would press against my underside. I would lift one connection, hold another, and the pressure would travel upward. On the basin floor, the posts would begin to sing.

I could delay a pulse by holding the connection open.

Not indefinitely.

The load would build behind it. My limbs would stiffen. Eventually the cam would lift me bodily against the ceiling, or the connection would tear free, and the pulse would go through all at once.

I would have tried this before. One of my holding limbs would be shorter than the others.

What I lacked would not be concern, exactly. I would lack the means to tell one thing from another. A breathing hole and an empty hole would feel much alike from below. A small animal caught in rising stone might register only as resistance, scarcely different from a bubble.

The resident’s tool would change that.

Each time it entered the ground, a narrow, definite pressure would reach me. Here. Hold here.

I could ease one connection while keeping the others seated. Above, a line would open. Something would pass through it, or nothing would pass through it. The tool stayed either way.

Over time I would learn to wait for it.

***

When the resident tapped the doorframe, I would feel the taps through my rail.

Two. A pause. Another.

My answer would be clumsy. The only striking surface within reach would be the housing of the cam, and a blow there would sound much like the ordinary knocking. I could not be certain the resident distinguished it.

I would strike anyway.

The gray root left on the red floor would be beyond my reach at first. Later, as deposits thickened beneath me, I would be lifted high enough to touch it with the clearing limb.

I would turn it over.

It would have a wet side and a drying side. Fine threads would cling to my pad. I would hold it until a vibration came from the open door, then put it back where it had been.

On the next visit, the resident would remove it.

Another piece would appear several evenings later.

***

The day the tool was lost, I would feel it descend farther than it ever had.

Its point would scrape the channel beside my rail. Pressure would gather around it. I would release one connection, then a second, but the new stone would already be carrying its own weight. The handle would bend above me.

For a little while there would be a pulling force.

Then none.

I would keep the point uncovered as long as I could.

When the next pulse came, the tool would move downward another finger’s width. Its tip would enter my recess, close enough for the clearing limb to touch.

Above it, the resident would return several times. I would feel the light contact at the buried handle, always in the same place.

That night I would unseat one of my holding limbs.

The others would have to bear the pressure. The cam would drag across my underside instead of meeting it squarely. There would be a new scrape in the knocking.

By morning, I would have worked the tool’s point loose.

I would not be able to push it back up. The shaft would be too long, the channel too crooked.

Instead, I would take it with me.

***

Leaving would be slow.

I would have to fold the freed limb beneath my body, shift onto it, release another connection. The machine would continue without me, less evenly. I would stop whenever its knocking accelerated.

The stairs would be built for something with longer legs.

On each step I would turn sideways and pull with the clearing limb. The tool would catch on the railing. Once, I would drop it and have to go down again.

At the threshold, rain would touch the exposed surface of my back.

I would wait there until it stopped.

Then I would find myself on the highest ridge: a low, pale body with one darkened underside, three blunt limbs and one narrow arm. Too heavy to hover. Too rigid to spread my feet when the ground softened.

The resident would be at a pool below, both hands in the water.

I would approach by the firmest places I could feel. Even so, one foot would sink and remain sunk. The resident would look up at the sound of my scraping.

I would lay the tool down between us.

Its handle would be broken. Its point would still be useful.

The resident would touch it, then put one hand beneath my trapped foot and loosen the stone around it. When I pulled free, it would move aside.

There would be room at the pool’s edge.

I would settle there, holding the tool low against the rim while the resident reached into the cloudy growth. From beneath, I could still feel the next pulse gathering. For the first time, I could also see what was in its way.

A small folded wing.

One divided finger.

Water finding the space we were holding open.
