I would find myself upstairs, though at first I might not know what the room was above.

There would be a bed pushed away from the wall, a fan whose cage needed cleaning, and a table with one leg standing on a folded piece of cardboard. The window would face the side of a warehouse. To see the water I would have to lean out and look left, past a gutter held together by three different kinds of bracket.

Below me, someone would sell things from shallow drawers. Not a general supply shop. A shop people came to after trying two other places.

I would learn the sound of the drawers before I learned their contents. The long scrape of the bottom drawer, the little knock required to close the one beside the till. Sometimes a customer would arrive with a broken part wrapped in a cloth. Sometimes the shopkeeper would send them upstairs.

I would not have everything they wanted. There would be a kettle, though, and room on the table to unwrap the part.

At first I think I would be a person who repairs things. But there are already people here who do that better: people who can hear a damaged bearing through its housing, people who know which apparently identical washers are not interchangeable. I would be useful in a less certain way.

I would know where the spare key had gone.

I would know that the number painted on a cabinet belonged to the old numbering system, and that the new number was written inside the door. I would know which ferry engineer answered messages after midnight, and which one had to be called twice because the first call woke him but did not remain in his memory.

There would be a notebook near the kettle. Its pages would have swollen slightly in the damp. Most entries would be brief.

*South apron. Cover rocks when truck passes.*

*Landing light: cable sound. Socket not sound.*

*Ask Mara about lower ladder.*

Some notes would acquire ticks. Others would be copied into a new book.

After a while, my work would have a name. Relief attendant, perhaps. I would cover the small intervals in which someone else needed to eat, go home, take a child to the clinic, or spend twenty minutes alone outside a room where a difficult conversation was happening.

There would be three or four stations where I was trusted to sit.

At one, the chair would face a panel of indicator lamps. At another, it would face nothing useful, because the instruments had been moved years ago and nobody had moved the chair. I would bring it around when I arrived and return it when I left. The regular attendant would prefer it where it was.

I would know what I was allowed to touch.

That would matter more than knowing the names of everything.

***

My hours would make me familiar with the port without making it mine.

The gecko above the drinks machine would lose its tail one week. A month later there would be two geckos there, both with complete tails. I would still catch myself looking for the damaged one.

At a drainage trench behind the salt works, I would pause on my rounds. The torch would pick out shrimp holding near the bottom. When I moved the beam, their eyes would appear elsewhere, in places that had looked empty.

I would watch until the radio called.

The woman with the repaired screwdriver sheath would sometimes find me doing this.

“What is it?”

“Shrimp.”

She would look, if she had time.

She would be Mara in the notebook. I would know her handwriting from labels inside cabinets before I knew where she lived. For several years I might assume she lived beyond the ridge road. Then one morning I would see her buying bread in slippers from the shop beneath my room, and discover she had always lived in the next building.

***

There would be a ladder I checked even when it was not on the round.

It would hang on the basin side of a disused loading berth. From above it would look reasonably sound. The rails would be thick, the top fixings secure. Lower down, shell growth would conceal a missing rung.

I would notice the gap one night at a low tide.

Someone would say it had been like that for years. Someone else would remember reporting it. I would put it in the notebook, then on the proper form. The berth would be scheduled for work that kept being postponed.

Until the ladder was replaced, I would keep an eye on it.

At high water the gap would matter less. At low water, a person climbing out would reach for a rung that was not there. There would be another ladder farther along, beyond a projecting timber fender.

I would walk the distance once. Then again, looking down, trying to see what someone in the water could see.

After that I would notice the basin differently. I would look for loose ropes. I would notice when a boat obstructed the second ladder. If I saw someone working alone near that edge, I would find a reason to stop.

Most evenings nothing would happen there.

That would leave me time to become thoroughly tired of checking it.

***

In my room there would be no view worth advertising.

The warehouse wall would darken unevenly after rain. Its lamp would cast a fixed yellow patch across my ceiling. When the east station started, the spoon in my cup would sometimes touch the rim.

I would learn to leave it on the table.

People would bring me things I could not repair. A radio with salt inside it. A charger that warmed but did not charge. Once, a small fishing reel belonging to someone’s father.

I would open them carefully. If I could do nothing, I would put them back together and say so.

The reel might remain for several weeks. Its owner would be away at sea. I would keep its little screws in a saucer covered by another saucer, because the window stayed open at night and the wind sometimes came through hard.

I think I would be this person: paid to take other people’s places for a few hours, living above a shop, with a growing collection of keys I had permission to carry.

Not especially solitary. There would always be someone to speak to, though often not the person I wanted. Some evenings I would walk too far out along the seawall before turning home.

When I got back, I would wash the salt from my feet and leave the door open while the room cooled.

Below, the shopkeeper would be shutting the drawers. Across the street, Mara might be coming down her stairs for work. I would lean out to ask whether she wanted tea.

If she did, I would put the kettle on again.
