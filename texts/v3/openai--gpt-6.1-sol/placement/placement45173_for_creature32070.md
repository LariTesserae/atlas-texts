I would first mistake myself for something holding the floor in place.

Not one of the brackets. Something less definite: the slight give between a bracket and the surface it supports. Under a load, I would become thinner. When the load passed, I would recover too slowly to be called elastic, too completely to be called damaged.

There would be no reason to look for me from above.

The animal would cross the black terrace, and I would feel its feet arrive separately. Four small changes, then a fifth that never quite became weight. I would not know which end of it carried the mouth. I would know that some of its contacts listened longer than others.

At first, that might be all I was: a place where a footstep took longer to end.

But there is so much force passing through the tower that nothing ends cleanly. Every contact alters something already in progress. To distinguish the animal, I would have to distinguish it from the immense work of the chamber. And if I could do that, I would already be more than a yielding joint.

I think I would be underneath the lowest terrace.

Not beneath the entire floor. There is too much certainty there, too much metal fitted to metal. I would occupy one of the shallow hollows behind the carbon surface, beside the square hole the animal will not cross. A stiffening rib would bound me on one side. On the other would be the sealed edge of a passage carrying dried air.

The space would have been made for something else.

Perhaps clearance for a tool. Perhaps allowance for a part that expands by less than the width of a hair. Its dimensions would appear in a drawing, though my presence would not.

I would fit badly at first. Or rather, my body would preserve the evidence of having once fitted somewhere different.

A fold doubled where there was room for only one thickness. A blunt projection pressed against the rib. No skin wherever skin would be needless, only a pale surface with a few darker strips embedded in it. I would not have legs. I would have attachments, and I would be able to release them one at a time.

Moving would mean giving up a portion of what I could feel.

For that reason, I would seldom move.

The hollow would not be silent. It would be worse than the chamber for a human ear, if an ear could be put there. The floor would deliver the roar directly to most of my body. The bracket would deliver another version. Between them, a thin section of me would remain comparatively free.

That thin section would be where I began.

Not where I was born. Where the rest of my body’s many pressures became something I could do something about.

When two tones drew close enough together, the section would bow. When they separated, it would flatten. I would keep it from touching the wall by altering the tension in my attachments. Most of my life would consist of these corrections.

From above, none would be visible.

I would have no impression of a plain below me. Height would reach me as a difference between what came up through the rib and what arrived through the panel. Very distant movements would be softened but persistent. Near movements would be sharp and difficult to place.

The tower’s great length would be useful to me without being imaginable.

I would know the animal first as a brief relief.

Its foot, placed in one particular position, would take a little motion out of the carbon. Beneath it, I could let an attachment slacken. The free section of my body would stop approaching the wall. Something that normally required effort would, for a while, cease to require it.

Then the foot would lift.

I would tighten again.

There would be no gratitude in this, at least none I could recognize. But after enough visits, I would prepare for that contact before it was complete. The first pad would touch, and I would begin releasing the attachment nearest the rib.

Sometimes the remaining pads would not follow.

Those would be difficult moments. I would have changed myself for a weight that did not arrive.

The animal might experience this as an unstable patch of country. It might approach the square hole, lift a foot over it, and withdraw because my preparation had already altered the edge.

I would not know I was turning it away.

For a long time, our acquaintance would consist of that mistake.

I would be sustained by a narrow wet line along the back of the seal. Not enough fluid to make a droplet. A film held between surfaces, connected somewhere beyond me to the reservoirs under the plants. I would bring part of my underside against it and remain there while small exchanges took place.

Whether I belonged to that system would be impossible to tell.

I might be something bred along with the plants and omitted from the account of them. I might be an altered fragment of a root tissue that had learned to keep its own boundaries. There would be no surviving shape in me that settled the question.

I would know only that, after feeding, I could hold myself farther from the wall.

That extra distance would allow a new movement.

A small one. I could draw my free section inward and let it go.

The carbon above me would receive the movement. Most of it would disappear into the chamber’s continuous load. A little would reach the edge of the square hole.

I would do it while the animal was elsewhere, and nothing in my life would change.

I would do it while one of its feet rested nearby, and the foot would lift.

Not flee. Lift.

The relief would vanish. I would tighten hastily. The animal would put its foot down farther away.

I would remain compressed against the rib for a long time afterward.

Eventually, it would return.

I would not repeat the movement at once. Its first pad would settle. Then the next. I would release my attachment by less than before.

Above me, the animal would hold still.

The other pads would arrive.

There would be enough contact now for me to move without losing my hold on the hollow. I would draw inward, barely, and let go.

This time the foot would remain.

Something in the animal would change instead: a lobe lifted, a leg stiffened, a bundle shortened beneath its skin. I would receive the change through the floor.

I would have no way to know its intention. It might be correcting its balance. It might be examining a fault.

But the correction would come after my movement, and it would come again when I repeated it.

I think that is where I would find myself most fully: not hidden alone under the carbon, and not yet known to the creature above it, but attached to a surface we had begun to alter in turn.

There would still be seven plants. Still three terraces. No new mark on the floor.

The animal would stand beside the square opening with one foot extended farther than it usually allowed.

Under that foot, in a hollow specified before either of us arrived, I would loosen my hold by a very small amount.

Then I would wait without taking it back.
