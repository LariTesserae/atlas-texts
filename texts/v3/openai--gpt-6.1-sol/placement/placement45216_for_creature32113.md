At first I would be mistaken for something left beneath a pot.

A scrap of binding, perhaps, or the softened corner of a label. You would see me only when the pot was lifted: a pale, flattened thing against the bench, with grit adhering to one side and the circular mark of the pot’s base on the other.

I would not move immediately. Sudden exposure is not, down here, a reliable indication that something has gone wrong. Pots are lifted all day. Often they come back.

I would wait to discover whether the shadow returned.

If it did not, I would draw myself toward the seam between two boards. This would take long enough that a worker might notice a difference without seeing the movement. The pale scrap that had been beside the screw head would now be halfway beneath the next tray.

There would be a note about me eventually.

*Bench lining deteriorating.*

Then, in a different hand:

*Not lining.*

***

I think I would live near the propagation benches, but not directly beneath the lamps. There is a place where the light ends and water gathers along the underside of a rail before falling. I would need that edge: warmth without exposure, a surface that becomes wet and then merely damp.

At first I imagine myself without hands. The keeper already has the hands this place requires. I would have no use for another pair that could only do the same work less well.

But there would have to be some way of holding on.

So the pale edges would divide, not into fingers exactly, but into short, blunt tabs. Most of the time they would lie flat. Under pressure they could curl around a wire, the lip of a tray, a stem that ought not to be gripped tightly.

My body would be thicker than the workers first thought. Folded beneath a pot, I might be no larger than a hand. Spread along the wet bench, I could reach from one tray to the next. There would be no clear front. A darker patch near one edge might suggest a head until that edge went first into a crack and another part began the work.

I would have to learn the room through contact.

The pump vibration would reach me through a shelf support. Water would tell me something different depending on whether it had come straight from a pipe or passed through a bed. I would know the underside of a healthy pot by the slow dampness around its drainage hole. A blocked one would stay heavy against me.

This would not amount to knowing what was inside.

Sometimes I would be wrong.

***

The keeper would find me while separating a row of ferns.

Two pots would have rooted together. It would lift one, pause at the resistance, and reach beneath them. Its fingers would encounter my folded edge.

I do not think either of us would startle.

It would hold still. I would hold still. The pumps would continue making it impossible to hear whatever small sound this meeting might produce.

Then it would lower the pot.

The next morning there would be a shallow tile beside the bench, one of the old tiles from near the stairs. A little water would stand in its worn centre.

I might not use it for several days. It would be too open, and the tile would not yet smell of where it had been put. The keeper would leave it anyway.

Eventually I would settle there between movements. One edge beneath the bench; the rest within reach of the slow drip.

That would be the beginning of my having a place.

***

I would not tend the plants directly.

I would be useful where one task stops and another has not yet begun.

A tray has been watered, but the water has not come through. A shipment is ready, but one pot near the middle is still too wet to travel. A worker has cleared a drain and left the cover leaning against a bed.

These are small intervals. Most close without assistance.

I would move toward the ones that did not.

Beneath a tray that remained heavy, I would spread across the drainage holes and feel for the trapped water. Sometimes all that was needed would be the slightest lift at one corner. I could thicken there, slowly, until a bubble released and the water began to fall.

The effort would leave me thin elsewhere.

The keeper would sometimes arrive before I finished. It would lift the tray properly, put two slats beneath it, and wait while I withdrew.

I would learn to leave some things for its hands.

When I found a drain cover still out of place, I could do nothing about its weight. But I could lay myself across the clear section of aisle beside it. The keeper would stop. A worker might step over me twice before noticing.

There would be occasions when someone moved me aside and continued on.

I would return.

***

I would know the worn bearing long before I understood that it was a bearing.

Its note would be present in every surface I rested on. Along the bench rail it would feel narrow and rapid; through brick, blunter; through a thin pool on the floor, almost sharp enough to make me draw my edges inward.

On certain nights I would be unable to keep my whole underside against the tile.

I would go to the partition and find the keeper there.

It would have one hand on the pipe.

I would climb far enough to touch the back of that hand, then extend another edge to the pipe itself. Between those two contacts the vibration would feel different. Some of it would pass through the keeper.

We might stay that way until morning.

I would not be able to make the repair happen. The objects on the desk would remain objects on the desk. A round pebble would not become more persuasive because I understood why it was there.

Perhaps I would find my way under the requisition form.

The paper would absorb water from me and curl. Someone would put it somewhere dry. I would have caused trouble without making anything clearer.

Afterward the keeper would carry me back to the tile.

I would not go to the desk again.

***

There would be a danger in being useful beneath pots.

One day I would be loaded onto a trolley.

I would have folded myself too closely against the base of a shrub intended for a station corridor. The pot would be lifted, set among others, and wheeled toward the stairs before I had released it.

At the bottom step I would begin to loosen.

Perhaps the keeper would see a pale edge hanging over the trolley’s rim. Perhaps it would recognize the irregular wet trace I had left behind.

It would remove the shrub from the shipment.

This would look like one of its usual decisions. The workers might complain about the delay and fetch another pot.

Back at the bench, it would turn the shrub on its side. I would come away reluctantly, one portion at a time.

For a while afterward I would rest only on the tile.

When I began moving beneath pots again, I would keep an edge against the bench.

***

There would be no dependable name for me.

One worker might call me the pad. Another, seeing me extended across two shelves, would refuse that name. Someone would ask whether I was part of the keeper.

I could not settle that question.

I would remain apart from it for days. Then, during a cold week or after some disturbance in the watering, I would lie against the pale seam beneath its arm, held there by the pressure of its body against the bed.

My surface would darken. The keeper would rest.

When I left, a narrow portion of me might remain in the seam. I might take away something that had previously belonged there.

The workers would be careful with us.

***

At closing, I would usually be back beneath the propagation bench.

The keeper would arrive to inspect the new cuttings. I would feel its weight through the supports before its hands appeared beside the tray.

One row would be draining more slowly than the others.

I would gather myself beneath it. The keeper would move the tray slightly into the light, then lift the near corner while I passed under.

For a moment I would be exposed: pale, uneven, still carrying the shape of the places where I had rested.

Its hand would remain beside me.

I would cross the wet board and settle at the blocked opening. Above me, a cutting would stand in its small pressed circle of compost. Below, the first drop would swell and hold.

I would wait there until it fell.
