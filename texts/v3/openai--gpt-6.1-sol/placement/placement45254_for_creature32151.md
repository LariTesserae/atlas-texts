I would find myself beneath a stair that has been filled in.

Not beneath the whole stair. Most of that space would be packed with rubble and a mortar harder than the original stone. There would be a narrow remainder beside the wall, where the mason could not get an arm far enough in to finish the work. Water would enter there through a seam and leave by a different seam, slightly higher.

From the passage, you could see the first two steps. On the second would stand a bucket nobody uses, its handle fixed upright by lime.

I think I would live behind that bucket.

At first there would be very little to distinguish me from the place. A translucent thickness in the wetness. Something a lamp might reveal by passing over it rather than resting on it. My underside would fit the stone so closely that the worn marks of a chisel showed through my body.

But I would not be a deposit. Left alone long enough, I would move.

I would be one of the small animals of the tunnels, though not quite the sort usually found on an inspection glove. Broader, perhaps, and slower. An old individual, if individuals of my kind can be old. One edge of my body would have healed badly after being caught beneath a cover. It would pull inward while the rest of me spread, giving me a shape workers could recognize if they happened to look twice.

Most would not.

My world would have distances, but they would not be measured primarily in lengths.

The underside of the stair would be near. A fresh trickle on the opposite wall might be much farther away than the floor at the bottom of a shaft. There would be routes I could cross only during a particular part of the pump cycle, when the water thinned without breaking. Others would be continuously passable for years and then cease to exist between one feeding and the next.

I would know nothing about south.

I would know the direction in which my body became easier to extend. I would know the place where the film carried a taste that made me fold my front edge under myself and wait. Warmth would arrive through the surface I occupied. Vibration would arrive through almost everything.

The city’s roar would not be a roar to me. It would be separate pressures: the steady grip of one machine, a slow beating from another, the brief disturbance made by a boot placed on a stair above my shelter.

Some disturbances would mean nothing.

Some would mean lift yourself into the crack.

I would have made that mistake once. That would be the reason for the crooked edge.

***

There would be a woman who found me often enough to begin looking for me.

She would come along the passage with her lamp held low. The light would reach me before the warmth of her hand, and the warmth before the small change in air that accompanied her kneeling.

I would withdraw at first.

She would learn to wait.

Her pencil would move on paper beyond the limits of anything I could perceive clearly. Once, she would hold a ruler beside me without touching the wet film. The next time, she would bring a little folding scale with wider markings. She would have discovered that her own shadow made the first one difficult to read.

I would not know that I had been entered on a plan.

I would know that this particular disturbance usually ended without injury.

There would be a place near the old scar where I could feel contact less sharply. Once she would touch me there with the blunt end of a clean brush. My whole body would shorten. She would not do it again.

That would be as close as we came to an agreement.

In winter, though the tunnel itself scarcely cooled, the water behind the stair would change. Something from farther beneath the shelf would reach it. I would stop feeding and spend a long time folded against the seam.

The woman would find the visible patch empty.

She would put her lamp behind the bucket.

I would be there, smaller and cloudier than before.

After that, when she came, she would look behind the bucket first.

***

There would be others of my kind, but I would not have a neighborhood in the human sense.

I might encounter another body at the edge of a feeding patch. Our fronts would touch. One of us would turn, or both would remain until the film between us became too thin.

Sometimes a much smaller animal would pass partly over me. I would flatten and let it go.

There would be no certainty that I had met it before.

Still, I think there would be preferences. A body whose contact made me contract. Another beside which I continued feeding. I would not need a name for the difference to keep it.

Once, three of us would occupy the underside of the second step for several days. New water would be laying down a soft film there, and we would advance across it at different speeds. Our cleared tracks would remain visible until the next layer formed.

The woman would draw all three.

By her next visit, one would have gone into the rubble. One would have crossed the face of the stair and disappeared along the ceiling. I would remain.

She might assume I was the same one.

This time she would be right.

***

The change would begin while the passage still looked ordinary.

The water at my front edge would become difficult to hold. I would extend into it, draw back, extend again. Behind me, the old seam would continue to feed the hollow under the stair. Nothing would have dried.

But each time I tried to settle, part of my underside would loosen.

I would move behind the bucket, then farther in, where the mortar had been left rough. There would be little food there. For a while I would remain anyway.

The next disturbance from the woman’s footsteps would be familiar. Her lamp would reach into the hollow. I would retreat beyond it.

She would come back sooner than usual.

Then other footsteps would arrive. Something hard would be placed against the wall. The vibrations would be brief and sharp, separated by pauses.

No one would move the bucket.

Eventually a new route would open above the filled stair. It would not appear all at once. Dampness would gather in three isolated patches, and I would reach toward the nearest without being able to cross the dry space between.

I would wait.

When the patches joined, I would climb.

It would take me most of a day to pass beyond the woman’s reach. I would leave the familiar hollow, the chisel marks, the underside of the step where my body fitted without effort.

The new stone would be coarse. I would keep lifting my front edge to find somewhere better, and there would be nowhere better until I reached a shallow depression above the passage arch.

There I would spread out and feed.

Below me, the bucket would stand in water that no longer moved.

***

I would not witness the woman’s retirement as a retirement.

Her visits would become less frequent. On the last one I could recognize, she would remain in the passage a long time. A second person would be with her. Their hands would interrupt the lamp beam. Once, the light would settle on my new patch, then move away before I had begun to contract.

Afterward there would be many days without her.

The second person would return alone.

They would inspect the hollow behind the bucket. They would shine a light along the old seam and put something into the water. They would leave.

They would do this several times.

I would be above them, feeding badly because the new film was still thin. There would be no way to bring them closer. If I moved, I would move too slowly for them to notice. If I remained still, I would resemble the wet stone.

One day they would lift the bucket.

Its handle would break off in their hand.

The disturbance would make me fold against the ceiling. Their lamp would rise, perhaps because they were looking for somewhere to put the broken piece.

For a moment the light would stay on me.

Then it would return.

***

I would not become important.

There would be no reliable warning in my departures, no simple correspondence between my feeding and the pressure under the shelf. Sometimes I would leave because the water changed. Sometimes because another animal had occupied the good surface. Sometimes I would remain when remaining was dangerous.

The new worker would learn this.

They would also learn to look above the arch.

I would live there through the fitting of a small upper drain and the first pale growth around its rim. My damaged edge would catch on the new mineral crust. I would turn slightly, find a smoother portion, and continue feeding.

During inspections, a lamp would pass slowly over the ceiling.

Beyond the passage, gates would be adjusted, supper prepared, rainwater carried to the trees. I would feel some of it through the stone.

Most of it would never reach me.

Under my body, a little water would arrive from the left. I would extend into it. The branching darkness inside me would contract, and slowly, leaving a clean patch no wider than a fingernail, I would follow it around the rim of the drain.
