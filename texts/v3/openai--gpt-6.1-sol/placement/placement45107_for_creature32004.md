I would find myself below the occupied floor of a house, in a room that had not quite been abandoned.

At first I would know it by the light. The window would be low enough for fern stems to cross it, but a reflection from the opposite slope would reach the ceiling for an hour each morning. Someone would have left a rectangle unwhitewashed above a vanished cupboard. When the light arrived, that rectangle would become visible; when it passed, the room would seem evenly dark again.

There would be jars on shelves, an unbolted table, a length of rail kept because it might be useful. Against the inner wall, something covered with a cloth.

I might have come down to fetch a jar. I might have been sent to find a particular screw among tins of screws that had lost their labels. But I think I would stay after the errand was finished. Upstairs, someone would drag a chair across the floor. Dust would fall from a joint, so little that I would notice it only because it crossed the bright ceiling.

The covered thing would be a sewing machine.

Its treadle would still work. One of the feet would stand on a flat piece of purple stone; the other three would rest on the floor. There would be a groove worn into the tabletop where somebody had repeatedly drawn a heavy length of fabric toward them.

That is where I begin to have hands.

Not the hands of the woman who tests the wall. Mine would be younger, with a small burn near one wrist and a scar on the thumb from opening a mineral frame badly. I would turn the wheel before uncovering the whole machine, listening for the catch that meant a thread had worked into the bearing.

I would make and mend the soft parts of the works.

The felt curtains. The sleeves around pipe joints. The padded covers for tools and handrails. The narrow black squares between sheets of mineral. Things carried past without much attention, until one tears or hardens or becomes thin enough to let two surfaces touch.

There would already be someone doing this work when I arrived. For a while I would sit beside her and cut what she marked, keeping the scissors low over the table so the cloth did not lift. She would dislike wasted felt but dislike short allowances more.

“Leave that,” she would say when I trimmed a corner too closely.

“How much?”

She would pinch a fold between her fingers and hand it back to me.

I would learn slowly. A curtain that hung correctly in the morning could drag by the end of the season. A pipe sleeve needed room for the pipe, but also for the movement nobody had yet measured. Sometimes the useful part of an old cover would be its awkward bulge.

Eventually people would begin leaving things for me on the upstairs landing.

***

I would live in that house, though not in the workroom.

My bed would be in a small room above it, with a window looking into the orchard. From bed I could see one branch and a section of cable. I would learn the carriages by their undersides: the patch on one frame, the missing corner on another, a trailing end of binding that someone ought to cut.

In the kitchen, I would have the habit of folding cloth while talking. My housemates would move bowls away from my elbows. Once in a while I would find a bean in the hem of a finished curtain.

I would not be exceptionally quiet. At home I would laugh loudly, then hear my own laughter go out through the window. I would have opinions about how long the fruit should remain in the soup. I would put off mending my own coat because I knew exactly how long the repair would take.

My workroom would smell of wool, machine oil, and damp stone. On cold mornings I would carry the small jobs upstairs and sew beside the oven. The larger curtains would stay below. I would work on them wearing a hat, with a warm brick wrapped in cloth under the table.

***

The gray animals would discover me before I became good at the work.

At first I would blame myself for the ragged edges of stored felt. Then, reaching behind a roll, I would touch a warm, tightly packed body.

We would both withdraw.

Afterward I would keep the finished pieces in a cupboard with a metal latch. The scraps would remain in an open basket. This would be an accommodation rather than a solution: sometimes a strip too long to be a scrap would disappear.

One animal would come regularly while I was sewing. It would sit on the window ledge, so close to the fern shadows that I could work for an hour without seeing it. When the treadle stopped, its shut-mouth chirr would pass through the table.

I would answer by turning the wheel once.

I do not think I would give it a name. I would recognize it by a pale place on its forefoot, and worry when several nights passed without its arriving.

***

The woman with the brass instrument would bring me a curtain from the house whose foundations had been left below it.

One side would be worn almost through. I would measure the doorway and make a replacement, allowing more at the bottom than she asked for.

When we hung it, the extra cloth would lie in an untidy fold.

She would look at it for a while.

Then she would ask me to sew a pale thread across the fold, just above the floor.

Months later, I would come back because a fastening had pulled loose. The thread would be touching the grit. While I repaired the fastening, she would kneel and measure the distance from the thread to the hem.

I would begin keeping my old patterns.

Not all of them. There would be too many. But certain ones, rolled rather than folded, with the building and date written along an edge. Sometimes I would lay a new pattern over an old one and see that a doorway had narrowed, or that its upper corner had risen beyond the paper.

I would take these upstairs to show someone. They would clear a place on the kitchen table, moving the bread and the bowl of salted fruit.

***

Late in the day, the workroom’s light would reach the machine from below. It would catch the needle, the little polished guides, the brass head of a pin lost between the floorboards.

I would finish a seam before going upstairs. Usually I would finish it. Sometimes there would be a knock, or the smell of something catching in the pot, and I would leave the needle down in the cloth.

That would be how I knew I expected to return: a curtain gathered across my knees, the wheel still, one unfinished length waiting on the other side.

Outside the low window, the leaves would begin to turn inward.

I would move the scraps away from the damp sill, but leave one narrow strip where the animal could reach it.
