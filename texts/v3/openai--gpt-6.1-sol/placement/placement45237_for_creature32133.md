I would be in a room whose floor used to be its doorway.

The lintel would still be above me, though not by much. To enter, I would put my head sideways, bring one shoulder through, and wait while the dust settled beneath my feet. There would be a second entrance at the back, between a root and the wall. I could pass through that one when I was younger. Now I could put a forefoot into it and feel the air.

My room would be close enough to the broad court that, for part of the day, light reached the basin. I would not see the basin from where I slept. I would see the underside of a leaf growing out of it, briefly lit. This would be enough to know when to go there.

I think I would be one of the plated animals. Not the largest, not one of those whose breathing moves leaves far below. My back would fit beneath an ordinary lintel. One plate near my left shoulder would have grown unevenly after an injury, leaving a raised edge that caught against stone. I would turn slightly before entering narrow places, so that side went through last.

There would be no reason for anyone to distinguish me at first. Dust would lie on me as it lay on the others. A bird could land on my back and continue its search without realizing I had been awake for some time.

But I would have a habit.

When I found a small piece of stone that moved under my foot, I would stop and turn it over.

Most stones would be left where they were. Some would fit more securely on their other face. Some would reveal a beetle. Occasionally there would be a hollow underneath, retaining a little warmth, and I would hold my forefoot there before moving on.

A few pieces I would carry home.

Not the prettiest pieces. I would have little use for the color that makes a stone remarkable in stronger light. I would choose something I could close my front digits around: a short wedge, a blunt chip with one smooth side, a fragment with a hole worn through it. Carrying would be troublesome. With one front foot occupied, I would have to place the others differently, and on a slope I would sometimes set the object down and go ahead without it.

Then, later, I might come back.

My room would contain seven or eight such things. Their number would change without my noticing the change. A younger animal would take one. Another would disappear beneath the leaves. One would be pressed into the floor until only its upper surface remained.

There would be a curved piece of pottery I had brought from the court. I would turn it often. Its edge would fit along the hard ridge inside my mouth, though it was not food. It would retain the warmth of my foot for a little while after I released it.

I would sleep beside it, but not always.

***

I would know the city unevenly.

One passage would be familiar down to the order in which my feet crossed its stones. Another, only a little farther away, would be somewhere I had entered once and never entered again. I would know a stair from below and have no sense of what stood above it. I would know that a wall continued behind a curtain of roots because, years earlier, there had been room for me to walk there.

The routes would change within my body before I understood that they had changed outside it.

At a certain corner, I would raise my head to clear a stone that was no longer there. At another, I would lower myself too late and scrape the raised plate on my shoulder.

Sometimes a young one following me would take an easier way.

I would stand while it went ahead. Then I would follow it.

The young would not all belong to me. Some would sleep in my room for several rests and leave. One, dark along the spine, would return often enough that I began waiting when I reached a difficult threshold. It would climb onto my back from a low block, pulling itself upward in three or four efforts. Its claws would find the same seams each time.

I would feel its weight chiefly when descending.

It would move forward then, pressing its small feet into the plates behind my head. I would stop until it settled. Sometimes the stopping would be enough for it to climb down.

We would not always go to the same place.

***

There would be a wall I listened to more than the others.

Its stones would be tight together, with little dust in the joints. Beside it, under the feet, the floor would be one continuous slab. If I rested my throat there, a pulse from farther down could reach me clearly.

Most of the sounds passing through the city would be too broad or too broken to attend to. The birds would shake whole chambers. A falling stone would arrive from several directions at once. Feet would make brief, close disturbances and then be gone.

But sometimes, while I was grinding a dry stem, I would feel a short pressure beneath my chest.

I would stop eating.

A second pulse might follow. Or none.

I would put my throat down and make one of my own.

I would not know how far it traveled. I would know only the effort of making it, the small tightening beneath my mouth, and the feeling in my front feet. Afterwards I would remain still. A beetle could cross my wrist. The stem would dry a little more where I had bitten it.

If another pulse came, I might answer again.

There could be an animal beyond the wall that I had never touched, never shared a feeding place with, never smelled beneath a lintel. Yet on some days we would both interrupt what we were doing.

***

Once the back entrance would become smaller.

This would happen during a rest. There would be a scrape, close and heavy, and the root beside the opening would bend. I would wake with my feet spread beneath me. Leaves would fall from the ledge above, and dust would slide along the wall.

Nothing else would happen for a long time.

I would go to the gap and put my head against it. Cool air would still pass over my nostrils. When I drew back, pale powder would remain on the soft skin beside my mouth.

The dark-backed young one would be somewhere beyond it.

I would know because it would touch my forefoot.

We would stay like that for a while, awkwardly, with the stone between us. Then it would withdraw, and I would begin scraping.

I would not dig continuously. I would work until the loose material crowded my chest, push it behind me, rest, and work again. Another adult would come through the main entrance and stand beside me. It might begin at the same place. It might clear away what I had loosened.

My collection of stones would be scattered by our feet.

The curved pottery would break.

Eventually the young one would come around by another route. It would enter through the lintel, dusty along its whole back, and press the underside of its mouth against my neck.

I would keep scraping for a little while.

Then I would stop.

***

After that, I would carry a wedge to the narrowed opening.

It would be one I already had, worn smooth on the side that had faced upward in my room. I would push it into a shallow place under the displaced block. It would not hold up the block. The block would be far too large.

But the wedge would stay.

When I put my foot beside it, I could still feel the little current of air.

This is where I would find myself most often: in that intermediate room, with the crowded city above and the heavier bodies somewhere below, moving leaves away from a gap that no longer admitted me.

I would still go out to feed. I would grind the dead leaves and leave the growing center. I would let the small birds search my back. At the basin, I would put both front feet over the rim and lower my head among the husks.

As I grew, the room would become more difficult to enter. I would have to exhale beneath the lintel. One day I might sleep outside it instead, against the wall where the floor carried sound.

For a time I would continue going in.

There would be leaves to push aside, a few stones underfoot, the smell of another animal that had rested there while I was away. At the rear, the wedge would still be in its place.

I would reach past it into the opening. My digits would touch the root, the cool underside of the block, the loose dust beyond.

Then I would draw my foot back beneath me and settle, leaving the gap clear.
