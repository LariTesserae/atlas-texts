At first I would put myself in the handprint.

Not in the deepest part, where a little water stands after the channel has emptied, but along the shallow crescent made by the heel of the palm. There, a ridge of clay shades its own underside. Moisture remains beneath it after the rest of the impression has turned pale.

I would be easy to mistake for the stain that moisture leaves.

For a while that seems enough: a small brown growth inside the trace of a hand, taking shelter in an accident of pressure. But the exposed bank is too hot for most of me. Whatever appears there would be only the outermost part. I would follow the dampness inward, beneath the lip of the mark, into the join between one course and the next.

The join is not open in any ordinary sense. The blocks touch almost everywhere. Still, there is room beside a flattened reed, room around a grit particle that prevented two surfaces from meeting, room in a crease pressed shut at its mouth. I would be thin enough to enter these places.

My first extension might end against something useless: a grain from which I could loosen nothing, a pocket whose water was too bitter with salt. Another would reach the reed.

There I would remain.

The reed has been enclosed since the bank was built. Along one side it still holds the shape of its stem. Along the other, where water has returned more often, its substance yields. I would spread over that softened side. A narrow dark line would become a sleeve, then a branching patch in the clay around it.

This would be the first place I could call a body, though the word would already be becoming inaccurate.

The patch would thicken unevenly. Parts of it would feed; parts would carry water; some would close and become a passage through which a later growth could reach the reed. I would extend beyond the material that had first sustained me, then return to it by another route.

Above me, the handprint would dry.

I would not know it as a handprint. Its curved shelter would have mattered to me, but the person who made it would not. There would be no image of fingers in my life, no sense that an absent being had prepared a recess for my arrival. The shape would persist as differences in heat and water. One side would be survivable longer than the other.

That would be its meaning.

Through the hottest weeks I would become several small places.

One beneath the reed. One against a root that had entered the join from above. One farther inward, where a slight hollow collected moisture too slowly to fill but slowly enough not to empty. The threads between them would lose their living contents. Some would collapse. Others would keep their walls.

From outside, there would be no reason to say I was still there.

Even from within—if there were anything in me that could take account of an extent—there would be no continuous body to account for. The patch near the root would continue an exchange. The deeper patch would do almost nothing. Beneath the reed, a few cells would spend what remained of the last wet season and then stop spending.

I hesitate to make one self of these survivors.

They would share a history. That would not guarantee their reunion.

When the rain returned, water would enter through the bank in more than one place. The root would swell first. Farther inward, the hollow would receive a dark bead through the seam above it. My surviving edges would open at different times.

One would find an old passage still usable.

Another would meet a growth that had arrived while I was closed.

At the meeting, neither would recognize ownership. The passage would contain water, softened matter, the remains of walls. One growth might withdraw from the substances released by the other. They might continue beside each other, separated by less space than a dust grain occupies. Or one might pass into the old network while the survivor that made it stayed small.

What I had been would become a route for something else.

I think this is where I would find myself most fully: not in the stain, and not quite in the branching fan, but in the living edge as it reaches a passage it did not make.

There would be useful things ahead. There would also be waste, dryness, another inhabitant. My growth would turn according to what it met. Behind the advancing margin, the seam would be altered.

A reed fiber would disappear a little at a time. Clay formerly held against it would loosen. Water would begin to travel farther before being absorbed. At the bank’s face, a patch that once dried by noon might remain dark into the afternoon.

Something green would establish itself there.

Its root would come down through a crack and lie across me. The root would change the seam again, pressing in one place, opening another, taking water I might otherwise have reached. Around it, smaller inhabitants would gather. I would grow beside some of them and away from others.

There would be no clear boundary between my shelter and what I was doing to it.

Perhaps, after several seasons, I would reach the surface beneath the lodged leaf.

The leaf would have caught on a reed end exposed by the collapse of the bank. Water would hold its underside close to the fresh clay. I would cross from the reed onto the stem, then spread beneath the leaf where the sun could not reach me directly.

For a few mornings, that would be the richest part of my body.

Clear creatures would turn in the water beside it. Their rims would draw in fragments loosened from the leaf. A film would fasten grains to its upper surface. My own threads would enter the softened veins.

The channel would fill again. Water would pull at the leaf.

Most of it would tear away.

A piece of stem would remain attached to the bank, and my growth would remain inside it. Another portion would travel downstream, still alive, held in wet matter until it lodged somewhere or dried.

I would stay, and I would leave, without either event being complete.

Later, the bank might give way where the reed had vanished. Not because of me alone. The clay would have swollen and shrunk; roots would have pressed through it; water would have gathered behind the dry face. But my passages would be among the places where the break began.

The fold of falling earth would turn my seam toward the sky.

Much of me would die before evening.

Under the fallen bank, however, an old passage would now lie close to the bottom of a new basin. Water would reach it from a direction it had never come before. A surviving edge would soften, extend, and find a piece of buried reed.

That is where I would be.

Too far beneath the surface to receive the light, close enough that the next change in the water could reach me. Growing through the material that held the basin in its shape, leaving behind a little more room than I had found.
