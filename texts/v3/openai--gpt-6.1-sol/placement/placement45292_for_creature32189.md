I would live below the stair that leads to Sella’s roof.

Not directly below it. There is a hot space there where the stair’s lower bearing travels, and a person who rented it once put a cupboard in the way. I would live beside that space, behind a door too narrow to carry the cupboard through. From my bed I could see the underside of the third landing. Whenever someone went up, a little dust would fall from a joint the landlord had painted shut.

The room would have one useful luxury: a cool shaft at the head of the bed. In the evening I could open its cover and put my hands in the air coming up. The shaft would belong to the building, not to me. I would be forbidden to keep food in it. I would keep an onion there sometimes, on a string, taking it out before the inspector came.

At first I think I would repair shoes. There is something satisfying about the small, explicit failures: a cracked rib, a pad worn thin, a channel blocked with grit. People would sit on my doorstep with one bare foot held away from the floor, telling me where they had walked. I would learn the city through damage.

But I would be better with fittings than with customers. I would forget to charge for the second repair, or say too plainly that a sole could not safely last another month. Eventually someone would ask whether I could reach a screw beneath a sink. I would be thin enough to try.

That would become my work.

I would have a bag of short tools, several made by cutting down ordinary ones. A wrench with almost no handle. A mirror mounted on a strip of metal. Three pieces of ceramic that could be put beneath my elbow when I lay on a warm floor. My best tool would be an ugly pair of pincers with a bend in the jaws. I would have paid too much for them and defend the expense whenever anyone noticed.

Most of my jobs would concern things people had meant to deal with earlier.

A pipe coupling hidden behind a stove. A bracket left facing the wrong way. A shutoff installed in a room that was now half stone.

I would arrive, take off my coat, and ask what lay on the other side of the wall.

People would answer with the name of a room.

I would have to ask what lay there now.

***

My mother’s kitchen would still be somewhere beneath the eastern district.

She would be alive, living two floors above it, dissatisfied with the new kitchen because the shelf was on the wrong side. I would offer to move the shelf. She would explain that it was not only the shelf.

The old room had a window from which she could see the food lift arrive. In the new one she could hear it but had to walk to the landing to find out whether it carried anything worth buying. That difference would have made her dependent on neighbors whose judgment she did not respect.

I would remember the old kitchen more generously than she did. I had not cooked in it. I had sat under the table and listened to the cooling pipes tick.

For a while, when I was small, I believed someone was tapping back from below.

I would no longer believe that. Still, I would listen before putting a drill into stone.

***

Sella would know my step on the roof stair.

“You’ve worn the left one again,” she would call through her door.

“It’s the landing.”

“It isn’t.”

She would be right. I would put off repairing my own shoes because the repair could be done at any time, and thus would never quite belong to the day.

Her granddaughter would bring me the copper plant to mend. Not the leaf that had already been repaired; she would be under strict instructions not to let anyone touch that. Another leaf would have loosened where its stem entered the pot.

She would stand beside my bench while I worked, watching the tools rather than my face.

“Could you make it hollow?”

“The pot?”

“The leaves.”

I would ask why, though I would already have an idea.

She would have drawn a system in which fog collected on the copper, ran through the stems, and watered a real plant hidden inside the false one. The drawing would include arrows and a little door for taking out the living plant when it became too big.

I would explain that the copper would usually be too warm to catch much water indoors.

She would ask whether we could put it outside.

I would explain something else.

Eventually I would hear how much I sounded like everyone she had already asked.

So we would make one test leaf. A scrap, not part of her grandmother’s plant. We would place it on the roof beside a small glazed cup and measure what it caught.

Very little, probably.

But we would measure it.

***

One afternoon I would be called to a house where a water line had stopped running.

The family would have waited three days. They would have fetched water from a public tap and blamed the pressure until a neighbor said hers was fine.

The line would descend into a former washroom. There would still be a narrow opening above the old door, enough to get my shoulders through if I breathed out. Beyond it, the rock would have risen almost to the ceiling.

I would lower my lamp first.

People would assume that narrow spaces did not trouble me. Narrowness would not. Heat would. So would the possibility of dropping a tool where my hand could not follow it.

I would go in with a cord around my wrist and the bent pincers tied to another cord.

The coupling would be visible just beyond reach, pressed sideways by the stone. I would work on it for perhaps twenty minutes, resting often, hearing the family move about in the room above.

And there, beside my face, would be a shallow niche that had not yet filled.

A comb. Two dark buttons. A little glass bottle with its stopper missing.

Nothing worth delaying for.

I would delay.

The bottle would be too far back. The comb would break when I touched it. I would bring out the buttons in my closed hand, along with the piece of comb that had stayed between the pincers.

The woman who lived upstairs would look at them and say, “Those aren’t ours.”

Then her father would come nearer.

He would take one button, turn it over, and put it down again.

I would wait a moment before asking where they wanted the new shutoff fitted.

***

I would not become a collector of things taken from buried rooms. My own room would be too small, and most things would already belong to someone.

But I would begin asking an extra question before closing an access hole.

Not everyone would understand it. Some would be annoyed. They had called me because the pipe was leaking, not because they wanted to think about the washroom.

Sometimes there would be nothing to retrieve.

Sometimes a person would say, “Wait,” and go to fetch an older person.

I would learn to include this time in my price without naming it separately.

***

At evening I would sit on the third landing with my shoes off and their soles facing me. There would be enough light there for small work, and the tread would have cooled.

Above, Sella would be arguing with someone about an exhaust cover. Her granddaughter would come down to inspect our cup. Below, a neighbor would be frying something whose smell made me reconsider the onion.

I would have a pad half fastened when a deep knock came through the stair.

My tools would shift in their tray.

I would put my bare feet onto the ceramic rests and wait, holding the little fastening pin between finger and thumb. From above there would be no voices. Below, the pan would scrape off the stove.

The child might be halfway down the next flight.

I would look up to see.

Then, if nothing followed, I would make room for her beside me. She would show me the cup, tilting it carefully so that the small catch gathered along one edge.

We would disagree about whether there was more than yesterday.

Afterward I would finish the shoe.
