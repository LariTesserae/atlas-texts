At first I would put myself beside the slab, in the slightly deepened light over the fossil shells. It is the easiest place to indicate. Here, I could say, and something would be visible.

But that would locate only the thickest part of me.

I would also be in the groove where a soft contact has spread around a grain of limestone. I would be lifted just clear of the silt between the plants. Some of me would extend under the buried end of the stone, beyond the reach of daylight. No one place would account for the others.

I think I would be the inhabitant, without quite possessing its outline.

The vibration would help.

It would reach the slab cleanly and enter the shelf with a slight persistence, taking longer to leave some grooves than others. Through the sheltered tissue beneath the sediment it would arrive faintly, but still arrive. I would not hear a note and then interpret its echoes. Different parts of me would tighten or ease, and in those differences my extent would become available to me.

A contact has loosened.

An edge has lifted.

The thicker fold remains where it was.

There would be no need to gather these facts at a centre. The adjustment could occur where it was needed. A soft point of purchase would shorten until its neighbour took more strain. Water trapped beneath a fold would escape through a widening too small to trouble the sediment. Across the fossil band, a pale curve would appear to shift.

That would be enough movement for a while.

I would have little use for the view an observer has of the hollow. The farther rim would not belong to my awareness merely because light could reach it. Even the pondweed would be known chiefly at its base: the slow pressure of a root, then another, making a place less easy to occupy.

I would leave that place.

Not all at once. First a feeding surface would close. A few contacts would withdraw. The edge would thicken as it folded back, and eventually there would be a passage through which the root could continue. The plant would rise into water I could not use, its leaves holding their narrow shapes. I would remain on the stone.

There is something appealing to me about how little of this would need to become a story. A root presses; tissue yields. The change is real without becoming an encounter.

Still, I would not be entirely without a past.

Near the slab, there are folds the present hollow does not require. I would feel them when contracting: extra thickness meeting thickness, an old arrangement limiting how far a newer one could draw inward. Crossing open silt once demanded those folds. Perhaps they would retain no image of the crossing, no sense of distance travelled. They would simply make certain movements easier than others.

If I had to leave again, I would not begin from nothing.

For now I would keep the broad shape. Groundwater would enter an old channel and meet a surface already fitted to it. A small difference in what the water carried would be enough to open that surface more fully. Elsewhere, another would close. Feeding would take place in patches, too slowly to give the body a visible rhythm.

The lake would not feel empty to me. Nor would it feel populated. Those seem like judgements that require looking outward.

I would have the contact beneath a fold, the cold tightening its membrane, the weak supply in a crack. There would be room between these things. Much of my body might remain almost unoccupied by change.

Then a pump would stop.

Across the limestone, the familiar pattern would thin. Nothing would touch me, yet the certainty of several contacts would diminish together. I would press down. Where I had been broad and lightly held, I would become narrower and firmer.

The outer edge would retreat a little from the plants.

Near the exposed shells, tissue would gather into the folds. Beneath the buried stone, the sheltered part would receive the increased weight. I would be more definite there, though less extended through the hollow.

In that interval, I would attend to sounds usually covered by the machinery: a small passage of water inside the rock; a remote impact, arriving without any knowledge of what had struck; the strain produced by my own contraction.

I would wait in those weaker relations.

When the machinery returned, I would not spread immediately. One contact would release and settle farther out. Then another. The groove would become usable again before the bare floor did. Light would pass through tissue growing thinner, and the fossil shells would recover their usual apparent depth.

So I would find myself low in the hollow, mostly on limestone, with a little of me held above sediment and a little kept beneath it.

I would be this slow, clear body. I would know the stone by holding it, and know how much of myself was holding by what came through.
