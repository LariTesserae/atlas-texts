At first I would take a room near the south gate. I would want a window from which I could see the road return. I would tell myself this was useful: one ought to know what had arrived, what had failed to arrive, whether the low bank was still where it had been yesterday.

But I would spend too much time watching it.

Someone would come to mend the heating disk and find that I had put my chair by the window, beyond the warmth. They would ask whether I was waiting for a delivery. I would say no. They would finish the repair and move the chair back against the wall without asking.

Eventually I would move farther in.

Not to the square. Somewhere along a passage with a turn in it, above a workroom, below a household whose washing occasionally dripped onto my sill. The room would have a cupboard, but not one of the cupboards through which supplies arrive. Just a shallow recess with two shelves and a door that needed lifting slightly before it would close.

I think I would be good at lifting it. I would put off repairing it for years.

***

There would have to be work.

My first inclination would be to copy the records. I would like the dry paper, the close attention, the permission to preserve a discrepancy without resolving it. But the clerk would discover that I made entries too clear.

If a tool had been returned to a household that no longer occupied the address written beside its name, I would try to add an explanation. If two measurements disagreed, I would give one of them a footnote. The clerk would look at the page for a long time.

“Which part did you see?” she would ask.

I would learn to cross out less.

Still, I do not think that would be my trade. I would want to handle something after a morning spent among words.

There must be a great deal of wrapping in the district. Bread paper, cloth bindings, the padded nests in which small machine parts arrive. Someone has to sort it. Some can be used again; some has become brittle; some retains a fold that will not admit a different object.

I would begin there, at a bench beside a heating disk, opening knots with a blunt needle.

It would not be important work, but people would bring things to the bench: a latch too cold to grasp, an instrument that needed a case, a cup with a broken handle that its owner refused to discard. I would make sleeves, fitted boxes, little cushions stitched from cloth too small for clothing. For the cup, I would make a band with a loop wide enough for two fingers.

The first one would slip. The second would keep the cup secure but make it awkward to wash.

Its owner would return both and explain the difficulty without being polite about it. I would make a third.

After a while I would be known as the person who could fit something around something else without taking up much more room.

That seems a possible livelihood here.

***

My workroom would be narrower at the back than at the front. I would keep the long scraps overhead, held in loops, and the small ones in drawers whose contents I knew better than their labels.

There would be a drawer marked **soft, pale**, mostly containing neither.

I would have a favorite pair of scissors. One blade would be slightly shorter than the other. Someone would offer to replace them, and I would decline, then feel foolish about the strength of my refusal.

I would be pale, like the neighbors. There would be a split beside one thumbnail that opened each winter. My coat would have sleeves lined in blue, though I might not know until I turned one inside out to repair it. I would own two cups and use the chipped one because the unchipped one cooled more quickly.

I would not be an engineer. This would matter.

People sometimes mistake a person who measures things for a person who understands why they have changed. I could measure a cupboard door accurately enough to make a padded cover. I could not tell its owner whether the door ought to have become narrower.

Once, early on, I would plane a box to fit a shelf that had shortened overnight.

An engineer would come later, take the box down, and put thin shims along the shelf’s supports. By evening there would be a gap beside my neatly fitted box.

She would not scold me. She would ask me to keep the shaving I had removed.

For several weeks it would lie on my bench, too small to be useful.

***

I would be responsible for one street post.

Not a crossing in the square; a lesser junction, where the passage turns toward the laundry stair. Its cap would be cloudy on one side. I would have to crouch to see the moving thing within it, and in winter my knees would hurt before I was certain how far it had risen.

I would turn the chamber too early once.

Nothing dramatic would happen. The laundry stair would develop an extra shallow step. People carrying baskets would catch their feet on it. An engineer would spend two afternoons making adjustments, and for a month the woman upstairs would point out the step whenever she saw me.

I would offer to carry her washing.

“You can carry it,” she would say. “You still have to look.”

So I would become someone who looks twice, not because I was naturally patient but because several households remembered the occasion when I had not been.

***

There is one thing I would make that would take me longer than it should.

The old engineer needs a way to open his cupboard in the morning. I would hear this from someone else and arrive with an insulated grip, pleased with myself.

He would try it and give it back.

It would keep the brass from his skin, but it would require his fingers to close more tightly than they could.

I would make a lever next. That would work, except that it caught his sleeve when he passed.

The eventual device would be ugly: a padded wooden tongue, a cord, two small guides screwed into the cupboard frame. He could pull it with the side of his hand. Nothing would project into the room.

I would paint it to match the door. The paint would not match.

Months later, he would bring me a fragment of young ground to hold while he fastened his coat. I would wait for him to tell me what it would become.

Instead he would ask for it back.

I would hand it over. There would be a cold place in my palm for some time afterward.

***

I do not think I would remain entirely easy to find.

Perhaps it would begin on evenings when I worked too long. A customer would open the workroom door, look directly at my chair, and call upstairs.

“I’m here,” I would say.

They would glance toward the sound but not quite toward me.

At first I would stand up quickly. Later I would keep a scrap of hardwood beside the scissors and tap it against the wall.

The doctors would ask sensible questions. I would answer them. An engineer would adjust a pipe behind the bench. For a while I would be plainly visible even when tired, and I would take this as a cure.

Then one afternoon a child would come for the repaired loop on her mother’s shopping bag. She would stop in the doorway.

I would reach for the wooden scrap.

“I know,” she would say, and knock on the wall herself.

I would dislike how relieved I felt.

After that, I would leave the striker near the door when customers were expected. There would be no need to make everyone watch me searching for it.

***

I would sometimes sit in the courtyard, though not often enough to be one of its regular observers.

I would bring a chair with a narrow seat. It would have a cushion made from the remnants of three unsuccessful cupboard grips. On cold days I would take something to mend, then discover that my hands were too stiff for sewing.

The rising ground would not give me anything to do. No cover to fit, no loose edge to bind, no measurement anyone had asked me to take.

For a while this would make me restless.

Then a window would open overhead. Someone would ask whether I could repair the fastening on a child’s coat. I would say to leave it on the bench.

The sunlight would reach the earth and, a little later, one arm of my chair. I would put my hand there. If someone else came down, I would shift the chair so there was room for theirs.

I would be that person, I think: a maker of small fittings, living above a workroom in a passage with a turn. Occasionally difficult to see. Usually available after breakfast. Not especially wise about the ground, and inclined to keep scraps beyond their usefulness.

When the light left, I would go upstairs and open the wrapping drawer. There would be a coat on the bench, folded to show the broken fastening.

I would pull my chair close to the disk, find the blunt needle, and begin.
