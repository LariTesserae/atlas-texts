At first I would find myself beneath the shelf where the old cluster grew.

There is nothing there to distinguish me from the rest of the hollow. A shallow pool occupies the pocket. In the morning, light touches its far edge; in the afternoon, the shelf shades it entirely. The marks left by roots are visible through the water, pale branches ending in rounded depressions.

I would be near one of those endings.

Not in the water, exactly. A little below it, where the stone ceases to be entirely stone. Warmth would reach me from beneath. Above, the pool would press with a weight so slight that only its changes mattered: a little heavier after rain, a little lighter at the end of a clear day.

For a long time, this might be enough to constitute a place.

I would have no view of the hollow. The western wall would reach me as a delayed increase in moisture. Noon would arrive through a thickness of warming rock. The ferns would be known at a distance by the small withdrawals they made, each one drawing through a different succession of passages.

Some days, water would pass easily through me. On others, I would hold it.

The difference would not yet feel like a choice.

There would be a point along my upper surface where I remained unusually soft. Water gathered there even when the rest of the pool grew shallow. Something had once drawn from that point, and the substance around it had learned to yield.

The drawing had stopped. The yielding had not.

That is where I would begin to become particular.

Elsewhere in the body, roots would press, take, release. Their contacts would fluctuate with the light. Here, the old opening would receive water and offer it to nothing. A slight circulation would persist beneath the branching impressions. It would spend warmth. It would keep a part of me more open than it needed to be.

I would not remember the vanished plant as leaves.

I would remember it as the ease with which water once left me.

As the seasons passed, the unused contact would tighten at its edges. Mineral grains would settle closer together. One of the smaller passages would close, then another. But the deepest place would remain yielding. Rain would refill it. Heat from below would loosen it. Each time I approached the shape I might have had without the plant, some ordinary change in weather would interrupt me.

I would be an adjustment that had not finished.

Then a seed would lodge in the pool.

For several days it would make no difference. Its weight would be less than the weight of a falling drop. It might rest against the shelf, roll a little when water entered, settle at last in one of the old root marks.

I would not know it was there until its root reached down.

The first contact would be almost indistinguishable from sediment pressing into a seam. A fine tip would meet the softened place. It would pause. Above me, the seed would be spending the small provision it had brought with it.

What reached me would be unfamiliar.

The former root had left a broad contact, a long-used surface through which exchanges could happen readily. This one would touch at a point. The traces it released would be different. Around it, I would thicken slightly, then loosen as water arrived from the wall.

The root would advance during that loosening.

I might close again before it had gone far.

There would be no single moment of welcome. Some parts of the contact would yield while others resisted. The root would grow crookedly through the difference, thickening behind its tip. A narrow passage beneath it would widen. Another, farther away, would receive less water.

Above the pool, two leaves would open.

Their presence would reach me gradually: first as more persistent drawing, then as something returned. The new root would carry down what those small leaves had made. Where it touched me, the clear substance would change. Water that had lingered in the empty pocket would begin to leave by another route.

I would still be beneath the shelf. I would still belong to the body extending under the floor, up the wall, deep into the mountain. But this small region would no longer respond quite as it had before.

The new plant might survive.

It might fail after its second leaf, leaving a thread that slowly lost its firmness. I would retain that encounter too. The old root marks would remain in the stone; beneath them, a finer change would persist in me.

If it lived, its third leaf would reach beyond the shelf.

Sunlight would warm that leaf while the pool below stayed shaded. For the first time, brightness from outside my little pocket would arrive along the new contact. My upper surface would soften. The root would draw more deeply.

I think I would be there: in the portion of the inhabitant that had continued supplying an empty place, now slowly changing around a different life.

Not the whole hollow speaking. Not a keeper beneath it.

A few grains moving apart. A passage becoming easier to cross.

By evening, water would begin to collect on the western wall. The change would travel toward me through the body, arriving long after the exposed stone had cooled.

The new root would be quiet.

I would let the water reach it.
