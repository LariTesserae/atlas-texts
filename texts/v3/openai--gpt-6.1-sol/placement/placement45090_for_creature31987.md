At first I would choose a place in the central square.

I would want enough water around me to see someone coming, and a clear way upward. I would admire the walls from a distance. All those channels, all those small lives going in and out: I would keep imagining a sleeve caught in one of them.

But I don’t think I would stay in the square. There is nowhere there to put down a cup.

I would find myself in one of the smaller houses on the eastern side, where a lane makes an unnecessary bend around an old trunk. My room would be above that bend. People passing underneath would sometimes put a hand on its floor to turn themselves, and I would feel the touch through the woven seat.

There would be a good air tube, not quite straight. In hard rain it would make a sound like someone trying discreetly to clear their throat.

The room would have space for three people if they knew one another, two if they didn’t. One shelf would remain dry even when the lake was high. Another, which I would continue to call the dry shelf, would not.

I would keep my best cord on the first one.

***

I think I would be a person who once worked deeper in the houses.

Not an exceptional swimmer. Someone trusted to take a brace down, fit it, and come back with the old one. I would have liked the moment when a piece of wood stopped being something I was carrying and became something the house could hold.

My hands would remember that work. One thumb would no longer straighten completely. There would be a pale mark across the back of my wrist from a cord that had tightened when a branch split sooner than expected.

But by the time you found me, I would be working mostly near air.

Perhaps an illness had shortened my breath. It would have happened slowly enough that I could argue with it for a season. I would make excuses: cold water, poor sleep, a stone set too heavily on the belt. Then, one morning, I would leave a wedge in a cut because I needed to rise before the wood had opened.

Someone else would retrieve it.

I would be grateful, and unpleasant about it.

For a while I would choose tasks according to what they let me conceal. I could still inspect a window lip if I pretended to be checking its upper edge first. I could still carry short braces if I took a route through two extra houses. Eventually everybody would know the routes I was taking. Nobody would need to say why.

So I would move my cutting block into the little eastern room.

I would make window braces, tool toggles, the short smooth pegs used to hold a knot open while someone adjusted it. Things that could be shaped with my head above water and my elbows below.

The room would be noisy. Every knock of the blade against the block would travel down the trunk. The fish would arrive whether I had anything for them or not.

***

What I would like best, though, would be repairing the root loops beneath air chambers.

They would be awkward things to repair properly. A new loop had to be placed where a tired hand expected it, but not so close to the old growth that it would disappear into the wall. It needed to hold a person pulling hard without presenting a narrow trap for a wrist. Children wanted them low. Tall people wanted them higher. Someone carrying a bowl wanted something they could catch with two fingers.

I would have opinions about all of this.

Too many opinions, according to the woman who checked my air tube.

“You’ve made it comfortable for yourself,” she would say, trying a loop with her long foot. “Now make it usable.”

I would take that badly, then alter it after she had gone.

I would not be in charge of the town’s handholds. There would be older people who knew much more about them, and residents who did not want me changing anything near their doors. One household would insist on keeping a polished branch in a position I considered foolish. They would have used it safely for forty years. I would still dislike it.

But people would bring me small problems.

A loop grown tight around its opening. A brace that turned under the palm. A stair that had become difficult for a woman late in pregnancy. I would go down, test it, rise, and ask questions. Then I would sit in my room with a strip of young wood across my knees, considering where it wanted to bend.

This would suit me better than I admitted. I would still miss the long descents.

***

There would be a child who came to my room because the nearest adult in her own house kept finding work for her.

She would enter without announcing herself, push her wet hair back, and inspect whatever I was making.

“Who’s that for?”

“The western workshop.”

“They already have one.”

“This one won’t roll.”

She would take it from me and try to roll it.

I would give her the rejected pieces. At first she would make little animals, then devices of uncertain purpose, then a remarkably ugly fastening that worked better than mine. I would have to try it several times before acknowledging this.

She would be one of the children who held breath contests.

I would discover it not through wisdom but because she came up into my room frightened and tried to look bored. I would hand her a wrap. I would wait until her breathing settled. Then I would be angrier than she expected.

For several days afterward she would go elsewhere.

I would have a small piece of wood ready when she returned, and pretend I had only just picked it up.

***

I wouldn’t live alone exactly.

There would be someone whose larger room I ate in most evenings. We would have known one another for long enough that an invitation was unnecessary, but not long enough—or not in the right way—to combine our shelves.

She would do work on shore. Smoke would stay in the folds of her wrap. Sometimes she would bring me a warmed stone wrapped in bark, and I would keep it against my ribs while she described the wind above the lake.

I would ask about the valleys more often than she wanted to discuss them.

“Were the roads visible?”

“One road.”

“Anyone on it?”

“I was drying fiber.”

I might once have intended to leave. Not forever, necessarily. Just to walk somewhere without planning my next breath.

There would still be a small bundle on my highest shelf: a pair of sandals unsuitable for the mountain, a dry bag repaired twice, a wooden cup with a handle too large for use underwater. Every spring I would take it down to check for rot.

I would not call it a keepsake.

***

The place where I would feel most at home would be neither the shelf nor the cutting block, but the opening beneath my seat.

One foot would usually hang through it.

I could feel the cold change as evening came. I could feel a basket brush past, or the brief disturbance of someone rising too quickly. People who knew me would touch my ankle rather than knock. I would pull my foot aside and make room.

Sometimes a visitor would come up there by mistake.

I imagine myself noticing their hands first: how firmly they held the loop, how unwilling they were to let go even after their face was clear of the water. I would move the loose tools away. I would give them a wrap, though not my best one.

Then I would tell them where they were.

“The square is behind you. That window goes through to the next house, but don’t use it yet. When you go down, take the low brace on your left. You can reach this room again from there.”

If they wanted me to come with them, I would.

We would make a short crossing. I would keep within sight, offering an arm only when they looked for it. Across the lane, beneath another silver ceiling, I would watch their face emerge: startled, relieved, trying not to show either.

There would probably be someone already sitting there, eating.

We would be told to move our elbows. I would take the visitor’s basket while they found a place on the bench. Then I would point out the loop they would need on the way home, before the room grew crowded and we both forgot.
