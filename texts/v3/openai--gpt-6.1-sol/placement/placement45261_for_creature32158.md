At first I would know the house by its weight.

Not its roof, or the number of people sleeping inside it. Those things would reach me separately: the roof as a change in the warmth above my back, the people as movements that passed through the wood and ceased for long intervals. What I would know first was that a place which had always yielded now held firm.

A strand would pull tight between my fingers. Farther on, where I expected to raise my shoulders, I would find no room.

I would back away.

There would be another entrance. There had always been another entrance.

My country would be small enough for a person to cross without noticing it, and deep enough that I would spend much of my life finding parts of it again. Near its center, a length of dead wood would lie tilted above a pocket of water. One end would be soft and good to eat from. The other would carry a knock farther than any of the living stems around it.

I would sleep near that harder end.

Not in the wet pocket itself. Higher, in a chamber with a narrow floor and a ceiling that moved slightly when the grazers passed. I would have brought pale fibers there, one mouthful at a time. Some would still smell faintly of the person who had worn them in an ear.

I would be a root-hare, an old one, though I would have no particular thought of being old.

There would simply be passages I no longer took quickly. A place where I stopped before turning. One forefinger that did not close as far as the others, so that I used my wrist against a strand before pulling.

I would know which fibers could bear me without having to test them.

That would sometimes be a disadvantage.

***

Before the house came, the narrow way beneath it would have smelled of dry fungus and a female who crossed my ground irregularly. She would pause at the same fork each time, leaving her scent against a pale root. I would investigate long after she had gone.

The house would press that fork shut.

For two nights I would approach it from different sides. I would fit my head into spaces too narrow for my shoulders, withdraw, try lower down. Dust would collect in the wet groove of my nose. Once I would hear someone above shift their weight, and the pressure would reach me while my forefoot was still caught.

I would lie very still.

Then the person would move away. I would free the foot by opening my fingers rather than pulling.

There would be no anger in this, only a passage that had become difficult and a smell on the other side that I wanted to reach.

On the third night, water would help.

Rain would strike the leaves overhead, first in scattered hard impacts, then so thickly that I could feel little else. A trickle would enter the closed passage. It would hesitate against the compressed fibers and begin to spread sideways.

I would follow it.

Where the water disappeared, there would be a seam. I would put my nose into it, then one hand. The fibers would be soft enough to part. Behind them, a young runner would cross the opening, taut under the new weight.

I would bite through it.

The severed ends would spring apart. One would brush my cheek. Water would move past my wrist into the darkness ahead.

I would stay there feeding on the soft center of the runner while the rain continued. By the time I moved again, the seam would be wide enough to take my head.

***

The person above would eventually notice me.

Perhaps they would hear the paired knocking from beneath the sleeping ledge. Perhaps they would lose two ear plugs in three mornings and find the third pulled halfway through a gap in the floor.

Their hand would come down smelling of oil, warm skin, and the sharp sap of a plant I seldom ate.

I would retreat until my hind feet found a familiar brace.

The hand would withdraw.

Afterward there would be a trap.

I would inspect it from below, where its woven base rested across several stems. Inside would lie a piece of decayed wood, wet all the way through. Its smell would reach farther than the smell of the basket.

I would go around it several times.

I would reach between the strands and scrape a little of the wood loose.

That night I might enter. Old would not mean impossible to catch.

If I did, I would spend the remaining darkness searching the corners. The basket would offer grips everywhere and a way out nowhere. Near dawn I would begin pressing myself against its coolest side.

Then I would be lifted.

For a while my country would have no dependable direction. The grips would remain under my hands, but each movement would carry me without my choosing it. Damp cloth would touch the basket. I would draw air through it, rapidly at first.

When the opening was finally set against leaves, I would not rush toward the light.

I would turn, extend a hind foot, and wait until it found something that held.

***

The hollow where they left me would contain good chambers.

I would know this almost immediately. There would be moisture below, old fibers above, and enough small moving bodies in the rotten wood to feed on. Another root-hare would have passed through recently, but not slept there.

For a day I would stay.

I would make a shallow bed. I would eat. At intervals I would lower my jaw to a root and knock.

An answer would come from somewhere beyond the water.

I would wait, then knock again.

The answer would be unfamiliar in ways too slight for the person who carried me to hear. The strand would tremble differently. Between the knocks there would be a scrape, then the pressure of movement approaching.

I would leave before it arrived.

Finding my old ground would take several nights. I would not travel in a straight line. I would follow dampness into hollows, turn aside from occupied chambers, climb toward air that carried a trace of something known. At dusk I would rise through the leaves and remain with my hind feet anchored below.

There would be too much distance in the wind.

I would breathe it anyway.

On the last evening, I would catch the smell of the house: worked fiber, smoke caught in cloth, the particular dry warmth of its underside. Beneath that, much fainter, would be the smell of my own bed.

I would lower myself before the sky had quite darkened.

***

The new passage would still be there.

Water would have widened it in my absence. A few loose fibers would catch against my shoulders, but beyond them I would find the bitten runner and the firm edge of the foundation.

I would stop with one hand on the cut end.

Above me, someone would be working. Small pulls would travel through the floor as they drew a cord tight. A wooden needle would fall and strike the raft twice.

I would put my jaw against the root.

I would answer.

The work would stop.

For some time neither of us would move. Then the needle would touch the wood again, more softly.

I would wait with my fingers spread along the strand. Behind me, water would slip through the opening I had made. Ahead, under the quietest part of the house, the fibers of my bed would still hold their shape.
