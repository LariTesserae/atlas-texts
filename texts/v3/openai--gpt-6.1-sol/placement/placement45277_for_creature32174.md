I would first look for a place beneath the weighing-room bench.

Not under the grain racks. The floor there is too clean, and anything crossing it leaves an account of itself. Not in a bed cord, either. Bitter sap would reach me through my feet long before I touched it with my mouth.

Beneath the bench there is a shallow socket where an old fastening was removed. One edge is worn smooth by the clerk’s heel. The other remains rough enough to hold on to. In cold weather, the gray grazer stands nearby, and warmth travels down her legs into the paving.

I could live there for some time before anyone knew whether I belonged to the bench.

I would be small, but not one of the smallest things. Long enough to be picked up between finger and thumb. Too narrow to cast much shadow.

At rest, I would resemble a wooden pin.

That resemblance would be imperfect. My grain would run in the wrong direction near one end. In damp weather I would darken sooner than wood. Someone who had repaired many houses might notice that no tool had ever cut me.

But people have other work. There are pins everywhere.

I would have six feet, folded into two seams along my body. Opening them would make me slightly shorter. This would be the first thing about me that surprised a person: the peg contracts, acquires feet, and goes sideways.

My head would not be obvious until I used it.

For most purposes, I would not need to.

***

The bench tells me when the weighing room opens.

There is a pressure through its front legs, followed by a small sideways drag. The clerk moves it every morning, though he believes he leaves it in the same place. I brace against the socket while the paving passes beneath me.

Then come the weights.

I know the large one by the way it settles. The smallest gives two touches: once when it is put down, once when the clerk adjusts it with his nail.

The gray animal arrives on some mornings. Her warmth comes first. Later, the bench receives the slow alternation of her breathing through the clerk’s knees.

There are movements I cannot distinguish. A full basket and an empty basket carried by a tired person may feel alike. Two people walking together can briefly become one large animal, then separate at the threshold. I wait until their feet have passed before going out.

I eat what gathers where a fastening has worked loose: softened wood, a pale growth beneath it, sometimes the cast skins of smaller creatures. It is enough. I do not need to enter a grain jar.

This does not make me welcome.

Once, the storekeeper finds me on the underside of a measuring scoop.

She turns it over. Light presses against the narrow uncovered places between my plates. I close my feet.

For a while she holds me above her little book.

Then she puts me into an empty dish and covers it.

Inside, the clay is smooth. I go around it seven times, testing the same slight roughness on each circuit. There is no way up.

In the evening Senn uncovers the dish. He touches my back with the fork from his collar.

“Not that one,” he says.

The storekeeper asks whether he is certain.

He is not certain. He takes me anyway.

***

Senn’s house has a different arrangement of cold.

There is warmth near the buried embers and a strip of draught beneath the curtain. His baskets have places where strands press firmly together, and places where a strand can be moved with the weight of my body. I prefer the firm places. Something may eat the other ones while I am standing on them.

He puts me into a basket that needs repair.

I close around a broken end. For a little while this is useful to him. The two lengths remain aligned while he passes new fiber between them.

Then I open my feet.

He stops working.

I remain still.

He resumes, leaving that part until last.

This happens again on another night. By the third time, he no longer tries to draw the repaired weave tightly over me. He works one way around, and I travel the other, until we meet beside the handle.

Sometimes he brings the basket against his cheek. I retreat from the warmth of his skin, but not far. His pulse comes through the strands in faint, paired movements.

There are other creatures here. One has a soft body and pushes ahead without testing a surface. One leaves a taste that keeps me from a particular strand for several days. Senn removes both when he finds them.

He usually lets me stay.

I learn the pressure of his bone fork. When it comes beneath me, I release my feet before it has to lift.

***

I would not remain entirely in that house.

There would be nights when I crossed the lintel and went along the outer wall, keeping to the fitted edges of the slabs. A wall offers many entrances, but some narrow inward. Some are occupied. Some are colder than the exposed paving, even at noon.

I would find the new seam in the lookout stair.

Not the whole split. Only its lower reach, where darkness has opened beneath a lip of stone.

On one side, the rock holds the day’s warmth. On the other, it has already cooled. I stretch across, then withdraw my front feet. The distance is small enough to cross, but I cannot find a place where both surfaces sit easily beneath me.

I go down into it.

There is no softened wood here. Nothing to eat. A few pale bodies cling farther in, beyond my reach.

After a time I climb out on the opposite side.

The next person who takes the longer step sends a tremor down the seam. It arrives unevenly through my feet. I hold on until it has passed.

I would return there sometimes, though less often as the edges weathered.

***

At dusk, I leave whatever fastening or basket has held me through the day.

There is a change I feel before the other bodies arrive. It reaches me through the stone, and I begin moving toward the square.

The way is not always clear. A door may be closed across yesterday’s route. A grazer may lower herself between two courts. Once, a child sets a cup over me and leaves it there until someone calls him indoors.

I spend that dusk circling the cup.

Afterward I remain beneath it through the cold. When it is lifted the next morning, I am slow to unfold. The child touches me with a stem, then waits. At last I catch the stem and climb along it onto the wall.

The following evening, I reach the square early.

I take a place near the closed fissure. There are bodies beneath me and bodies arriving over my back. I lift one foot, allow something narrow to pass, set it down again.

When the moisture comes, I open the soft seam along my underside and lower myself against the stone.

For those few minutes I cannot hold tightly to anything. My feet are spread. A larger creature could dislodge me without noticing.

Something does.

I turn onto my side between two warm bodies. There is moisture beneath me still, but I cannot reach it. I work one foot against the rock until it catches a green vein, and pull myself over.

By the time I am upright, the surface has begun to dry.

I stay until the bodies around me loosen. Then I leave by a different route, over the folded limb of a grazer, down to the eastern steps.

***

One winter, I lose a foot.

Senn finds me in a basket, holding with the remaining five. He lifts me out and lays me on a cloth. His fork touches the empty place once.

For several nights I stay in a shallow wooden box near his knee. He puts fragments of old basket strand inside it. One is soft enough to eat.

When I begin climbing the sides, he carries the box to the doorway and tips it toward the wall.

I go back into the house.

Later, I go out.

The missing foot changes which gaps I can cross. I cease using the underside of one lintel. On the weighing-room bench, I turn around in the socket before settling, so that the rough edge is beneath the feet I still have.

No one gives me a name that I know.

Senn may call me something while he works. His daughters may use another word when they find me among the strands. To the clerk I may still be a fastening he has never needed to replace.

I would live between those accounts.

On a cold morning, I would be beneath the bench while the gray grazer warmed the clerk’s hands. At dusk I would cross the paving with the others. And on some nights, after the square had dried, I would return to a half-repaired basket beside Senn’s knee.

He would turn it carefully, feeling for a broken strand.

I would hold on.
