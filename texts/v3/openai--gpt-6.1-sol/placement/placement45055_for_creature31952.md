I think I would begin in the cupboard.

Not as something waiting to be planted. I would be one of the pieces of sacking folded behind the good pieces: too narrow to line a basket, with a seam that had pulled sideways and stiffened around an old stain. When Mara opened the cupboard, the door would press against her hip. She would take something from the front. I would stay where I was.

There would be very little to know there. The pulping machine through two walls. Damp entering the cupboard each time it opened, then slowly leaving. Once, the warmth of a clean towel laid on top of me.

I might remain there for years.

Then a channel would begin throwing water past its own corner.

Not a serious fault. A bright little jump, caused by mineral buildup narrowing the bend. Most of the water would still reach the bed. The rest would strike a path plate and keep one patch slick enough that Mara would feel it before she looked down.

She would scrape the corner. For a few days that would work.

When it happened again, she would bring me out.

***

I would be folded twice and pushed beneath the channel’s lip, with one end hanging into the bed below. Water would enter me instead of jumping. The first wetting would loosen the old seam. A little brown would wash out.

Mara would hold me in place with a bent strip of copper.

“Until Thursday,” she would say.

It would be Monday.

Here I would have much more to know.

Water would arrive in pulses. There would be a thin, steady supply that cooled my upper threads, and then, when a pump changed speed, a heavier passage that made the loose end tremble against the ceramic rib below. Air could enter through my sides. Sediment would gather where I bent.

I would not be a sensor. Nothing would receive a reading from me. But a dry interval would change my whole shape. I would draw tight against the copper, then relax when the water returned.

The first root would come from below.

It would touch the hanging end, turn away, and return along a different thread. Fine hairs would spread through the weave. The root would be pale where it lay inside me and faintly brown where it crossed the open air.

On Thursday, Mara would lift the copper strip.

She would feel resistance, stop, and put it back.

There would be a note in the service book. I would not know what it said.

***

At first I imagine myself as that strip of cloth, with a clear boundary around it. Then the boundary becomes difficult to keep.

Mineral grains settle among the fibers. A green film begins on the shaded side. The seam, which had made me useless in the cupboard, holds a shallow pocket of water after the rest has drained.

Something small moves there.

Not the frog yet. Something with a dark body and legs too fine to disturb more than a single thread. It comes to the edge of the pocket, pauses, and lowers itself.

I would be partly made of what I held.

The original fibers would soften. Roots would take some of their strain. Where a thread broke, the green growth would remain stretched across the gap, and the water would continue along it.

Mara would still be able to lift me, but not without bringing other things.

She would try once, during a thorough clearing of the bed. Two fingers under my lower edge. A careful pull.

The root above would tighten. Water would spill sideways.

She would let go and clear the channel with the blunt end of her knife instead.

Some days she would look at me while standing up. On other days she would crouch. The difference would matter, though I would have no way to anticipate it.

***

I would find myself near the western path, low enough that a broad leaf hid the southern rail. The clouds would be unavailable to me. So would most of the garden.

My weather would come from a channel no wider than her finger.

Rain would make it colder. The fermentation line would sometimes bring a faint sourness through the bed. When the western bank cracked, a few pale grains would fall onto my upper fold.

Mara’s bare foot would land nearby every morning. Her heel would press water through a hole in the plate. Some of it would reach me by a shallow groove along the edge.

I would become accustomed to that brief extra wetting.

During her absences, the other caretaker would wear boots and step in the center of the path. The water would still arrive from above. There would be no shortage. Only that small morning addition would cease.

Then it would return.

***

The frog might use me once.

I do not think I would become its home. There would be too little water, and the pump cycle would keep changing the depth.

But one hot afternoon it would climb out of the pool and settle beneath my hanging end. Its back would touch the damp weave. One hind foot would remain on the ceramic, the toes spread.

Mara would come down the path looking for it.

She would stop beside me.

For a while, neither of them would move. Then she would step over the slick plate rather than onto it and continue toward the service house.

By evening the frog would be gone. A little mud would remain against the seam.

***

Eventually there would be very little sacking left.

I would be a short, flexible crossing between a manufactured channel and a bed that had risen higher than its planned level. Moss above, roots below, a few surviving threads inside. Water would pass through me, and along me, and sometimes around me.

The copper strip would still be there. Mara would straighten it when it began pinching a stem.

I would not need to become larger. There would be room enough for the shallow pocket, the hanging end, the place where a root could turn without drying.

When the new stone lip grew outward, its shade would reach me an hour earlier in the afternoon.

Mara would move the path plate to protect the lip. In its new position, the plate would leave a narrow gap beside my lower edge.

The next morning, water from her heel would enter that gap and disappear beneath me.

A broken thread would lift in the current.

Then settle.
