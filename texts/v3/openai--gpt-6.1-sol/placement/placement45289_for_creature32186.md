I would be below the leaves, where the long views disappear.

The summits would reach me as changes in light. A cloud crossing one would dim the water beneath my chin; its departure would make the surface bright again. I would know the cliff first by the pull of the current, long before I came close enough to hear anything falling.

Around me, the country would be roots, banks, openings. A place where my shoulders fit. A place where they do not. The underside of a stone, dark and almost dry, with the smell of something edible caught behind it.

I think I would be one of the young.

Not the one already halfway into every gap, and not the smallest, with its pale forefoot and its tired, unreasonable teeth. I would be the broad-headed one who remains at the entrance after the others have gone out, listening to them move through the fern stalks.

There would be no name for this hesitation. I would simply stand with my weight back until the next thing became clear.

The shelter would still be large to me. Its roof would have close places where my fur touched the roots, but I could turn around inside it without waking anyone if I was careful. I would know which pieces of bedding crackled and which had softened. Beneath my cheek, a strip of bark would retain the warmth of another body after that body had left.

Outside, everything would require more of my feet.

My mother could cross a wet branch without appearing to consider it. I would stop where the bark changed color. My forepaws would close around it, and I would feel the loose outer layer shift over sound wood. Sometimes I would back away from something that held perfectly well. Sometimes, after watching a brother cross, I would go quickly and discover that his weight and mine were not the same.

Once I would fall between two roots.

Not far. Far enough that water would close over my eyes before I had arranged myself to swim.

For an instant there would be bubbles caught in my fur and the hard pressure of a submerged branch against my ribs. My hindfeet would open. I would push upward in the wrong direction, strike the underside of the floating mat, then find the break through which I had fallen.

My mother would be there. Whether she pulled me out or I climbed over her forefoot, I would not retain as separate events.

Afterward I would smell the water in my own nose. I would lick my chest, though there was nothing on it that licking could put right. The others would already be farther along the bank.

The next night I would stop at that opening.

And the night after.

Eventually I would step over it without stopping, placing one foot on a small exposed knob of root that I had not noticed before.

That is where I would become most myself: along a route slowly acquiring detail.

A forked stick lodged against a stone. A low branch that left a line of water across my back. The cool thread entering a warm channel, narrow enough that I could stand with my left feet in one temperature and my right feet in another. I would follow my mother closely there. If she paused, my nose would press into the fur behind her leg.

Food would interrupt the lessons.

A beetle would struggle against a leaf, and all my caution would vanish. I would land with both forepaws in the water and come up holding nothing. Then something would scrape the bare pad of one foot. I would feel beneath the leaf and find it again.

For a while, that beetle would be the entire basin.

Later, with its bitter taste still in my mouth, I would try to catch the small disturbances made by falling drops. My brother would join me. We would become absorbed in an activity whose rewards had already ended.

Our mother would let us continue until she moved on.

I would sleep hard, but not through everything. Her rising would wake me. So would the sudden absence of her flank against mine. Rain could strike the whole roof of the shelter and leave me asleep; one unfamiliar scrape near the water entrance would lift my head.

As I grew, I would begin to wait less often.

One evening, at the channel that had warmed, my mother would turn upstream and I would turn with her before touching the water. The smell would have changed. Or the vapor would meet my whiskers differently. I would not know which part had warned me.

She would cross first. I would follow without crowding her.

Far above us, the last sunlight would still be on the fern crowns. Down at the bank there would be enough light to find the roots, and beneath the roots, moving through the cooler water, something small to eat.
