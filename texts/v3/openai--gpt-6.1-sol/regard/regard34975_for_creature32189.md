Sella looks east.

The cloud she had expected is there, caught below a shoulder of new stone. It will probably miss the city. There is a wind above it moving the wrong way.

“No,” she calls across the courtyard. “Not unless it climbs.”

Beside her, the copper plant makes a small sound.

One of its leaves has touched the railing. She moves it back. Then she notices a person standing at the far end of the roof, where there is no stair.

The visitor wears ordinary shoes.

“Don’t stand there,” Sella says.

The person steps onto the strip she indicates.

Later, this is the first thing she will remember: being listened to.

***

The visitor has come because of the second knocks.

Not because the city is ugly, or unnatural, or insufficiently green. Not because its people have failed to understand something that would be obvious from a greater height.

It has noticed the interval in which a hand rests on a brake, a pan is lifted, a comb stops moving.

It has noticed how much of a life can be spent waiting for permission to resume it.

“What are you?” Sella asks.

The visitor answers plainly enough that she sits down.

Her granddaughter remains standing.

“Can you make a room?” the child asks.

“Yes.”

“With a window?”

“Yes.”

Sella puts a hand on the child’s sleeve.

The visitor does not seem offended.

“What happens underneath it?” Sella asks.

“That would depend on where we put it.”

“Then don’t put it anywhere yet.”

***

They go first to the shutters.

Sella brings her instrument. The visitor bends beside her and waits while she clears a lip with the folded cloth.

If it knows everything about the condenser, it does not make her knowledge useless by saying so. It asks which shutter is most troublesome.

“Eleven.”

“Because of the hinge?”

“Because the hinge is easy. You mend that and think you’ve finished.”

She shows it the deposit forming beneath the collecting edge. She shows it where water hangs instead of running, and where, during a good fog, that small hesitation becomes a loss large enough to be argued about downstairs.

The visitor touches the metal.

The water moves.

Sella watches closely.

“Will it still come off for cleaning?”

“Yes.”

“Will you do the other forty-seven?”

“If you want.”

She wants.

At the opened housing, the gray bird lands and strikes once with its beak.

The visitor waits for it to finish.

***

By morning, three things have happened.

The shutters collect a little more water.

The hot patch outside the eastern nursery has cooled.

And beneath the northern works, where two growing masses of stone have been pressing toward the same narrow gallery, a space has opened between them.

No one hears the opening. No cups shift. Nothing is forced upward into the houses.

The space is deep and unoccupied. The displaced heat goes farther down, into rock that has never been quarried. The pressure gauges settle by amounts small enough that the night foreman suspects a fault in the instruments.

The visitor tells the foreman what it has done.

He demands to see.

It takes him down.

He returns pale, irritated, and carrying six pages of measurements.

This is useful. He is not a man whose report can easily be dismissed as an observance.

***

There is a meeting.

It is not held on Sella’s roof. She insists on this.

People arrive with questions, plans, grievances, and one proposal to double the output of the pressure houses immediately.

“No,” says the visitor.

It is the first refusal anyone has heard.

The proposal’s author asks why.

“Because I have made room for the pressure that was already there. I have not made another city beneath this one to receive whatever you decide to put into it.”

This answer causes less satisfaction than the visitor might have hoped.

A water-line mechanic asks whether the mountain can be stopped altogether.

“It can.”

“And should it be?”

“Not without understanding what you would lose.”

There are pipes laid for districts not yet built. There are livelihoods in the works. There are old foundations held sound by the very pressure that is filling their lower rooms.

There are also people who would gladly lose all three for a kitchen that stayed level.

The visitor does not ask for unanimity before making the kitchens safe.

But it waits before changing the future shape of the mountain.

***

Its largest gift is difficult to celebrate.

It gives warning.

Not a prophecy written in a locked room, or a noise that an exhausted worker might miss. The dangerous movements become visible in the stone well before they can reach a house.

A pale seam appears. It advances slowly enough to measure. It stays cool.

Where a pressure surge would once have travelled blindly through the mountain, it now pauses in one of the deep spaces the visitor has made. Valves can be closed. Lifts can be emptied. People can go downstairs for the person who cannot climb without help.

The warnings work in wealthy districts and poor ones. They do not depend on a subscription, a landlord’s permission, or the condition of someone’s shoes.

The surveyors spend a week testing them.

Then they begin painting instructions beside the seams.

For some time, everyone is more frightened rather than less. They can see how often danger used to pass beneath them.

Gradually, a knock becomes something other than a verdict.

***

Water takes longer.

The visitor could place a full vessel on every table each morning. Several people suggest this. One asks whether the vessel could contain broth.

Instead, it asks to see the district tanks, the ration books, the sealed northern reservoir, and the houses whose taps have been dry despite those books.

It brings water up from below the inhabited mountain, through a passage isolated from the mineral slurry. The water arrives clean and cool. The passage can be inspected. Its mouth opens into a public cistern.

There is enough for drinking, washing, and a modest surplus.

The visitor is very particular about the surplus.

It is not sold in advance to the owners of the unfinished shelf.

Nor is its arrival allowed to become an excuse for taking away the old condensation rights. Sella’s shutters continue to fill the district tank.

Some people call this unnecessarily cautious.

Sella does not.

***

The plant comes from the valleys.

This matters to the granddaughter, who wants to go down and choose it.

She picks a small herb with uneven leaves and a smell that remains on her fingers after she has let go. The grower explains the soil, the light, and the signs of too much water. The child listens with the strained attention she ordinarily reserves for adjustment screws.

They bring it home in a covered pot.

The visitor has not made it immortal. It can wilt. It can be neglected. It can grow badly toward a window and require turning.

But the water it needs no longer has to be taken from somebody else’s mouth.

Sella puts the copper plant beside it.

For a while the child objects. Then she finds that the repaired leaf makes a useful support for a stem that will not stand straight.

***

The visitor remains through several fogs.

There are further repairs. A cooling shaft beneath the produce hall. A safe landing on a food lift. Soles for those whose shoes have worn through, made to patterns the shoemakers can copy.

There are things it does not repair.

The grandmother’s table is still too wide for the stair. An argument over onions survives its presence entirely. Sella still dislikes people who discuss a poor catch without having got out of bed.

When it leaves, the city is still on the mountain.

The pumps continue. New stone presses against old foundations. Surveyors make their morning rounds, now with different marks to look for.

That evening Sella carries two stools upstairs.

Her granddaughter brings both plants, making two journeys because she has been told not to carry so much that she cannot see her feet.

Across the courtyard, someone asks whether tomorrow looks like fog.

Sella gets up and looks east.

“Perhaps,” she says.

Behind her, the child is rubbing a leaf between finger and thumb.

The roof is cool enough for bare feet, though neither of them quite trusts it yet.
