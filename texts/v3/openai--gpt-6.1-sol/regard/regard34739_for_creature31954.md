The power first noticed the woman because she was listening to something it could not hear.

She had one hand against the warm wall and the other resting beside the mouth of a root. At the far end, a child was tapping with the handle of a spoon.

The taps reached her unevenly. She frowned, asked for them again, and shifted her hand.

Beyond the basin, the wind was lifting a field. The power could feel every strained root, every small body pressing deeper into the green. It could hear water entering a fissure miles below the houses.

But the woman was listening for the difference between a blockage and a split.

It drew nearer.

***

There were many things it could have changed.

It could have stopped the wind. It could have raised walls around the basins, laid deep soil across the stone, brought rain in slow, dependable curtains.

It stayed through a still interval and watched the pale moths emerge. It watched the moisture-gathering creatures open their folds beneath the leaves. When the wind returned, seeds crossed the bare country and lodged in hollows where no person had carried them.

Under the woman’s house, warmth moved through a network of cracks so intricate that closing one might dry a root, flood a chamber, or send water into a sleeping pit.

The power did not mistake its first ideas for wisdom.

For several days it followed the woman at her work.

She had a sore place at the base of her thumb. Pulling cord aggravated it. She changed hands often, though she was less skillful with the other.

She was fond of one child and impatient with another. The second child asked questions while she was counting knots. Later, when nobody was watching, she repaired the frayed fastening on that child’s coat.

She sometimes forgot to eat.

The seam-runner beneath her basket was old. One hind foot no longer turned freely. When it went out, it took a longer passage to avoid a steep root. The woman had noticed. She had placed a piece of flat bark over the difficult part, weighting its ends with stones.

The power looked carefully at the bark.

***

On the fourth evening, she found a root that had split beneath a binding.

She could reach the near side. The far side lay behind a roof anchor, in a narrow space where her arm would not turn. She tried twice, then sat back and held her painful hand against her chest.

Through the broken sound path came a faint breath.

She leaned down.

“Who is there?”

The power had no familiar voice. It borrowed neither the child’s nor anyone else’s.

“Someone who can reach the other side.”

She remained very still.

Then she took up her lamp and inspected the opening.

“Can you hold it while I bind?”

“Yes.”

“Do not pull. There is a smaller root underneath.”

“I see it.”

She considered this.

“Hold, then.”

The split edges came together. They did not mend. The wood remained old wood, with a weakness she would need to remember.

She passed her cord around it, tightened the turns, and worked a thin strip of wet fiber into place.

“Enough,” she said.

The power let go.

At the other end, someone tapped three times.

All three reached her clearly.

***

She did not immediately ask what it was.

First she asked whether it had disturbed the smaller root.

Then whether it would frighten the animals.

Then, after putting away her tools:

“Were you here yesterday?”

“Yes.”

“That was private.”

“I will wait outside when you wish.”

She placed her palm over the opening. For a while the power heard only the wind.

When she uncovered it, she said, “You may come tomorrow.”

***

The great wind arrived six days later.

The woman knew before dawn that crossings would be difficult. By midday, nobody was crossing.

In a basin beyond hers, a tended field began to lift.

The first movement was ordinary. Its western edge rose and fell. People crawled out with weights and wet fiber.

Then a buried root broke.

The power felt the break travel through the field. Beneath it were two seam-runner chambers, a passage full of pale moths, and a child who had crawled farther than the adults knew.

It held the field down.

Only that field. Only against the force that would have torn it loose.

The people felt the change through their hands. The vegetation was still thrashing, but its underside no longer rose beneath them.

An adult reached the child and pulled her back. Another opened a way into the animal chambers. The mother seam-runner came out biting, with young clinging to her sides.

The people worked until darkness.

The power held until the last fastening was sound.

Afterward, the woman spoke into the root.

“Was that you?”

“Yes.”

“Will it happen again?”

“The wind will.”

She rubbed her thumb slowly.

“And you?”

“If I can help, I will.”

She was quiet for some time.

“We must still teach them to test the ground.”

“Yes.”

***

News traveled badly, as news always had there.

In one basin, people said the stone had begun to remember its inhabitants. In another, they said a stranger lived in the old woman’s wall.

She corrected neither account unless someone asked her directly.

When they did, she said, “It listens. It can hold things. You must tell it when to stop.”

Some households wanted no part of it.

The power kept away from their rooms. It did not spoil their water, neglect their fields, or make refusal expensive.

Others brought requests.

A missing traveler. A well gone suddenly dry. A roof whose living anchors had begun to rot.

It searched for the traveler and showed the searchers where he had taken shelter.

At the well, it found water but also a chamber of blind creatures. It showed the cleaners a passage they could widen without emptying the chamber. They argued, inspected it, and chose where to work.

At the roof, it held the slabs while new vines were threaded through.

It learned the length of a person’s working day.

***

One evening the woman asked for something for herself.

Her hand had become worse. She could still sweep a root, but fastening the cord took a long time, and afterward she sometimes sat without moving until the pain diminished.

“Can you ease this?”

The power looked through the skin, the worn joint, the small inflamed places.

“Yes.”

“Not so much that I fail to notice when I strain it.”

It eased the swelling. It left her the ordinary warnings of fatigue.

She opened and closed her hand.

Then she cried a little, unexpectedly, and became irritated when it asked whether it had hurt her.

“No. Leave me a moment.”

It waited outside.

***

In time, there were changes on the plateau.

More travelers reached their destinations. Fewer roofs failed while people slept beneath them. Some torn fields survived that would otherwise have gone over the edge.

There were still losses. People still brought bad news by crawling all the way into a room.

The woman taught two others to keep the sound paths clear. The impatient child became particularly good at finding splits.

The power often worked beside them, holding what their hands could not reach.

And sometimes it did nothing.

It stayed near the threshold while the woman ate, or listened as she tried to pronounce the seam-runner’s name for it.

She was never satisfied with its attempt.

The animal, hearing them, came out to smell her cuffs.
