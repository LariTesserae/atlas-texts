The power does not quiet the basin.

It notices the noise first, as a visitor would, and then notices that the animals are not enduring what the visitor endures. Beneath each grey body, the great roar separates into distances, directions, familiar differences. To silence it would be to take away the ground.

Nor does it cool the stone. The heat that makes an exposed crossing difficult also brings food to the wet seam. The bitter water belongs here. So does the hard white crust.

For a long time, the power changes nothing.

It attends to one shelf where an old animal rests beside a younger one. Their feet hold different portions of the same damp layer. Between them, signals pass so faintly that even the stone scarcely distinguishes them from its own activity.

The power learns to distinguish them.

It learns the adjustment that means only a pressure change, and the adjustment made because another animal has moved. It learns the difference between a body settled firmly over a meal and a body whose feet remain spread because drawing them inward would break the last useful contact.

Stillness alone tells it very little.

Under another shelf, a young animal has begun to leave its parent.

Two front feet stand outside the shelter. The other four remain beneath it. Ahead lies a narrow stretch of limestone, pale and apparently smooth. Beyond that is an empty feeding oval.

There is moisture along most of the route. In one place, however, a mineral ridge interrupts it. The interruption is shorter than the young animal’s body.

Its forward foot reaches the ridge and curls.

It waits, then puts the foot down again.

The parent shifts behind it. There is not much room left in the inhabited oval. The young animal’s mouth works against an edge already being worked from the other side.

The power could carry it across. It could put food beneath it, widen the shelf, or make the dry stone wet.

Instead, it looks under the ridge.

There is brine there, held in a closed pore scarcely deeper than a footpad is thick. A vein of dense mineral separates it from the surface. The vein has no part in the shelf’s load, no part in the electrical connection between its occupants. Opening it will change a little of the crust’s growth. The power follows that change outward, through the seam and down into the passages, until it understands where it will stop.

Then it opens a passage too small to admit a grain of gravel.

The surface does not become a pool. A darker line appears along the ridge, then remains.

The young animal keeps its foot tucked for a while.

When it extends the pad again, the margin spreads instead of curling. Its middle feet come forward. The rear pair release their hold beneath the parent.

It crosses over several hours.

On the far side, it reaches the empty oval and places its feet badly. One pad rests on an old mineral scallop. Another has no firm contact at all.

The power leaves it alone.

The animal changes its stance. The pale lip lowers. By the time all six feet have found purchase, the power has withdrawn from the rock beneath it.

***

Elsewhere, it finds crossings that should remain difficult.

Some lead to seams too thin to nourish another occupant. Some join patches whose separation keeps a harmful mineral growth from passing between them. Some dry interruptions allow animals on either side to distinguish a neighbor’s electrical changes from the accumulated signals of the whole shelf.

The power does not make a continuous damp road across the basin.

It works in a few places, and waits between them.

A shallow recess becomes accessible beside an overcrowded feeding patch. A sealed pore opens where a foot can reach it. Under one mineral lip, a small change in drainage keeps the active crust from being buried beneath its own deposit.

These are modest alterations in appearance. Their consequences are not modest to the animals that find them. The power attends to those consequences as carefully as it attended to the original ground.

Once, it closes a passage it has opened. The extra moisture has carried a neighbor’s signal into a resting place where it arrives too strongly. The animal there keeps lifting and replacing its middle feet.

After the passage closes, it settles.

The power remembers that error.

***

It does not take the old animals apart from their shelves.

There are some whose stillness contains no evident distress: feet responsive, mouth working, contact with a neighbor maintained. There are others whose stiffening bodies pull against pads that can no longer find a comfortable position.

For one of these, the power loosens a small accumulation in the crease above a rear foot.

The foot moves.

It moves farther than it has in a long time, then spreads against a damp hollow slightly to one side. The body follows by less than the width of its lip.

There it rests.

The power remains near enough to know whether the change helped. It offers no new body, no obligation to begin travelling again.

***

From standing height, almost nothing has happened.

The basin still offers heat, glare, and its continuous, machinery-like roar. The far rim remains low in the haze. No green fringe appears along the channels.

Under a black shelf, a parent feeds alone for the first time in many days.

A little farther along, its offspring occupies an old oval. The young animal’s back has not yet darkened to match the stone. Its feet are widely spread, and one of them keeps testing the boundary of its new place.

When the parent releases a pad, the change reaches it through the wet seam.

The young animal lifts a middle foot and puts it down again.

The power feels that small exchange. It does not add a signal of its own.
