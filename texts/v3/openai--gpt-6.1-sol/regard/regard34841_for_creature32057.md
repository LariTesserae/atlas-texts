The power first noticed the second parcel.

There were larger things to notice: the living country, the districts folded into one another, the water passing through roots older than the names of rivers. But Iven had opened both parcels, although he knew his wife packed the same food in each, and then carefully closed the one he was saving.

The power remained beside him for a while.

It did not occupy the other chair. There was very little room in the watch office, and the other chair held a coat drying over its back. It did not put a voice into the knocking. Iven had spent eleven years learning which sounds required him to go out into the cold. Adding another would have been an unkindness.

Instead, it listened.

It listened through the last stroke, through the settling of the factory walls, through the small sound Iven made when he straightened his knee beneath the desk. It followed the pressure downward.

There was no single chamber at the bottom.

There were chambers that had once been at the bottom, and older ones below them, and places where a chamber had become a thickness in a wall. Water moved through this accumulated country by routes that would have been impossible to draw on one sheet. Some were healthy. Others had been held open so long that the work of holding them open had become part of their substance.

Far below the cutting floor, a narrow vessel bent around a buried shaft.

The vessel was not blocked. Each inspection would have found water passing through it. But when the pumps above stopped, its walls contracted, and the pressure caught behind the bend moved in short, heavy pulses. Those pulses reached the old press through the shaft’s living sheath.

There was something else there, too.

The power waited longer.

The thing below the shaft did not think in words. Nor was it merely water or wood. It had the persistence of a creature that had once found a tolerable position and then been unable to leave it. Over the centuries, buildings had grown around its resting place. Their drinking vessels had crossed it. Their waste heat had warmed one side and left the other cold. It had accommodated all of them.

Now one of its slow movements tightened the bend.

The knocking was not an appeal. It had never known there was anyone to appeal to.

The power could have lifted the entire factory. It could have separated every vessel, set the districts at fixed distances, made the deep passages open directly onto the places people meant to reach.

Before doing anything, it followed those passages.

One led beneath a school. One held the roots of six cisterns. One passed close to a room where an old man was asleep beside a woman who had arrived with an added street seventeen years earlier. Beneath the pumping station were several warm cavities whose occupants withdrew when attention touched them.

The power withdrew its attention.

Then it looked for an approach from the factory yard.

***

Three mornings later, Iven found a bead of water on the inside of the inspection cover beside the press.

He wiped it away. It returned.

The cover had been dry for as long as he remembered. He wrote this down in the space usually reserved for faults, and the day watch called a water inspector.

She came with a young assistant and expected a split drinking vessel. They opened the cover. Behind it was a small recess, empty except for a ridge of fresh tissue along the lower edge.

The inspector touched the ridge with the back of one finger.

“Get the lamp nearer.”

The assistant did.

There was an old access seam beneath it, nearly grown shut. It appeared on no current drawing. A search through the factory books found it on a plan drawn before the upper cutting floor had been built.

The plan called it a relief passage.

That was enough to begin with.

Opening it took nine days. The crew worked slowly because the tissue around it was wet and because, on the second afternoon, something moved beyond the wall.

They put down their tools.

The movement stopped.

The power waited with them. It held nothing still by force. Where the crew had cut too close to a vessel, it gave the injured edge enough strength to close. When the thing below shifted, it allowed the newly opened passage to yield rather than split.

The crew leader noticed this yielding.

“Leave that side,” she said. “It’s making room.”

They left it.

At the far end they found a cavity long enough for three people to stand in, though only one could stand upright. A pale growth passed through its floor and wall. It had no face, no useful resemblance to an animal, and no clear boundary beyond which it could be said to end.

The assistant raised his lamp.

“Is that—”

“Lower it,” said the crew leader.

They stood in the dimness for a minute. Moisture gathered on the wall at the height of their mouths.

Then she sent for a building physician.

***

The physician did not diagnose the growth.

She examined the passage, took water samples, and asked for the pumping records. She had the crew place listening rods above and below the old shaft. Two of the rods received the same pulse at different hours.

For a week, there were disagreements about what that meant.

The power did not settle them. It kept the weakened passage from failing while the people worked.

Eventually the physician proposed a trial. Water would be diverted through the reopened relief passage for two hours before dawn. Nothing would be cut from the growth. No attempt would be made to wake it.

The superintendent objected to altering the pumping schedule.

The water inspector showed him how much pressure was being lost at the bend.

He agreed to three mornings.

On the first, the press knocked seven times.

On the second, it knocked twice.

On the third, Iven stood beside the wall until daylight and heard only a drop falling inside the inspection cover.

He entered the hour at which he had begun waiting. In the next column he wrote the weather.

Then, after some thought, he added:

*No knocking.*

***

The growth took most of a season to move.

It moved by less than the width of Iven’s hand. In doing so it loosened the bend, opened two smaller vessels, and made a low place in the relief cavity where water could remain without being drawn immediately into the pumps.

The building physician insisted that the cavity stay closed to ordinary traffic. The crew put a door across the entrance and fastened a warning to it.

There was no mention of a sleeper on the warning. It concerned water pressure, restricted access, and the danger of striking the lower wall.

People called it the sleeper’s door nevertheless.

The power stayed until the new route had survived both a dry spell and a week of cloud. It asked nothing of the growth. There was no need to make it understand gratitude, or the factory, or the names of the people who had worked above it.

Once, as water entered the low place, the growth ceased its slight, constant bracing.

The power let it rest.

***

Iven’s knee still hurt.

This was not something the power had overlooked. The damaged joint could be eased, but the watchman was also spending his nights crossing cold ground, and there were eleven other employees at the factory with injuries that worsened in winter. The power attended to those things as well.

A sound piece of timber was found when the relief passage was cleared. The factory carpenter used it to repair the bench at the far end of the yard. A narrow run of waste heat, formerly vented through a broken hood, was led beneath the watch office floor. The office dried out. Iven no longer had to put his boots beside the stove halfway through the night.

The doctor noticed that he was walking better.

“Keep doing whatever you’re doing,” she said.

“Mostly sitting down,” Iven told her.

She wrote something on his card.

There were improvements elsewhere, though not enough for anyone to declare the city improved. A cistern wall that had repeatedly failed was found to be receiving hot waste from a workshop. The waste was redirected. A housing clerk, comparing two sets of maintenance reports, realized that families in closing rooms were applying for transfers years before their applications were being treated as urgent.

That regulation was changed after a bitter meeting.

The first family moved under it complained that their new apartment was too far from the tram. It was. A stop was added the following spring.

The power helped where it could without making the living country grow faster than it could bear. It did not bring rain to a plateau whose vessels had learned to hold mist. It did not fill the plazas with trees. It made no lost person appear at a kitchen door.

It spent a long time beneath one stairway, keeping a passage open while two surveyors found their way back.

They emerged hungry, angry with each other, and carrying measurements that did not agree. Both went home.

***

One morning, months after the knocking ceased, Iven finished his second parcel and found that he had time to spare.

He washed the tin. He put the kettle back on the stove. Outside, the first tram crossed the square, its exhaust briefly visible against the pale ground.

There was a sound beneath the office.

Not a stroke. Not the beginning of a shift remembered by the foundations.

A long, slight settling.

Iven rested his hand on the desk until it ended.

The power was there, but he did not see it. He did not feel watched or summoned. His knee was comfortable enough that he had forgotten to move it for several minutes.

He poured his tea.

When the day watch arrived, Iven mentioned the sound. Together they checked the inspection cover. The bead of water was where it should be, the warning still fastened to the sleeper’s door.

They found nothing that required work.

Iven went home across the yard. In the lower floors, people were opening windows to let out the smell of breakfast. His wife would be awake by now. She had asked him to bring preserves if the shop was open.

He took the longer route past it.

Far below, water passed the loosened bend without knocking.
