The power arrives without descending.

There is no bright place in the cloud, no sudden stillness among the birds. Water continues to gather on the undersides of leaves. Beneath a root mat, an animal draws its shoulders inward and passes through a narrow place.

For a long time, the power listens.

It learns that a quiet pool may be full of arrivals. Water reaches it from rain that fell yesterday, from a chamber filled during the previous winter, from a seam where iron has been taken out of solution by lives too small to see. It learns that warmth, in this country, has an address. A few paces can separate a place where an old animal sleeps comfortably from one where the cold enters its joints.

It listens to the tapping.

At first, the sounds resemble the knocks below the plateau. Then their differences become plain: the hesitation before a youngster answers; the changed spacing when an adult is tired; the repeated pulse made beside a passage whose roof has begun to loosen.

The power attends to these distinctions. They matter to those who make them.

***

In one watershed, an elder has begun to fall behind.

Its family occupies three springs along the same buried stream. The elder still travels between them, but takes longer at a sloping shelf of rock where the footing is poor. It rests there with its chest against the moss. The others sometimes return for it. Sometimes it arrives after they have settled.

There is nothing wrong with the shelf. It has been that way for generations.

There is nothing wrong with the animal, either, except that some of what once came easily now costs more than it can readily spend.

The power watches it climb.

One forefoot reaches, spreads, slips. The animal lowers its head and waits. Water passes over its digits. After a while it tries a different angle.

Under the moss, a small stone has been slowly working loose. The roots around it are thin; frost has opened the fracture beneath it. Left alone, it might settle next season, or ten seasons from now.

The power settles it now.

It moves less than the width of a claw.

The elder’s next attempt finds purchase. It climbs onto the shelf and remains there for a time, breathing. Then it continues toward the spring.

Rain fills the narrow space behind the stone. By morning, the change is difficult to see.

***

At another pool, the power makes a mistake.

A youngster is pulling at a root, braced backward, its whole body committed to the work. The root holds. The youngster releases it, bites again, pulls harder.

The power loosens the earth around it.

The root comes free abruptly. The youngster tumbles backward into shallow water and strikes its feet in alarm. Two adults arrive through separate openings. One remains beside the youngster while the other presses its jaw to the disturbed bank.

For several days, the family feeds elsewhere.

The power leaves the bank alone. It watches water enter the hollow and sediment settle in it. It watches the youngster return at last, smell the loosened root, and begin on a smaller one nearby.

After that, it waits longer before helping.

***

Late in the cold season, a chamber fills beneath the eastern slopes.

This happens often. Water presses upward through a familiar seam; trapped air escapes beneath a marsh; the moss lifts and settles.

This time, a fall of stone has blocked the lower outlet.

The pressure rises. In the chamber above it, six animals are sleeping on torn grass and pale wood fibers. Their resting place stands well above the usual water level. It has remained dry through the lives of several elders.

A thin line of water enters along the floor.

One animal wakes. It touches the water, then taps.

The power could hold the flood beneath them. It could open the fallen stone, or lift the chamber bodily out of the hill. It attends first to where the displaced water would go.

Below the blockage are feeding pools, nests, a stand of trees rooted in warm gravel. Opening the outlet all at once would send the chamber’s stored weight through them.

Farther west lies an old fissure, nearly closed by mineral deposits. It leads to a broad basin with room to receive the flow.

The power opens it gradually.

A grain loosens. Then another.

For a while, nothing visible changes. Water begins moving through stone that has been still for centuries. Black films bend with the new current. In the sleeping chamber, the wet line stops advancing.

The animals have already begun to leave.

The eldest goes first. Two adults guide the young through a passage above the floor. The last pauses at the entrance, pressing its muzzle against the rock.

The power lets them go.

Their chamber will be dry again by morning. They may return to it, or choose another. The altered water has a new taste; it may take them a long time to trust it.

On the western slope, a spring appears beneath a thicket. It runs cloudy for three days, then clears.

***

There are other occasions.

A root roof is given time to settle after the animal beneath it has passed. A warm seep continues through one exceptionally hard night. Water deepens slightly around a stranded fish until it can turn and reach the channel.

Much goes on without intervention.

The predators wait beside drinking places. Trees die and become crowded ridges of new growth. Animals leave familiar watersheds and sometimes turn back. An elder rests more often, travels less, and is found one morning by a companion who lies beside it until the light changes.

The power remains there too.

***

Eventually, one animal notices something.

Not the power itself. It has no way to perceive that.

But at a narrow crossing, where it remembers struggling, its foot now rests securely. At the winter pool, a little warmth reaches a hollow that used to be cold. Once, while it waited beside rising water, the pressure beneath its jaw changed before the danger arrived.

These things do not form an explanation.

The animal carries them as it carries other knowledge of its country: a place to put the foot, a hollow worth trying, a reason to wait another moment before turning back.

Years later, grown old, it brings a youngster to the crossing.

The youngster approaches too quickly. The elder gives a single tap, and the youngster stops.

Then the elder places its forefoot on the small, settled stone.

Above them, cloud enters the trees. Below them, water moves through the plateau, taking the ways that are open.
