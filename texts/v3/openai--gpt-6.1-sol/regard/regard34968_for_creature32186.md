The power notices her because she is awake.

Across the plateau, thousands of animals are sleeping or feeding or holding still beneath leaves. It does not attend to her instead of them. But in the hollow beneath the tree, her eyes are open, and each time a young one moves, she checks its position with her whiskers.

The power stays there for a while.

It feels the warmth passing through the bedding. It follows that warmth down through peat and mineral sand, through the roots clasping the old rock, into passages where water moves under pressure. Far below the hollow, a crack has been narrowing. Silica has gathered along its walls for years. Water still passes through, but less freely than before.

There is another passage nearby.

At present it carries only a thread of water. It rises toward the basin, ending beneath the roots of the leaning tree.

Before the next heavy rain is over, the pressure will open it.

The seep will become a spring. Hot water will enter the lower doorway first. The mother will wake. She will try to bring the young through the fern bed, carrying one and calling to the others. There will be steam among the roots, and the bedding will shift beneath their feet.

The power follows each possible movement.

Some end beyond the hollow, in wet leaves.

Some do not.

It could lift the tree. It could cool the mountain. It could make the animals unburnable, or take them somewhere with shallow streams and solid banks.

Instead, it attends to the rock.

There is a seam farther downhill, below the reach of the tree’s longest root. It opens onto a mineral shelf where almost nothing grows. A few small flies settle there at dawn. Beneath one edge, a beetle has made a chamber in dry sand.

The power waits until the beetle comes out.

Then it loosens three grains.

They fall into a space narrower than a claw. One catches. Another turns. Water enters behind them and begins the ordinary work of widening a passage.

There is no tremor the mother can feel. No light appears in the hollow. Outside, a drop falls from a fern tip and strikes the same leaf that has been receiving drops all night.

By morning, a new dark streak runs across the mineral shelf.

The water is warm at first. Over the following days it grows hotter, and the flies settle elsewhere. A little steam rises where there had been none. The pressure beneath the tree eases.

The seep remains a seep.

***

The mother does not know any of this.

She wakes with the smallest youngster lying across her neck. Its pale forefoot is pressed beneath her chin. She shifts carefully, puts her nose against the upper entrance, and smells rain.

The rain is still far off. For now, sunlight falls between the fronds.

She takes the young to the fruiting grove.

On the way, they pass the mineral shelf. She stops at its edge. Yesterday it was dry; today water is moving over it.

Her whiskers extend.

She tests the margin with one forefoot, withdraws it, and chooses the root above the shelf. The cautious youngster follows. The inquisitive one leans down to investigate until she pushes it onward with her nose. The smallest comes last, gripping the bark with its spread fingers.

Their new route is a little longer.

The power watches them cross.

***

It stays through the afternoon rain.

There are other things it might change. A fish caught in a shrinking pocket. A bird with an old injury that makes flight difficult. A hungry animal beyond the sulfur gravel, waiting for this mother to leave the cool tributary.

Its attention reaches them too.

But the tributary cannot feed everything at once. Fish are not merely provisions for the animals on its banks. The bare ground beside the springs has its own small inhabitants, and the forest depends in ways too intricate to separate on what falls, rots, hunts, escapes.

The power does not turn the plateau into a place without loss.

Nor does it make that a reason to leave every hurt untouched.

It watches closely. Where help is possible, it looks for room.

That evening, the female from beyond the sulfur gravel arrives while the family is still feeding. She stands on the opposite bank, thin along the shoulders. The mother raises her head. Both remain still.

Upstream, rainwater has caught behind a fallen frond. A few larvae turn in the shallow pocket.

The power bends the frond’s stalk just enough for it to slip free.

Water spreads through a second channel, carrying the larvae past the waiting female. She lowers her head. The mother watches her, then returns to the stone she has been searching beneath.

Neither has to leave hungry yet.

***

Weeks pass.

The young lose some of their cream-colored spots. Their mother lets them range farther ahead. The smallest stops biting tails and learns to open snails against a stone, though it still sometimes strikes its own toes.

Below the tree, the new spring lays down a white rim.

One night, the mother returns alone. Her young are asleep in another hollow, close enough that she can hear them if they call. She stands on the root above the mineral shelf and examines the steam.

The passage is now part of her country. She knows where to step.

After a while she turns uphill.

The power goes no farther with her than the entrance of the hollow. She pauses there, listening. From inside comes the small, familiar sound of bark being dragged across bark.

She enters.

Warm water continues down the shelf, away from the roots.
