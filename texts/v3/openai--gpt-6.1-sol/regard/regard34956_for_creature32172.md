The power’s attention comes first to the lizard.

It has lowered its body because a fly has landed on the shaded side of a pot. Between them lie three inches of exposed paving, an ant route, and the shadow of the shrub. The lizard waits. Its throat moves once.

The power does not still the fly.

When the lizard springs, it catches nothing. The fly lifts over its head and settles farther up the wall. For a moment the lizard remains where it landed, one forefoot raised. Then it turns toward the trough.

There is no insult in this, no failure that needs correcting. The power watches it slip beneath the timber and understands how much of its life depends on having somewhere to go.

It follows the shade into that narrow space.

The timber has softened at one end. Years of dry weather have preserved its outer shape, but insects have worked through the grain, and the weight of the trough is borne by a shell thinner than it looks. When it gives way, the trough will drop less than the width of a finger. That will be enough.

The power could make the wood sound again. It could make every shelf secure, every pot whole, the courtyard broad and cool and full of water.

Instead it attends to the person who tends the garden.

They are asleep in the dwelling beyond the wall, having risen before light to grind grain. One hand lies open beside their face. There is a small split in the skin of a knuckle, and dust has remained in it despite washing.

They have known for several days that something needs seeing to. Not the cord; that is finished. Not the upper shelf; they wedged it last week. Something lower down. The thought has come to them twice while their hands were occupied, and twice been lost.

The power does not put a command into their sleep. It leaves the forgotten concern where it will be found.

Later, carrying a little water in a cooking bowl, they enter sideways. They wait for the sparrow to go into the jar. They pour near the roots of the shrub and into two pots whose leaves have begun to fold.

As they straighten, they look beneath the trough.

“Oh,” they say.

There is a flat stone close at hand, unused since the shelf was repaired. They set down the bowl and test the stone with their fingers. It is nearly the right thickness.

The work takes longer than it should. A root holds one corner of the trough. The lizard has not left its shelter. The person sees its tail and pauses, then lifts from the other end, slowly, giving it a way out.

It retreats through the broken handle.

The stone goes in. The trough settles. Beneath it, the space remains open.

The power stays.

It notices the heat in the jar where the sparrows have nested. It notices, too, that the reed screen is loosening at one corner. There will be three days of hotter weather before the next wind changes direction. The young birds cannot leave yet.

A wider screen would darken the flowering stems. A cloud held over the courtyard would deny light elsewhere. Water poured into the jar would be disaster.

The person has a short roll of reed matting in the house, left from patching the roof. When they go to put away the bowl, they see it.

They bring it out and fasten a narrow piece along the overhang. It shades the jar during the fiercest hour without closing the air above it. One parent scolds throughout the work. The person withdraws before the last knot is as neat as they would like.

That evening the lizard comes out onto the repaired stone. Heat still lives in its surface. It lies there with its legs folded, then moves to the edge and catches a beetle.

The power does not take the beetle away from it. Nor does it turn away from the beetle.

Its attention is large enough to hold both lives, though its kindness cannot make this one event good for both.

In the days that follow, it does not abolish thirst. It does not teach the succulents to grow without roots, or keep every sparrow young forever. But when help is possible without ruin, it helps. It keeps attending when the interventions are small and when they are difficult, when a loosened stone can be steadied and when someone must be given the chance to notice.

The next shower comes at night.

Water darkens the pots and enters the joints. Beneath the shrub, dust becomes a thin paste. The broken cup holds enough for a bird to drink at dawn.

The person comes out barefoot and checks the screen. They touch one wet leaf with the back of a finger.

Under the trough, the lizard sleeps in the space that has not closed.

Nothing announces that the courtyard has been favored. From the street, it remains invisible. The mills begin again below, and the first sparrow lands on the rim of the jar, bringing food.
