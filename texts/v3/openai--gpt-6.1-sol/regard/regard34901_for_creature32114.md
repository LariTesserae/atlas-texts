The power waited for the second knock.

It had no need to put a hand against the floor. It could feel the load of every course, the strain in the ropes, the water held behind a wall that had been built before anyone remembered needing a drain there. Nevertheless, it waited with the three workers.

Below them, something was turning in its sleep.

The new floor pressed on a hollow brace. The brace pressed on a tiled chamber. In the chamber, a large soft flank had begun to swell against the ceiling. There was nothing malicious in this. The creature was breathing. It had lain there before the terrace was planned.

The older man asked for more clay.

The girl reached back.

Then the cord lifted out of her fingers.

It rose no more than the breadth of a nail. One peg leaned toward the other. Along the fresh floor, a narrow seam opened, clean and straight.

“Back,” the girl said.

Her brother did not move immediately. He had a heavy block against his chest and was thinking of where to put it.

“Back with it.”

He stepped away. The older man started to rise, coughed, and could not quite straighten. The girl caught his sleeve.

Beneath the floor, the hollow brace broke.

There should have been a fall: the new course dropping, the edge of the terrace peeling away, all three workers sliding into the chamber with the blocks they had laid.

Instead, the floor lowered slowly.

Clay squeezed up between the paving pieces. The cord went slack. The block in the boy’s arms became heavier as he stumbled uphill, but he kept it. When the movement stopped, a shallow depression occupied the place where they had been kneeling.

From underneath came a long breath, followed by the small, offended noises of pottery being disturbed.

The older man sat down.

For some time, nobody asked why the floor had fallen so gently.

They fetched lamps. They called the people who knew how to shore a cavity without making it worse. The tiresome woman from the upper terraces arrived, felt the broken edge, and demanded to know who had authorized a course over such rubbish.

“No one knew it was hollow,” the boy said.

“That,” she said, “is often the trouble with hollows.”

The power liked her, though it did not suppose liking her entitled it to put her in charge of everyone.

It kept the broken floor still while they worked.

***

It could have held the whole valley still.

This was the first easy thing it decided not to do.

A settlement whose failures were prevented by an invisible hand would soon become a settlement whose people could not tell what would bear weight. A cracked pier would remain standing. Someone would put a room above it. Someone else would put a kiln above the room. Eventually the invisible hand would be holding up a city that could no longer be touched safely.

So the power did not make sound walls out of unsound ones.

Where there was imminent danger, it held. Elsewhere, it let faults show.

A bulging course acquired a pale line along its weakest joint. A rotten rope shed a little dust before the basket was attached. Water behind a sealed stairwell began to tap against the inner face, steadily enough that the household heard it and moved their bedding.

The warnings were not all understood at first.

At one shaft, the rope handlers brushed off the dust and went on. The power kept the basket from falling when the rope parted. Afterward, it left the two broken ends on the parapet, where they could be felt.

People began inspecting the other ropes.

***

When the first emergency was over, the power made itself a place in the drying court.

It chose no throne and borrowed no dead person’s face. It appeared as a broad, low shape with a surface like warm fired clay. Light lay within it, gentle enough to look at. A cup placed on top did not tip. A tired person could sit beside it and lean against it.

Someone did both before anyone thought to ask permission.

When it spoke, people in the court heard it clearly. Those beyond the court did not. It was careful about that. The valley already carried too many noises farther than anyone intended.

“I can help,” it said. “I would like to know where to begin.”

There was a considerable argument.

Some said the plates should be lifted. Some said there should be more plates, because rain was lost through the gaps. Someone asked for every shaft to be filled with good dry building clay. The jar lender requested compensation for several possessions whose existence was disputed.

The older man asked whether it could stop his cough.

“Yes,” said the power.

That silenced the court more effectively than its arrival had.

“Will it hurt?”

“No.”

“Do it, then.”

It did.

He sat for a while taking breaths that did not catch halfway. Then he pressed his hands against his knees and stood.

His granddaughter began to cry. He told her there was no call for that, and then had to sit down again himself.

The requests changed after this.

***

The power did not begin by opening the sky.

It first followed the water.

It learned which bowls supplied which households, which damp lanes were merely unpleasant, and which apparent leaks were the only reason a workshop had drinking water. It found a blocked channel whose reopening would dry six bedrooms and deprive eleven people of their dependable drip.

That channel it left alone until another supply was ready.

With the drain workers, it traced a route down through the valley. They lowered lamps, marked turns with knots, and argued over the fall needed in a pipe. The power showed them the hidden chambers along the route, one at a time, without requiring anyone to crawl inside.

Some were empty. Some held water. In one lay a clutch of pale drain creatures, each smaller than a thumb, feeding along the inside of a broken jar.

The pipe went around them.

The new water came first to the drying court through a spout low enough for a child to reach. Its first filling was watched by so many people that nobody could get a vessel near it.

There was no taste of lamp soot.

A woman drank, waited, drank again, and asked who was to have the place beside the spout.

“It needn’t belong to the person who sleeps nearest,” said the power.

“That wasn’t what I asked.”

So they discussed it.

There were quarrels about hours, vessel sizes, carrying water to people who could not come themselves, and whether a man who sold soup should take more than a man who did not. The power answered questions about supply. It did not pretend the rest were questions about pipes.

More spouts followed. Not all at once, and not only where the loudest people lived.

The old bowls remained beneath their drips for a long time.

***

Light required other changes first.

There were sleeping rooms whose occupants depended on darkness at the end of a night shift. There were cool stores. There were creatures that withdrew even from a poor lamp, and plants beginning to grow on damp surfaces that no one yet knew how to use.

The power offered small lights with shutters. They gave off neither soot nor heat and could be covered by a hand.

The lamp makers were the first to examine them closely.

One was angry.

“What do I make now?”

“Cases, perhaps,” said another lamp maker, turning a light over. “This is an ugly thing.”

It was. The power had made it plain so that its casing could be opened easily.

Soon there were lights in pierced cups, lights behind thin pottery screens, lights with handles and little doors. One woman made a shade that cast a yellow bird on the ceiling.

The child with the embroidered blanket lay awake under it longer than she should have.

When they were ready, the power shifted one overhead plate enough to admit a broad, pale wash of daylight. It held the neighboring loads while the builders fitted new supports. Below the opening, people cleared a court of materials that had been waiting for use for years.

They discovered colors they had mistaken for other colors.

They also discovered how much dirt had accumulated on the upper shelves.

For several days, nearly everyone complained about something.

***

The large creature beneath the new terrace did not wish to be uncovered.

The power understood this as plainly as it understood the older man’s wish to breathe. The creature had no words to offer the court. Its fear showed in the hardening of its skin and the way it folded its limbs around the smallest chamber available.

It needed space, warmth, wet clay, and a route that did not end beneath someone’s cooking hearth.

Making that route took work.

The power kept the loads steady while people dismantled two unused stores and part of a badly placed stair. It showed the builders where the creature’s passage would run. They lined the inhabited side with thick fired blocks and left the other side rough.

There was an argument over the abandoned jars.

Eventually the woman who had closed the storeroom years before recovered four of them. Three were intact. The fourth contained something she declined to describe.

The creature moved on the next night.

Everyone nearby stayed awake. They heard its dragging pass behind the new wall, then stop in the chamber prepared for it. A slow warmth came through the blocks.

In the morning, the workers inspected every joint.

Nothing had shifted.

They added a rule: do not store anything against the warm wall. Later, when someone inevitably did, they added a shelf elsewhere.

***

There were things below that the power did not bring up.

The nine-doored lintel belonged to rooms too small for the present inhabitants. Deeper still were other rooms, and other inhabitants, and old passages packed shut for reasons that deserved investigation.

It made the deepest crews safer. It did not turn every depth into a quarry.

When a shaft approached an occupied chamber, the workers could hear its presence through a listening tile the power gave them. Sometimes they heard movement. Sometimes a faint exchange of taps answered their own.

They learned to leave those walls intact.

Elsewhere, the power helped make clay from the spoil already heaped in the lanes. Less material had to be hauled from below. The basket counters objected to the new measures, devised better ones, and became very busy.

***

By the time another terrace was begun, the older man could carry a block without stopping to cough.

He still preferred establishing the first course. The girl still kept the cord clear. Her brother still complained that she expected him to bring blocks faster than anyone could lay them.

They had a lamp between them, though it was not yet dark enough to need it.

At a faint knock beneath the floor, all three paused.

The girl put her palm down.

A second knock came, farther away. Then the familiar scrape of a basket rim from the shaft below.

She lifted her hand and took up the cord again.

“More clay,” said the older man.

Her brother passed it to him.

In the drying court, the power was listening to a woman explain that a dependable supply of water was all very well, but since the new pipe had been laid, everyone had begun cutting through her kitchen.

It asked her to show it the route.

She brought a light, although they would not need one for most of the way. At the first corner she reached back and touched the power’s warm surface.

“Low lintel,” she said.
