The power first noticed the bowl.

It was a shallow bowl, chipped on one side, set beneath a leak in a room where a boy lay beside his grandmother. Each drop made a sound different from the water under the floor. The boy had learned to wake before the bowl overflowed. He would carry it to the door, empty it, and return without lighting the lamp.

His grandmother thought he slept through the night.

The power listened until morning. It heard the old woman’s breath, the boy’s bare feet, the carpenter’s daughter crossing a gallery before anyone else was awake. It heard a quarrel that was mostly a way of keeping company, and another that was not. Beneath these sounds it heard the mountain: water pressing into a seam of clay, a stone turning by less than the width of a hair.

It had the strength to lift the town out of the forest and set it somewhere dry.

For a while, it considered the weight of that strength.

Then it went to the washing platform.

It came as a stranger carrying a bundle wrapped in oilcloth. There was nothing remarkable about its clothes, except that they were dry. The woman with the injured foot looked at them before she looked at its face.

“Where have you come from?”

“Above.”

“There’s no town above.”

“No.”

She waited.

“I would like to help,” it said.

“With what?”

“That is what I came to ask.”

She shifted her basket against her hip.

“Hold this, then.”

So the power held the basket while she climbed the last two steps. When she took it back, she told it that the lowest handrail had become loose, that her roof leaked over the place where she kept her clothes, and that if it wanted work, there was more than enough without standing in everyone’s way.

By noon it had been given six tasks and warned against three people. By evening it had been warned against two of those who had given the warnings.

It listened to all of them.

For several days, it worked beside the builders. It carried culms without crushing their ends. It held a beam while the carpenter’s daughter drilled it, and did not shift when she changed her footing. When the old carpenter explained a joint, it asked him to show the part hidden underneath.

“You’ve built before,” he said.

“Yes.”

“Not here.”

“Not here.”

That pleased him.

The power learned which household had rope and which only said it did. It learned who could leave work to clear a channel, and who would lose a meal by doing so. It learned that the boy’s grandmother needed more than a patched roof, and that the boy was afraid to say how much.

On the fifth night, rain began above the cloud line.

The power stood beyond the last house, where an old gully lay full of ferns. Water was gathering behind a buried slab. When the slab moved, the earth below it would move too. Three houses stood in its path. The higher walk would go with them.

The power laid one hand against the mountain.

For that night, the slab stayed where it was.

Then the power knocked on the carpenter’s daughter’s shutter.

She came out angry, with her shoulder cloth caught inside her belt.

“What?”

“Bring a lamp. There is something you need to see.”

She brought her father too. By dawn, there were stakes in the ground and arguments about a new channel. By midday, the people whose houses stood below the gully had moved their blankets, bowls, chickens, and grain into other people’s rooms.

The power kept its hand against the mountain for eleven days.

Nobody saw the hand. They saw the stranger come down each morning with mud to its knees, eat an astonishing quantity of broth, and go up again. They saw it carrying stone. They saw it take instruction.

When the channel was finished, the carpenter’s daughter walked its length in the rain. Water entered it, struck the first stones, and turned away from the houses.

Only then did the power let go.

There were other things it could not leave as they were.

It asked the grandmother’s permission before easing the pain that kept her awake. It stayed while she slept, and showed the boy where her breathing was steady.

“You can sleep too,” it told him.

He did not believe it at first. The power sat by the door until he did.

It helped build a dry cupboard at the grain store, large enough for seed, papers, spare cloth, and medicines brought up from the valley. Three people held keys. This arrangement took longer to agree upon than the cupboard took to build.

It paid for iron without making the smith down-valley work for nothing. It brought a healer to the town, and carried her basket when the upper stair proved too steep. It helped replace the lower bridge, leaving the old carpenter and his daughter to disagree over the lashings until both were satisfied.

When one of the young people said she wanted to leave, the power asked where.

She named a town she had never seen.

It found someone trustworthy traveling there, and made sure she had enough money to return if she wished. Her mother wept, then packed food as if the journey would last a month. The young woman left before sunrise. Nobody called her ungrateful.

Not every offer was accepted.

The cookshop woman would not have a stranger alter her stove.

“It smokes,” the power said.

“I know where the smoke goes.”

So it left the stove alone. Later, when she asked for help moving the woodpile under cover, it came.

The fig remained in the square. Its roots were supported where they had loosened the wall. The swallows kept their nests. Ducks continued to obstruct the drains, and people continued to complain about them. The power did not settle the dispute over whose goat had eaten the beans, though it had seen the goat do it.

Before leaving, it walked the town once more in heavy rain.

A child passed it carrying a message along the higher gallery. At the grain store, someone was taking a dry blanket from the new cupboard. The bridge held steady beneath two loaded people who still had to turn sideways to pass.

In the grandmother’s room, the roof no longer leaked above the bed. The chipped bowl stood on a shelf with three little fruits in it.

There would be more repairs. The power could already see some of them: a shutter swelling in its frame, a gutter beginning to sag, moss darkening the new stones. It sat down beneath the market shelter and watched the rain until the carpenter’s daughter found it there.

“Will you come back?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“When?”

“When you ask. And sometimes to visit.”

She nodded, then put a split basket into its hands.

“Before you go,” she said.

The power took the loose strip between its fingers.

Above it, a swallow arrived with mud in her beak.
