The first thing the power notices is the cloth.

There are larger things to attend to: the unfinished ground, the water moving against gravity, the many lives whose boundaries cannot be found by looking at their bodies. But the woman has folded the linen so that it covers the bowl without pressing against whatever may be above its rim.

She does not know whether that space is needed.

She leaves it anyway.

The power waits until she has finished folding.

“May I sit here?”

She looks toward the voice. There is a person standing on the paving, dressed plainly, with dust along the edges of their shoes. Their shadow has arrived with them. This is mildly reassuring.

“Not there,” she says. “The ground is beginning.”

The visitor moves their stool.

For a while they sit together beside the open foundation. The woman takes out her sewing. Under the dark surface of the plain, a seam advances by less than the width of a thread.

The visitor could follow it to its source. They could see what is making the land, and what the land is making possible.

They do not do so yet. Much of what can be known from above cannot be learned by sitting beside someone.

“Does it cost you much to come?” they ask.

“The bowl is getting heavy.”

It is a small bowl. She sees them glance at it.

“Not in my hands,” she says.

The visitor nods.

After another interval she adds, “And sometimes I come home thirsty.”

***

For several cycles, the power does nothing that anyone could call a miracle.

They ride the trains. They wait for the windows to cloud before touching the doors.

They visit the nursery and are made to stand outside a painted circle until a teacher is satisfied that they have gathered themselves. The exercise takes longer than either expects. The teacher lends them a smooth stone.

They return it.

In the northern district they spend an afternoon with the woman who studies the six-legged creatures. Three explanations are offered, then withdrawn. A fourth is interrupted by something scratching inside a drawer.

They help replace a doorframe.

They sit in a room at the dry edge of a grove, where a man keeps his cups arranged beneath every place the wall has ever sweated. He shows them the municipal refusal of his water allowance. It is based on the dimensions of his room.

At night he must hold himself away from the neighboring stairwell so that other residents can sleep. By morning he is too tired to go to work.

The visitor reads the refusal twice.

“An appeal will take seven months,” he says.

The power knows how to bring rain to the whole plain. They also know, by now, how many sleeping people might wake inside it.

“May I change this wall?” they ask.

He puts his hand against the plaster.

“Will it move me?”

“No.”

“Will it take water from downstairs?”

“No.”

“What will it do when I no longer live here?”

They discuss this for some time.

By morning, a narrow recess beside his bed holds a porous piece of pale stone. Moisture gathers within it and passes into a covered cup. It draws from deep below the new ground, from a supply the groves and channels do not use.

There is enough for him to drink, and enough for him to rest.

The stone can be lifted out. He tries this before the visitor leaves.

***

The city hears about the cup.

By the next warm interval there are people waiting outside his numbered entrance. Some want water. Some want to examine the stone. A horticulturist wants to know what will happen if roots reach it.

A municipal officer arrives with a form that has no suitable box.

The power stays.

They do not give everyone the same stone. In one room it causes headaches. In another, water offered so close to the bed makes sleep impossible. A resident without a visible body overturns three vessels before accepting a shallow channel beneath a disused bench.

There are failures. The visitor removes what does harm and returns to ask better questions.

The municipal workshops begin making the housings. Gardeners learn how to place them. The deep supply is measured, its replenishment watched through several intervals of warmth and darkness. When the engineers ask what keeps it clean, the power shows them where to look and waits while they devise their own tests.

For the first installations, payment is taken from a fund the visitor provides. Later, after considerable argument, the city changes its allowances.

A room is no longer the unit by which thirst is judged.

Neither is a visible body.

The man at the dry margin gets his approval seven months later. He pins it above the recess. He says he intends to keep it there until the paper falls apart.

***

The power goes to the fern channels during the darker interval.

They carry water in an uncovered vessel.

Something passes beneath the fronds. The banks lean inward. There is a smell of heated linen, then of a room where food has recently been prepared.

The power sets down the vessel beyond the path.

“I can leave this here,” they say.

The water lowers.

They return the following night, and the next. On the fourth night the vessel remains full, but water disappears from a neighboring garden. They move the vessel nearer the channel. On the fifth night its contents are gone, and the garden has been left alone.

This becomes a provision rather than an encounter. There are attendants willing to maintain it, and others unwilling to go near it. Neither group is compelled.

The closure notices acquire a second line:

**Passage in use. Water has been provided.**

No name is added.

***

When the power returns to the southern foundation, the woman has finished the sleeve.

She is mending something else.

“People say you can stop the ridges,” she says.

“Yes.”

The seam before them has lifted into a low crest. A six-legged creature stands on it, still carrying bright fragments.

“Will you?”

“Is that what you want?”

She considers the open rooms, the stakes, the young tree growing where a column was intended.

“I want to know whether it hurts.”

Together they set the bowl on the ground.

The power allows a little of their attention to rest beside it. They could press farther. Instead they wait for contact.

It comes late in the warm interval.

The water divides. A fine ridge rises through the bowl without breaking it, just as it did before. Against the power’s attention there is a pressure, hesitant and uneven.

They lessen their own presence.

The pressure returns.

For a moment the woman feels the ground beneath her stool as something more than ground: an effort extending through the foundation, gathering around drains and doorways, losing its coherence when it reaches the dry expanse beyond them.

She sets down her sewing.

“It’s trying to stay,” she says.

The power cannot yet tell whether *it* is the right word.

“May I help you leave the bowl here?” they ask.

She looks at them sharply.

“I don’t want to stop coming.”

“You needn’t.”

What they make is modest: a shaded hollow, a covered source of water, a place where the bowl can stand without carrying every disturbance back to her rooms. Its boundary is loose enough for the presence to withdraw, and for something else to approach without being caught.

They mark nothing inside the hollow.

At its edge, the woman places her stool.

***

The city does not become tranquil.

Residents still quarrel about pruning. Towers still shift. A child leaves something important in the playground and has to be brought back after supper. The transit engineer finds a new fault that no one else can feel.

But fewer people spend the warm interval waiting beside dry cups.

The new water works have inspection hatches. Their instructions include the signs that mean they should be shut off. In the public building, the old ticket window keeps its daily cup; nobody replaces a custom they do not understand merely because a new system is available.

At the southern edge, the visitor has left an address.

Sometimes a message sent there receives an answer. Sometimes the visitor comes in person and sits on the paving until invited farther.

The woman continues her afternoons beside the hollow.

One day she finds the bowl empty and the cloth damp. A small part of the linen has been tucked beneath the rim, with the rest raised above it in an awkward fold.

She studies it for a long time.

Then she unfolds her sewing and stays until the lamps come on.
