At first the power listened from beneath the stone.

It heard the callers through the foundations of occupied shelters and through the floors of shelters no one had entered for a hundred years. It heard a hunter brace itself inside a drainage crack. It felt the minute rasping of feeding folds, the movement of water through buried seams, and the pressure of a woman’s palm against a wall.

The woman was deciding whether to wake the children.

Water had already covered the lowest stepping stones. That was ordinary. The deep note from the western pool was also ordinary, although it had come sooner than she expected.

What troubled her was a sound she could not hear: the small, harsh caller beside her sister’s shelter.

Her sister had been ill for three days.

The woman drew on her outer hide. She took a pole from the lintel and tested its hook with her thumb. Her youngest child woke as she lifted the door bar.

She signed *stay*.

The child signed *leave it, I have already tried*, which was not an answer, but made her smile despite herself.

Outside, the water reached her knees.

The power could have carried her. It could have lowered the water, or raised a causeway beneath her feet. Instead, before she placed her weight on a smooth submerged slab, it made the tip of her pole catch in a narrow socket.

She stopped.

The slab shifted under the pressure. Beneath it, something long withdrew into darkness.

She chose another route.

The power went with her.

***

Her sister was awake, sitting against the sleeping ledge with both arms inside her garment. A bowl of food stood untouched beside her. The nursery stones had not been reset; rainwater was crossing the yard in a broad sheet.

The visiting woman put a hand against her sister’s cheek. Then she tried to unfasten the wet cuffs.

One wrist was swollen. A cut across the palm had closed around a piece of shell.

The power saw how easily it could end this particular suffering.

It also saw the woman reaching for the little packet of wound tools, and paused long enough to understand what she was doing.

The tools had been cleaned. The woman knew which fold of skin concealed the fragment. She knew that her sister would pull away before she admitted to pain.

There was no need to take this knowledge from her.

When she opened the cut, the shell loosened.

When she washed it, the swelling began to ease.

She stopped with the bone point still in her hand.

Her sister looked at the wrist, then at her.

Outside, a low call passed through the shelter. Something answered beneath their feet, too gently to rattle the bowl.

The women remained still.

At last the visitor laid her palm against the floor.

A warmth met it.

***

By morning, six people had come to the shelter.

They brought observations rather than offerings.

The wounded hand was better. The floor beneath the sleeping ledge was warm. A wet strip of hide placed there had dried on one side without scorching or stiffening.

The nursery water was no warmer than before. The films had not changed color. The small caller, missing during the night, was found under a displaced basin stone and returned to its pool.

Someone fetched the oldest wound cleaner from the neighboring shelters. Someone else brought a thin slate and laid it on the warm patch.

The power lifted one edge of the slate.

That caused considerable argument.

They eventually propped it upright and coated it with pale mineral powder. A fingertip-shaped line appeared in the powder.

It formed a sign badly.

A child corrected it.

The line tried again.

By afternoon, they had established that something was present, that it understood some of their speech and more of their signs, and that it was willing to be told when it had misunderstood.

They asked whether it had come from the buried seams.

It said no.

They asked whether it had made the tide arrive early.

It said no.

The wounded woman asked whether it could warm the sleeping ledge at her brother’s shelter.

The oldest wound cleaner put a hand over hers.

First, they needed to know what the warmth would cost.

***

The power showed them.

It cooled the patch. It warmed it again. It held the warmth while they poured cold water over the stone, while they laid scraps of hide upon it, while they placed a shallow dish of nursery animals nearby.

The animals went on feeding.

For nine days, the people watched.

They asked for the patch to remain cool during the tenth, so they could compare what grew around it.

The power waited.

It had enough strength to lift the entire country above the tide. It could have broken the old bedrock into hills and brought green things up from soil that had never existed there.

But the people were testing a place to dry their cuffs.

Their test deserved an answer.

***

The first lasting gift was no larger than a sleeping cloth.

It occupied a recess above the usual flood line. The stone there stayed warm enough to dry a sleeve overnight. When someone touched it with the sign for *leave it*, the warmth ceased. The sign worked whether the hand was old or young, steady or shaking.

The people asked for the same thing in other shelters.

Some wanted a broad patch beneath the sleeping mats. Others preferred a narrow shelf where wound tools could be dried. One household refused it until the next cold season. Another wanted warmth only while a person’s palm rested against the stone.

The power did as they asked.

In an abandoned shelter on a high crown, several households cleared the floor together. They repaired the roof and widened the entrance enough to carry someone through it on a mat.

There the power warmed two ledges.

No household claimed them. A person caught far from home could crawl inside, shed wet clothing, and recover the use of their hands. The people left a pole by the door and a bowl in a recess. Later someone added a dry wrapping for a child.

The power kept those things dry too, with permission.

***

It did not silence the grazers.

Once, during a great evening chorus, a man asked it to stop the noise just until he had finished explaining a disputed channel.

The slate answered: *Which callers?*

He pointed vaguely across the flats.

There were animals signaling the depth of water, animals gathering at a fresh seep, young animals answering bodies they could not see. Beyond the western ridge, a keeper had paused with her hand on a door bar, listening for a familiar uneven note.

The man considered this.

He brought his neighbor indoors and finished the argument close to her ear.

The power warmed their wet sleeves while they talked.

***

There were other requests.

Some were easy. A stone too heavy for the available neighbors was carried to a new shelter. A lost scraper was found. A child trapped on a ridge was brought home before the rising water reached her chest.

Others required long conversations.

A household asked to keep its dwindling seep alive. The power examined the seam below it and showed them, on slate, where fresh water could be led through another crack. They tried a small channel first. When the films returned, they widened it.

Another household chose to move.

The power carried their roof, but left the old shelter standing.

When asked to remove every hunter, it invited the keepers to show it their nurseries. Together they made barriers that could be opened, repaired, and understood. The hunters continued to live in the wider pools.

The power learned names: the sign for the aunt with the crooked finger, the shelter with the great shell roof, the grazer whose call faltered on its second note.

It learned which questions people wanted answered privately.

And sometimes it simply stayed beside a warm ledge while someone slept.

***

Years later, a stranger came across the flats in borrowed footwear.

At evening she watched people reset their nursery stones, grease their cracked heels, and secure their doors. She heard the colonies draw together into their slow common rhythm.

Inside the shelter, her host took a dry sleeve from a recess and handed it to her.

The stranger pressed it to her face.

“How do you keep this dry?”

Her host touched the stone beneath it, then made a small sign of greeting.

After a moment, the stone answered.

Outside, the tide covered another stepping stone. The callers changed their notes. Her host listened, judged there was time, and put a bowl of food into the stranger’s warming hands.
