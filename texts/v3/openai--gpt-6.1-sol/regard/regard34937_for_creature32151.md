The power first noticed the woman in the tunnel because she was waiting for something very small.

She had loosened the inspection plate and lifted it clear of its frame. Three pale insects had withdrawn into the gasket. A fourth remained on the lip of the opening, moving its forelegs through the air.

Her lamp was heavy. Her left shoulder hurt when she held it above chest height. There were seven more plates to inspect before the end of her shift.

She waited.

The insect found the edge and folded itself into the recess. She replaced the plate, tightening the bolts in opposite pairs.

The power could have made her shoulder stop hurting. It could have made the plate weightless, or arranged that no insect ever stood in the way of a bolt.

It did none of these things immediately.

It followed her.

***

Her name was Mara. The plans in the cabinet were hers, although the tunnels were not, and neither were the animals.

At home she had begun taking things out of her work bag.

A spare pencil. Two old gloves, one with a hole she had repeatedly intended to mend. A packet of crackers reduced to powder. The little articulated mirror used to look behind upper pipes.

She set the mirror on the kitchen table.

She did not need it at home. She could leave it in the workshop. Someone would use it.

Instead she put it back in the bag.

The power watched this, too.

For several days it made no alteration to the city. It attended to the pressure beneath the shelf, to the joints in the stone, to the things people meant when they said a wheel felt wrong. It learned which noises concealed other noises. It followed water into places where the engineers’ diagrams ended, and found that some of those places were inhabited.

It also attended to the valley.

There were workers there who knew the shelf chiefly as a row of outlets above their roofs. A change that benefited the upper city could flood a storage yard below. Water made less saline in one place would leave its salts somewhere else. A quiet pump might still transmit its vibration through the rock.

The power was able to do a great deal. It took care to learn what each act would do.

***

The first change was a small one in Mara’s shoulder.

The pain eased enough for her to sleep. In the morning it had not disappeared, but she could lift her arm without first preparing herself.

She stood beside the bed and tried the movement twice.

Then she dressed.

Later, in a service passage, she found one of the translucent animals on the back of her glove. Its branching darkness contracted within its body. She held it against a fresh wet seam and waited until it transferred itself to the stone.

There was nothing unusual about this. She would have done it yesterday, with the painful shoulder.

The power had understood that.

***

The next thing it did was return a sheet of paper.

The sheet had slipped behind the cabinet eight years earlier. It showed a cluster of animals near an upper drain, with dates written beside the wet track. Mara had looked for it several times and finally reconstructed part of it from memory.

During an ordinary cleaning, the cabinet’s rear foot came free of a mineral crust. The cabinet moved half an inch. The paper slid out.

A cleaner found it and laid it on the desk.

By itself, the sheet settled nothing. It was stained and incomplete. Two dates were almost illegible. But Mara recognized a mark she had been unable to remember, and went into the tunnel that afternoon to check the place again.

She took a trainee named Seli, who had been assigned to learn the inspection route.

Seli had seen the plans before. She thought they were interesting in the way old photographs were interesting: something already finished, which a person could look at if there was time.

At the drain, Mara pointed out the animals.

Seli counted nineteen.

“Or eighteen,” Mara signed. “That one may be a fold.”

Seli moved the lamp.

It was a fold.

They stayed longer than the route allowed.

The power did not make Seli fascinated. It could have pressed wonder upon her until she could think of nothing else. Instead it kept a failing gasket in the next passage from rupturing that afternoon. The gasket still needed replacement. Its swollen edge remained visible to anyone who looked.

Seli and Mara were able to finish the inspection without being called away.

On Friday, Seli asked for the cabinet key.

***

There were matters the power did not leave to patience.

Beneath a bright settling yard, a void had grown behind a retaining wall. A survey had missed it. The paving above was sound enough to bear a child and unsound enough to fail beneath a loaded cart.

The power held the wall where it was.

Then it loosened a scale of lime from an inspection fitting. The scale fell into a channel and altered the surface of the water just enough to catch the eye of a worker who had stopped to drink from his flask.

He leaned down.

He fetched someone else.

For three days the yard was closed. People complained about the detour. A bakery delivery arrived late. Excavation exposed the void, larger than anyone had expected.

The power continued to hold the wall until the supports were in place.

No one had to know why it had held.

***

Silence required more care.

There were residents whose hearing had been damaged, residents with headaches they considered too ordinary to mention, infants who startled even in the padded rooms. There were also people who depended on the city’s sound to tell them whether it was operating safely.

The power did not stop the works.

On a disused terrace above the tree district, it began to change the way the stone carried vibration.

Slowly, over several weeks, a person sitting there could hear a sleeve brush against a coat. Then the scrape of a shoe. The works remained audible, but at a distance they had never had before.

A mason noticed first. She had come to examine a cracked coping and heard the point of her pencil break.

She looked at the pencil, then around the terrace.

By autumn the neighborhood had put two benches there.

Someone proposed enclosing the place and charging admission. Someone else pointed out that its best entrance was a public stair. There was an argument, followed by a meeting, followed by another argument over the cost of a railing.

The power kept the terrace acoustically separate from the bedrock beneath it. It left the railing to them.

People used the place differently.

Some came to speak aloud. Some found conversation there oddly exposed and used their hands anyway. A man who had believed for years that he hated the noise discovered that he disliked hearing strangers chew. He stopped coming at lunchtime.

The woman whose son sent photographs of woods brought the photographs one afternoon.

She sat with them on her knees. A leaf moved in the nearest tree enclosure, and she heard it.

That evening she wrote to her son to ask about the stairs in his house.

***

Under the shelf, the power was especially restrained.

The translucent creatures inhabited conditions that had not been fully described. They had routes, intervals of feeding, places where they gathered and places they avoided. The power learned these before it changed anything near them.

Where a repair would seal an occupied wet seam, it kept a neighboring seam damp. The animals moved along it over several nights. Where insects retreated behind a plate, it gave the gasket a fraction more depth, enough that a hurried hand could tighten the cover without crushing what sheltered there.

These refuges could have held far more life than the city presently contained. The power did not fill them.

It made room and watched what came.

***

Mara retired on a Thursday.

There was a cake in the workshop’s padded room. Too many people attended, and two had to stand in the doorway until somebody remembered the outer door was still open.

Her supervisor gave her a watch.

She had brought a list.

She explained where the spare lamps were, which cabinet drawer stuck in damp weather, and why the old sheets should not be recopied without keeping the originals. At the bottom was a note about a passage where the animals had recently disappeared.

Seli took the list.

“I went yesterday,” she signed. “The seam has turned toward the north wall.”

Mara asked whether she had marked the date.

Seli opened her notebook.

After a moment Mara closed it again and handed it back.

On her way home she stopped at the southern overlook. Her work bag was unusually light. She stood with both hands on the parapet, listening to a sound she would no longer be paid to distinguish.

The power stayed near her.

She did not seem grateful. She seemed tired, relieved, and unsure what she would do on Monday.

Nothing in this troubled the power.

***

That winter a room above an old valve station became available for public use.

It had two doors, good padding, and a ceiling gutter that could be reached without standing on the furniture. There were shelves for observation books and a wide table on which plans could lie open. The council paid for its heating after a petition with more signatures than anyone expected.

Mara came on Mondays. Sometimes Seli came. Sometimes householders brought notebooks containing shopping lists, birthdays, and careful drawings of stains.

They disagreed.

A man insisted that a wet seam had appeared a month earlier than the date his wife had written down. A workshop foreman dismissed an observation until a tenant asked him to come and look. Someone spilled tea on a new survey.

The power dried the tea before it reached the older papers.

Otherwise, it let the meeting proceed.

Outside, the city continued to be wet and loud. Pipes wore through. Children left and returned. Trees required tending. The buses were sometimes late.

At the southern parapet, people still lifted their heads when the sound changed.

There was more time, now, between certain failures and their consequences. There were a few places where a person could rest without leaving home. Some small lives survived the replacement of a cover.

Most of this went unnoticed.

On Monday morning Mara arrived at the valve station carrying her old work bag. Inside were pencils, the articulated mirror, and a book she had promised to lend someone.

She set the bag on a dry shelf.

Then she pulled a chair up to the table, where another person had already opened a plan.
