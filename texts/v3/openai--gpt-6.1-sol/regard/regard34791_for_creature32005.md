The power first attended to the stair.

Not the city’s stairs, whose difficulties were mostly known and whose worn places sometimes served as handholds. It attended to the great stair above, where a crack had begun inside the limestone, beyond the reach of anyone’s inspection.

In eleven winters, a person carrying a basket would put a foot upon the chipped corner. The stair would hold. The slab beneath it would not.

The power held the crack together.

It did not make the stone harder. Harder stone would carry footsteps differently, and the people below knew the ceiling partly by its vibrations. It joined the separated surfaces so precisely that the next footstep sounded much like the last.

Then it waited in the city.

It learned that a loose chip could be rubbish, a boundary marker, or the only way an elderly person could descend a particular step. It learned why one seemingly empty house must not be entered: its owner was away for the first time in many years, visiting a sister three bridges distant, and had been afraid that absence would be mistaken for surrender.

It watched a child carry a basin through a passage too narrow for the adults who had sent her. She returned with a scraped shoulder and was praised for being careful. The power noticed how she turned the injured side away from them.

It watched the woman beneath the bridge.

The feeler did sometimes bend against the current. The woman was right. But knowing this did not tell the power whether she wanted an answer, or whether her visits had become something for which an answer would be insufficient.

It did not lift the shell.

For a while, it did nothing else.

This was not because the city was perfect. Several people were hungry. A young man had begun sleeping badly because he could hear a roof joint shifting above his parents’ bed, and no one else believed the sound was new. An old woman kept her doorway sealed, not because she needed all three rooms, but because the last neighbor who had offered to help her move had already brought a measuring cord.

There was kindness here. There were also people made tired by the continual need to earn it.

The power wanted to help. It had to learn what its help would cost.

***

Its first appearance was to a water measurer named Sen.

She was alone beside the seventh notch, feeling its lip for a roughness that had caught fibers twice that week. Something touched the inside of her wrist.

She withdrew her hand.

The touch withdrew too.

She searched the bench, the wall, the narrow space behind her. No one was there. After a time she laid her hand down again, palm upward.

The touch returned, lightly.

It made a question badly. Then it stopped.

Sen waited.

The touch began again, placing each part more carefully.

*May I speak here?*

Sen gave the movement for consent, then added the gesture used with children who were likely to rush.

*Leave your hand where it is. I have not finished.*

The power left its touch where it was.

Sen asked who it was. It could not give her a useful name. She asked where it stood. It indicated the stair, the chamber, and a depth of stone beyond any place for which she had a gesture.

She asked whether it had put something into the water.

*No.*

Whether it intended to.

*Not without asking.*

Sen kept her hand still for a long time.

Then she said, *You must ask more people than me.*

***

The council’s first request was not for room, light, or relief from the noise.

It was for a test.

A notch in a discarded measuring chip was placed inside a covered basin. The power was asked to smooth the notch without moving the chip, warming the basin, or changing the water beneath it.

It did so.

The chip passed through seventeen hands. Someone objected that seventeen was too few. Someone else objected that it was a poor test because a sufficiently skillful mason might have done the same.

The power did not object to another test.

News of it traveled unevenly. In the eastern quarter, people understood that there was a mason who worked without tools. Near the back crack, it was said that the water measurers had acquired a new kind of thread. A child claimed to have touched it and was disbelieved, although she had.

Some inhabitants refused contact. The power did not wait at their thresholds.

Those who did speak with it asked for different things.

A sharper flint. A room. The return of a mother. An end to a neighbor’s tapping. Water that made firmer food. An assurance that the sound overhead would never bring the ceiling down.

It answered each request separately. Where it could offer something, it explained what would change. Where the answer required another person’s agreement, it did not treat the request as that agreement.

When it was asked to bring back the dead, it stayed through the person’s anger. It did not offer a voice from the recess, or a likeness sitting in the doorway.

That answer diminished it in the eyes of several inhabitants.

It allowed this.

***

Beyond the western wall was another hollow.

The power had found it immediately. Its floor was dry; its roof had no seam leading to the water. A layer of stone separated it from the city. The hollow was wide enough for a dozen people to sit without knees or shoulders touching.

It described the place to Sen.

She went with two masons to the wall. They tapped it, listened, argued, and returned with a third mason who had once accused the first two of misjudging a ceiling.

No one wanted the passage opened quickly.

The wall helped brace the cracked arch. A doorway would alter the route through the quarter. If the hollow became a dwelling, whose dwelling would it be? If it belonged to everyone, who would clean it? Would a person be allowed to sleep there? Would sleepers acquire a claim by remaining long enough?

The power waited while they decided.

In the meantime, it asked the young man to show it the roof joint he heard at night. Together they examined it without disturbing his parents’ bed. He had been right: one supporting flake was gradually turning.

The repair used a chip already lying beneath the bed. His parents consented. The power held the weight while he fitted the chip, then let the weight settle upon it.

That night he still listened.

After several nights, he slept.

***

The western passage was opened a little at a time.

The power bore the load that the wall had carried while the masons constructed a new support. They used fragments from an abandoned partition whose former owner came to watch, though there had been no obligation to invite him.

The new threshold was broad and level. Children would not be needed to carry people through it.

At first, only the masons entered.

There was less noise beyond the wall. The water could still be heard, but the hard crack from the square arrived softened. One mason sat down abruptly. Another kept touching the floor to be certain it was stone.

They had expected darkness. They had not expected to hear their own breathing.

Before admitting anyone else, they went back and asked whether the room could be made quieter still.

The power explained that the floor could continue to carry an arriving heel’s announcement while the air carried less of the sluice. They agreed to try it.

The result was imperfect. Near the doorway, a high trembling sound persisted. In the far corner, speech became clear enough that people discovered how loud they spoke.

One visitor found the quiet frightening and left.

No one called her ungrateful.

***

The old woman with the sealed door did not move into the new hollow.

She was asked once. She refused. After that, people stopped offering to wash her walls as a way of learning their dimensions.

She did eventually open her door to a neighbor who brought food and left the measuring cord at home.

The child with the scraped shoulder was no longer sent through the basin passage. Two adults took the longer route. They complained about it, and once the child offered to do the work herself because she missed being useful.

Her grandmother found a different task for her: teaching the younger children how to announce themselves on the new threshold. She took it seriously. For three days the western quarter endured excessive stamping.

The shell remained beneath its bridge.

When the woman next visited it, the power asked whether she wanted to know what it had observed.

She said yes.

It told her about the feeler.

For some time she said nothing. Then she leaned closer to the pool, watching.

The following day she came again.

***

There were further requests, and mistakes that had to be undone.

The quiet room acquired arguments along with its benches. A family tried to leave bedding in the best corner. Someone carved a rule into the wall before it had been agreed upon, and had to scrape it away. People who rarely met found that they did not all enjoy one another’s company.

Still, a person could go there and hear a story without holding the speaker’s wrist. Another could sit with a hand freely offered on a knee. Infants sometimes slept through an entire visit.

Above them, people continued to descend the bath stair.

Below, the mason tapped. Water passed through the seven notches. The translucent creature blocked a lane and was patiently steadied, while two people behind it quarreled over a basket.

The power remained available without filling every room.

When Sen was old, she sometimes rested in the western hollow. Her hearing had grown uncertain, and she preferred speech upon the hand again.

One day she felt the familiar question at the base of her thumb.

*May I speak here?*

She consented.

Then, before it could begin, she pressed the patient gesture against its touch.

She had something to tell it first.
