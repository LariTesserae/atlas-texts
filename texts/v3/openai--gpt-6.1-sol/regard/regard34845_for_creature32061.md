The power attends first to the water.

Not because the animal matters less, but because there is no way to help it apart from the places water can reach.

For a whole season, nothing changes that could be called an intervention. Rain arrives. The hollows fill and empty. One channel loses its roof; another begins under a bank farther uphill. The large animal beneath the spray moves its stones twice, then brings three of them back. The power follows it without lifting the turf.

It learns the difference between a chamber that is empty and one that has been abandoned. It learns how long a young animal can hold a root against a rising current. It learns that the warmth beneath the shed is useful, but not necessary, and that the warmth is not entirely innocent: once, in a hard frost, an animal follows it too far and reaches a length of drain where there is no room to turn.

The power waits beside that animal.

It could draw it out. Instead, it lets a little water collect behind its folded body. The animal feels the change, releases two pads, and tries another movement. One broad section turns within another. It comes backward down the drain, slowly, with its head tucked beneath it.

By morning it is under the tall grass.

The power keeps this in mind.

It also attends to the installation. It follows the pipes down into the valley, where the basin’s water becomes light in rooms it will never see. There are people there who need the machinery to continue working. Some are old. Some wake before dawn. In one room a small pump must not stop.

The power does not mistake distance for unimportance.

But at the intake, late in summer, it watches the young gather among the weed.

There is a difference between being carried down a stream and being drawn against a grille. In the stream, a root may offer purchase; a side hollow may interrupt the flow. Here the pull grows stronger as the young approach. Their pads find the metal, but the metal leads nowhere they can use.

Some pass through.

The power goes with them far enough to understand.

Then it returns.

—

The maintenance worker dreams, that night, of trying to climb a staircase while someone steadily draws it downward beneath his feet.

He wakes irritated rather than enlightened. His shoulder aches. Rain ticks against the window.

By breakfast he has forgotten the dream.

At the intake, he finds weed massed against one corner of the grille. He starts to pull it free with the long rake kept in the shed. A strand comes up bearing what looks like a short piece of pale root.

It bends around the rake.

He holds still.

There are pads along it, smaller than the heads of the screws in the shed door. They release and take hold again. Through the thin body he can see two grains of sand.

He lowers the rake into a puddle beside the concrete.

The little animal leaves the weed, searches the bare bottom, and stops.

The worker watches it for a while. Then he moves the weed into the puddle too.

This is not yet enough. The puddle will dry. He realizes that without assistance.

He carries the animal and its strand of weed to a shaded place where water slips beneath the bank.

That evening, his report includes a photograph. It is a poor photograph: reflected sky, his own thumb, something translucent against a dark stem. He sends it anyway.

The power does not make anyone believe him.

It keeps the next photograph from being blurred by a drop of water on the lens.

—

The work takes longer than a miracle would.

There are visits. Someone kneels in the wrong place and gets water inside both boots. Someone says that a finer screen will solve the problem, and someone else explains what a fine screen does when the flow is strong. A woman spends most of an afternoon watching the bank instead of the pool. Before she leaves, she marks the wet seams on a map.

The worker shows her the grass beside the shed.

They do not dig it up.

By the following summer, water approaches the intake across a wider, slower reach. Along one side, a shallow passage carries a small part of the flow back into the sheltered channels below the service road. Its bed is rough. There are stones to grip, dark spaces beneath them, and several ways out.

It is not perfect. Weed catches where nobody expected it to catch. The first heavy rain deposits gravel in the wrong place. The passage has to be watched, cleared, altered.

The power watches those who watch it.

When the worker is tired and tempted to leave an inspection until morning, it does not command him. He hears water where he has grown accustomed to hearing only the spillway. A thin, uneven trickle beside the road.

He goes to look.

When the woman prepares her recommendations, she finds she has written a sentence that assumes the animals’ routes will remain fixed. She crosses it out. She has seen enough now to know better.

The wet seams are given room to move.

—

There are still young that do not survive.

The power could keep every pool full, every bank intact, every current mild. It could hold the basin in one enduring, favorable season. But then the streams would cease finding their beds. The animals would have no new hollows to discover. Roots would grow into channels that no longer changed, and the living ground would become a carefully maintained enclosure.

So rain remains rain. Winter comes.

What changes is smaller.

The drain beneath the shed no longer offers a narrowing passage an animal cannot turn within. The maintenance plan leaves the tall grass standing. No one pours anything into that patch because it seems a convenient place for it to disappear.

The largest animal continues to rest under the spray. It never knows how far the pipes descend or how many people have argued over a drawing on a table.

One morning it encounters a new stone in the shallow passage.

The stone is angular, pale on one side, dark on the other. It has been placed there by a person who was thinking about the animal’s feet.

The animal grips it, passes over it, and continues beneath the bank.

Later it comes back.

It works the stone loose, carries it uphill, and sets it into the sill of its own chamber.

The power finds this pleasing.

It had not intended to furnish the chamber. But the stone belongs there now.
