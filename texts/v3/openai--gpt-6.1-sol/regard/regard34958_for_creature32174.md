The first thing the power did was take its weight off the paving.

It had come into the hollow at night, intending no disturbance. But the woman nursing the infant felt something through her bare heel that was neither a footstep nor the movement inside the wall.

The suspended bed turned a little on its cords.

She drew her foot away.

Outside, the power stopped. It understood that it had arrived badly.

For the rest of that night it remained above the threshold, without touching the stone. The small animals continued beneath it. Once, the woman looked toward the doorway. She saw nothing she could name, but the bitter smell of the bed cords had become less oppressive.

“Not nearer,” she said.

It did not come nearer.

By morning she had slept.

***

There were several things the power could have done immediately.

It could have filled the hollow with water. It could have softened the mountain’s sharp rim, warmed the paving, made the grain impossible to spoil. It could have removed every creature that bit, pinched, spoiled flour, or waited above a child’s route.

It watched a grazer pull greenery from a cushion. In the gap left by its mouth, a pale flowering plant unfolded. Three small animals entered the exposed stems. One of those animals was caught by another.

The power waited.

During the morning, Tava caught her sleeve between a branch and the wall.

She set down her collecting vessel and tried the branch with her good arm. Then she looked at the place beside her where light had acquired an unfamiliar depth.

“If you mean to help,” she said, “hold it there.”

The power held the branch.

“Not back. There.”

It corrected the pressure.

Tava freed her sleeve and examined the cuff. Nothing had torn.

“You can let go.”

It let go.

She resumed her work. After a little while, she offered it the hooked pole.

***

News of the visitor moved through the town without an alarm.

By midday, people had come to the northern court carrying things they wanted explained or repaired. A cracked jar. A knife worn almost out of existence. A child who had been coughing for nine nights.

Others brought nothing and stood well back.

The power did not make the jar whole before asking whether the crack had been deliberate. It had not. The owner wanted the vessel to hold water, but wanted the notch inside left where it was.

The knife’s owner wanted a wider blade, not a different knife.

The child’s father said yes before the power had finished asking. Her mother asked whether it would hurt. The child wanted to know whether she would still have to drink the steeped leaves.

It took the sickness from her chest without taking the chest’s knowledge of how to heal. She coughed twice more, out of habit and irritation, then fell asleep against her father.

The roof-keeper watched this closely.

He did not bring his apprentice forward. Later, when the others had gone, he asked the visitor to come and listen to a roof.

***

Water took longer.

Some people wanted more. Some wanted enough. These proved to be different requests, and neither had an agreed measure.

The storekeeper brought her vessels into the long room. Tava brought a leaf. The man who had built the straight stair brought drawings for channels, although the power could see through the mountain.

Seeing through it was not the same as knowing what the channels would do.

Far below the pale square lay chambers that widened and narrowed with the seasons. Water gathered there in thin films. The green veins carried it upward at dusk, accompanied by substances the animals needed and the seedlings tolerated.

There were living things in the chambers.

The power could not promise that opening them would leave those things undisturbed. It said so.

This disappointed almost everyone.

“Then leave the square alone,” said the woman whose arm had swollen.

For once, no one disputed her.

Instead, the power asked to examine the empty house beside the storehouse. Its roof had sagged, but the walls were sound. There had been no collecting branches above it for eleven years.

In the court behind that house it made a vessel of dark stone.

The vessel was no larger than a grain jar. Its lid fitted tightly, and its rim was polished. Water gathered inside it from the air, slowly, without cooling the surrounding walls or drawing moisture from the leaves.

The first morning, it held less than a cup.

“That will not be enough,” said the storekeeper.

“No,” said the power. “Not yet.”

For twelve mornings they measured it against the trees. Tava watched the youngest leaves. The roof-keeper checked the walls. Children were sent to count the moss islands, a task they performed with enthusiasm and inconsistent results.

Nothing nearby dried.

On the thirteenth morning, the storekeeper asked for the vessel to gather more.

By the end of the season it supplied enough drinking water for the town. People still tended the trees. The animals still went to the square. The storekeeper kept accounts, because the vessel could be made larger and someone would eventually ask.

There were arguments about where its water belonged.

The power attended two of them. At the third, the grandmother told it that being able to make water did not give it a useful opinion about her sister’s marriage.

It accepted this and went outside.

***

The broken lookout stair was easier.

The power waited until people had shown it how they crossed, carrying an empty bier for the purpose. Then it joined the two heights with a broad, slightly sloping tread.

It left the seam visible.

The next time someone died, the bearers paused at the place out of long practice. One adjusted his grip. Then all four went on without the longer step.

Senn heard about it afterward.

“Good,” he said. “Now do something about the wind.”

The power asked whether he wanted the ridge sheltered.

“I want not to be on it.”

His daughters began to explain.

“I heard him,” said the power.

It did not promise he would never die. It did offer to ease the pain in his knees. Senn considered this a more sensible conversation, and made it wait while he finished a difficult part of his basket.

***

It stayed through the cold season.

Not all its work was successful at first. A warmer floor attracted creatures that had previously remained inside a wall. A repaired lintel interrupted a route used by the flat blue animals. The power undid the warmth, cut a shallow passage behind the lintel, and listened more carefully thereafter.

When a hunter settled above the children’s doorway, it moved the hunter to an outer tree. When the hunter returned, it moved it farther.

The children were still taught the three strokes of the alarm.

No one was required to be grateful. One woman disliked the visitor because her husband now asked it questions he ought to have asked her. The power declined to settle that complaint without him present.

Sometimes it was asked to do nothing.

It became good at staying close without pressing upon the paving.

***

The infant acquired a separate share before she acquired a name.

By then her mother slept in longer intervals. She still checked the cords and turned the clothing inside out. The scraping in the wall had been traced to a narrow creature that ate the bitter sap and little else. The power had offered to remove it.

She had asked whether there were more.

There were.

They repaired the seam instead.

One evening, with the baby asleep, the woman sat in her doorway beside the visitor. Across the hollow, leaves lifted toward the dark. A small animal touched her foot and continued along the wall.

“Will you stay?” she asked.

“If you wish.”

“Not in the doorway. People have to pass.”

The power moved aside.

After a while she put the child into its keeping and went to wash.
