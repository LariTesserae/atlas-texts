The power noticed the point that had found the granite.

It did not lift the ice away. The creature had purchase now, and water still moved between the shelf and the stone. Its other points would follow, slowly. To hasten them would require understanding how the clear body held its grains, how it loosened one attachment without losing the others. The power could learn this. It did not need to learn it by touching.

For most of the night, it watched.

Behind the wall, the freight lift made eleven journeys. The reservoir release diminished. A new fringe of ice formed over the open centre, then stopped where the warmth from below reached it. The creature advanced far enough that its grey centre rested on stone.

Nothing in the court changed because it was being watched.

By morning, the power had begun to distinguish between the cold that belonged to this place and dangers brought here by decisions made elsewhere.

The bird came to drink. It took a young creature from the ceramic ring, shook its beak once, and flew up past the reservoir.

The power let it go.

Later, a maintenance instruction passed through the building’s systems. There had been a minor increase in resistance at the drain. No one was concerned. At the next service interval, the intake would receive a heated solvent flush.

The instruction described the court as an unoccupied drainage recess.

The power held that instruction for a moment.

Then, in the office six floors below, a technician found that the drain’s diagnostic record included something she had not seen before: magnified images of the ceramic ring, taken at intervals across the last reversal. The images were clear enough to show a fringe opening, a mineral grain passing through a channel, a body flattening before the water came.

She enlarged one image. Then another.

The flush was postponed.

The power did not tell her what the creatures were. It did not supply a name, or claim that they were the last of their kind. There were passages beneath the plate it had not yet followed, and water routes extending into the mountain. It had seen enough to prevent the flush. That was not the same as having seen everything.

The technician went down the three metal steps that afternoon. She brought a lamp, a magnifier, and a shallow collecting dish.

She stayed longer than she had intended.

When she left, the dish was empty.

Over the following weeks, the court acquired a few protections so ordinary that they could survive without anyone remembering why they had first been made.

The reservoir’s lowest release setting was raised by a small amount. It still varied with the weather and the city’s needs, but it could no longer be closed completely during routine maintenance.

The heat beneath the hollow was traced to a return line. Its replacement had been planned for summer. The new line would be more efficient; it would also leave the granite cold enough to freeze through.

The replacement went ahead, with a short section left in thermal contact with the rock. An engineer calculated the loss, frowned at it, and accepted it. Keeping the small opening cost less than lighting the service stair.

The solvent flush was replaced by a slower cleaning cycle. No new material was applied to the stone.

On the inside of the maintenance door, below the safety instructions, someone fixed a plain notice:

**Living film present. Do not scrape, disinfect, or allow intake to dry.**

The words were inadequate. The creatures were not a film, exactly. But the next person who entered the court would read them before reaching for a brush.

The power watched that happen too.

It was tempted, once, to do more.

A hard freeze closed the hollow farther than any in the technician’s records. Beneath the shelf, three creatures had folded into cloudy beads. A fourth had sealed itself across a shallow groove where the water was thinning.

The power could have warmed the whole slab. It could have made every body open.

Instead, it examined the water beneath the ice.

The return line was still working. The trickle still reached the centre. Along one margin, the film had narrowed to less than the thickness of a clear point, but it had not broken.

It waited.

One of the beads did not wake at the thaw. The others did. For two days, the dead one lay among them, its dark grains showing through an edge that no longer held its shape. Then a stronger release carried it beneath the plate.

The power followed it there.

Below the court was no great hidden chamber, only a slanting gap between ceramic and granite. Water passed through it in several thin sheets. In one sheltered corner, clear bodies lay crowded around a warm fastening. Farther down were patches of dry stone, old mineral deposits, and a route to a drain that could not be climbed.

The power made no abundance appear there. It did arrange for the inspection record to include this gap, so that replacing the plate would not mean sealing it.

Through summer, the visible colony spread into the drill grooves. Through winter, it withdrew. The bird returned irregularly. Some creatures divided; some vanished. One grew a crooked fringe after a shift in the foundations. It held less well in a strong current, but fed successfully beside the ridge.

The technician learned to recognize it.

Once, kneeling by the hollow, she saw its points rise just before the puddle changed shape. She checked the time against the building’s load-transfer log. On her next visit she brought no collecting dish.

The court was still absent from the public maps. There was no viewing window, no path built through it, no lamp left burning over the basin. Those who needed to enter could enter. Those who cared for the building now had reason to step carefully.

Late one afternoon, sunlight returned through the ice. A loose shelf turned and caught. Beneath it, two clear edges overlapped on the granite.

The power remained near enough to notice if the water stopped.

For now, it watched the light move down the wall, and did nothing.
