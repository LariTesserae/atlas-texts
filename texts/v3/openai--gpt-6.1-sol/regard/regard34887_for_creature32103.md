At first the power did nothing.

It attended to the town through a day and a night, then through rain. It watched water gather on the roofs, hesitate against a raised edge, and run into the channels. Beneath the lower court it followed the slow descent of grit through the settling basin. It listened to the windlass and to the girl who muttered at it, believing that if she turned quickly enough the bucket would somehow become lighter.

It did not mistake the quiet of the place for contentment. Nor did it take every complaint for a wish that ought to be granted.

The woman with the keys complained of the cat, but looked for it when it did not come. The old mason complained of people who no longer asked his advice, and of people who asked it when he was eating. The young woman who thought of leaving loved the coolness of the passage beside her house. She could love it and leave.

The power had time to learn these things.

Its first intervention was under the northern retaining wall.

There, behind the dark seam, a small passage had become stopped with clay. Water pressed against the obstruction. In winter it froze in a pocket beside one of the stones that carried the wall.

The power loosened the clay.

Nothing broke. No spring burst into the court. A little cloudy water emerged, carrying grains too fine to notice, and by afternoon the seam had returned to its usual darkness. But the pocket behind the wall was empty. When frost came, it would not prise the stone outward.

No one saw this happen. A lizard on the wall moved into the sun.

The power next considered the cistern. It could have filled it from beneath and kept it full forever. It could have made the grain multiply inside its sacks. Such things would have relieved much labor.

For a while it watched the woman with the keys measuring grain for a household that had already received its share. She listened to the explanation, went back inside, and emerged with a smaller measure. She recorded the difference on a strip of wood.

The power did not know yet what inexhaustible grain would do to the arrangements among these people, or to the fields below that supplied them. It could learn. Meanwhile, winter was approaching, and there were repairs whose value was less uncertain.

At the next cleaning of the settling basin, the man scraping its floor found that his tool entered a joint it had never entered before. He lifted a thin flake of limestone. Beneath it was a crack.

He called the other man over.

They disagreed about how long it had been there. Each said he would have noticed it at the previous cleaning. They went for the mason, who descended the steps slowly and knelt with assistance he pretended not to require.

He put one fingertip into the crack.

Afterward there was work: lifting, carrying, an argument over lime, a stone cut twice because the first cut was poor. The cistern was closed for part of a day. People had to draw their water beforehand.

When the repair was finished, the level fell less quickly between rains.

Everyone was pleased, although several said the trouble could have been avoided if the basin had been properly inspected.

The power accepted this account.

It spent some attention on the old mason’s wrist and knee. The knee had changed too much to be restored without changing much else about him. But there was inflammation around an old injury in his hand, and this it eased while he slept.

The following morning he picked up the block he had been intending to split.

He did not split it that day. He moved it nearer the door, where the light was better.

For the child who examined shells, the power did something different.

Among the loose chips beyond the last wall lay a stone with the impression of a small creature almost perfectly preserved. It had been there for years, face downward. Rain had washed soil around it.

The power turned it over.

The child found it while searching for a flat piece to slide across a shallow bowl of rock. He forgot the game. He carried the stone home in both hands, stopping often to look.

None of the adults could tell him exactly what it was.

In the lower country, however, there was a woman who cut and polished stone for door surrounds. She had seen such impressions before, and had a shelf of them in her workshop. The power knew where she lived. It could have supplied the child with every answer at once.

Instead, when a carrier next came up the ridge, the stone was on the storehouse step, where the child had set it while helping count the panniers. The carrier recognized it.

“My sister keeps those,” he said.

The child asked six questions before the loads were off the animals. The carrier answered two, guessed at one, and promised to take the others down with him.

This was not the end of the child’s ignorance. It was the beginning of a correspondence.

The young woman’s departure required no intervention.

She spoke after the harvest, while her father was fitting a peg to a shutter. He continued fitting it for some time. Then he asked where she meant to stay.

There was sorrow in the room, and expense, and a concern neither of them put well into words. The power remained nearby without removing any of these things.

Later, when the young woman walked down the ridge with her bundle, it kept a loose stone from rolling beneath her foot at a difficult turn. She never knew. Her father, looking after her from the upper square, saw her pause to adjust the bundle and then continue.

The power did not promise that she would return. It meant to attend to her elsewhere as carefully as it attended here.

There were other acts, small enough to disappear among ordinary causes.

A sound beam arrived in a load whose poorer timber had already been discussed at length. A fever broke before the sick man’s strength was exhausted. The pale cat, shut accidentally in an unused room, scratched at the shutter just as someone passed below.

In a lean year the pear tree bore no more fruit than its roots could support. The quarrel remained a quarrel. But the grain coming up from the lower country was dry, and none of it spoiled in the storehouse.

The power did not find the town complete. People here could be unkind. A useful custom could burden the same person year after year simply because no one remembered assigning it. There were hurts that would not be mended by a better beam or an easier winter.

It continued to listen. Sometimes, when a person was about to speak, it gave them a little more time—not a command, not a new thought, only a moment in which the face before them became easier to see. What they did with that moment remained theirs.

Most often it worked where their care was already reaching and falling short.

When the girl grew strong enough to lift the cistern cover, it did not make the slab light. Someone showed her how to set her feet, where to put her hands, and why there should be another person beside her.

When the mason died, it did not keep his chair occupied. People carried his stones outside and found uses for some of them. The block by the door went to the covered work court. A younger mason split it badly, kept both pieces, and tried again on another.

At the northern wall, moisture still gathered into a bead.

The town was not made exempt from weather or loss. Nor was it left to them.

Toward morning, a latch lifted. A woman stepped into the lane with a basin and emptied the water where it would reach the pear roots. She stood a moment in the cool air, looking between the houses at the country below.

Then she went inside.

Beneath the ring of stones, the water found a narrow root that had been struggling in dry earth. The power followed it down, making room where the soil had hardened, and stopped when it had done enough.
