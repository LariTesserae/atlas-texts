The power does not wake the lizard.

It attends first to the weight of the animal on the trunk, the warmth that has not yet reached its back, the foot hanging loose beside the wood. It notices the healed tail and the shortened toe. Neither needs to be made new. The animal can climb, swim, find shelter, catch food. The old injuries have become part of how it moves.

There is something less settled in the tissues beneath its scales: substances gathered a little at a time from fish, from mud, from the animals that have fed in the outer channels. They have no smell the lizard can distinguish. Its tongue gives it no reason to turn away.

The power follows these substances back.

It follows them through a fish’s gills and the fine sediment of a creek, through water that enters the basin on the returning tide. Beyond the trees, it finds a discharge pipe. Beyond the pipe, a settling tank, a faulty valve, a number copied from one report into the next.

The machinery is not malicious. It is working as it has been made to work. The people who tend it know different portions of its operation. One knows that a pump has begun drawing more current. Another knows that the water downstream sometimes carries an unusual smell. Someone has asked for a repair and been told it can wait.

The power could close the pipe at once. It sees where the backed-up water would go.

Instead, it makes the fault impossible to overlook.

During the morning inspection, the valve fails to return to its marked position. The worker tries it twice. A supervisor comes down to look. The flow is diverted into the holding tank, and this time the sample is taken while the fault is occurring.

There will be expense, argument, an interruption of work. There will also be wages protected during the interruption, and help for those whose work must change. The power does not purchase the wetland’s safety with someone else’s hunger.

It stays with the matter until the repair is more than an entry on a form.

In the basin, dawn continues.

Sunlight reaches the lizard’s trunk. It moves forward, drawing the hanging foot up beneath its body. A fly settles near its eye and is shaken off.

The power does not keep the crabs from its mouth. Nor does it lead prey toward it. The crab taken during the night had its own movements through the roots, its own shelter, its own use for the coming day. Attention to the lizard does not erase attention to the crab.

For now, the animal has eaten. It has a place to warm itself and a chamber in which to cool. The power lets those things be enough.

Elsewhere, there is work to do.

An old channel has narrowed where earth was piled beyond the wetland years before. Water that once spread slowly through the basin now reaches some hollows late and leaves others too quickly. The power does not cut a straight new course through the reeds. It brings the obstruction to the notice of people who can remove it, and gives them time to learn where the water used to pass.

The work is done from the outer bank. Little is removed at first. Through a dry season and a wet one, the creek is watched.

When water returns along the old route, it comes shallowly. It darkens the clay around roots that are still alive. Small fish enter behind it. Some pools remain separate; some ridges remain dry.

The rain, when it comes, is not a reward arranged for the lizard. It falls across a much wider country. The first drops strike leaves too dry to bend under them. Then the sound thickens, and water begins running down the trunks.

The lizard leaves the low chamber before the creek fills it. It goes inland to the hollow beneath the fallen palm. The power has checked that the way remains open.

For several days the basin is louder. Frogs call. Water touches wood that has been dry for weeks. A branch falls, taking a length of vine with it. Beneath the bank, part of the chamber’s ceiling gives way.

The power allows the branch to fall. It allows the chamber to change. Holding every root and hollow in place would eventually leave no room for what grows next.

It does prevent a road from being laid across the basin.

That takes longer than moving earth. It takes measurements made in the wet season rather than only in the dry, and maps that include the smallest connecting creeks. It takes people listening to those who have watched this water for years. The power lends its strength to their attention, their patience, their ability to keep returning to the question until another route is chosen.

The lizard knows none of this.

Weeks later, it swims through the outer channel. Its head lies low on the surface; behind it, the bent tail moves from side to side. It crosses the place where the warmer inflow used to gather fish, finds little there, and continues.

At the next bend it climbs out among the roots. It pauses with its belly on the bank, then pulls itself into the shade.

There is no sign waiting for it. No voice explains what has been done.

Ahead lies ground it can still cross, water it can still enter, and a fallen tree with a hollow large enough to receive it.
