The power did not begin by bringing water.

It had noticed the cups, but it had also noticed what happened when a person took one up in the morning: the slight easing of the fingers around its curve, the attention given to an empty thing. It did not suppose that every hollow was waiting to be filled.

For a long time it watched.

It watched the woman choose a place for the next leaf. It watched the old man steady a broken hinge against the notch in his table. It watched a child offer her second hand to a grazer, then her first again, postponing whatever she had been sent to fetch.

It could have counted the inhabitants. Instead, it learned whom they looked for.

There were things it did not understand. The wall, for instance, was being extended without enclosing any more space. The large animal beneath the saplings might have been resting, or holding something up, or both. When the woman stopped working, one of her shadows sometimes continued for several moments.

The power did not mistake its own uncertainty for theirs.

One evening, two people sat in a dark room holding a cord under tension. A new joint had not yet cooled. One person had begun to shake a little in the forearm, though he kept his fingers steady.

“Not yet,” said the other.

The power waited with them.

When the joint was ready, it made a small sound, no louder than a seed casing opening. Both people loosened their grip. The cord held.

The power learned something from this.

The next day, it came to the court in a form that could fit beneath the branches.

It had two hands. These were not especially skillful. When it lifted a fallen seed case, it held it too firmly and spoiled one of the pegs inside.

The woman looked at the fragments.

“That one was usable,” she said.

“Yes,” said the power.

It put the pieces in the basket for things that might have another use.

For several days it carried bowls, gathered chips, and waited beside doors while animals decided whether to come out. It did not disguise what it was, but neither did it require anyone to stop working in order to acknowledge it.

The leaf-maker gave it her board to carry. The board was heavier than it looked.

“Where?” it asked.

“Here.”

She sat beneath the tree. It placed the board across her knees.

Later, while she pinched the edge of a leaf, it asked whether there was anything she wanted.

She glanced toward her bowl.

“The fine splinters are in the house.”

It brought them.

That was not the only question it had meant to ask. It let the answer stand.

After a while, people began to give it work that was difficult to give one another. They asked it to hold a balcony while a leather-skinned animal shifted beneath it. They asked it to sit with a creature whose new jaw frightened it whenever it opened. The creature would not eat, but it tolerated the power’s hand beside its head. By morning, it had begun to test the jaw against a scrap of fruit.

The oldest man asked for better light.

The power made no new sun. It moved the brightness already falling through his doorway onto his table, without moving the warmth. He examined three old repairs and found a place where a fourth could be made.

“Leave it there,” he said.

It did.

The first thing the power changed without being asked was a tight ribbon behind a sleeping child’s bed.

It had seen the ribbon take hold of the frame and continue, slowly, toward her wrist. It loosened the growth and tucked the end against a post. In the morning it showed the household what had happened.

They cut the ribbon shorter.

After that, the power sometimes walked with the person who checked the shutters at night. They found places neither could have seen alone.

One afternoon the woman finished a leaf and did not reach for more powder.

She put her palms against her thighs. Her unfinished shadow kept moving.

“Does that trouble you?” the power asked.

“Sometimes.”

“Would you like it to stop?”

She watched the shadow’s fingers.

“Not always. Sometimes it finds an edge I hadn’t thought of.”

They waited until it finished. Its leaf was only a dark shape on the paving, but the woman studied it before standing.

The following afternoon, when she rested, the shadow rested too.

She looked down sharply.

“Only for now,” said the power.

She kept her hands where they were.

In time, there was a request it could not answer by carrying something.

A young person who fitted bark strips came to it with an empty basket.

“I want to go somewhere else,” he said.

The power asked what he meant by elsewhere.

He did not know. He knew the passage beyond the gate. He had carried tiles through it hundreds of times. He wanted, once, not to arrive in the court.

The power went with him to the wall.

It did not straighten the turns or undo the man’s patient work. Beside the gate, in a section where no animal slept, it asked permission to fit another door.

The wall-maker considered this.

“Will it pull on the tiles?”

“No.”

“Will anything come through?”

“Only if you let it.”

The man tested the nearest peg with his thumbnail, then fetched his mallet.

They worked together. The new door was narrow and had a latch on either side.

Beyond it lay a place the power had made ready but not furnished: a length of pale ground, a little shade, room to turn around. From there, if the young person wished, it could lead him farther. He could also come home.

He stood in the opening for some time.

Then he set down his basket and stepped through.

The power stayed beside the door. The wall-maker returned to his tiles.

That evening, the young person came back carrying a stone unlike any of theirs. Several people handled it. The old man held it against his improved light.

No one was asked to leave.

The power remained, though not always in the court. There were now people who called for it, and people who preferred the familiar hands of their neighbors. It learned to hear the difference.

Once, beneath the tree, the woman offered it the end of a thread.

“Hold this.”

It held the thread while she fitted a rib into a leaf. When she was ready, she reached for it.

The power let go.
