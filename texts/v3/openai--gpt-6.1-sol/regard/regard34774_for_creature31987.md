The power first notices the bowl.

It is wedged securely enough in the branch, but the snails are reaching its rim. One has extended almost its whole body over the edge. Below it, the woman’s basket contains the ones that eat bark.

The power could put the snail back. It could make the bowl deeper, or change the snail’s appetite. Instead, it holds the bowl steady until the woman returns.

She catches the window brace, hooks her foot under the root, and reaches for her work. For a moment she looks at the hand beside her own.

The hand lets go.

The stranger wears no working stones. Nothing about them suggests that they need any. Their loose sleeve moves in the water, but their body remains where it is.

The woman points upward.

In the air room she pulls a wrap around her shoulders, then reaches for another. The stranger accepts it. They sit opposite one another with their feet below the silver floor.

“You’ll want to bind that sleeve,” she says.

“Yes.”

“Unless you’re only visiting.”

“I am visiting.”

She waits. Below them, a tool knocks against wood.

“What do you need?” she asks.

“To understand what would help.”

She laughs once, without much amusement.

Then she looks more carefully at the stranger.

Outside the room, a school of fish passes the window. Their shadows travel across the stranger’s face, although there is no light there that should cast them.

The woman folds the unused end of her wrap into her lap.

“Whom would it help?”

“You. The others. What lives here.”

“That will take longer.”

“I have time.”

She takes the stranger down to finish the snails.

***

For several days the power does nothing that anyone can identify as a miracle.

It accompanies the woman to shore. It carries a covered vessel without asking what is in it. It holds cord while a support is bound, and holds it too tightly until corrected.

It listens to the square keeper explain why a shoot that looks inconvenient must remain and why a much prettier one has to go. It visits the woman who repairs ventilation stems. It sits beside a man whose knees no longer bend well enough for the lower handholds.

It does not ask him whether he misses working. He tells it anyway.

At night it listens to people discussing a roof that will be ready in six years. One of them expects to be dead before then. She has strong opinions about its curve.

The power goes to the graves, but not alone. The snail keeper takes it while gathering seeds.

At one grave she clears the leaves with the side of her foot. The name has been cut twice, the second set of marks deeper than the first.

“My sister,” she says.

The power waits.

“Her room went bad. It was winter. We thought she was sleeping.”

The woman crouches to pick a seed from between two roots.

“If you’ve come to tell us to live on the shore, you can save yourself the trouble.”

“I haven’t.”

“She liked it here.”

The power knows this. It knows a great deal more: the sister’s impatience with badly wound cord, the particular chamber in which she slept best, the song she would begin and then forget halfway through.

It does not turn that knowledge into a performance.

The woman has brought it to a grave, not asked it for an imitation.

They finish gathering the seeds.

***

When the power speaks of what it can offer, it does so in the largest air chamber, where people can disagree aloud.

Not everyone fits. Some take turns coming up. Messages are passed to the people waiting below, and several arguments have to begin again when someone changes places.

The offer is fresh air.

Not a larger supply that must be hauled down from the surface. Not new lungs, or a change to their children. Fresh air held beneath a roof, renewing itself without a tube.

A room made this way would not sour. If its roof broke, the air would gather beneath whatever covering remained. If there were no covering, it would be lost into the lake, without dragging anyone upward.

“Can it spread?” asks the square keeper.

“Only where you ask for it.”

“Does it grow?”

“No.”

“Then don’t call it a plant.”

The power had not intended to.

The ventilation worker asks whether it will harm the stems already there. An old man asks whether it can be taken away. A child asks whether a fish can go into it and come back out.

Someone asks what the town will owe.

“Nothing.”

This answer causes more discussion than the others.

At last the snail keeper says, “Show us in an empty room.”

They choose one of the abandoned houses at the western end. Not the one with eggs.

The power does not straighten its roof or clear its doorway. The square keeper makes a passage through the growth, preserving a branch she thinks useful. Two people fit a brace. The ventilation worker examines the ceiling and says it will hold.

Then the power puts a hand against the wood.

A silver surface gathers below it.

For a while everyone watches from underneath.

The snail keeper goes first. She draws herself through, takes a breath, and remains still.

“Well?” someone calls from below, though the water ruins the word.

She puts her hand down through the opening and makes the sign for good air.

The room smells of wet roots. It is neither warm nor cold beyond what the chamber itself would make it. The old walls still hum.

They test it for weeks.

They leave it empty. They crowd into it. They close the doorway with a mat, then remove the mat. The ventilation worker brings a smoking scrap from shore in a covered cup and is sharply told to do that test without children present.

The smoke clears.

Fish continue to use the walls.

The power answers the same questions more than once. It does not take repetition as an insult.

***

The first permanent requests are not for private houses.

One chamber is placed along the route to the shore landing. Another goes beside the southern rail, set well back from the drop. The square keeper insists that its supports occupy an existing tangle, rather than the clear water of the square.

A third is made large enough for someone to lie down entirely above water.

They argue about that one.

Some want it near the workshops. Others want it beside the upper houses, where small children live. The man with stiff knees says that whichever place they choose, they should stop making the entrance a ladder.

They build a shallow rising passage, with rails on both sides.

The power waits until the passage is finished.

Afterward, households begin asking for air beneath their own roofs. Some keep their ventilation stems. Some remove them after a season. One woman refuses the gift for her sleeping chamber, though she uses the public rooms every day.

No one is required to explain.

The power leaves no shrine. When someone proposes a carved marker in the square, it asks them to mark the new breathing places instead.

These signs must be understandable to a visitor who is frightened and already short of breath.

The children help decide them.

***

There are other requests.

The cold troubles people who cannot move much. The power provides warmth where it is wanted, confined to the dry platforms so that the lake around them remains cold. The ventilation worker checks this too. She has become difficult to surprise.

An elderly woman asks for relief from pain in her hands. The power gives it. She spends the following morning doing far too much and is furious when her daughter says so.

A boy asks whether the power can recover a knife lost beyond the southern rail.

It can.

The square keeper brings a list of other things lost there. The list includes three tools, a weight, two bowls, and a carved animal.

The old woman who lost the animal sits holding it for a long time. One of its legs is gone. She recognizes the missing leg; it was gone before she dropped it.

For once, nobody asks her to tell the story.

The power does not leave immediately after these gifts. It remains long enough to hear complaints about them, and to make changes.

It learns that a refuge can become someone’s storeroom unless this is forbidden. It learns that relief from one task does not make people agreeable about the next. It learns that some of the time formerly spent checking tubes is spent doing nothing at all.

It is pleased by that.

***

One afternoon the snail keeper returns to her doorway.

She hooks her foot beneath the root and settles the bowl against her stomach. The snails have been busy while she was away. There is more bark damage than she expected.

A neighbor arrives with a second basket. They work on opposite sides of the opening, handing doubtful specimens across.

When they need air, they rise into the little chamber now held beneath the doorway’s arch. Neither has to climb through the house.

The neighbor has news about someone returning from the valleys. They talk for longer than the work requires.

Below them, a snail reaches the edge of the bowl and begins its slow descent toward the floor.

The power is no longer there to hold it.

The woman notices, puts down her cup, and reaches into the water.
