The power noticed the woman when the door struck the wall.

It had already noticed the fault beneath the ridge, the water held in narrow beds of stone, and the small animals sleeping below the frozen basins. It knew where the next rockfall would begin. But the knock of wood against limestone drew its attention to something that knowledge of the mountain did not explain.

The woman stopped. The boy did not.

“Behind you,” she said.

He looked behind himself.

“The door.”

By then the damage was done: a shallow scrape along the edge her father had fitted so carefully. She reached back to feel it, could not reach, and became angrier.

The power could have lifted the door into its new frame. It could have made every door fit every opening in the country.

Instead, it appeared on the landing, leaving room for someone to pass.

The boy saw it first. Years afterward, he would remember its appearance differently whenever he tried to describe it. What remained constant was that the dog on the roof stood up, considered barking, and came down the steps.

“May I take the lower end?” the power asked.

The woman looked at it.

“Can you keep it off the wall?”

“Yes.”

“Then take it.”

They carried the door uphill.

At the new room, the opening was too narrow. The aunt’s eldest daughter said she had warned them. The woman said she had warned nobody in words that could be understood. Someone went for a plane.

The power held the door steady while they worked.

It did not remove the scrape. When the woman ran her thumb across it, she said that her father would have had something to say about this.

“Would you like it mended?” the power asked.

She considered.

“Not that part. The bottom corner. It catches in wet weather.”

So the bottom corner was mended.

By evening, people had heard that something extraordinary had arrived and was helping hang a door. Several came to see. A man brought a sick lamb. A girl brought her grandmother’s spectacles, broken at the bridge. Another man brought papers establishing, in his opinion, that his household had been cheated of water for eighty-three years.

The power examined the lamb first.

This offended the man with the papers, who had arrived before it.

The power did not confuse his offense with his claim being false.

***

For a time it stayed in an unused room beside a cistern. It accepted no rent exemption and therefore caused a dispute about what rent could reasonably be charged to a being that did not sleep, eat, or require shelter.

It listened.

Not only at meetings. At meetings, the people accustomed to speaking generally spoke. It listened beside ovens, in workshops, on grazing benches, and in the rooms of people who could not reach the cistern steps. It asked questions of children without assuming their answers were charming. It listened to the household that wanted its failing district saved, and the household next door that wanted help leaving it.

It learned that “we have always done this” could mean several things, including “this works,” “I am afraid,” and “my grandfather profited by it.”

There were requests it could answer immediately.

An infected wound did not need to wait for a council. Neither did a child struggling for breath. The power healed where healing was wanted. It did not require the healed to become grateful, agreeable, or useful afterward.

The woman who had carried the door asked about her knee only after asking about two other people.

“Yours too,” the power said.

She sat down reluctantly, as though this were an indulgence she might have to account for.

When she stood again, the pain was gone.

She walked to the landing, came back, and walked to it again.

Then she began to cry, angrily, because she had forgotten how much of each day had been spent deciding whether a journey was worth the hurt.

The power stayed beside her. It did not explain what her suffering had taught her.

***

Water required longer consideration.

There was water beneath the plateau, but opening every hidden seam would empty some springs, drown burrows, and draw salts into soil that had taken generations to gather. A large river descending through the settlements would have been a catastrophe disguised as abundance.

The power worked with the channel keepers, herders, diggers, and people whose warnings had previously been easy to ignore.

It opened a few deep reserves slowly. It made the most dangerous cisterns sound. It showed the builders where a covered channel could carry water without freezing, and helped them construct it. At the new outlets, enough flowed for drinking, washing, animals, and a margin against a bad year.

A man proposed that the new water should belong to the owners of the ground above it.

“No,” said the power.

This was among the first occasions on which everyone heard it refuse someone.

The arrangements took weeks. The old shares were recorded, including the disputed ones; nobody lost a legitimate claim merely because help had arrived. But no household could be denied the water needed to live.

The keepers still had work. Channels still collected grit. Children still learned to carry jars without breaking them.

They no longer had to learn quite so early how to recognize that there would not be enough.

***

The power quieted the fault beneath the most crowded ridge. Elsewhere it secured unstable faces and marked ground where building would remain dangerous. It did not stop the whole mountain from moving.

Where a spring was beyond saving, it helped people move. Beams, jars, doors, sleeping platforms, and the stones someone’s mother insisted were essential all went over the ridge. Those who wanted to remain were given time and safe routes, not abandoned to make their stubbornness instructive.

On certain steep streets, broad landings appeared. Handrails followed. A route usable by people who could not manage stairs wound between districts, longer than the old paths but not impossibly long.

There was argument about whose wall it passed.

There was also, on the first warm afternoon, an old man sitting in a courtyard he had not visited for eleven years. He disliked the new paving and said so to everyone who came near him.

For winter, the power placed warmth where it could be shared safely: in ovens, washing rooms, and small sheltered chambers. The warmth could not be locked away by whoever happened to own the nearest doorway. It reduced smoke, spared brushwood, and left more dung for the fields.

People continued to gather around hearths. Some preferred the old fires. Some discovered that what they had loved was sitting together, and were pleased to do it without coughing.

***

Not everything improved at once.

A prosperous lender tried to collect a debt from a family whose rescued flock was suddenly worth pursuing again. Two districts quarreled about the new road. Someone stole tools from a public store. There were people who disliked the power, including a few who had good reasons to distrust anything so difficult to oppose.

It made room for those people.

It would not permit a person to be beaten for refusing a ceremony in its honor. It would not let its name become a new ownership mark.

When asked what it wanted in return, it answered differently according to what was really being asked.

“No payment.”

“No, your daughter need not serve me.”

“Yes, you may criticize the channel.”

“I would like you to stop kicking that dog.”

***

The following spring, the woman carried a small bench out through her father’s door.

Her knee was sound. The door did not catch.

She set the bench where the afternoon sun reached the landing, leaving enough room for loaded people to pass. From there she could hear the new outlet running below and the familiar hammer in the metalworker’s courtyard.

The boy, somewhat taller, arrived carrying a jar.

“You can leave that here,” she said.

“I know.”

“Then why are you still holding it?”

He was watching a dog.

She took the jar from him.

Across the ridge, there were still roofless houses. Birds occupied their wall joints. Farther off, the wild grazers moved over ground that had not been turned into pasture merely because it could be.

The country remained enormous, and cold, and full of things people had strong opinions about.

But there was water for washing the bench before they sat on it. There was warmth waiting indoors. And when the woman thought of visiting her husband’s aunt, she could decide according to whether she wished to see her.
