The second time Dessa called, someone moved the pot.

It was the young man who slept beside the export fiber. He had come to borrow salt. He lifted the pot with a folded cloth, set it on the cooler part of the stove, and discovered that he had forgotten what he had come for.

The power that had noticed the town did not take credit for this.

It waited at the foot of the stair until the deep note had passed.

***

Dessa found it standing beside the public shed, watching the white insects cross a threshold.

It had taken a shape that could fit through the doors. Its coat was plain. Its hands had no marks on them, which made her glance twice.

“Are you waiting for someone?”

“I was hoping to speak to whoever tends the returns.”

“That’s me.”

“Are you finished for the day?”

She held up the torn sleeve.

“What do you think?”

The visitor considered this, then stepped aside so she could reach the washing groove.

It did not tell her that it was wise. There was no useful way to establish that in advance.

***

At first she thought it had come about the animal.

This was reasonable. News of the third return had traveled. Three people had offered traps, one had offered poison, and the pump specialist had suggested that she was hearing a loose joint and allowing it to acquire a personality.

Dessa had brought him down to look at the scratches.

Now she took the visitor there too.

The gallery narrowed beneath the mill. They went sideways past a hot coupling, stepped over a channel that could not be stepped in, and crouched at the inspection flap.

“Something with elbows,” Dessa said.

“Yes.”

“You’ve seen it?”

“Not yet.”

She disliked that answer until the visitor lay down on the wet floor and put its face beside the opening.

For some time nothing happened.

Above them, the cutters struck a hard band. The gallery shuddered. A drop fell from a valve onto the visitor’s neck.

Then something behind the bark shifted.

It was neither a loose joint nor quite the creature Dessa had imagined. A narrow head emerged, followed by two forelimbs folded sharply backward. The feet had soft, spreading pads. Its eyes were filmed over. Along its throat, small openings trembled in the damp air.

It held a bundle of pale fibers in its mouth.

“Oh,” Dessa said.

The animal withdrew.

Beyond it, where the channel bent, there were four smaller bodies.

Dessa sat back on her heels.

“That explains the filter.”

The visitor nodded.

“She’s pulling the loose weave through. Making a bed.”

“Yes.”

“She can’t stay there.”

“No.”

Dessa looked at it sharply.

The visitor was still looking into the channel.

“She cannot stay there,” it said, “and the young cannot yet leave.”

***

A trap would have been easy.

So would a command that every creature in the return gallery depart. The power could have made the channel clean in less time than it took Dessa to shake water from her sleeve.

Instead, it asked where the old return had run.

“There isn’t an old return.”

“There was one before the mill was widened.”

“That’s behind the west wall. Closed over.”

“May we look?”

They fetched the workshop measurer, because Dessa would not open a load-bearing wall on the advice of a stranger with clean hands.

He brought three instruments and someone else carried them.

What lay behind the wall was not a passage anymore. It was a pale seam, compressed between layers of growth. The measurer traced it as far as the public shed and made a doubtful sound.

“You’d have to ask the households above it.”

They did.

One wanted to know whether the floor would become wet. One wanted to know who would move a cupboard. The third refused to discuss anything until morning.

The power returned in the morning.

***

By the following rest evening, several people had seen enough to understand that the visitor was not an ordinary visitor.

The roof grafter watched a severed feeder root join again beneath its fingers without a bead of sap being lost. Elian saw it hold a copper strand whose thinning he had measured that afternoon, and knew that it could restore the metal if it chose.

He put out his hand.

“Please don’t do that without telling me how much.”

The power laid the strand in his palm unchanged.

Later, they weighed the whole reserve together.

***

There was a meeting in the public shed.

The visitor was offered a cup. It covered the cup when a moth landed beside it, having noticed that this was what people did.

Dessa explained the return channel. The measurer explained the floor above it. The pump specialist explained why a second route would be useful even when there was nothing with elbows in the first.

Someone asked the visitor what it wanted.

“To help you make a repair.”

“What else?”

It looked around the shed: the tired shoulders, the covered dishes, the lamps whose brightness had been negotiated against the pumps. Beyond the door, insects were drinking from a groove.

“I can supply enough copper for a sound reserve. I can help reopen the old channel without damaging the rooms above it. After that, you would have two ways to return sap from the mill.”

The copper keeper set down his cup.

“How much is enough?”

They worked that out. It took most of the evening.

They also worked out where the copper would be kept, who would record its use, and whether the new lamp in the shed should still have to wait.

It did not.

***

The work lasted nine days.

It could have lasted a moment, but the floors above the old return held beds, a cupboard full of cups, and people who wanted to know what was happening beneath them.

The power opened only what the measurer had marked. It kept the exposed tissue moist while the graft tenders fitted a lining. It held a heavy section motionless for an afternoon while the woman with three missing fingers made an adjustment she would otherwise have attempted under load.

“You can let go now,” she said.

It did.

No one’s latch moved.

In a sheltered recess beside the old channel, they made a chamber for the animal. Dessa chose the nesting material. The power knew which fibers would suit the young, but she knew which ones could be spared.

They left a shallow route between the recess and the third return.

For two nights the mother refused it.

On the third, she carried one of the young through, then went back for the others.

Dessa inspected the abandoned bend with a lamp.

There were scratches, stolen weave, and a small accumulation of bones.

“Still going to need a guard on that filter,” she said.

The visitor agreed.

***

With two returns, the mill no longer had to be stopped whenever one was cleaned.

This did not immediately give anyone a shorter working day.

The first proposal was to increase the export cut.

The old woman who listened through her feet sent a grandchild to the workshop. Then she came herself, which settled nothing but made the discussion harder to evade.

In the end, part of the saved time went to production and part became a maintenance turn with the mill idle.

The silence was uncomfortable.

On the first afternoon, three people went to check the belts. A baby woke and objected. The root-born machinist lay down, failed to sleep, and got up again.

But Dessa cleaned a return without shouting to the person holding her lamp. Elian worked with his shoulders straight for longer than usual. In the shed, someone sang a whole verse before realizing that no adjustment was necessary.

The next week, fewer people checked the belts.

***

Elian’s daughter left for the orchard before the power departed.

Her father had found another task to show her. She stood beside his tray and learned it, then closed her bag.

The visitor did not offer her a window underground.

It helped carry her things up the stair.

At the top, she stopped beneath the leaves. Wind passed over the orchard in a visible darkening. For a moment she listened as if the surface itself had machinery somewhere beyond hearing.

Then she went to meet the person who had offered her work.

The power brought Elian’s empty carrying basket back down.

He thanked it for that.

***

Before leaving, the visitor walked the town once more.

The new copper was measured, marked, and stored. The bypass carried a steady flow. In the nesting recess, something small was learning to climb. The cupboard remained where its owner wanted it.

There were still insects in the food sometimes. The hump in the street had not been settled. The flywheel still clicked at its binding, although now a replacement could be made before the old one failed.

Dessa was waiting beside the stair.

“You could widen my kitchen door,” she said.

“Yes.”

She looked toward her house.

“Not both sides. There’s a shelf.”

They went back and marked the edge she meant. The power rested a hand against the jamb. Growth loosened, shifted, and settled. Dessa fetched her widest pot and carried it through without turning sideways.

“That’ll do.”

The power took its hand away.

***

It left at dusk, while the moths were rising.

Dessa had gone to the mill. At the inspection flap she found the mother animal watching her from the new recess, its head barely visible above a folded forelimb.

She checked the guard, listened to the return, and entered the reading in her book.

Across the square, someone called for her.

The floor began its deep note.

She waited with one hand on the warm bark. When the sound had passed, she could still hear the caller.

For once, she did not have to ask them to start again.
