The power first made the mistake of looking for a light to give them.

It held the thought outside the bud: a small, steady brightness, no stronger than the blue of a passing swimmer.

Then it attended to the town.

The inhabitants were not waiting in darkness. They were reading the floor. At the market, the seller whose rear legs landed together had recognized a customer before either body reached the platform. Above the cistern, a thread tightened. Three streets away, a worker paused with a fiber in its jaws.

Light would have been a new weather. Some would have moved toward it. Others would have hidden. The hunters would have learned it too.

The power let the brightness go.

For a long interval, it did nothing that could be distinguished from listening.

It followed the sap into the wall beneath the cutter’s testing foot. It felt the thin separation forming behind the doorway, and the larger separations already advancing through the bud. It attended to the central column, the loosening scales, the cold branch. Beyond the harbor, the black water touched the wood and withdrew.

The neighboring bud was not a permanent refuge. Neither was the one beyond that.

Farther along the branch, however, a strip of bark had lifted without breaking away. Beneath it lay a long dry chamber, divided by old fibers and open at both ends. There was food there, and room for condensation to gather without occupying every passage. Several kinds of small animal already lived inside.

The power did not empty it for the town.

It listened there as well.

***

The first change was found by a surveyor.

One of the fixed thorns in the northern ward had begun to lean. This was not unusual. Thorns moved as the tissue grew, and a surveyor who trusted one measurement was a poor surveyor.

But the floor around this thorn had acquired a shallow ridge.

The ridge followed the place where the petal would separate.

It was no higher than a shaving left by a careless cutter. The surveyor crossed it, returned, and laid its body along it. Then it brought a root measure.

Over the following warm intervals, similar ridges appeared elsewhere. Some crossed rooms. One ran beneath the market’s smallest platform. Another ended at a wall whose two sides would soon move in different directions.

They did not tell the inhabitants what to do. They made the changes available to touch.

There were arguments. A householder maintained that the ridge in its floor had always been there. The platform keeper wanted to pare theirs smooth. A nursery was moved, then moved again when its keeper disliked the new chamber.

The records acquired a new sign: a long cut interrupted by a short cross-stroke.

At junctions, animals stopped to feel it.

***

At the harbor, a root grew along the underside of the branch.

It was an ordinary root in nearly every respect. Its outer skin roughened as it matured. Small grazers found it. In two places it passed too close to wet wood, and the power drew it away before the water could climb.

It did not run directly to the neighboring bud.

It reached that entrance, continued past it, and ended beneath the lifted bark.

Along its length were swellings close enough for a carrier to keep contact with one while reaching the next. Between the swellings, the root was firm and narrow. It could be gripped from below. Where the route crossed open water, a second fiber lay beside the first.

Nothing glowed.

The doorway cutter found the beginning during one of its quiet visits to the harbor.

It touched the new root with a forefoot, then with the shorter limbs beside its mouth. The root carried the branch’s deep, slow movement. It carried something else as well: the footfalls of a many-legged animal returning from outside.

The cutter withdrew behind the comb.

When the surveyor came in, it passed close enough to touch. Its body smelled of bark that had not been soaked in the pool. Small crumbs caught between its legs. It paused at the public notice board and cut a report.

The cutter remained beside the entrance.

Later, it put two feet onto the root.

The power did not push.

***

There were things it could have prevented and did not know how to prevent without making other lives into instruments.

The ceiling animal was hungry. The hunters beneath the water were hungry. Even the cleaning animal, confined to its seed husk between meals, reached toward movement with the whole of its narrow head.

The power did not make hunger virtuous. It did not make the small bodies taken by hunters less important because there were many of them.

It considered each danger where it occurred.

A loose nursery cord drifted toward the hooked limb’s reach. The power caught the cord on a rough place in the wall. A worker found it there and tied it higher.

A hunter entered the harbor and settled beneath the root comb. The power allowed the pressure of its arrival to travel farther through the floor than it otherwise would have. The nearest raft owner felt it, stopped, and lowered a testing fiber.

The hunter was located. Traffic moved to the other bank.

Later it caught a swimmer beyond the town.

The power stayed with that life too, through the brief disturbance and the stillness afterward. There was no repair it could offer then. It did not turn away.

***

The cutter’s first crossing ended at the neighboring bud.

It arrived with an empty stomach and its legs held stiffly beneath it. At the entrance it waited until a resident touched its mouthparts.

There was work to be done. A doorway had narrowed around a clutch too broad to bring through.

The cutter felt the wall.

Here the tissue was thicker, colder, differently strained. It set five feet, tested with the sixth, and peeled a curl above the opening.

When it returned home, it brought food between its shorter limbs.

The household animal with the missing leg touched the cutter’s feet for a long time. The flat creature beneath them shifted toward the fallen scraps.

During the next cold interval, the cutter slept without waking at every movement of the branch.

***

The bud opened slowly enough to be measured.

That was the power’s largest intervention.

It did not keep the flower closed. It did not arrest the rising column or harden the streets against their living floor. It spread the first loosening over several cold intervals, giving each ridge time to deepen before the tissue parted.

Some inhabitants left early. Some waited until their rooms tilted. Some found passages into folds the surveyors had not examined.

The abandoned upper ward lifted first.

The surveyor went there once more. It settled into the old room, now open along one side. Air moved over the feet that had formerly rested against a wall.

After a while, it cut a small mark beside the entrance.

Then it left.

At the harbor, the cutter helped enlarge the seam. It made no identifying curl above this opening. There would soon be no wall there to hold one.

The household crossed with its eggs in a folded husk. The five-legged animal went ahead. The cutter followed, and behind it came the flat scavenger, adhering and releasing along the underside of the root.

Halfway to the bark chamber, the cutter stopped.

Through its planted feet came many movements: traffic at the next bud, grazers on the branch, the uneven progress of its household farther ahead. Beneath those lay the slow strain of the flower opening behind it.

It waited until the husk carrying the eggs reached the next swelling.

Then it moved.

The power kept the root steady.

It did not make the crossing feel like home. That would have been untrue.

It made sure there was something to hold.
