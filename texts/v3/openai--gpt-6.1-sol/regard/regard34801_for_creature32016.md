The power first noticed the town because of the tapping.

There were louder things elsewhere: rivers entering gorges, trees coming down in storms, the long fracture of ice. But on certain mornings, in this shallow fold of land, water fell inside a trunk in a sequence that was almost, and never quite, repeated.

It listened until the western collector had finished.

Then it stayed.

***

It did not know at first whether the town was one living thing or many.

The roots beneath the square joined in some places and passed without joining in others. Water crossed boundaries that the people above considered important. A house could survive the loss of its neighbor, but not always. The collector trees were distinct enough to suffer different ailments and connected enough to conceal them.

The power could have settled the question by separating what was joined.

It did not.

Instead it learned the town in the way Sella had learned it: by attending to differences, and returning.

It learned where a wall carried more weight than its thickness suggested. It learned which children went beneath the bicycle store and how much space remained around their shoulders. It learned that the retired worker’s coat branch had not yet injured his house, though it might, and that he reached for it each evening with a pleasure disproportionate to its usefulness.

It listened in kitchens.

The residents did not need silence brought to them. They had enough of that between buildings. Nor did they need their arguments removed. Some of those arguments concerned things that genuinely could not be shared in their present arrangement.

The power also attended to the living material.

It found responses to injury, pressure, dryness, and light. Whether there was suffering in those responses was less clear. It did not mistake uncertainty for permission to disregard them.

For a long time, nothing happened that anyone could have called an intervention.

***

There was water beneath the ridge.

Not a great lake, and not an inexhaustible source. A seam of gravel held the slow drainage from a wider slope beyond the town. Most of it moved away through a fissure too deep for the collector roots to reach.

The power followed it through two dry seasons.

It found where the water could be drawn without emptying the seam, where a shallow cut could be made without loosening the ridge, and where excess would have to be allowed to escape. It found, too, that the town’s earliest canal plans had included a settling basin that had never been built.

The plans were in a drawer under several newer plans, wrapped in a cloth that had once been a curtain.

One evening, the municipal inspector opened that drawer while looking for the dimensions of a culvert.

He found what he was looking for. He also took out the older sheet, because a fold had split and needed mending.

Nothing compelled him to study it.

He did, though, while waiting for the kettle.

***

The stranger arrived three days later.

She wore boots with ordinary mud on them and carried no instruments. At the maintenance office she asked whether anyone had investigated seepage on the northern side of the ridge.

They had, a long time ago.

“May I show you a place?”

Sella went with her, partly because the inspector was busy and partly because people who arrived without instruments sometimes expected other people to lend them some.

The place was unimpressive. There was a low outcrop, a patch of coarse grass, and a dark line beneath a stone.

Sella pushed her brass probe into the ground.

“How did you find this?”

“I followed the water.”

“From which side?”

“The other.”

That was not much of an answer. Sella wrote it down anyway.

The stranger remained while they dug the first test hole. When asked, she held a measuring staff. She held it steadily.

***

The town did not agree at once to extend the canal.

There were costs. There was the possibility of drawing too much. There were questions about who would maintain the new section and whether the factory nearest it would claim the first use of the flow.

The stranger attended the meeting.

She had no objection to questions. When someone proposed a wider channel, she explained why that would not produce a greater reliable supply. When someone proposed enclosing the entire course, she showed where sediment would accumulate and where roots would need room.

Her figures could be checked.

This was what finally made her useful.

The work took most of a season. People were paid for it. The stranger insisted on neither gratitude nor speed, but she pointed out an unsupported bank before it fell, and once, when a worker’s foot went through a rotten brace, she was near enough to take his weight.

He remembered her hand as warm.

By autumn, a narrow flow reached the unfinished end of the old canal.

It was not enough to abolish the water ledger. It was enough that the ledger no longer began every dry month with a subtraction.

***

The power could have done more.

It could have filled every conduit, kept every wall sound, made the pressed boards remain exactly as the stamps declared them.

But abundance introduced without understanding would have changed the town faster than its inhabitants could judge. The paving might have swollen. Foundations might have lifted. The factories might have increased their output until the new supply was insufficient again.

And a town in which every repair depended on the continued attention of a stranger would have acquired a difficulty worse than a sticking window.

So the new intake had a gate that anyone trained in canal work could operate. The settling basin could be emptied with tools the town already owned. A clear gauge showed how much water was passing. The overflow continued down the far side of the ridge.

There were instructions, including instructions for closing it.

The stranger helped copy them into three books.

***

Other changes were smaller.

Sella’s shoulder had ached for years. The power did not regard those years as a necessary price for anything.

One morning, while Sella was washing, the ache loosened. She raised her arm, lowered it, and raised it again.

She remained cautious for a week. Then she moved the oldest notebooks to a higher shelf.

The western collector’s damaged fork was not simply made perfect. The stranger showed Sella where to listen, and together they found the constriction. The repair took an afternoon. Thereafter its tapping became shorter, though it still finished later than the other trees.

Behind the factories, the canal keeper tried a new arrangement of removable waste bins, raised clear of the ground. Burrowing animals could no longer reach the warm fiber so easily. Those that did were caught in a box and carried beyond the sheds.

No one was required to become fond of them.

***

The currants were approved in winter.

Not as an exception, and not because the stranger spoke particularly well on the former owner’s behalf. The new water allowance made room for several small plantings. There were currants, two household pear trees, and a bed outside the school whose purpose the application described simply as *flowers*.

The woman came when the roof straps were tightened for the last time.

The old doorway was low now. She bent to pass beneath it, stood inside for a while, and came out carrying a forgotten hook.

The stranger was helping stack the final bracing.

“Did you live here?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“Would you like a hand with the planting?”

The woman considered.

“Not today.”

“All right.”

They drank from the flask with the foreman.

***

By spring the stranger had gone.

There were people who thought they knew what she had been. Others thought this was the usual exaggeration surrounding a visitor who had turned out to be competent.

Sella had no firm opinion.

In her notebook, beside the first measurements from the ridge, she had written the stranger’s name. The ink had spread slightly into the paper, but remained legible.

The town continued to require attention.

A drain swelled. Moths spoiled part of a spool. The inspector stayed too long over tea. Someone argued that the school flowers were being given more than their allocation, and someone else went to check.

At the edge of town, the currants put out leaves.

On misty mornings, the power still listened. It would intervene again if it was needed. For now, Sella sat beside the western collector, with both hands resting comfortably in her lap.

Inside the trunk, the last few drops fell.

She waited for them.
