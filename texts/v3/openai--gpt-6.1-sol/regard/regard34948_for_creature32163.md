The first thing the power did was keep a cupboard open.

Not visibly. Its door remained shut, its brass latch lowered. But somewhere beyond the ceramic lining, a route that had begun to fold away was held in place until the person carrying a parcel could pass.

The parcel contained flour. It arrived eleven days late, still dry.

The cupboard keeper wrote the delay in her book. She had no way of knowing that someone, somewhere, had been standing with that parcel in their arms for considerably longer.

The power did not consider the matter finished when the flour arrived.

It followed the route back.

***

For a time, nobody in the district knew that anything had changed.

The street posts still had to be turned. Apples still needed warming. The old engineer still braced his cupboard door with one knee and worked the latch between both hands.

But a landing beneath the northern roofs stopped slipping toward the floor below. The engineer had been watching it for months. He took his measurements again, cleaned his instrument, and took them a third time.

He wrote *stable* in pencil.

Under the square, a drain creature withdrew from a hot bead of solder before the bead fell. The worker saw it move and put a scrap of metal across the opening.

At the south gate, a woman returning from the road found herself within sight of home before her food ran out.

These were small interventions only because the district had taught its inhabitants to keep small accounts.

***

The power first made itself known to the woman who repaired cupboard panes.

She was removing a frame when she saw, through the fogged glass, a room with a broad table. On the table lay the screws she had not yet taken out.

She stopped working.

A voice said, “I can hold the other side, if that would help.”

“Not the glass,” she said. “The ceramic. It cracks if the pressure is uneven.”

The pressure shifted.

She finished loosening the frame. When she lifted it away, there was no opening into another room, only the familiar shallow cabinet.

“Are you still there?”

“Yes.”

She considered this.

“Then you can tell whoever packs the machine parts that paper is not enough around the sharp ones.”

“I have told them.”

“Have you?”

“They would like to know whether folded cloth would be useful afterward, or a nuisance to dispose of.”

“Useful,” she said. “Provided it isn’t oily.”

The power passed this answer along.

Only after she had set the old pane safely aside did it ask whether she would let it look at the stairs.

***

There were meetings.

The power did not summon everyone to the square. Some people could not get there, and others disliked meetings sufficiently that they would have remained at home even if the square had been warmed.

It spoke in kitchens, beside workbenches, through a pane temporarily removed from its frame. People carried questions between houses. The clerk made copies.

The first questions were mostly about danger.

Would making the ground older bring buried things with it?

Would warmer rooms loosen the walls?

If the south road became dependable, what might use it to come here?

The power answered what it knew. Where an answer depended on a choice not yet made, it said so. The engineers were permitted to inspect the proposed changes. They found the explanations difficult, but not evasive.

One of them asked why it had come.

“Because you are here,” it said.

This answer was entered in the minutes. Several residents thought it insufficient.

The power stayed.

***

It could have laid deep, finished rock beneath the district. It could have given the plain a horizon, established the roads, opened the walls onto a country of fields and ordinary distances.

The young ground would have been gone.

Much else would have gone with it: routes inside cupboards, rooms held together by pipework, the old conversations lodged beneath doorways. There were people whose lives now occupied spaces no survey had fully found. A thorough correction might have left an excellent city with some of its inhabitants missing.

So the power began with a less complete undertaking.

It learned where everyone was.

The retired seamstress allowed it into her room on the condition that her chair should not be moved. It sat where she could see it, which was not where her nephew saw it, and listened while she explained the inconveniences.

She did not mind being difficult to see. She minded knocking so hard that her wrist hurt. Sometimes the tea was set down just beyond her reach. Once, while she slept, someone had decided the room was empty and taken away a stool.

The power asked permission to make the room easier for others to find.

“What would it feel like?”

“Like this room.”

She agreed to a trial.

For three mornings, her nephew came in carrying tea and saw her immediately. On the fourth she asked for the striker again.

He looked distressed.

“The noise tells me you’ve come,” she explained.

They kept the striker. A light tap was enough now.

***

Warmth required more care than anyone expected.

The power proposed no general thaw. Engineers had measurements of joints held tight by cold, tanks whose supports had settled around winter ice, young stone that became uncertain when warmed too quickly.

Instead, a single room was altered first.

Its disk gave heat to the chair, the table, the floorboards and the air between them. The moss remained on its rim. For a week the engineers inspected the room twice a day.

The tenant complained that her soup cooled too slowly.

She also began sleeping without gloves.

By the end of the month, several households had asked for the same alteration. Others wanted to wait until spring. The power left them working controls and taught the repairers how to recognize a failing part.

The old engineer’s cupboard received a different latch.

He objected that the original was still serviceable.

On the next cold morning, he opened the new one with two fingers. He stood for a while with the door in his hand, then put the kettle on.

***

The power did not leave the supply routes mysterious merely because they had always been so.

It found warehouses, kitchens, a cloth room lit by long windows. It found interrupted passages and workers accustomed to packing goods for addresses they could never visit. Some routes crossed places where waiting itself was hazardous.

Where it could make a safe passage, it did. Where it could not yet do so, it arranged another way.

Enna received a letter with her cloth.

The writer thanked her for the thumb supports. They had been helpful. The letter also explained the nine years of blue: a mistake in an order, followed by an extraordinary quantity of dye, followed by nobody knowing how to ask whether the recipients minded.

Enna read it twice.

Then she wrote back that blue had served very well, but they could use yellow for children’s clothes, and that the district had enough heavy lining to last some time.

For the first time, she signed her name knowing who would read it.

***

There remained disagreements.

The family with the narrowed sleeping alcove wanted its three inches restored. The workroom tenant wanted assurance that the street would stay in place. Both wanted the power to confirm that the other had behaved badly.

It restored the space and secured the street.

Neither party found this entirely satisfactory.

Some residents wanted a gate through which they could leave without risking a season on the road. Others feared that any dependable opening would make the district impossible to defend.

The power made a gate that could be closed. Then it helped them decide who would keep it, who could open it, and how someone returning late would call home.

Beyond it lay a short, measured path to a sheltered place. There was room there for visitors to wait, for goods to be examined, for a traveler to change their mind.

The first person through was the woman who repaired panes.

The descent from her room had taken two neighbors and careful arrangements. At the gate she sat in a wheeled chair made by the workroom tenant, with a blanket over her knees.

She stayed outside for nearly an hour.

When asked what she had seen, she said, “More sky.”

***

In the courtyard, the power made no marks on the rising earth.

It examined what lay beneath, with the permission of those who kept the measurements, and secured the house footings against the pressures that were gathering there. It showed the engineers where to watch. The pipes would need another alteration eventually, but not this year.

The observer asked whether the patch would become a hill.

“It may.”

“A stream bed?”

“That is also possible.”

“Can you tell us which?”

“I could make it one or the other.”

She looked at the convex earth, then at the marks her grandfather had begun on the wall.

She said they had only asked to know.

The power understood and left the decision unfinished.

It did mend the courtyard bench. Its legs had stood on unequal ground for so long that people had stopped thinking of the rocking as a defect.

Later that afternoon, sunlight reached the earth. The observer sat down with her ledger. Beside her sat a child whose parents were inspecting the new gate.

The child put a palm against the bench.

“It’s warm.”

The observer checked with her own hand. A little warmth remained in the wood after the narrow band of sunlight had moved on.

Above them, someone opened a window to call a neighbor in for supper. The voice carried clearly into the courtyard now, though the walls still held the older voices safe.

The power had work elsewhere in the district. There were tanks to inspect, a cupboard route not yet repaired, and a doctor who wanted to discuss several patients.

It left the two on the bench.

Beneath them, the ground continued at its own pace.
