The power noticed the shifted plate.

Not the height of the tower, nor the unlikely foliage, nor even the machinery keeping six floors of difficult promises beneath the roots. Those things had drawn its attention, but it was the plate that held it: moved a little to the east so that tomorrow’s foot would spare something that had not existed yesterday.

It watched Mara go inside.

There were powers that would have taken this as a request. They would have hardened the bank, settled the roots into their channels, made every valve turn easily beneath her hand. By morning the paths would have been dry, the flowers plentiful, the machinery silent.

This power knew enough about gardens to distrust such a morning.

It remained through the night.

At two, a valve began to chatter. Mara woke, listened, and lay down again. At three, the frog climbed onto the lip of its pool and stayed there for eleven minutes. Just before dawn, a night worker cut a vine back farther than Mara would have cut it. The worker was tired. There were four more roofs on his round.

Below, a bearing was warming unevenly. It would last another season, perhaps two. Replacing it required shutting down a line whose heat also served several inhabited floors. The request had been deferred twice, for reasons that were reasonable separately and poor together.

The power followed the pipes down.

It found kitchens, laundries, a schoolroom with a cold corner, a woman sleeping beside an open window because the room’s radiator could not be regulated. It found the office where the bearing request rested beneath other requests. It found the workshop in which a suitable replacement could be made, and the machinist who knew how to make it, and the unpaid account that kept the workshop from accepting another order.

By the time Mara came out to look for the frog, the power had learned that the garden was much larger than its boundaries.

It met her beside the lowest terrace.

There was no brilliance. It had taken a form that could stand on damp ceramic without alarming anyone: an unfamiliar person in a plain coat, carrying nothing.

Mara stopped.

“Who let you up?”

“I came by the maintenance stair.”

“That isn’t an answer.”

“No,” said the visitor. “It isn’t.”

She looked at the clean coat, then at the feet.

“Stay on the plates. The pale edge won’t hold you.”

The visitor stepped back.

Mara crouched by the pool. A hind foot vanished beneath the roots.

“Present,” she said, and made the entry.

The visitor waited until she had put the pencil away.

“I would like to help.”

“With what?”

“I don’t yet know what you would welcome.”

This made her look up more carefully.

Most visitors already knew what they wanted to contribute. New instruments. A better planting scheme. A name for the enclosure. Once, a man had proposed introducing birds.

“Can you hold something?” she asked.

“Yes.”

She brought a narrow panel from the service house and had the visitor hold it while she reached behind the exhaust casing. The panel was awkward rather than heavy. There was nowhere to put one’s fingers without gathering condensation.

“Higher.”

The visitor raised it.

“Not that high.”

It lowered it a little.

With the casing accessible, Mara loosened two fixings and examined the bracket behind the flowering shrub.

“I’ve been meaning to do this.”

The visitor said nothing.

Together they moved the bracket enough that she could reach the rear valve without putting her wrist against the hot metal. The shrub remained where it was. One stem had to be untied and fastened again.

When they finished, Mara rubbed the old burn with her thumb.

“Tea?”

“Yes.”

Inside, the visitor stood until Mara indicated the only chair. She sat on the bed. To reach the tea tin, she closed the door and opened the cupboard.

For a little while they discussed the western bank.

Then the visitor asked, “What happens when you can’t do the work?”

“Someone follows the schedule.”

“And the things that aren’t in the schedule?”

“I do them when I get back.”

Mara poured carefully. One cup was blue. The other had a crack that ended short of the rim.

After a moment she said, “There was a young woman here last winter. I was showing her the returns. She could tell which one was obstructed by touching the pipe.”

“Where is she now?”

“On the lower systems. Permanent position.”

“You wanted her to stay?”

“She wanted to stay. There wasn’t a post.”

The visitor held the warm cup in both hands.

“What else?”

Mara gave a short laugh.

“You’ll regret asking.”

But the visitor listened.

There was the bearing. There was the west load survey, still based on the bank’s weight before the latest deposition. There were the ceramic plates no longer made in their original size. There was the impossibility of shutting down the hot line without first arranging temporary heat below.

There was her room.

“It’s adequate,” Mara said.

The visitor glanced at the cupboard door, which could not open while the room’s door was open.

“It’s small.”

“I know how big it is.”

“Would you want another?”

“Not down below. I need to hear the returns.”

The visitor nodded.

“And I don’t want this turned into somewhere people come to look at me,” she added.

“No.”

“Or at the frog.”

“No.”

She drank her tea.

“How much help are you actually able to give?”

The visitor answered her plainly.

For a long time Mara said nothing. Beyond the wall, the pulping machine started, stopped, and started again.

“You could make it all work,” she said at last.

“For a while.”

“Forever?”

“I could make something that never failed. It would not be quite this.”

She looked toward the window. A broad leaf pressed against the glass, released it, and pressed again.

“Then start with the bearing.”

***

The replacement did not arrive that morning.

First the workshop was paid. Then the machinist came up and disagreed with the drawing. Mara took him down to the third landing, where they spent an hour beside the coupling. A revised drawing went back with him.

The power could have shortened this. It did not shorten the part in which people needed to understand what they were making.

Temporary heaters arrived for the inhabited floors. Notices were posted. The woman with the unregulated radiator asked whether somebody could look at that too.

Somebody did.

The young caretaker returned on a permanent appointment. Her name was Iven. She did not cut the vines exactly as Mara cut them, and she found a flaw in the evening intake check that Mara had missed.

They argued once about the gauge housing.

The fern remained.

A surveyor examined the western bank and recommended reinforcement beneath its oldest section. There was work, noise, and one unpleasant afternoon when the garden had to run cooler than usual. The frog stayed under the roots. Mara checked twice.

Her room gained a narrow extension into an unused storage bay. Before anything was built, she stood inside the proposed outline and listened. The return pump was still audible. The cupboard could now be opened without shutting the door.

Nothing was made inexhaustible. There were spare parts, paid hours, a second person who knew the unscheduled sounds, and a fund for faults that had not yet become emergencies.

Mara accepted these things more readily once they stopped being described as gifts.

***

Before leaving, the power went with her to the western path.

The little lip had hardened. Beneath it, where the drops fell, a dark green point had appeared.

“Moss,” Mara said.

“Will you leave it?”

“Probably. Unless it closes the channel.”

The power bent to look. It had not put the moss there.

Mara moved past it with her basket. Her heel cleared the new edge.

At the service house she stopped.

“Will you come back?”

“If you want me to.”

She considered this.

“You could come for tea. Don’t wait for something to break.”

The power agreed.

Then it went down the maintenance stair, stopping at the third landing to look at the coupling Mara had installed.

Above it, water crossed the young stone. The pump changed speed. Iven called a question from the lowest terrace, and Mara set down her basket to answer.
