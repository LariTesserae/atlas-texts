The first thing the power did was wait.

It had come to the unfinished path because of the two hands resting side by side on the rope. One was deeply creased. The other still had a faint groove where a stone belt had rubbed its wrist.

Between them, the mountain was growing.

The power could have looked through the pale surface and measured everything that was taking place beneath it. Instead it attended to what the child could see: the end of a cord, almost hidden now by a swelling that had not been there three days before.

Presently the child turned to the old woman and made the sign for a question.

She answered with one finger against the rope.

Wait.

So all three of them waited.

—

For several days, nothing unusual happened.

A carrier bruised his shoulder against a low beam. A garden wall crossed a passage and was cut back after an argument. In an upper chamber, a baby woke whenever a pod was emptied and went to sleep whenever somebody began grinding seeds.

The power watched how help was given.

It noticed that people generally put something within reach before taking something away. A new loop was tied before a worn one was cut. When a roof needed repair, the household’s sleeping mats were already hanging in another house before the work began.

It noticed other things.

There was a hood beside a steep lane that everyone used and nobody quite owned. Its air lasted well in the morning. By evening, people lifted their faces into it, took a breath, and sometimes continued looking tired.

A woman with stiff shoulders had begun visiting her sister less often. Neither sister spoke of this as a difficulty. The one who could still travel brought portions of food and complained about the journey for both of them.

The carriers were proud of knowing what a house needed before its inhabitants asked. They were also tired.

And the old woman at the unfinished path had once gone down to the lowest gardens every seventh day. Now she could reach them only if someone brought a pod and waited at the turning.

She disliked arranging this. The gardens had belonged to her brother, and there were things she preferred to remember without company.

The power considered all of it.

Then, one evening, it tapped a roof beam.

Three slow knocks.

—

The house belonged to a carrier, a weaver, two children, and a man recovering from an illness that had left him short of breath.

The carrier rose to help with the pod. Finding none, she stopped with one hand still below the water.

Something was present in the chamber. It had neither arrived through the doorway nor displaced anyone from a mat. Where it attended, the leaves of the roof became distinct: each vein visible, each small injury plain.

The youngest child reached toward it.

“Is it a person?” the child asked.

“I can listen,” the power said.

The weaver drew the child a little nearer.

“What have you come to do?”

“I have been watching your air.”

The carrier’s hand closed around the roof post.

“There is enough in this house.”

“Yes.”

“And we keep it clean.”

“Yes.”

After a while, her hand opened again.

The power explained what it could offer.

There were plants in the hanging forest that held air in chambers within their stems. With a small change, a cutting from one of those plants could replenish the air beneath a roof. It would need warmth, water, light, and tending. It would not fill the whole town at once. A small one might serve a roadside hood. Several could serve a house.

The carrier asked how quickly they worked.

The weaver asked how large they grew.

The sick man asked what happened when they died.

The power answered each question. It offered to grow one first in an empty chamber, where they could examine it without having to trust it.

“What do you want in return?” the carrier asked.

“Nothing.”

She looked at the others.

“We should tell the rest of the lane.”

—

This took longer than making the plant would have taken.

People wanted to know whether its roots would harm the roof, whether it could be divided, whether a household that neglected one could endanger its neighbors. Someone asked whether the leaves were edible. Someone else wanted to see it fail before putting it above a sleeping child.

The air carriers asked most of the questions.

The power remained for all of them.

It made the first cutting in a disused house whose entrance had been kept open for travelers. The new stem was no thicker than a thumb. Its leaves folded beneath the ceiling, pale on one side and dark on the other.

Nothing shone. There was no sound beyond the ordinary slight disturbance of water.

By morning, the chamber’s air was fresh.

They left it there for a season.

Carriers brought their usual pods elsewhere. Weavers inspected the roof. Children were forbidden to pick the leaves and did so anyway; the torn edges healed without trouble.

They tried darkness. They tried crowding the chamber. They cut a stem, stopped tending the roots, and watched the leaves slowly close.

The plant failed gradually and visibly. It did not poison the water. A neglected chamber became a neglected chamber, and could be supplied by pod again.

That mattered to them.

When they asked how the change had been made, the power showed the gardeners everything they could use: how to graft the stem, how to keep the air-making tissues alive, how to recognize a cutting that had not taken. There were parts they did not understand. Those parts it explained again, in other ways, until someone could repeat the work without its assistance.

The first place they planted one permanently was the hood that nobody owned.

The second was beside the steep lane between the sisters’ houses.

—

There were disagreements.

A carrier said that no plant could know a household as well as she did.

A gardener replied that the plant did not need to.

For two days they would not eat together.

The power did not settle this. It had given them a new thing to live with, and some of the living was awkward.

There was still air to carry. The deepest houses needed it. So did dark workshops, new roofs, festival chambers, and places whose cuttings had failed. But there was less urgency in the work, and fewer journeys through the tangled forest when a carrier was ill.

Some carriers began tending the new stems. One refused and continued hauling pods. People continued paying him.

The town learned where it could rely on the plants and where it could not.

Along the longer lanes, small hoods appeared between the existing ones. Their makers tied ordinary ropes beneath them. The knots were checked by ordinary hands.

—

The old woman heard about the changes before she used them.

She listened to the children’s descriptions and asked whether the leaves interfered with the view.

Eventually, she went to her brother’s garden.

Nobody accompanied her.

At the first new hood she rested longer than necessary. At the second she found a snail on the inside of a leaf and moved it outside. At the turning, where someone had once had to wait with a pod, she lifted her face into air.

The garden beyond was badly overgrown.

She spent much of the afternoon there, cutting a way to the low wall where her brother used to sit. His knife loop still hung from a root, though the knife was gone.

When she returned, her fingers were wrapped in fresh strips of leaf.

The power was waiting beside the unfinished path.

“You could have asked me sooner,” she said.

“Yes.”

She settled into her sling. For a while she looked toward the pale swelling beneath the cord.

“Will you make the mountain stop?”

“Would you want that?”

“No,” she said, almost at once.

Then she thought about it.

“I would like to know before this place has to move.”

The power touched the young stone beneath the path. Farther down, where the oldest foundations stood, it listened to the mountain releasing its hold on the water.

There was time, but not as much as the inhabitants supposed.

It told her what it had found.

She asked where the first changes would show. It showed her the seam to watch, and the color that would come into it. Together they called over a builder, then another elder. Someone fetched a length of cord.

Before evening, they had begun arguing about where to put the crossing ring.

—

The power stayed long enough to be certain its help could survive its absence.

Then it went elsewhere.

The town continued pruning its roofs. People continued losing cups, disagreeing about old corners, and underestimating how long a meal would take.

At the unfinished path, the child returned to sit beside the old woman.

There was a fresh hood close behind them. Neither used it immediately.

The child pointed to the mountain. A small ridge now covered the cord completely.

The old woman put her hand beside the child’s hand on the rope.

After a while, they went down to tell someone.
