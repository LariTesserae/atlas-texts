The first thing the power does is take the weight off your feet.

You are on the observation platform when it happens. You have been leaning against the railing longer than you intended. Across the gulf, the small gold window is still visible through the telescope, though the coin ran out some time ago and you have been holding the mechanism open with your thumb.

There is no apparition.

The bench is simply closer than you remember. You sit down, and the ache in your knees begins to ease. The engines remain loud. The opposite shore remains far away.

Beside you is a cup of something warm.

You look at it for a long time before picking it up.

It smells of the bread from the station kitchens, but differently: toasted, perhaps. Something has happened to it. Something has been allowed to happen.

You drink.

For once, no one needs your hand.

***

The power does not begin by stopping the machines.

It listens to them.

It listens beneath the roar, where the pressure is held without passage and the heat arrives without expenditure. It follows the pipes through their impossible descents. It attends to the bridges, to the loads suspended in their members, to the water leaning forever against the same square edges of stone.

There are consequences waiting here.

If everything were simply released, some of the consequences would be beautiful. Water would break into spray. Reeds would bend. The sun would make its first small journey across a window.

Others would not.

A shoe raised over a stair would come down too quickly. A vehicle would arrive at a curve with a driver who had not yet regained the movement of his hands. Pressure accumulated outside duration would enter pipes made for ordinary hours.

The power learns all this before it touches a valve.

It does not mistake the condition of the country for the wishes of its people.

***

You first notice it with the elderly woman in the tiled room.

You have returned with the resident from the staircase. The brass key on their coat has turned sideways. You straighten it almost without thinking, then help them cross the threshold.

The woman is where she was, spectacles folded beside her glass.

You kneel at her chair.

Before you can reach for her hand, she looks at you.

Not the slow, laborious adjustment you have learned to wait for. Her eyes turn as eyes should.

Then she looks past you.

There is someone standing in the doorway.

Later, you will be unable to describe them. At the time, there is nothing difficult about their appearance. They look like someone who has come to visit and is waiting to be invited farther into the room.

The woman studies them.

“Is it over?” she asks.

Her voice is dry. The visitor brings her the glass, and she takes it herself.

“No,” they say. “But it can change.”

She drinks a little water.

“Will it hurt?”

“Some things already hurt. I can help with those. You needn’t hurry.”

She considers this, then sets the glass down. Her fingers remain around it.

The resident beside you has begun to cry.

They do not have to spend their movement on the crying. They do not have to decide whether to use the next breath for a word instead.

You find that you are still kneeling, holding out an empty hand.

***

The power goes to everyone.

This is not something you see all at once. You find out gradually, from conversations that become possible.

The technician says someone sat on the low stool beside him and asked about his wrist.

The woman on the balcony says she was asked whether she wanted to stay in the sunlight.

The man in the loading bay says the broken handle has been mended, but the crate is still where he asked you to put it.

In the waiting room, the porter has been helped upright. He is embarrassed by the fuss until he discovers that one of his knees had been trapped against the trolley. Then he sits down and is quiet for a while.

The boy in the yellow coat receives an answer about the clouds.

He is disappointed. He had imagined a second country on their undersides.

His mother keeps one hand on his shoulder while he asks whether there is anything farther down.

There is time for the answer.

There is time for another question after it.

***

At first the rooms change more than the country.

A curtain moves. A bed takes the new shape of someone sleeping in it. Steam rises from a kettle and thins against the ceiling.

People are allowed to come free without being delivered suddenly into everything they have missed.

Those who are frightened can sit with someone. Those who are confused can ask again. There are meals ready for people whose first restored sensation is hunger, and quiet places for those who cannot bear another sound.

No one is required to be grateful.

One woman shuts her door.

The power leaves water and food within reach, and tells her how to ask for company.

A man in a gallery is furious. He wants to know who did this, and why, and whether all his years of standing there will be returned to him.

The power does not call his anger a failure to understand.

It stays while he speaks.

***

You have trouble putting your hands away.

On your first visit to the pump hall after the change, you reach automatically for the technician. He is walking toward you with a small tool in his fingers.

You both stop.

Then he takes your hand anyway.

His palm is warm. The marks around his wrist are fading.

He asks you to come and look at something.

Behind the guard window, the piston moves.

It travels slowly at first. The polished rod enters the housing, and the great flywheel makes a fraction of a turn. The bolt you have watched at the top descends along the rim.

You feel a peculiar grief.

For weeks you wanted nothing more than for that bolt to move. Now its departure proves how long you stood looking at it.

The technician watches your face.

“Come outside,” he says.

You had not known until then how badly you wanted someone else to lead.

***

The country is not set running by a single command.

The power works through the places that need care.

A bridge is relieved of its strain before the wind reaches it. Drivers wake with their vehicles still. A suspended spoon is allowed to lower into its bowl before anyone has to answer a knock.

In the pump halls, the people who know the machinery are consulted. They are given instruments that tell the truth about what lies inside the pipes. Where there is danger, they can see it. Where there is work they cannot safely do, they are helped.

Some wish to leave the machines entirely.

They are not told that understanding a pump obliges them to spend the rest of their lives beside it.

The roads acquire places to rest. The vast distances remain vast, but cease to be sentences passed on anyone who needs another person.

Across the eastern gulf, a message arrives from the gold-lit window.

The person there saw you raise your hand.

They would like to know your name.

***

There are losses, even with help.

A leaf yellows.

A woman finds it beside a drainage channel and carries it home. For several days she keeps it on the kitchen table.

People sleep through things. Someone misses a visitor. A cup is dropped, and this time the fragments cannot simply be persuaded back into the moment before the fall.

The power helps sweep them up.

It does not remove every sharp edge from living.

But neither does it preserve a danger because danger might teach someone a lesson. The cliffs receive barriers where people want them. The bridge shelters are warmed. No child has to discover the depth of a gulf by leaning too far over a rail.

When the waterfalls are released, their basins are made ready to bear them.

You are on the platform when the first one falls.

For a moment it holds all its familiar folds. Then the bright crease halfway down comes apart.

Spray lifts from the base.

A fine wetness reaches your face, borne upward through miles of air.

Beside you, the resident with the brass key laughs. You have heard them laugh before, a breath at a time. This laugh surprises even them.

They put a hand over their mouth, then take it away.

***

The power remains available.

Not always in a doorway. Not always in any form you could point out to another person.

There is a way to call, and an answer when called. There are visits, explanations, repairs. When people disagree about what should be done, the power does not make agreement appear inside them. It helps them hear one another, and makes sure the quietest person can finish speaking.

Its presence does not keep the country at the edge of a perfect afternoon.

Evening comes.

The first evening is difficult for many of them. Windows that have never known darkness become mirrors. People stand in the covered streets and watch the blue diminish above the roofs.

Lights are brought where lights are needed.

In the tiled room, the elderly woman asks to have her chair turned toward the door. She wants to watch people passing in the corridor.

You sit with her for a while.

Eventually she notices how tired you are.

“Go on,” she says. “I’m all right.”

You hesitate.

She reaches out and pats the back of your hand.

Then she lets it go.

***

Later, you return to the observation platform.

The bench no longer trembles. Far below, the falls have become a continuous movement, too intricate to hold in the eye. Across the gulf, a light goes out in one window and comes on in another.

You hear footsteps behind you.

The resident from the staircase has brought two cups. They sit down, carefully, because the cups are full.

For a while you say nothing.

A wind moves through the fields beyond the station. It reaches the platform carrying the cold mineral smell of the reeds.

Your companion looks toward the sound.

A little water has spilled over their thumb. They wipe it on their coat, give you one of the cups, and settle back against the bench.

The sun has gone below the far shore.

Neither of you needs to leave yet.
