The power first attended to the interval between the knock and the falling dust.

There was more in that interval than the hollow could hear. A fracture below the floor was lengthening. Farther up, behind the plates that held the delayed rain, a narrow bridge of stone had begun to bear more weight than it could carry. Each knock was a small surrender. The mountain was not about to fall, but one wall of the hollow would move before the next season of storms.

The movement would be slight by the measure of mountains.

It would close the water-catcher’s crevice, overturn the warm stone near the slit, and bring down a plate whose lower edge hung above several crowded seams. At the deepest end, the large inhabitant would brace itself as it always did. This time the rock behind its claws would break. Its hold would last long enough for the two sides of its crack to close upon it.

The power attended to this without making a sound.

It also attended to the four worn animals behind the water-catcher. Their cups had not broken all at once. The rims had thinned over many seasons of raising, lowering, and pressing against stone. One still lifted a front foot whenever a drop struck nearby. The movement ended halfway, though the foot was capable of going farther.

The power did not replace it.

A new cup would have required changes elsewhere: in the joints, in the nerves that measured its fullness, in the animal’s habit of carrying its weight. The old body was not merely a damaged version of a younger one. It had learned where to rest against the others. It knew the underside of the catcher’s left shield, where water lingered longest.

For a while, nothing changed.

A grazer passed over a fresh patch and left its adhering specks. Beneath a stone, the hunter rubbed its forelegs. The animal it caught struggled hard enough to turn the stone a little, and three nearby bodies withdrew from the movement.

The power watched that too.

Its attention did not make every mouth harmless. Nor did it turn the hollow into a place where nothing was lost. It followed the hunter’s need as closely as the struggling animal’s, and the growth beneath the grazer as closely as the grazer itself.

Below them, the fracture lengthened.

There the power acted.

Deep in the mountain, beyond any root or claw, it changed the way two surfaces met. A ridge that had caught against another ridge eased past it. Pressure found a broader face of stone. The strain was not removed; it was carried downward and outward, into rock that could bear it.

A low knock entered the hollow.

All the animals stopped.

The large inhabitant closed its eye and pressed its ridges against the crack. The larvae ceased moving. The water-catcher lowered its cups, spilling what little remained in them.

Dust fell from one plate.

Then the sound passed.

The bridge behind the upper wall still held, but no longer bore the weight alone. In years to come it would wear, split, and shed its fragments. They would fall separately. The hollow would continue to change by pieces small enough that some of its inhabitants could move aside.

The power waited until scraping resumed.

There was water above the catcher’s station, caught in a pocket behind the wall. It had gathered there through three clear days, slowly dissolving the pale mineral that sealed its lower edge. Eventually it would break through and empty too quickly for any animal to use much of it.

The power opened a passage no wider than the finest hair.

It did not open it toward the waiting mouth.

The first moisture appeared farther back, within reach of the four old animals. It darkened a short seam. For a long time that was all.

One of them touched it with a worn cup.

The cup could no longer hold a falling drop, but its remaining edge could gather a film. The animal drew its foot toward its mouth. Afterward it touched the stone again.

The others felt the movement.

By the time the water-catcher returned, two had shifted away from the shelter beneath its shields. One remained pressed against it. The fourth stood between the new dampness and the old resting place, turning slowly, testing both.

The catcher raised its front feet.

Above it, the drop formed as before.

The power made no effort to teach the animals what had happened. There was no brightness, no new vibration to follow, no mark on the wall. The small passage would persist while the stone permitted it. Later, growth might narrow it. An inhabitant might wedge a flake beneath it and guide the moisture elsewhere. The water would enter the work of the hollow.

Before withdrawing, the power attended once more to the deepest crack.

The large animal had opened its eye. A pale grazer stood on its brow, cleaning between two mineral grains. Within the stone, its farther limbs slowly loosened.

The power left those limbs their strength. It left the mouth its sideways opening and the smaller animals their caution. But the rock behind the claws now held firmly enough that the creature could retreat when warmth called it farther inward.

There would be space for its next small growth.

At the other end, sunlight entered through the slit. It touched the accustomed stone. Animals gathered along the shadowed side, and one, finding the old approach blocked by another body, tried a longer way around.

Nothing announced that the hollow had drawn an answer.

The walls were still dark. The plates still leaned overhead. Food would still arrive irregularly, and a missed footing could still carry a winged animal beyond the reach of its remaining fan.

Beneath the drop, six feet held to stone.

In the crevice behind them, an old animal worked its ruined cup along a damp seam. It paused to swallow, then reached again.

The drop swelled.

When it fell, the catcher was ready.
