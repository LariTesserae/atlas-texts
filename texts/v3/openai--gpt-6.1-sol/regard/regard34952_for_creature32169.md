The power notices the foot before it notices the bird.

One foot is in the water. The other is folded into the feathers. The water has risen far enough to cool the first foot, and presently the bird will wake.

It does not prevent this.

The bird wakes, unfolds its other leg, and steps higher. Four others move with it. Their shelf is disappearing, but for the moment there is somewhere to go. They know how to do this. The power watches them find their balance on the dark sand.

Then it attends to the ridge ahead.

At its upper end, where the birds cannot yet see, the sand narrows to a soft crest. Beyond that is deep water. Within an hour, the discharge will reach the crest too.

The power could lift the whole basin. It could silence the engines, turn back the river, give every small bird an island.

But the river is carrying ships and rainwater. The engines are keeping water away from houses whose floors are lower than the tide. The sediment has come from a channel that must remain open. There are people asleep behind other banks, and fish passing through other waters, and worms in the sand under the birds’ feet.

It follows these connections for a long time.

Then it makes a small change.

A timber cradle beneath the discharge pipe has been settling unevenly. At the next inspection, before it can fail, the defect becomes unmistakable. A worker catches the altered angle in the beam of a torch. The crew supports the pipe properly and shifts its outlet toward the compartment scheduled to receive the next deposit.

The current slackens beside the ridge.

Water continues to rise, but the resting shelf remains above it until morning.

No one knows that anything has been granted. The worker is pleased to have caught a maintenance problem early. The birds stand with their bills tucked into their shoulders. One shakes rain from its back.

The power stays.

In daylight, it learns that the bird is lean. Not starving, not injured, but spending nearly everything it finds. It learns how much rest is lost when a shelf vanishes, how much feeding time is lost when a person walks toward it, how far the bird must fly to find another margin at the proper depth.

It learns also that fresh dredgings are not always a gift. Among the worms and broken shells are substances the bird cannot taste or recognize. Some deposits are harmless enough. Others carry an older history out of the shipping channel.

The power does not make the bird wiser about poisons. There is no useful lesson its bill could learn from them.

Instead, a sample taken before the next delivery gives a result that cannot be dismissed. Another sample confirms it. That load goes to a contained compartment where birds cannot feed on its settling edge. The clean sediment continues elsewhere.

This costs money. It takes time. Someone objects.

The power allows the objection to be heard. It also allows the answer to be clear.

There are people here who have long suspected that the tests were too infrequent. Now they have evidence, and someone with authority willing to act on it. Their work becomes easier without becoming effortless.

By the following week, a surveyor has begun marking the birds’ resting places as well as the changing ground levels.

She starts because she has repeatedly seen them crowd onto the last dry strip at dusk. She returns because the strip is different each evening. Her first marks are quickly out of date. So she makes another kind of drawing: not a fixed outline, but a sequence showing where shallow water forms, where sand firms, where birds gather when the tide and discharge rise together.

The drawing reaches the person who assigns the pipe outlets.

A compartment can rest for several days before receiving its next layer. Another can receive the slurry meanwhile. A broad, low ridge can be left undisturbed through the season when the largest numbers of birds pass. The work will still proceed. It will proceed somewhat differently.

The power does not ask the basin to remain unfinished forever.

It asks that those making it notice what the unfinished ground already supports.

Beyond the site, it attends to an older reach of shore: firmer mud, a tidal creek, a bank high enough for resting. That place is threatened too, though no pumps are running there yet. A decision concerning it has been treated as routine. The power helps it become a decision people actually examine.

The surveyor’s records matter. So do the records of people who have watched that older shore for years. Alternatives are considered before commitments make them inconvenient. The creek remains connected to the tide.

It is not a sanctuary from everything. A hawk still crosses it. Cold weather still comes. Birds still quarrel over feeding patches, and some journeys are too long.

But there is somewhere to go when the basin becomes roads.

For now, the small wader knows none of this.

It knows that the mud near the outlet is yielding food. It knows that a person on the walkway has stopped, and moves away along a slanting course. It knows the sudden pull of a worm and braces its fine legs against it.

At dusk it returns to the ridge.

The pump is still running. Rain has begun again. Across the basin, a worker checks the new outlet while another closes the workshop door. Their lamps tremble on the water.

The bird climbs onto firm sand. There is room beside it for the others, and above it another few inches that the night’s water will not reach.

It folds one leg.

The power remains attentive, but does not keep the bird awake.
