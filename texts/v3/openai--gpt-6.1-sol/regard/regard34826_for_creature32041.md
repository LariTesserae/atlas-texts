The power watched through one complete working day and the night that followed.

It did not enter by the opening in the mesh. An entrance of that size was a fact about the place, not a requirement for attention. It watched from within the warmth of the brass, from the dark behind the drain cover, from the space between the beetle’s egg and the ground to which it had begun to adhere.

It could have lifted the platform.

For a while it considered the weight of the platform, and all that depended on its remaining where it was.

Then it left it alone.

The beetle pulled again. A white filament stretched between the egg and the sediment. The shell yielded slightly.

The power held the filament where it joined the ground.

The beetle’s next pull succeeded.

There was no flash, no sudden coolness. The beetle staggered backward with the egg and stood still until its legs were properly beneath it. Then it continued into the dark.

That was the first thing.

For the rest of the night, the power watched.

It learned that the third finger of the glove was not simply the wettest. The smallest finger was wetter, but its water had a taste that checked the moss. It learned where the centipede went after rubbing itself against the copper tube, and which of the spider’s seven webs supplied most of her food. It followed the red animal down the column and waited while it worked beneath the fallen grain.

The grain was not ready to be carried back.

The animal left it, climbed a ridge, and found another.

Near dawn, the white spur broke.

The fracture began well above the beetles’ old chamber. A hairline opened, paused, and ran downward. The outer half tipped into the basin.

The power did not put it back.

It let the spur fall, but turned it slightly as it fell, so that its broad side struck an empty patch instead of the fold of leaf where the beetles had taken their eggs. The impact made a low arch. Powder settled on the leaf. One beetle came to the edge, touched the new wall with its antennae, and withdrew.

By midday a mite had found the arch.

By evening there were tracks beneath it.

The power stayed through another day.

There was an old mortar seam beside the entrance, packed with grit and a strip of blackened paper. Behind it lay a narrow hollow. Air could enter, but no animal larger than the pale things could pass the constriction.

The hollow was cooler than the basin. Its floor had formed years before the pipe split. Nothing there rose overnight.

The power loosened three grains of mortar.

It did this slowly. The first dropped during a spin cycle and was lost among other small movements. The second came away at dusk. The third remained hanging until an ant pulled at it, seeking something caught beneath.

The ant dragged it clear.

Beyond the seam was room enough to turn around, to lay eggs, to sleep without waking on a stalk. Not a second basin. Only a recess, with several cracks leading deeper into the wall.

The power left the loosened grit outside. It might be useful.

For two days, no one entered.

Then a snail put its head through the opening. Its chalky fin caught on the edge. It backed away.

The power noticed the fin. It did not remove it. The snail’s shell had grown beneath that weight, and the animal knew how to carry it.

Instead, a grain on the upper edge of the opening lost its hold.

The snail tried again that night.

Inside, it rested on its side for a long time. At dawn it came out and returned to the bottle neck.

The power did not need it to stay.

Under the tap, the brown animal stretched down from the last exposed rung. Its foot brushed the tile without finding purchase. It drew itself up, hung for a moment, and crossed to the cleared strip of cable.

The power watched it make the journey twice.

The cable led almost where it needed to go. Near the bottom it curved away from the catchment, leaving a gap too broad for the animal to cross while carrying its sweeping fiber.

Inside the cable were seven strands. One had already broken beneath the sheath.

The power eased that strand outward.

Over several warm afternoons, a short loop emerged. It reached toward the tile and stopped just above it. Its surface was rough with old oxidation; a hooked foot could hold it.

The brown animal found the loop on its fourth journey.

It tested it with one foot, then two. It descended without the fiber, climbed back, and descended again carrying it.

The following morning the tile was clean at the edges as well as beneath the tap.

The minute bodies gathered where they always had. The brown animal stepped over them.

Rain still fell when the carts passed overhead. The power had felt the force of it against bodies smaller than a drop. It had also watched the two young animals drink, and the green film lift itself into bubbles afterward.

At the pipe’s lowest joint, it bent a loose whisker of lint until the tip touched the waiting water.

Some drops now traveled along the fiber before falling. They struck the base of the wall and ran into the old black channel.

Others fell as before.

The washer still offered shelter. The powder still received new craters. But beneath the seam in the mortar, a small damp patch persisted after the rest had dried.

An ant found it first.

The power waited to see what that changed.

Nothing changed quickly.

The red animal continued to tend its grains. Its left rear foot continued to scrape against glass. Once, on the journey home, it entered the new recess and felt along the old floor with its raised forelegs. It stayed until a mite came through from the other direction.

Then it returned to the brass.

The three occupied grooves remained occupied. In the highest one, the fringe of fibers stirred when a cart crossed above.

Late that evening, the red animal inspected the grain beside the nick.

It lifted it, fed beneath it, and set it down.

The power could have made the bloom richer. It could have kept the brass at a constant warmth, cleaned every joint, held every ledge in place. Watching the animal settle the grain with two small adjustments, it decided against these things.

It had not yet learned enough to know what they would cost.

So it remained attentive.

Weeks later, when the rising ground reached the new loop of wire, the brown animal was already using a second route. The recess held eggs, then empty shells. One of the small red animals spent a night there and left carrying a flake of paint.

The basin went on making undersides.

At evening, the animal in the brass nut touched each of its three grains. Below, an ant crossed the pale column with a fragment of bread.

The animal waited.

The power waited with it.
