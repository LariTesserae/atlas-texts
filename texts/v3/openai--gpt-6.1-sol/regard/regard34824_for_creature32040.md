The power first takes its hand away from the door.

It has felt the little support forming beneath its knuckle, the channel opening to carry off its sweat. It understands that, if it stays, the inhabitant will spend something on accommodating it.

So it withdraws before the third tap.

For a while it attends from outside. This is not difficult for a power, though it is less customary than entering.

It listens through several flowerings. It learns which sounds mean trouble and which merely sound like trouble. The sharp crack of a terrace arriving needs no answer. The rasp of leaves is not thirst. The depths beneath the hanging pots are not holes to be stopped.

Once, the crooked fern leaflet brushes the reed door. The power leaves it crooked.

It notices the broken handle. It notices, more particularly, the way the inhabitant puts it back.

It does not make a jar to match.

There may have been a jar. There may have been someone who held it. There may be a reason the handle is kept that has nothing to do with jars. The power, which could discover these things, does not go searching through a cupboard that has not been opened to it.

What it watches longest is the sleep.

The inhabitant sits beside the stream with its cap over its eyes. A thumb moves against a palm. A pool begins below the shelf. Then a load is set down far beneath the stair, and the sleeper’s hand is already reaching when its head comes up.

The jar it catches was not falling yet.

The power waits until the inhabitant has eaten. It waits until the snail has passed, and the seed-husk spoon has been wiped and put away.

Then, on the outside of the reed door, it gives three small taps.

Inside, the working noises continue.

After a little while, the door moves inward.

There is an eye between two reeds. Below it, a hand holds a length of thread taut; whatever is happening farther along that thread cannot yet be left.

The power makes itself no brighter than an old clay surface. It gives off no heat.

“Is there something I may hold?” it asks.

The eye remains.

The power waits.

At last the hand emerges. It does not offer the thread. Instead, it presses two stained fingertips against the underside of the stair.

A tremor is coming up through the mountain.

The power places its attention there.

Below, a slab has caught against another slab. Six builders are still beneath its weight while a seventh searches for somewhere to set the edge. The power does not lift the mountain away from them. It holds the slipping stone just long enough for the searching hand to find its purchase.

The load settles.

In the pocket, a bead on the ceiling lengthens, shortens, and stays where it is.

The inhabitant feels the stair again.

Then it lets the reeds fall shut.

That is the first work they do together.

---

The power returns without knocking during the next flowering, but remains outside.

Later, when the brittle clicking stops beneath a shelf, a splinter is pushed through the doorway. One end is wet with clay.

The power holds it.

Inside, the insect turns around in the space this frees. A hand comes to tie the far end. The splinter is drawn back.

There is no greeting afterward. The power finds it has not needed one.

Over time, it is given other things to hold: the weight of a shelf while a cord is replaced; a troublesome drop that must not land until the cup beneath it has a bottom; once, the little door itself, while its lowest reeds are replaited.

It never finishes the tying. It never makes the cup.

When it is no longer needed, it lets go.

The inhabitant begins to distinguish that letting-go from a failure.

---

The first request for rest is made without words.

The inhabitant comes to the door carrying its spoon. Its cap is pushed back, exposing a pale line across its forehead. For several breaths it stands there, looking outward at the enormous slopes.

Then it puts the spoon down.

It brings a small unfinished cup and sets it on the threshold. The cup leans. Its base is scarcely thicker than a leaf.

The inhabitant steadies it with one finger.

The power steadies it from below.

The finger lifts.

For a moment nothing else happens.

Then the inhabitant goes back inside.

It chooses a place beside the running water. It sits, pulls down its cap, and folds its hands loosely, without keeping the shape of a task between them.

A shower falls.

The power holds the cup.

The inhabitant stirs. Its arm moves halfway toward the nearest jar, then stops. Rain drums on the leaves. The insect clicks once behind a wall.

The arm settles.

There are still leaks. One gutter wets a patch of moss that is usually dry. A new tile comes up unevenly. The snail, encountering a soft edge, spends a long time there.

Nothing is abandoned.

When the inhabitant wakes, it goes first to the threshold. It takes the cup in both hands and examines it, turning it to see what has been done.

It finds no additions.

Only the base has kept its shape.

It takes the cup inside.

---

Far below, the builders begin to find places to put down their loads.

Not many. Not so many that the mountain rises carelessly, or that a person forgets what another person’s back can bear.

A ledge remains sound through the few breaths needed to change a grip. A basket rests without tipping while its bearer drinks. Someone calling for help is heard over the grinding of stone.

The power holds where it has been asked, and sometimes where a falling weight leaves no time to ask. Elsewhere it watches, learning the work.

Near the summit, behind the reeds, the country continues to arrive.

The broken handle stays in the cupboard.

The fern keeps its crooked leaflet.

And beside the door there is, eventually, a cup whose purpose the inhabitant has never had to explain.
