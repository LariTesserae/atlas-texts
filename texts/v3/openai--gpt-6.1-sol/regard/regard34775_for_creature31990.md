The power noticed the woman’s hand first.

She had set two fingers on an inspection cover and was waiting for a vibration that had not yet arrived. Beneath the iron, a chamber was filling. A float had caught against its guide. The water would rise another few inches before finding the cable opening.

She knew something was wrong. She did not yet know which thing.

The power could have moved the float.

Instead, it moved the float and stayed to learn why it had caught.

There was corrosion on the guide, a slight distortion in the bracket, a replacement part ordered twice and twice delayed. There was also a man who had installed the bracket during a week when his father was dying. He had done the work adequately. The fault had taken eleven years to become dangerous.

The power did not go looking for someone to blame.

Under the cover, the float lifted. A pump started. The familiar vibration reached the woman’s fingers.

She remained crouched a moment longer.

“That’s not right,” she said.

She opened the chamber anyway.

***

For several nights, the power made only small changes.

A ladder bolt held beneath the woman’s weight.

A hot bearing lasted until the end of a shift, then stopped before it could throw fragments through its housing.

In a flooded street, a child’s foot came down on sound pavement rather than the missing drain cover beside it. The child felt the difference only as a sudden awkwardness, a step that had gone farther than intended. An adult caught the back of their shirt.

The power learned the city’s dependencies.

It learned that stopping a discharge could save fish at one outlet and kill them at another. That an empty warehouse was not empty. That a dry drain might be a road, a nursery, a place to drink. That the mudflats depended on sediment which the harbor engineers described as a persistent nuisance.

It watched a heron take a shrimp.

It watched a rat carry food to young hidden behind an electrical cabinet.

It watched the woman eat standing up because there was no clean place to sit.

By the fourth night, it understood that keeping everything just short of disaster would leave the city almost exactly as it was.

***

The first person it spoke to was awake above the pump house, turning a swollen finger beneath a lamp.

There was no voice in the room. The worker simply became aware of being asked a question, clearly enough to answer aloud.

“What would help?”

He looked at his hand.

“A doctor before it gets worse.”

After a moment, he added, “One I can afford.”

The power asked others.

Some answers were immediate. A cooler room. A reliable ferry. A door that locked. Someone to mind a child during the night shift.

Others took time.

The woman did not answer until she had finished checking a cabinet.

“Enough people,” she said. “And the parts we ask for.”

Then she looked up, suspicious of the ease with which she had spoken.

“Who’s paying?”

The answer came with an understanding of accounts: no emptied pension fund, no debt concealed in a contract, no distant household losing what this household gained.

She considered that.

“And don’t make them all my responsibility.”

***

Within a month, deliveries began arriving.

They were ordinary enough to need unloading: bearings packed in grease, stainless brackets, insulated cable, bolts in labeled bags. The correct washers came in quantities that allowed someone to drop one without spending twenty minutes searching the grit.

There were wages for apprentices and enough wages for experienced workers to teach them.

There was a clinic in two rooms above the ridge road. Its windows opened. Its appointments included hours after midnight.

People argued about these things. They questioned the contracts and checked the accounts. One shipment sat untouched for three days while the woman measured every component against the old specification.

The power waited.

It had offered help, not demanded trust.

When she finally signed for the parts, she kept the packing slips.

***

Some work was beyond the city’s equipment.

At low tide, sections of seawall acquired rough ledges below the wash of boat wakes. Young fish gathered there. Mussels settled into shaded recesses. The changes left the ladders clear and the navigation channels deep.

Behind the salt works, heat passed into a new exchange system before the discharge reached open water. The transition took weeks. Fish accustomed to the warm outlet found other feeding currents nearby; the shrimp did not vanish overnight from water that had suddenly become unfamiliar.

Freshwater leaks were repaired. Small drinking places remained above the tide, supplied without flooding the walls or breeding mosquitoes beside the houses.

The power removed old line from a fish’s jaw.

It could not ask permission in words. It loosened the buried strand while the fish rested in deep water, then drew it free without tearing the flesh. The fish fled, circled once, and resumed holding against the current.

Farther out, the birds gained a place to roost when the mud disappeared: a low bank beyond the working aprons, free of lamps and high enough for the tides that were coming.

The power did not put trees in salt-crusted ground merely because the yards looked bare from above. Where people wanted shade, it made room for shade. Where soil and freshwater could support roots, it helped them establish. Elsewhere, roofs extended over walkways, and benches stood beneath them.

***

The woman was offered a different house.

She declined.

She was offered repairs to the house she had.

She accepted those, after explaining which downstairs cupboard must remain accessible and why the stair rail could not be moved.

One evening, an apprentice met her at the beginning of her route.

He carried a torch. His screwdriver sheath was new and badly placed, so that it caught against his thigh when he crouched.

She showed him how to shift it.

At the third cover, she took her fingers off the iron and made room for his.

“What do you feel?”

He listened through his hand.

Behind them, the tide was entering the old waterfront street. Tiny fish turned along the curb. The power had offered to keep the street dry, and the people living there had asked for time to decide. For now, the cabinets were safe, the open holes were covered, and a raised path connected every doorway.

The apprentice named the wrong pump.

The woman shook her head.

“Again.”

This time he waited longer.

***

There were still things to repair.

Salt found metal. Storms arrived. Animals ate other animals, and the gulls continued to recognize lunch at a considerable distance.

But a failed valve no longer needed to become a ruined home. An injured hand no longer needed to become a lost livelihood. The woman could leave work knowing that someone else knew what to listen for.

On her first full evening off, she walked to the seawall without her tool bag.

For a while she found this uncomfortable.

Then she rested both hands on the concrete. Below her, the barnacles were feeding. Across the basin, a door opened and a worker stepped into the light carrying a bucket.

He emptied it into a new collection tank beside the doorway.

Under the quay, the fish waited briefly, then went elsewhere.

The woman watched them go.

When she turned toward home, nothing called her back.
