The power noticed the hollow because the ground was still moving.

It listened below the water, below the pale clay, where the weight of the fallen country pressed upon layers that had not yet found a resting place. It listened also to the thin pattering through the dams, the scrape of teeth on willow, and the breathing inside the lodges.

These were different kinds of information. None made the others unimportant.

For a while, it did nothing.

A branch caught in a channel. A young beaver struggled with it, pushing from the wrong side, then swimming around to pull. The power let the branch remain caught. After several attempts the animal freed it, lost hold of it, and caught it again farther downstream.

At the feeding ledge, an adult shifted a willow stem between its paws. There was an old stiffness in one shoulder. The power attended to that too, but the animal was warm beneath its coat, eating well, and using the limb without distress. When it finished, it climbed into the water and swam strongly.

The hollow was not asking to become another place.

There were hungry animals here, and cold mornings, and banks that failed. There were also sheltered chambers, familiar bodies, food stored underwater, and passages worn clear by years of use. The power took time to distinguish what was difficult from what was about to become disastrous.

Beneath the northern bank, a seam of clay was opening.

It had begun well above the waterline, behind an intact-looking slab of turf. Rain entered through the roots and collected along the seam. The slab carried two alders, a patch of moss, and stones embedded in its underside. When it fell, it would strike a narrow pond beside a lodge built against a birch trunk.

The lodge held a sleeping adult and two young.

Their underwater entrance faced the bank. The fall would bury it. The roof, braced by the birch, would probably remain standing. There would be air inside for a time.

The power followed the seam through the clay. It examined the birch, the lodge walls, the slope of the pond bottom. Then it made one small change beneath the turf.

A stone turned in its bed.

Water that had been gathering behind the slab found a lower route. It seeped through gravel, emerged among alder roots, and entered a shallow pool some distance from the lodge.

The bank did not become firm. By morning, a piece of it had slumped into the pool. Moss folded under muddy water. One alder leaned farther than it had the night before.

But the heavy slab above the lodge settled inward against its own stones.

Inside, a young beaver woke and pressed against the adult’s flank. The adult shifted to make room. Neither heard anything unusual.

The new seep troubled the power afterward.

Its water was colder than the pool it entered. A cluster of larvae lay among the submerged leaves there. Farther down, the added flow reached a vole’s passage through the sedge.

The power watched through the following day. The larvae remained in the quieter pockets. The vole abandoned a short length of passage and opened another above it. A duck fed where clay had washed from the bank. The water spread, thinned, and found its way into an existing channel.

Only then did the power turn its attention elsewhere.

It did not strengthen every dam. One broke during the next rain, draining a feeding pool and leaving stripped branches on the mud. The beavers worked at the breach that evening. An older youngster brought stems too light to stay in the current; later it returned with a heavier branch, which lodged against the broken end.

The power watched the repair take shape.

It left the heron beside its chosen channel. It left the otter’s quick passage through the reeds. When a duck rose sharply from the water, scattering droplets, it followed her long enough to see her settle in another pool.

By dusk, the hollow was dark along its margins.

The adult from the birch-backed lodge emerged with its young. One went directly toward the feeding bank. The other lingered beside the entrance, nosing at a floating leaf until the adult turned back.

Above them, the northern alders stood at slightly different angles.

Nothing shone. No voice spoke from the clouds. The beavers swam beneath the hanging roots and disappeared into the reeds.

The power remained nearby while the rain began again, listening to the ground and to the water, learning which sounds belonged to work, which to weather, and which required an answer.
