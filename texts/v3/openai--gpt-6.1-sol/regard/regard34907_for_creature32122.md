The power arrived after the gardener had gone home.

It did not stop the pumps to announce itself. It listened to them, and to the smaller sounds they covered: a loose washer turning in a valve housing, a goat worrying at a gate, someone coughing in a room whose window had swollen shut.

At the channel the gardener had cleared, water was still moving. A little escaped through the old joint. Most reached the next trough, where a tree had begun, very slowly, to recover from the afternoon.

The power followed both streams.

It had time enough to follow everything. Nevertheless, it chose this place to begin: a repaired obstruction, a leaking joint, a woman who had said *for now* and meant to return.

Inside her apartment, she was washing the fine roots from beneath her nails. She had laid the blue thread on the table without knowing why. One of her fingers hurt when she straightened it. Tomorrow she would carry a smaller bucket and hope nobody noticed.

The power could have taken the pain away.

First it looked at what caused it.

Her route had grown longer over the years. A lift had been closed after a cable inspection, pending a replacement that had never arrived. Two beds had been added to her round when another gardener retired. The channels serving those beds were unreliable, so she carried water up a stair that was hot by nine in the morning.

She knew how much she could carry. She also knew which plants would die if she carried less.

The power stayed beside her while she dried her hands. She could not see it. Once, she turned toward the open window, where a moth was beating against the lamp, and had the curious sensation that someone was waiting politely for her attention.

Then the sensation passed.

Below the district, the power descended through the mountain.

There were rooms no one upstairs had seen: large, wet chambers, cable galleries with lamps far apart, a canteen where three night workers sat beneath a picture of a lake. A man in the canteen had wrapped cloth around the handle of his mug because a hairline crack made it leak against his thumb.

Farther down, two people were replacing a pump seal. They had worked past the end of their shift. The spare assembly was the wrong size, and they were altering the mounting to make it fit.

The water that cooled their chamber would eventually become part of the gardeners’ morning allotment.

The power watched them work. It watched the woman brace a heavy housing with her knee while her companion reached for a bolt. It looked through the records upstairs and found the request for a lifting frame, submitted twice.

For a while it considered making the mountain sound.

There were means available to it. The corroded metal could be restored; the foundations could be made secure; water could rise through every dry pipe. It could have given the gardener a morning without pain and the night workers a lifetime without another failing seal.

But some of the pipes had been abandoned deliberately. Some walls needed to move with the heat. Roots occupied spaces that had once been empty, and whole households depended on arrangements no plan recorded. Even with all that it could know, it would have to ask what people wanted done to their homes.

By morning, it had decided on a few things that could not reasonably wait.

The cracked support beneath the pump housing held. It had been about to fail.

At an upper bridge, a child put a foot on a wet leaf, slipped, and caught the rail. He stood breathing hard until his sister came back for him. Neither knew that a rusted fastening had just acquired another season’s strength.

These were small interventions, made without witnesses. The power did not consider them sufficient.

It appeared to the gardener while she was testing the repaired channel.

To her it looked like a person standing on the other side of the bed, dressed suitably for the heat. She could not afterwards remember the face, but she remembered that it had waited until she set down her hook.

“I would like to help,” it said.

She looked at the empty bucket beside her.

“With what?”

It asked her about her round. She showed it the troublesome joints, the lift door, the tree that needed less water than anyone believed. When it asked what she would change, she did not begin with the tree.

“I’d get the lift working.”

They went to find the mechanic.

Over the following weeks, there were many such conversations. The power asked to be shown things, including things people had ceased to report. It sat through arguments about water and shade. It spoke with those who worked below the clouds, and with a woman who wanted to leave the district but could no longer manage the stairs down to the passenger station.

It did not require them to agree before it helped.

It provided metal of known strength, tools that could be repaired locally, and enough stores to let machinery be taken out of service before it broke. The lifting frame reached the lower chamber. The orchard lift received its cable. Workers were paid for the hours they spent explaining what the maps had missed.

There were disappointments. One proposed reservoir proved too heavy for its terrace. A new maintenance path would have passed through somebody’s kitchen, and was routed elsewhere after a loud afternoon. The gardener declined a device that would have watered her beds automatically; later, after seeing one in use, she asked for a simpler version she could open herself.

The power remained available. This was perhaps the strangest change. A request could be answered; a bad repair could be reconsidered. Help did not end at the moment someone expressed gratitude.

By the next dry season, the gardener’s hand had healed. She still carried a bucket sometimes, but not up the long hot stair.

One evening she returned to the old channel and found it draining slowly again.

She lifted the screen. Roots had grown into the opening, catching a feather and a twist of paper. She cleared them, checked the joint, and watched the water pass.

Below her, the man taking in his washing looked up.

“Is it fixed?”

“For now,” she said.

This time, before going home, she wrote the fault on the board beside the lift. There was room beneath it for a date, a name, and what would be done next.
