At first, what came to the cavern mistook the noise for distress.

It had heard distress in other places: the splitting of a house, the cries of creatures crowded against a closed passage, the long scrape of something dragging an injured limb. Here those sounds never ceased. They crossed and thickened until the whole country seemed to be suffering.

The power reached toward the nearest stem.

Then it waited.

A seed case burst overhead. The child beneath it shut both outer lids and hunched around the grazer. Fragments struck his shoulders. He shook them off without putting the animal down.

Across the basin, the fallen climber found another purchase. Its scales rattled. A grazer column divided around a young shoot and joined itself again beyond it.

Esset shifted her listening peg half the width of a fingernail.

The power withdrew its hand.

It began to listen more carefully.

***

There were hurts here. There were also joints opening as they should, animals feeding as they must, houses coming apart because their owners knew how to take them apart.

The burrower beneath the shelter was not attacking anything. It had reached tissue too dense to swallow and was pushing beside it. The rib was beginning to twist.

Esset felt the twist through her teeth.

She took the peg from her mouth and struck three times against the floor.

The shelter keeper stopped undoing the trays. He had been working from the wrong end.

Two people moved to the opposite corner. One dropped to her knees and reached beneath it for a fastening that had tightened under the load.

Her arm went in to the shoulder.

The ground rose again.

The power could have held the leaf still. It could have held the burrower still. It could have carried the six houses elsewhere without loosening another knot.

But the woman beneath the floor was already pulling her arm back. The shelter keeper was raising the corner with a lever. Esset had planted the hard edge of her foot against a fiber and was bracing his hip with both hands.

They needed very little.

The power took the weight of the corner.

The woman’s hand came free.

For a moment the shelter keeper continued to lean on his lever. Then he noticed that it was no longer bearing anything. He glanced at Esset.

She looked down at her feet.

The leaf had not stopped moving.

***

They finished dismantling the shelter.

No one left a tool behind. A bowl of sorted seed cores went into a carrying frame. The large brace was divided at its mismatched corners. The boy passed the grazer to a girl whose sleeping wrap was already tied across her chest; she tucked it into the fold, where it began eating the hem.

When the last floor tray was lifted, the rib split lengthwise.

Amber drew bright threads between the opening edges. Below them something broad and densely ringed moved through its own shadow.

The power kept the broken edges from closing on it.

The burrower passed.

Its last segments rubbed against the split with a deep, almost musical rasp. Then it was gone, widening another stretch of tissue farther along.

The leaf settled crookedly.

The six houses could not remain there.

***

The new strip across the basin looked promising. That was the trouble with it.

It had shelter, warmth, and an easy approach along the central rib. Nothing had yet walked on its surface. Nothing had tested the seam beneath the overhanging leaf.

The two residents studying it sent back a bundle with its last knot loose.

Esset rubbed the knot between her thumb and finger.

Beside her, something touched the back of her wrist.

She turned.

There was no person there. There was only an impression in the leaf fur, broad enough for a foot, and a small patch where the dust hung motionless against the moving air.

The touch came again.

It was gentle, but not uncertain.

Esset looked toward the others. They were loading the trays. The man with the repaired carrying frame had his wall strip strapped along one side. Its scratched marks faced inward.

She held out her hand.

Something met it.

It was warm. It had learned the shape of a hand, though not quite the weight of one.

Esset kept her fingers open.

The presence did not close them.

After a while she took its hand.

***

It did not put knowledge into her head.

It led her three steps to a portion of rib she would otherwise have passed, then released her.

Esset set her peg against it.

Under the feeding rasp and the distant knocks, she felt the new leaf taking its load. The upper skin was stretching evenly. Below it, three thick bundles of fiber pulled in different directions.

One was giving way.

She moved her peg, listened again, and signaled the nearest scout.

The scout came back. Esset showed him where to place his own peg.

He listened for a long time.

Then he tightened one knot in the route bundle and untied another.

They would use the strip, but not the sheltered seam. They would have to go farther around.

The power stayed beside Esset as she returned to the carrying frames.

She touched its wrist—or the place where its wrist seemed to be—and laid two fingers beneath its elbow.

*Hold this weight.*

It held the large brace.

***

Moving six houses was work even with another pair of hands.

The route crossed a steepening patch where the leaf fur had been grazed almost smooth. People went sideways, hard foot edges set downhill. Loads were lowered on ropes and brought back up where the rib flattened. Twice they stopped while columns of animals passed.

The power did not part the columns.

It stood with the others.

When a floor tray began sliding, it caught the tray. When a child reached for an explosive casing, it placed a warm palm between the child’s hand and the seed. When the repaired carrying frame caught in a fork, it helped the owner lift it clear.

The wall strip came loose.

The man snatched for it and missed.

It fell down the slope, turned once, and lodged beneath the belly of a grazer.

The power could see the scratches. It could see the places where a blade had hesitated, where someone had gone back to deepen a mark, where the youngest child’s first height sat barely above the edge.

It retrieved the strip.

It did not make the marks brighter or the wood stronger. It brought it back with a little leaf fuzz caught along one corner.

The man took it and pressed his covered forehead briefly against it.

Then he found another fastening.

***

Esset’s foot began to fail on the last climb.

The splint beneath her smaller gripping pad had turned. She could walk, but each step drove its end into the fold of skin.

She stopped in a draft and opened the slits along her ribs.

The presence waited beside her.

She pointed at the splint.

A slight pressure lifted it away from the sore place.

Esset sat down.

From her bundle she took a length of old route cord, the one her granddaughter wanted. Its surface was smooth from years of unknotting. She cut off a short piece and passed it toward the waiting hand.

The power held her foot while she repaired the brace.

Her pad did not become young. The old fold did not disappear. But once the splint was turned and tied, she could put her weight on it without flinching.

She stood, tested it, and clicked her outside edge against the rib.

The presence followed.

***

They raised the houses on the new strip.

By then the crystal light had not changed, but everyone was tired enough to speak more slowly.

The first floor went crooked. They lifted it and tried again. A young shoot under the work shelter was fitted with a brace, and its tip was guided away from the place where food would be warmed.

The power was offered one end of an awning rope.

It took the end.

Someone rubbed a thumb across its knuckles.

The lashings had been checked.

***

Only when the wall screens were lowered did the inhabitants discover another thing it could do.

The new house trembled with all the ordinary events of the cavern. Its floor carried footsteps from outside, the scrape of feeding mouths below, and a heavy tearing noise from the far basin.

Yet within the screens there was a small space where voices could be heard without bringing faces together.

The man who had saved the wall strip spoke to his partner from across the floor.

She looked up sharply.

He repeated himself.

They both laughed, and the sound was strange enough that the child between them laughed too.

Esset came to examine the house. She pressed her jaw to the peg, then laid a palm on the floor.

The ground had lost nothing. She could still feel the burrowers and the growth.

Only the airborne roar was softer.

She touched the warm presence at the back of its wrist and pointed toward the hanging screen.

The quiet followed the screen when she raised it. When she lowered it again, the room became quiet once more.

She raised it a second time and left it open.

There were things she wanted to hear until the settlement was secure.

The presence waited without objection.

***

Later, her granddaughter came for the polished cord.

Esset gave her what remained.

The girl noticed the new binding beneath her grandmother’s foot. She ran a bent finger along it, then looked at the little patch of still dust beside the doorway.

“Did that help?”

Esset nodded.

“Will it stay?”

Esset put her hand out.

The presence met it.

Beyond the house, the broad leaf stretched. A shell animal snapped beside the food stone. Someone in the next room set down a bowl too hard and apologized.

Esset considered the hand resting against hers.

Then she turned it palm upward and placed her listening peg in it.

The power lowered the point to the rib.

For a long while it listened.

When she reached for the peg, it gave it back.

It stayed.
