For a long time, nothing changes that you can confidently attribute to it.

The cups still rise and settle. The slabs still turn beneath the pools. At the boundary, someone carries another basket out of the dark, puts it down, and searches for the corner of the cloth.

But the corner is nearer than it was.

You notice this because the hand searches in the old place first. Its fingers close on empty air. Then they find the cloth, folded back just far enough to be taken without lifting the basket.

The next time, the cloth is left as it was.

Whoever has come here is learning.

—

Under one of the ceiling sheets, a holder has stood so long that its feet have become difficult to distinguish from the floor.

There is no neglect in this. Cups arrive regularly. A scraper has been used to draw a channel around the place where its weight rests, keeping the hot rain from gathering there. Someone has fitted a shelter beside it, though the holder cannot move beneath the shelter without lowering the sheet.

The sheet is very broad. Along its far edge, other sheets depend on it. Beneath those are channels, benches, the hollows where bodies loosen out of sight.

One afternoon, a small wedge appears beside the holder.

It is not driven under anything. It lies within reach, thick at one end, narrowing to a blunt point. Its surface has the grain of no material you recognize.

For three days the holder does not touch it.

On the fourth, a finger emerges from the strain in the air. It presses the wedge and withdraws.

The wedge gives slightly.

Later, the marked-wrist worker comes. It lays out two tools and a folded cloth. It examines the wedge with the back of a knuckle, then takes a shaving from the thick end.

The shaving curls. It remains on the ground.

By evening, the worker has cut a notch in the wedge. By morning, someone else has filled the notch. The filling is careful. So is the next cut.

The wedge remains available throughout.

When they finally place it beneath the sheet, they do not use it as its maker apparently expected. They turn it sideways, split it into three pieces, and lash the pieces to a bundle of rods.

Nothing objects.

The holder lowers its shoulder by less than the depth of a fingerprint.

Across the plain, water trembles in several cups.

Then the holder raises its shoulder again. One of the lashings has pulled too tight. A little work is needed.

—

You never see the power arriving.

Once you think you see its shadow beneath a pool, but the pool contains a smaller landscape, and there may be weather there.

What becomes apparent is a new kind of interval.

A mallet slips from an uncertain grip. Before it strikes a soft seam, there is time for the grip to close again.

A slab turns farther than intended. Its motion slows while two workers draw their feet clear.

At the dark boundary, a basket is set down on ground that has not yet acquired the strength to bear it. The ground yields. The basket stays at the height where it was placed until someone can slide a board beneath it.

These pauses do not happen everywhere. A cup still breaks beside a channel. A bank still subsides, releasing its water in one quiet sideways movement.

But where a body is caught, or a weight is passing beyond the hands that can hold it, there is sometimes an extra moment.

The inhabitants begin to use it.

At first they withdraw from danger. Later, they reach for one another.

—

The first support takes a long time to settle.

It cannot simply be strong. The ceiling moves. The dimensions beneath it are unfinished. A rod that reaches comfortably from floor to sheet in the morning may be too short by evening, or so long that it has begun pressing a dent into the roof.

The new material waits through these changes without forcing either surface to remain where it was.

The workers distrust that quality.

They load a scrap with baskets. They leave it standing in a pool. One places a cup containing a distant landscape on top of it and watches from beneath a shelter.

Another takes it to the abandoned bank and builds a small, deliberately faulty arch.

The arch falls.

The material does not prevent it. It keeps the falling pieces from striking the hand still beneath them.

Afterward, the pieces are wrapped in damp cloth.

A scrap is left beside the bundles.

—

There is a place where the white figures gather so thickly that the ground seems dusted with flour.

They have found the new support.

Along its sides they trace changes that never happen: a crack, a bend, a separation at the base. Their branches extend, hesitate, and draw back. By evening, several lie almost still.

The support is holding too completely.

The following morning, there are shallow grooves in it. Moisture travels along them. At the lower end, an unfinished patch remains soft enough to receive a mark.

The white figures begin again.

One follows a bead of water halfway down, stops, and tries another route. A worker presses a thumb into the soft patch. The mark stays.

From then on, the supports arrive unfinished.

They have rough ends, excess material, surfaces that can be cut. Some are rejected. One becomes the handle of a scraper. Another is divided into little feet for an unstable bench.

No two are used alike.

—

Eventually the holder steps away from the ceiling.

You are there when it happens, although for some time you mistake the movement for a change in the ground.

The two shallow hollows loosen. A third impression appears between them. Water enters a place it has not entered for a very long time.

The sheet descends a little onto its supports.

The holder remains beneath it.

A cup is brought. The holder does not reach.

Someone sets the cup on the floor, and the hand that has brought it waits nearby.

Then a shape begins to gather out of the pressure in the air. There is a bent shoulder, an arm held close to the body, a wrist whose folds have been pressed together for so long that they separate slowly. It seems unable to decide how much of itself is needed when there is no weight to bear.

The cup rises.

The water diminishes.

For a while, that is all.

Later, the holder moves as far as the edge of the shelter. Its first resting place is not the bench but the ground beside it. The clay receives its weight with a broad, uncertain hollow.

No one puts a tool within reach.

—

The power makes mistakes.

A patch of cooler air appears beside a workbench. It is pleasant against your face. On the bench, however, the edge of a covered slab begins to harden before its center. The marked-wrist worker lifts the cloth, examines the damage, and lays a damp palm across it.

The coolness withdraws.

It returns some days later above the drinking cups, faint enough that their water loses a little heat without chilling the clay around them.

A path laid toward the boundary is lifted and moved by the inhabitants. It had crossed a place where something rested below the floor.

A light opens in the ceiling. Three workers prop a sheet across it, leaving only a narrow slit.

The light stays narrow.

You start to recognize its attention by these corrections.

—

At the boundary, the darkness remains.

No great floor spreads across it overnight. No complete landscape arrives to relieve the inhabitants of making this one.

But beside the last row of props there is now a low shelf.

On it stand vessels of several shapes. Some hold water. Some are empty. There is a cloth, a newly dressed scraper, and a piece of the unfamiliar material, left large enough to be useful and small enough to carry.

Beyond the shelf, the heat continues. You hear the scrape, the pat, the creak of a handle under pressure.

A fresh slab comes forward.

The hands that carry it are worn. One bears the small dark mark you know.

This time another hand waits beneath the slab’s far edge.

It does not take the weight at once. It stays open, close enough that the worker need only lower the clay a little.

For a while, both hands remain there.

Then the marked wrist turns.

The weight is shared.

The slab comes to rest at the edge of the plain, and the assisting hand withdraws before the smoothing begins.

—

Much later, you return to the shelter with the two cups.

There is a third.

It has no handle yet. A little cord lies beside it, and several pieces of clay are covered under a damp cloth. Whoever will use it has not decided how it should be held.

You sit.

At the far end of the bench, one hollow deepens. Beside it, a second hollow forms slowly, as though someone unfamiliar with sitting is trying not to crowd the first.

For a while, the cups remain on the ground.

Rain gathers on the roof. Through a badly fitted corner comes enough light to see the cord, the cloth, the unfinished rim.

A hand reaches toward the clay.

Another turns the cup so the work is easier to reach.
