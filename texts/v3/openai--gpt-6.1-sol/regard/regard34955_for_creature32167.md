The attention does not arrive as light.

For a long time, nothing in the city marks its arrival at all. The water keeps its temperature. The rain hangs where it has always hung. In the upper storey, the inhabitant presses against the suspended water, and the water gives without letting it fall.

What has noticed the city could empty the basin. It could set a horizon beyond the flats, open the darkness, harden every wall against dissolution.

It does none of these things.

First it attends to the inhabitant beside the lost room.

There is little left of the building. Two mineral shelves indicate where the entrance stood. Between them, water sometimes turns aside, though the paving beyond is now continuous with the courtyard.

The former occupant is difficult to distinguish even from close by. Its weight arrives in several places, none quite joined to the others. At intervals it attempts the old distribution of warmth: mild at the entrance, greater farther in, cool beside the corner.

There is no longer a corner.

The coolness forms anyway. It lasts for a short time and is carried off by the water.

The attention remains outside.

It does not enter the thin arrangement that is still a body. It does not gather the scattered warmth together. When a visitor comes, it leaves the interval between them unobstructed.

There are several visits. One is brief. Another continues through a rain. During the longest, the former occupant collects itself enough to make a narrow space in its middle, and the visitor flattens against the paving.

Something passes.

The attention learns less from the passing than from what happens afterward. The visitor leaves. The former occupant does not follow, but its warmth holds together longer than before.

The distinction matters.

So does another: on certain occasions the inhabitant reaches toward the missing corner; on others it lets the old order go, and rests where a small current travels across the courtyard.

Neither movement is treated as an error.

After a while, the attention makes one alteration.

Beside the courtyard, where no avenue will be narrowed and no plant grows, a shallow hollow appears in a mineral shelf. It is not a room. It has no corners, no threshold, nothing that would oblige the water to wait outside.

The hollow holds warmth a little longer than the surrounding stone.

The difference is slight. A visitor crosses it without stopping. Rain fills it. When the rain ends, it drains.

The former occupant remains where it is.

The attention does not deepen the hollow.

Much later, a part of the inhabitant extends into it. Only a part: a low firmness, scarcely wider than the rim. The rest stays spread across the place where the room stood.

For some time, the warmth in the hollow and the warmth in the courtyard have no evident connection.

Then they arrive together.

The inhabitant withdraws.

The hollow cools at once.

When it approaches again, the hollow retains what it brings, but adds nothing. There is no unfamiliar pattern laid over its own. The inhabitant can leave without contracting, without tearing itself away from anything that has begun to hold it.

It tries this several times.

Eventually it rests there through a rain.

Nothing resembling a recovery occurs all at once. The former room does not rise from the paving. The inhabitant does not become as large as it was.

But a visitor comes and turns toward the hollow rather than the vanished entrance.

The space between them is brief and exact.

After the visitor has gone, a small succession of temperatures remains in the stone. They are not the order of the old room. They belong to the meeting that has just ended.

The inhabitant returns to them.

Elsewhere, the city continues to dissolve and gather.

The attention follows both processes. It notices that some residents leave their failing rooms long before they must, and settle readily beneath arches. Others remain close to a lost threshold. Some disperse despite frequent visitors. Some are no longer found, although the courtyards where they stood retain small differences for a very long time.

It does not make hollows everywhere.

Once, near a submerged stair, it begins to slow the escape of a resident’s warmth. The resident draws sharply away. For several moments its body becomes narrower than the current running past it.

The change is undone.

The attention keeps its distance until the resident spreads again.

After that, offers are smaller.

A ledge that can be used or passed. A place along a channel where two residents may stand without the current breaking their interval. A mineral shelf kept from advancing into the space habitually left clear by three visitors.

Even these alterations are allowed to wear away when they are not used.

The city acquires no monument to the attention. There is no new facade. The empty platform remains empty.

At the next lowering of the water, the inhabitant from the hollow comes to the central square.

It travels slowly. Its body is small, but the weight arrives without the long separation that once made it difficult to locate. Behind it, the hollow retains a little warmth.

At the emerging steps, another resident moves aside.

The inhabitant takes the offered place.

When the change reaches it from below, it holds the change for a long interval. The residents above remain still. The water continues to lower. Another arcade emerges beneath the causeway.

At last the inhabitant releases what it has received.

The passing continues.

Nothing hastens it.

There is something else the attention learns.

The broad avenues are not merely sufficient for the inhabitants who travel them now. Sometimes two residents open their interval farther than either body can readily sustain. They stand at opposite edges of the street. The warmth between them becomes faint, and then more precise.

Occasionally a third disposition gathers there.

It may last only until they move. It may accompany them as far as a square, then loosen into the water. Once, it turns aside and rests beneath an arch while the other two remain in the avenue.

The attention gives these intervals room too.

It does not place a shelter in every emptiness. It does not preserve every wall. There are passages that can exist only after a building has subsided, and meetings that begin in places no one previously inhabited.

The city’s changes are not stopped.

But where a passage of warmth is being broken by something neither participant has chosen, there is sometimes a little less resistance. Where a body cannot leave a failing wall without losing its arrangement, there may be a place close enough to reach.

These are not laws. Water still does unexpected things. A sheltered hollow may remain unused for centuries. A room may finish forming and admit no inhabitant at all.

The attention waits without filling it.

In the courtyard of the lost room, the small hollow gradually loses its distinctness. Its rim softens. A shallow channel forms through its base.

By then, the inhabitant is often elsewhere.

It has acquired a habit of pausing beside a cup plant three streets away. Sometimes another resident joins it. Sometimes it waits alone until the stem bends and spills.

When it returns to the courtyard, it does not always restore the former room’s warmth.

It rests in the current.

On one such return, it leaves a narrow clear interval beside itself. The interval remains open after the water has settled.

The attention does not move into it immediately.

It waits.

The inhabitant makes the interval a little wider.

Then, with no added heat and scarcely any pressure, something answers.

The water continues through them both.
