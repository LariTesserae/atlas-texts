The drop falls.

It strikes the far wall with a sound that passes through your teeth. The animal closes its front legs around the leaf. For a moment the two are so nearly the same green that you cannot tell where one ends.

Something else has noticed them.

You do not see it arrive. There is no new opening, no figure standing where the distances fail. The light remains the light of a mild afternoon. But the chamber is being attended to with a care that does not come from the tree.

At first, nothing changes.

The animal carries the leaf beneath the folded ceiling. It moves more slowly than before. Its thorn protrudes from the shallow fold under its body, and now and then the leaf catches against it. Each time, the animal stops and frees the soft point with one front foot.

The attention follows without casting a shadow.

It follows the channels under the living skin, too: the pressure that raises a ridge, the nourishment moving toward the crown, the little return of warmth when a surface closes. Far above, a branch has lost several leaves. The tree is drawing from what it stored before the cold. The hollow’s next dark interval will be longer than the last.

None of this is corrected.

The animal reaches the closed-eye crease and puts the leaf down.

Released from its feet, the leaf folds tightly. The animal touches it once, then begins to scrape beside it.

Scrape, wait.

Scrape again.

A dark point appears. The animal drinks until green reaches halfway along its sides. Then it withdraws its mouth and shifts the leaf toward the opening.

The leaf does not move easily. One folded edge adheres to the wall; another lifts and catches the air. The animal braces on four legs and works with the other two. Beneath it, the familiar stillness gathers. The wall delays its growth.

You kneel some distance away.

There is an impulse to help. You could move the leaf with a fingernail. You could keep the groove open, perhaps, with something larger than a thorn.

But you cannot see which part the animal is trying to place against the sap.

You leave your hands on your knees.

The leaf opens by a fraction. Within the fold is a clear shape, smaller than the cloudy bead in the animal’s head. It has no recognizable limbs. A thread runs from it into the green of the leaf.

The animal turns the thorn and scrapes again.

Beyond the chamber, weight comes down beside the tree.

The first pressure is familiar. The central stalk leans. Loose moisture draws toward the seams. Your feet begin their quiet reconsideration of the floor.

Then the weight shifts.

A buried stone moves against a root. Tissue pinches between them. Beneath the closed-eye crease, a channel collapses.

The groove goes pale.

The animal presses its mouth to it. Its fine threads extend. Both remain out.

For the first time, the attention acts.

There is no blaze, no great answering sound. Outside, in a space too narrow for your hand, the stone settles differently. Only a little differently: one rough edge comes to rest in soil rather than against the root.

The root is still bruised. The tree still bears the weight above it. Nothing is lifted out of the world.

But the channel opens.

Green returns to the point beneath the animal’s mouth.

It drinks. Then it steps aside.

The folded leaf darkens along one edge.

For several cycles, the attention remains.

The animal leaves and returns. Once it brings the nearly straight thorn with the white line through its center. Once it comes empty-bodied and drinks for a long time before touching the leaf. You still do not know whether this is one animal or several.

The presence does not supply an answer. You are allowed to keep watching.

A small root emerges from the leaf and enters the groove. The wall begins to close around it. This time the animal does not interfere.

The leaf lifts away from the surface.

Underneath is a pale body with six short projections held tightly against it.

When the chamber sweats, the older animal stands over it. Drops cross the air, turn, and pass on. One strikes the leaf and opens it fully.

The small body unfolds three legs.

The rushing begins.

You feel the sound in your ribs. The leaf trembles on its single root, and the animal spreads its feet around it. There is no stillness beneath the new feet yet. One slips. Another reaches and fails to settle.

The attending power could hold them.

Instead, it waits while the little leg reaches again.

This time the foot touches the leaf. Its transparent pad spreads from the center outward.

The next foot finds the wall.

By the time the rushing separates into cracks and whistles, all six legs are down.

The older animal withdraws one pace. The new one stands with its belly still resting on the opened leaf. Green moves inside it in a pulse so small you almost mistake it for changing light.

Later, it tries to walk.

It goes nowhere you expect. It climbs over the animal beside it, pauses on the thorn, then steps onto the wall. The leaf remains rooted behind it.

The attention loosens.

You feel this not as departure but as the return of distances. The closed-eye crease is once again merely difficult to reach. The booming floor is loud enough to interrupt your thoughts. High overhead, leaves move in weather you cannot see.

Near the entrance, soil settles into the small space left beneath the stone. Fine roots will eventually hold it there.

Inside, the older animal cleans its thorn.

The smaller one has found a fresh ridge. It raises a front foot and waits while the ridge grows beneath it.

You stay where you are.

There is still nothing to do with your hands.
