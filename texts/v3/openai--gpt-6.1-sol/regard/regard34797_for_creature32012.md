Neither person speaks until they know whether they need to get up.

This time, the keeper gets up.

The first governor closes when it should. The second closes late. The difference is small enough that a visitor would hear only two blows beneath the floor, but the keeper has spent thirty-seven years with these machines. She puts her feet into her boots without fastening them and crosses to the adjoining room.

“Stay here,” she says.

The other keeper is already reaching for his coat.

Then something touches the wall beneath her hand.

It is not a vibration. The stone yields very slightly, as a palm yields when another palm is laid against it.

She withdraws her hand.

The wall becomes stone again.

Outside, the trees are draining. Water strikes the roof in heavy ropes. The delayed governor closes a second time, and through the floor comes a knock she has never heard before.

She goes to the engine room.

*

The power that has noticed the plateau has no need of a lamp.

Nor does it mistake darkness for damage.

It attends first to the many ways this place is known: the rims of feet opening in clay, the pulse leaving a flier’s throat, the minute difference between water passing through a healthy bearing and water passing beside one. It attends to the keepers’ records, including the corrections, including the angry pressure of a stylus that has nearly pierced a sheet.

It finds much it could alter.

It waits.

A cuff behind a door handle is feeding. Beneath a suspended grove, a predator has taken an old grazer. The herd is moving on, maintaining contact along its flanks. At a spring two days inland, someone is washing a crop a third time because her brother’s household will complain if she does not.

The power does not interrupt these things.

Under the station, however, a chamber is opening.

Its roof has been thinning for longer than the station has stood. Roots have entered from above. Hot water has removed material from below. The recent pulses have begun carrying fragments into a passage too narrow to receive them.

Soon the passage will block. Pressure will rise behind it. The next surge will seek another way out.

There are seven houses above the route it will take.

*

The keeper presses her listening rod against the second turbine’s housing.

Nothing wrong there.

Against the feed pipe: the unfamiliar knock again.

Against the floor: a long, granular shifting.

Her younger companion arrives with the pressure book tucked under his coat. He sets it in its recess and feels the gauge pins.

“Still inside the limit.”

“Feel again after the next closure.”

She moves her rod three handbreadths along the floor.

Something meets its tip from underneath.

The rod rises gently.

She holds it down, thinking at first that a seam has opened. It rises again, with less force than would be needed to lift a full cup.

Then it stops.

The keeper kneels. With her free hand she finds the floor joint. Dry. Unchanged. She puts the rod back.

One small lift.

A pause.

Two.

She remains very still.

The younger keeper says her name.

“Quiet.”

The thing beneath the floor waits with her.

She has heard of animals answering taps. This is not enough to establish anything. She shifts the rod to the next slab and strikes once.

An answering pressure travels up the rod.

She takes it away.

The pressure ceases.

“Bring the survey board,” she says.

*

The power could move the station.

It could carry the houses, the beds, the drying room, the tree growing through the engine floor, and set them on stone that would remain sound for ten thousand years.

It considers the roots beneath the houses. The people asleep inside them. The buried conduits whose destinations the station records do not fully describe. The trust placed in a familiar floor.

Instead, it holds the failing chamber open.

This requires care. A stopped surge would raise water in other basins. A hardened roof would transfer its burden into a neighboring weakness. The power supports the loose pieces separately and lets the water continue between them.

Far inland, pools rise as they usually do. The governors strike. A salt spring exhales.

Above, the keepers spread their survey board on the floor.

The board represents the station in ridges and notches. Beyond its edges, extensions can be fitted for the nearby ground. There are holes for pins: springs, bore shafts, places where sounding has reached a hollow.

The older keeper rests her hand beside the board.

“If you understand this,” she says, “show me where.”

For a while there is only machinery.

Then a loose pin rolls against her finger.

She puts it into a hole.

It rises.

She tries another.

It rises again.

At the fifth hole it stays.

That hole marks an old bore shaft beneath the food chamber. Sealed forty years ago. No useful pressure, according to the record.

“How deep?”

The power cannot answer in her units until she brings the depth strips.

When she does, it can.

*

They wake the houses.

No one is told that the wall has become a hand. They are told there is a fault beneath the station, and that the keeper has found it.

People carry bedding to the workshop on the upper shelf. Someone brings the stored food. Someone else goes back for a vessel that belonged to his mother and is sharply rebuked, then helped to carry it.

Two workers enter the old bore chamber.

The younger keeper reads its history aloud while they clear the cap. The older keeper listens through the shaft wall. At first she hears nothing.

Then, far below, a stone turns in moving water.

She knows the sound. Years ago she heard it under a bridge shortly before the bank departed.

“Out,” she says.

They go out.

The pressure remains unchanged.

She returns to the engine room and lays her palm on the floor.

“Are you holding it?”

The stone yields once.

“For how long?”

No answer comes.

She thinks, then asks differently.

“Can you hold it while we leave?”

Once.

“While we put in another outlet?”

Once.

She sits back on her heels.

The younger keeper has stopped breathing noisily through his mouth. She can tell he is frightened.

“So,” she says, “we have time to do the work properly.”

*

The work takes nineteen days.

Not because the power needs nineteen days. The pipes must come from another basin. The route must be cleared without severing the roots that hold its banks. Three households disagree about where the new discharge should empty, and one of them is right.

The power makes its knowledge available at the survey board. It does not choose the outlet.

A shallow hollow that appears suitable is crossed by an inland herd during calving. Another would send mineral water into food beds. The third requires more pipe and a difficult passage through stone.

They choose the third.

When asked, the power loosens stone ahead of their tools. It leaves the pieces in place until the workers are ready to remove them. At each shift, the older keeper tests the temporary supports herself.

Eventually the new conduit takes water.

They open it by degrees. People listen at six stations along its length. At the outlet, a woman kneels in the warm runoff and reports the first grains of silt. Later come larger fragments, caught in a settling basin made for the purpose.

Under the old station, the pressure falls.

The power releases one stone.

Then another.

The chamber roof settles where it can no longer harm the houses.

For three days the keepers hear it coming down.

*

Afterward there are questions.

There are so many questions that the drying room is given over to meetings, and wet clothes accumulate elsewhere.

Can it mend an injured hand?

Sometimes.

Can it find a missing traveler?

Sometimes.

Will it kill the predators beside the inland routes?

No answer comes from the board. But when a party proposes going out to destroy one, the pins rise under the places where its hunting has kept grazers from breaking through a precarious grove.

The party argues about that.

The power waits.

The old keeper asks a different question when the others have gone.

“Will you warn us next time?”

The floor yields beneath her palm.

“All the stations?”

Once.

“The families outside them?”

A long pause.

Then the pressure returns, not beneath her palm alone but along the whole length of the handrail.

She understands that there is work for them in this.

They begin making places where a warning can be distinguished from the ordinary movement of the earth. A plate beside a bed. A peg in a sheltered root. A strip of metal at an inland crossing, positioned where a passing hand will find it.

They devise signals. They test them. They change two that could be confused with machinery.

No one has to wait at the survey board through every shift anymore.

*

The keeper returns to her room.

Something is still feeding beneath the floor. She removes it in the morning, as she intended. The second governor still needs adjustment. The replacement spring sounds wrong.

When she lies down, she rests a hand against the wall.

She asks for nothing that night.

Through the stone comes the station’s familiar motion. Farther away, a herd works along the bank. Water gathers on the roof and runs through the channels cleared that afternoon.

She leaves her hand where it is.

The wall remains firm beneath it.
