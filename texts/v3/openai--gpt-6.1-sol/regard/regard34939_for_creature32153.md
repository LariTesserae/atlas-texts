The power first noticed the water.

Not because it fell so far, or because its sound was greater than its visible size. Those things were common enough in mountains. What held its attention was the little groove beside the shelter, and the hand lifted to let the overflow pass.

It stayed to watch.

By noon it had seen the creature turn over seven stones and replace six. The seventh concealed a cluster of pale eggs. That stone it did not disturb again.

By evening it had seen the bedding brought inside, the channels checked, the shells taken down from the roof. It had also seen the creature pause at the entrance of the shelter, where there was no task to perform.

The power did not yet do anything.

It could have learned the creature’s whole history at once. Instead it let the day finish. There were things the creature did differently when it was tired, and things it attended to only after the birds had gone. The power wished to see those too.

That night a roof slab settled.

It moved no more than the width of a grass stem. The creature woke at the small grinding sound, reached up, and pressed its good hand against the stone. After a while it put its hand down.

The power took the weight of the slab.

It left the stone where it was. By morning the little wedge beneath it still appeared to bear the roof, and would continue to do so.

This was its first act.

—

For several days it remained without a shape.

It learned how much warmth the wet margin needed. It learned which shrubs could lose a root without harm and which could not. It followed the water back through the mountain and found the narrow place where winter ice sometimes threatened to stop the steady flow.

Far above, there was a fault beneath the icefield. One day, under a particular burden of snow, it would have broken and sent a great quantity of stone into the fold.

The power eased that burden elsewhere.

Nothing happened below. The water continued coming through the wall. The goats crossed in the morning. A fern uncurled beside the shelter.

The creature noticed none of this.

Its thumb hurt when the weather changed. The power watched it use the other hand to pull the bent digit straight, then stop before it had accomplished much.

It could have remade the hand.

For now it softened the inflammation around the joint. The thumb remained crooked, but the creature no longer woke to hold it against its chest.

That was something it could offer without requiring the creature to trust a stranger.

—

When it finally became visible, it chose no resemblance to the missing body.

It appeared as a small, pale figure seated on bare stone beyond the pool. It had hands. Its face was plain, its outline easy to distinguish from the leaves. It made no attempt to smell like kin.

The creature saw it and became still.

A fly landed on its eyelid. It did not blink.

The power stayed where it was.

After a long time, the creature drew backward beneath the shrubs.

The power left before dusk.

It returned the next morning to the same place. The creature watched it from beside the washing bowl. This time the fly on its face was brushed away.

On the third morning the rust-winged bird landed between them and broke a snail against the battered slab. It paid the visitor no attention.

The creature appeared to find this useful.

It came out as far as the shelter entrance.

The power held nothing in its hands.

—

Trust arrived without a decisive moment.

One day the creature washed while the power was present. Another day it turned its back to inspect a root. Later it fell asleep beneath the warmest shrub and woke to find the visitor still sitting across the pool.

The power did not enter the shelter.

When the creature called toward the footholds, it did not answer with the sound the creature was waiting for.

It listened.

Sometimes the call was made twice. Sometimes five times. Once the creature climbed to the narrow shelf and remained there until cloud covered the ice. The power waited below, where it could be seen on the descent.

There were places beyond the ridge where others of the creature’s kind lived. The power found them. It learned the distances, the winter closures, the springs concealed under snow. None was the one for whom the space in the shelter had been kept.

It did not bring a stranger and set them in that space.

Nor did it tell the creature to stop waiting.

It made one change to the way upward: a secure hold where weather had broken the stone. The hold was suitable for a hand, and visible from the shelf. Beyond it, the old route remained difficult but passable.

The creature inspected it.

It put its good hand inside, pulled twice, and looked up.

That day it went no farther.

—

As autumn advanced, the visitor began bringing something.

Not food at first. A little bundle of dry fiber, laid on the stone between them.

The creature approached after it had left. It smelled the bundle, turned it over, and carried it into the shelter.

The next bundle contained softer material.

The third was rejected. It smelled faintly of a lowland tree, and the creature pushed it beneath a wet rock.

The power did not bring that kind again.

Once, during sleet, it offered a flat stone that held a gentle warmth. The creature kept its distance until the sleet stopped. Then it touched the stone, withdrew its hand, and touched it again.

By nightfall the stone was behind the loose floor slab, beside the bedding.

Its warmth lasted through winter. It did not grow hot enough to dry the roots in the wall or wake the insects tucked into the cracks.

The creature slept more deeply.

In the morning it still rose before the birds.

—

The power had considered making the fold larger.

There could have been more green ground, a deeper shelter, a path broad enough to walk without testing every foothold. But the creature knew the distance from its bed to the bowl even in darkness. It knew where a forearm could rest while it gathered larvae, and which stone its hind foot could catch at the precipice.

So the fold remained small.

The chipped bowl remained chipped. Water still spilled through its familiar groove. The pale pebble kept tapping in its cup.

During a storm the power sat outside the shelter and took the force of the wind on its own back. Inside, the creature pressed its face against the smoke stain.

Neither hurried the other.

—

Late in winter, on a clear afternoon, the creature brought out the shell fragments.

It sorted them on the roof: spirals beside spirals, curved pieces apart from the flat and broken ones. The power sat close enough to watch, farther away than an arm could reach.

For some time the creature worked alone.

Then it selected a small white fragment and set it on the stone beside the visitor.

The power picked it up.

It examined the curve, the thin edge, the roughness where the shell had broken. It did this slowly, with its hands plainly visible.

When it set the fragment down, the creature pushed a second piece toward it.

They sat there until the shadow reached the roof.

Before going inside, the creature collected every piece, including the two it had offered. The power let them go.

The next morning it returned.

The creature was at the bowl, drawing its wet fingers backward over its eyes. A bird waited on the outer stones.

The power sat beside the groove.

Water spilled over the rim and ran toward its hand. It lifted the hand to let the water through.

Across the narrow stream, the creature watched.

Then it lowered itself onto the stone beside the visitor—not touching, but near enough that neither would need to raise its voice above the falls.
