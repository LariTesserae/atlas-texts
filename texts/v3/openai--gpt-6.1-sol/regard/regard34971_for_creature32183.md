The power first noticed the city by its shadows.

There were so few of them, and so much life fitted underneath.

From a greater height than the birds could reach, the roofs seemed to float above their dark rectangles. Beneath a loading platform, a woman had put down her tools beside a sleeping scavenger. Its young clung to the loose skin of its belly. Neither the woman nor the animal was asleep deeply enough to stop watching the other.

The power watched until the woman rose.

Then it followed her, not closely enough to be felt.

She was not Sera. She repaired the ceramic housings around the cables, and one of her boots had begun to separate at the sole. At every junction she chose the longer route because the shorter one crossed a shallow run whose water would finish dissolving the adhesive.

This was the first thing the power learned: a difficulty could be small, and still occupy an appreciable part of a life.

It had no wish to make all the roads dry.

***

When it came to Sera, she was standing at the gate with the black-ringed wheel.

She had finished taking her sample. She had not finished considering it.

The power stood on the next pier, leaving room for a trolley.

Sera glanced toward it, then back at her glass. A little swimmer had entered with the sample. It held itself just below the surface, its fins moving too rapidly to see.

“Are you waiting for me?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“This may take some time.”

“Yes.”

She disliked being watched while she worked. After a minute she said so.

The power moved behind the gate housing. Its presence remained perceptible, but no longer as attention directed at her hands.

Sera held the glass against the light again.

“Not there either,” she said. “You’re changing the reflection.”

It moved farther away.

When she finally returned the swimmer and its water to the channel, the stranger was sitting on the end of an unused stair. It had found a piece of shade scarcely larger than a folded cloth.

“What do you want?”

“To understand what makes your work difficult.”

Sera leaned her tool against her shoulder.

“People who think I understand it.”

The power waited.

“And the heat,” she said. “And the samples going warm before I get them where I’m taking them. And being asked afterward why I didn’t know something nobody knew beforehand.”

She looked at its unmarked hands.

“What are you?”

It answered her plainly.

Sera sat down on the other end of the stair.

For a while she said nothing. Then she pointed toward the black ring.

“Can you tell me what would happen if that were opened?”

The power looked through the metal, through the pale sleeve of deposits, into the still water beyond. It considered the two flows meeting, the powder forming, the changing pressure, the lives occupying cracks too small for Sera’s instruments.

“Yes,” it said. “If it were opened now.”

“And tomorrow?”

“Tomorrow I would need to look again.”

This answer pleased her more than its first one had.

***

For eleven afternoons, it accompanied her.

It did not turn the wheels.

When she wanted an opinion, she asked. Sometimes its answer was immediate. Sometimes it asked where a channel had gone in earlier years, or why a notch had been widened, or what she meant by a water being tired.

It could see things she could not. It could not, merely by looking, know why the northern sheds had been built beyond a stretch of otherwise useful land.

Sera told it about a basin that had killed three people before her grandmother was born. The basin was gone. The prohibition remained.

On the fourth afternoon, the power noticed her tremor.

On the fifth, it asked whether she wanted anything done about it.

She held her left hand out between them. The fingers moved slightly.

“Can you?”

“Yes.”

“Will it change anything else?”

“I can show you exactly what I would change. You need not decide today.”

She drew her hand back.

“I’ll ask my sister.”

The power did not bring it up again.

On the seventh afternoon, Sera sent it to fetch another keeper.

“Which one?”

“Davan. Eastern gates. Tell him I want a second pair of eyes, not a second opinion delivered from his kitchen.”

Davan came annoyed and left thoughtful.

By the eleventh afternoon, the power understood that Sera’s pauses were part of the work. So was having someone available to stand beside her during them.

***

Its first proposal was for a cool room.

Not cold enough to be startling. Not a house of winter planted in the middle of the plateau. A place where someone could sit until their pulse slowed, where a baby could sleep without being passed continually from one hot pair of arms to another, and where a sample could remain close to the temperature at which it had been taken.

The city already possessed rooms intended for these purposes. In the hottest weeks, they failed.

The power offered to alter one of them.

There were objections.

The district officers wanted it beside the customs office, where it could be supervised. The refinery wanted it beneath a laboratory. A landlord offered two rooms and then explained how much their rent would increase.

Sera wanted it near the stair where the power had first sat.

“That’s inconvenient for almost everyone,” said the landlord.

“About equally,” Sera replied.

They chose a disused inspection house at the meeting of three walks. It stood above a deep channel, but drew no water from it.

The power explained what it would do. Heat would pass out of the room without being discharged into the basins. The mechanism would require no fuel, and would remain when the power was elsewhere. It could be shut down from inside. Nothing would happen to anyone who entered except that they might become cooler.

An engineer asked how it worked.

The explanation took most of the night.

By morning, the engineer understood enough to stop being frightened, and not enough to stop asking questions.

***

Before the work began, they inspected beneath the house.

There were two nesting birds in a broken vent, a broad-backed grazer attached to the underside of a landing, and a pale animal none of the inspectors recognized. The last had threaded itself through a row of old bolt holes. Moving the landing would have torn it apart.

They changed the design.

This delayed the opening by six days.

The power could have made the bolts disappear, moved the animal without injury, and put everything back. Instead, it helped the carpenters make a second landing around the first. There would be repairs in years to come, when it might not be present. The inspectors drew the animal on the plan.

They did not give it a name. They marked the bolt holes that had to remain open.

***

When the room opened, people entered cautiously.

An old man sat for three minutes and left because he distrusted feeling so comfortable.

A freight handler fell asleep with an empty lunch tin in his lap.

Sera’s sister brought a bowl of materials whose setting times she wanted to test. She was told that one shelf would be reserved for work, but the chairs were for people.

A mother arrived with a crying infant. After a while the infant stopped crying. The mother continued to hold it.

Outside, a scavenger folded beneath the new landing. The room had not cooled its shade. It had merely made the shade wider.

Someone proposed excluding animals from the steps.

Someone else asked how.

No rule was made that day.

***

The power did other work, much of it difficult to see.

With the keepers’ permission, it examined the hidden faces of gates. It marked a bearing that would fail before the end of the season. It found an old passage carrying concentrated water into a foundation whose owner had been blaming his tenants for the cracks.

It did not announce these discoveries from the mountain.

It put them on the maintenance sheets.

When asked to make the dangerous gate safe forever, it declined the word *forever*. It could replace the spindle, relieve the pressure, and provide a second way to keep the waters apart. Those were substantial improvements. They would still need inspection.

Davan complained that even miracles generated paperwork.

For several evenings this was considered a good joke.

***

Sera eventually brought her sister to meet the power.

They talked in the cool room after the other visitors had gone. Her sister asked questions Sera had not thought to ask. The power answered all of them, then waited while they spoke privately outside.

When Sera came back, she offered her hand.

The change took less time than fastening a sleeve.

She rested her elbow on the table and watched her fingers remain still.

Then she picked up a sampling rod.

The next afternoon she braced her glass against the shaft of her tool out of habit. Halfway through, she realized she did not need to.

She kept it there anyway. The support was useful.

***

Her nephew asked the power about forests.

It answered until he became impatient.

“I want to see one,” he said.

“Yes,” it said. “I understood.”

It did not bring a forest to the plateau. It helped him write to a freight company below the clouds, and told him which parts of the offered contract he should ask someone experienced to explain.

Sera disliked this assistance for almost a whole day.

Then she brought out the money she had been putting aside for his departure.

***

There were things the power chose not to change.

The films continued to grow gray, amber, and violet in their seams. Birds continued to steal wire. The large animal under the bridge remained there, unseen except when it displaced the reflected windows.

The woman near the northern sheds kept her tree. With her permission, the power repaired the leaking vessel that held its cool water. She still carried fallen leaves out to the children.

The city remained hot.

There was still work that had to be done carefully, and people who wanted to leave, and arguments over which repairs mattered most. The power attended some of those arguments. It was occasionally persuaded.

At night it often sat beside the inspection house. People learned that they could approach it there, and that they could also pass without speaking.

One evening Sera came down the walk with her tools wrapped against noise. She stopped on the pier.

“Nothing today?” she asked.

“Nothing that requires you tonight.”

She considered the distinction, then nodded.

Below them, a grazer tested the new landing with two searching feet. Four others held it steady.

Sera went home.

The power remained while the animal found its way.
