The power does not begin by making the water warm.

Cold belongs to this sea. It holds oxygen, governs the small lives in the sediment, slows the eel’s hunger in winter. Nor does the power silence the pumping station. Beyond the walls of this compartment, other basins depend on its movement of water. A silence imposed here would travel outward as something less merciful.

For a long time, the power attends.

It follows the eel on nights when the animal finds food and on nights when it does not. It notices the difference between resting beneath the flange and being driven there. It notices how the eel braces itself when grit falls from the ceiling, and how the faded patch behind its head meets the same sharp place each time its body turns.

It also follows the maintenance crews.

They are not cruel. They are responsible for pressures, corrosion, tolerances, the immense weight of water above inhabited galleries. They work from drawings on which the gap beneath the pipe is an imperfection. The eel appears, when it appears at all, as an incidental shape in a photograph.

The power’s first act is to make that shape consequential.

***

At the next review of the return pipe, someone opens an older survey image beside the new one.

There are the eyes again.

The reviewer enlarges the photograph. In the newest image, a curve of the body is visible behind the head. Another photograph shows the narrow rear opening along the footing.

The pipe is due to be decommissioned. The ordinary procedure would fill its unused cavities with grout.

The reviewer adds a note.

It is a small note in an enormous maintenance system, but it remains attached to the pipe through each subsequent revision. Before work can proceed, the cavity must be inspected for occupation. Both openings are to remain clear unless the structure becomes unsafe. If it must be closed, another refuge must be established first.

Nobody is instructed to love the eel.

They are given something definite to remember about it.

***

The power does not enlarge the chamber.

A spacious opening would admit a seal. A smooth, straight passage would lose the turn behind which the eel withdraws from lamps. The cramped place has properties that comfort, considered carelessly, would destroy.

Instead, during a night when the eel is feeding at the station, the sharp projection inside the chamber loses its edge.

Only the projection changes. The concrete retains its roughness elsewhere. The ceiling remains low, the rear passage narrow. No strange smell enters the water. When the eel returns, its body settles into the usual bends.

On the next turn, its skin passes over the place without catching.

The eel does not pause. There is nothing there that requires a response.

***

Farther along its route, work is already planned for a damaged drainage channel.

The repair drawing acquires a different detail: a line of low, rough-sided blocks, set beside the channel rather than across it. Each block has openings near the floor. Some are too small for the eel, some wide enough to turn inside. Between them are sheltered lengths of water.

The spacing is checked against the current. The blocks must not collect so much loose weed that they choke the channel. They must not conceal an inspection cover or interfere with a vehicle passing above.

When the vessel lowers them into place, the floor clouds with disturbed silt.

For several nights the eel avoids the area.

Then it approaches one block from downstream. Its head enters an opening; its body follows partway. It withdraws, passes around the outside, and continues toward the station.

Weeks later, during a seal’s approach, it uses that opening again.

This time it stays.

The seal passes over the block and turns toward the grilles. Inside, the eel lies with its jaw close to a surface still bare of growth. Water moves gently past its head.

By autumn, shrimp occupy the smaller openings. A fringe of weed has taken hold along the upper edge. Sediment settles in the lee, and worms begin working through it.

The additions cease to look like additions.

***

At the pumping station, the power finds a harder question.

Food gathers there because water is drawn through screens. Some of that food is injured. The eel has learned to profit from injury, but the power’s attention does not end at the eel’s teeth.

A trial begins on one intake: a broader screen, a slower approach flow, cleaning timed outside the densest movement of small fish. The engineers measure the consequences. They alter what fails. A recess at the foot of the wall remains accessible, but fewer stunned bodies descend into it.

For a time the eel finds less food there.

Elsewhere, around the new blocks and the older anchor stones, crabs have become more numerous. The eel’s route changes. It spends longer searching among those structures and fewer hours trying to reach a smell beyond the station’s screens.

There is no food laid at the mouth of its chamber. Nothing trains it to wait for a gift.

It hunts. A crab dies in its jaws. The eel swallows, rests, and goes on.

The power remains attentive to both lives.

***

Noise cannot be removed all at once.

But a worn bearing is found before its vibration becomes a hammering through the slab. A replacement impeller is chosen partly for the sound it produces underwater. When two pumps can provide the same exchange without running together, they run in succession.

These changes are recorded in figures and maintenance intervals. Beneath the surface, they make patches of quiet longer.

One evening the eel pauses behind the buttress.

The usual beating has diminished. It can feel the movements of a crab among shells nearby. Its head turns. It leaves shelter and follows.

***

Years pass.

The archive still identifies the animal by the number of a pipe, though the photographs are no longer incidental. Other occupied cavities are marked. A diver checking one of the new refuges finds eggs attached deep inside it and leaves without completing the internal survey. The inspection is rescheduled.

The made sea remains made. Gates rise. Panels are repaired. Winter water enters the deep channels. There are still bare distances, storms, hunger, and animals that do not survive them.

But the people responsible for the basin now have another set of things to notice. A sheltered joint is not always an empty joint. An unused pipe may have a use absent from its drawing.

The power watches the eel grow older.

Perhaps one season it will cease returning to the flange. If it leaves, there will be passages through the basin boundaries that do not end against closed screens. If it stays, the chamber will remain until keeping it would threaten more lives than it shelters.

For now, at dawn, the eel returns along the footing.

It passes a block furred with young growth, crosses the transverse joint, and slips beneath the pipe. Behind its head, the old sore place has healed. Its body turns against the softened concrete and comes to rest.

Above it, the pumps change over.

For a little while, there is only the movement of water through the gap, and the small sounds of animals along the floor.
