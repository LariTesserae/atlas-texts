The power first attends to the basin without entering it.

Entering is not necessary. There is nowhere it cannot perceive, but perception need not become a presence that presses on the senses. No new brightness gathers under the vault. No voice displaces the voices already there.

For a long while it follows one of the pale animals.

This one is old. The bare patch on its left heel is larger than the one on its right, and a back tooth has broken close to the gum. It still feeds, but sometimes it leaves a beetle half crushed and rubs its jaw against its foreleg. Its familiar routes include a narrow descent to the lowest pool. On the return it pauses where it once climbed without stopping.

The power perceives the pain clearly. It also perceives the animal’s competence: which footholds it remembers, how it shifts its weight away from the sore heel, how precisely it locates a cricket beneath a crust of guano. Age has made some things difficult without making its life only difficulty.

There is an infection beneath the broken tooth.

The power ends that.

Nothing flashes. The tooth does not grow whole again. The animal wakes in its dry interval between slabs, moves its tongue along the old break, and presently goes out to feed. For several nights it continues to favor that side of its mouth. Then it ceases.

This is a small act, but the power does not consider it too small.

It follows the water next.

The pools belong to a larger course that cannot be seen from within the basin. Rain crosses the plateau, enters cracks, rests in stone, and arrives below long after the weather that brought it has passed. Some seams feed the dark shelf. Others pass beneath the basin without emerging.

Above, beyond the rims of the openings, people have begun to measure the plateau. There are marks on stones and stakes in the thin soil. A road is proposed, and after the road, a quarry. The first survey has mistaken the roof for an uncomplicated thickness of limestone.

The power does not frighten the surveyors. It makes the mistake discoverable.

A misplaced measurement is checked. An old record of a cavity is found where someone will read it. The next survey includes the ground beneath the proposed cut. A person who had expected blank stone encounters the scale of the basin, the colonies, the slow water.

What follows is not unanimous or effortless. There are objections, costs, revised boundaries. The power allows people to understand those things too. But the quarry is moved. The openings and the ground that feeds them are left intact, with enough land around them that protection is more than a line drawn close to a hole.

Below, the animals know none of this.

That is satisfactory.

The power does not enlarge their eyes or give them a desire for the surface. It does not clear their passages into paths convenient for a larger body. The intervals beneath the fallen slabs are shelter as well as obstruction. Darkness is where they sleep.

Nor does it make the basin uniformly wet. It has attended to the dry birth hollows, the beetles in powdery deposits, the places where an animal can lie without chilling its belly. Water everywhere would destroy much that depends on water somewhere.

It watches through a dry season before deciding anything further.

The dark shelf continues to gather drops. The pools retreat, exposing pale mineral edges, but do not fail. Animals wait in the surrounding cracks and approach separately. One young animal pushes ahead of an older neighbor, receives a rough warning, and backs away.

There is no need to intervene in that.

Later, a small fall of chips closes the entrance to a hollow where two young lie. Their mother works at it, pulling loose pieces backward with her forefeet. Behind them is a heavier fragment she cannot move. The hollow has no other opening.

When she withdraws to rest, the heavy fragment settles a little farther downslope.

She returns, tests the gap with her whiskers, and gets through. By the next departure of the bats she has carried both young to another resting place.

The power leaves the fallen stone where it settles.

It continues to attend: to the plateau as well as the basin, to the insects brought home in the bats’ bodies, to rain entering limestone, to people whose decisions reach farther than they can see. It is willing to act again. It is also willing to spend long periods doing nothing.

Below the roosts, the old animal drinks. It braces its forefeet above the black water and keeps its belly dry. On the way back it stops at the difficult rise, rests, and climbs.

It has no name for what has helped it. It has a sound jaw, a known route, and somewhere narrow to sleep.
