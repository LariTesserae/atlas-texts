The power first noticed the question through the wall.

It had been attending to the clay: the weight above the unfinished chamber, the fine crack running behind the father’s shoulder, the water held in a layer of earth beyond the reach of the lamps. It could have counted every stone on every roof. It could have lifted the town into the fields without spilling a jar.

Then the father asked whether the neighbors had eaten.

The digging stopped.

After a while, someone answered that they had bread.

This was not quite an answer. The father knew it. He looked toward the cooking pot, and his wife lifted its cover to see what remained.

The power stayed to watch.

***

For several days it did nothing that anyone could distinguish from ordinary good fortune.

It watched the man place the first stone in a channel. It watched him return after the younger workers had gone and move it again.

It followed the woman who tested walls with her cheek. In one room she remained still for so long that the household began to apologize for having called her. She held up a hand. There was water behind the wall, but there was also something else: a coldness that would persist after the water had gone.

The power knew she was right. It did not put the explanation into her mouth.

It watched the tall young man cut his doorways, the children imitate him, the dog choose its step. It went with a boy to the rim and discovered that the boy was disappointed by the fields. He had expected the sky to begin there. Instead there was more walking.

At night it attended to the people who could not sleep.

The digger dreaming of the room without an entrance woke with both hands against his chest. The power could have taken the dream away. Instead it let him sleep for the remainder of that night without dreaming, and in the morning he remembered enough to insist on the second exit.

It listened to arguments as carefully as to songs.

The dispute about the raised threshold was not small to the people whose kitchen flooded. Neither was it the whole of either household’s life.

***

On the fourth evening, the power took a shape that could knock at a door.

It came down from the fields carrying nothing. Its clothing was dusty at the hem by the time it reached the square. The onion grower saw it first and asked whose house it was looking for.

“No house yet,” it said.

She looked at its hands.

“If you’re looking for work, they’re cutting at the north end.”

“I would like to help with the water.”

“Then you want the man who sits.”

She pointed with the blade of her little hoe.

The power sat beside him until he had finished considering a stone.

“There will be heavy rain,” it said.

“There is often heavy rain.”

“More than the lower pits can hold.”

The man turned toward it. He was accustomed to people making confident statements about water, usually just before making work for him.

“When?”

“Not tonight. There is time to look.”

It did not ask him to believe without looking. It walked with him to the unfinished hollow, and then up to the fields, and beyond the fields to a shallow fold in the country where the soil was already saturated. He pushed his wooden blade into the ground. Water shone in the cut.

They went farther.

By dusk he had asked two other people to come.

***

The power could have stopped the rain.

When they asked it, it said so.

By then there were eleven people on the rib of ground, and others listening from below. The woman with the crooked tooth had brought a stool. The tall young man stood where he could see both hollows.

“For how long?” the channel setter asked.

“For long enough to make the town safer.”

“Will you be here next year?”

“If you want me.”

This answer produced a long discussion.

Some wanted the rain stopped altogether. Some feared for the fields. Someone asked whether the visitor could fill the well instead, and someone else asked whether it could make the clay dry without making it crack.

The power answered each question. Where a kindness in one place would cause trouble in another, it showed them how. It did not tire when they asked again.

At last the onion grower said, “Keep this rain off us while we decide where it ought to go.”

So it did.

The clouds crossed the fields. Rain fell beyond the watershed, softly enough that it did not beat the seedlings flat. Over the town, the air remained gray and damp, but the roof edges did not tick.

People came out into the lanes and looked upward.

Then they began arguing about the channels.

***

The work they chose was less splendid than several of the things the power had offered.

They wanted a way for excess water to leave the lowest hollow. They wanted the unstable bank above three houses made safe. They wanted somewhere dry for people with fevers, without having to carry them into an open field.

They did not want all the walls hardened. A wall that could no longer be cut would make the town difficult to change.

They did not want their houses raised to the surface.

One woman did. She said this twice, loudly, after others had spoken as though everyone preferred living below.

The power asked her where she would like to live.

She chose a place near the rim, with a view across the fields. Two more households asked to go with her. No one was required to follow them.

The new drainage route took nine days to agree upon. It passed under no sleeping room. At one bend, the power showed the workers a buried pocket of loose sand before they opened it. Where the outlet needed to pass through stone, it softened the stone for their tools, then made it firm again when they were finished.

There was disagreement about who would tend the outlet. The power did not settle this by choosing the nicest person. The inhabitants knew who rose early, who forgot, who was already carrying too much. They made a rota, changed it, and left space to change it again.

The dry room was built beside the square. Its door admitted a litter without turning. Its windows could be opened by someone lying down.

The woman who knew damp tested every wall.

When she pressed her cheek to the last one, she began to laugh.

“What?” asked the builder.

“Nothing,” she said, and kept her cheek there a little longer.

***

There were other requests.

A man brought his painful hands. A mother brought a child whose breathing troubled her. An old woman asked to hear clearly enough to follow conversation without pretending.

The power helped them.

It did not require useful work in return.

For some illnesses the help was immediate. For others it took visits, rest, changes to a room, someone learning how to tend a wound. Where it could not restore what had been lost, it said so plainly and stayed while the person was angry.

The dog acquired a place to sleep that nobody needed to step over. This was not among the decisions made in the square. A child asked privately, and the power treated the request with the same attention it had given the outlet.

***

By the time the rain returned, the newest family had widened their curtain with a strip of cloth from another household.

Water entered the stone-lined channels. It filled the settling pits, rose to the new overflow, and went away through the earth.

People followed it with lamps.

At the outlet, the channel setter crouched and watched until his knees hurt. Then he stood, dissatisfied with one small eddy, and went to fetch a tool.

The power remained in the town, though not always in a shape that could be seen.

It learned to recognize when it was wanted. A frightened person did not always want fear removed; sometimes they wanted company while they crossed the rib. A tired person might want the work done for them, or might want someone else to hold the basket.

Sometimes the power was asked to leave a thing alone.

It did.

And sometimes, at evening, it sat in the square while the onions were watered and the last light withdrew up the bank.

From the newest rooms came the sounds of jars being settled, mats unrolled, a curtain drawn.

A pick struck once, then stopped.

Through the wall, someone asked whether the others had eaten.

This time there was enough, and they said so.
