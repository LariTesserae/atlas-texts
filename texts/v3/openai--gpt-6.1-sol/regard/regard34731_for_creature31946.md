The power first noticed the thread.

It ran for less than an inch through the new precipice, blue wool in pale stone. One end had been worked into the mountain by the white bird. The other still carried the small curl left by years of being part of a cuff.

The power could have followed it back to the sheep, the loom, the woman who had bought the coat. Instead it attended to where the wool entered the rock.

There was a little warmth there.

Not enough for a person to feel through a glove. Enough for the bird to return to that seam rather than another.

For some time the power watched. It had learned, elsewhere, that a place could be injured by being understood too quickly.

It watched the clouds come up through the streets. It watched a track worker put his bare palm on a rail and wait, although his supervisor was calling from the points. It watched the saucers beneath the stairs, and the three evenings of tea in rooms whose occupants had died.

When Mara came to the bench, it watched her lower herself with one hand pressed against her knee.

The bird inspected her left cuff. Mara turned her wrist to make the loose thread easier to reach.

The power did not speak until the bird had gone.

“Does it hurt?” it asked.

Mara looked along the path. There was someone standing beside the survey stakes, wearing a coat without pockets.

“The knee?”

“Yes.”

“Mostly coming back.”

The stranger looked toward the long descent to the inhabited terraces.

“Would you like it to hurt less?”

Mara was accustomed to offers from people who intended to sell her something.

“What would that involve?”

“Your permission. A little time. Nothing else.”

She considered this.

“Less would be useful,” she said. “I still need to know when I’ve strained it.”

The stranger sat at the other end of the bench.

The bird returned once, found neither cuff interesting, and went away.

When Mara stood, her knee was still stiff. But the deep, grinding pain had gone. She took three steps, then walked back.

“You should come to the laundry,” she said. “There’s a woman there with both hands.”

“I will ask her.”

“Yes,” Mara said. “Do.”

---

The power found more to do at the laundry than it had expected.

There were the woman’s hands, and a belt that slipped under a full load, and a floor drain that drew mice into it when the large boilers emptied. The woman wanted relief, but not during her shift; she distrusted anything that made her feel different while she was responsible for steam.

The belt could be repaired by Iven. He had requested the appropriate part three times.

The drain required a small ledge inside its throat. The mice could reach the ledge if it were rough enough. Water could pass beneath it.

The power obtained the part from a depot in the western district. It did not create a second one: there was already a box of them on a shelf, entered in the stock book under an obsolete name. It brought the box to the laundry and waited while Iven checked the measurements.

“Who sent these?”

“They were wanted here.”

“That isn’t quite the same thing.”

“No,” the power said.

Iven looked at it for a long moment.

Then he opened the box.

They built the ledge after the evening wash. Mara held a lamp. Iven reached down into the drain with an arm wrapped in sacking.

When they had finished, the power remained to see whether the mice could use it.

They could.

This pleased it more than the ledge itself.

---

The city did not become safe all at once.

Some of its dangers could not be separated from the lives moving through it. The power saw a way to make every hour settle firmly, and saw the bathhouse garden disappear under the same act. It saw the rail things stranded in steel that had become ordinary steel. It saw several residents lose memories they had relied upon for years, without knowing afterward what had been taken.

It left the hours alone.

But it began to attend to the moments before trouble.

At a staircase in the northern district, a woman carrying a sleeping child stopped when the handrail grew warm beneath her palm. She waited. A minute later, seven additional flights unfolded below her.

At a foundry, the blank-faced clock gave a small click before its needle began to tremble. The foreman had time to halt a pour.

These warnings were not everywhere, and the power did not pretend they were. It brought what it had learned to the people who maintained the clocks.

They were suspicious of the handrail.

“Warmth can mean a steam fault,” one said.

“What would you use?”

The discussion occupied three afternoons.

They settled on a vibration, faint but distinct, repeated twice. Not every disturbance announced itself in time. The revised notices said so in plain language. Children practised feeling the signal on a rail in the schoolyard, and were taught that its absence proved nothing.

Mara approved of that sentence.

“Otherwise,” she said, “someone will carry a wardrobe down a staircase just because it hasn’t objected.”

---

There were requests the power could not fulfill without asking other people.

Mara’s daughter wanted the journey shortened.

So did several hundred others. They had been requesting it for years.

The railway had surveys, estimates, rejected estimates, and a proposed connection laid out across a basin where the mountain was expected eventually to rise. The power could have raised the rock ahead of its time. Instead it spent a week under the freight station, watching the grove and the animals that passed through it.

Then it walked the proposed line with the engineers.

A suspended section was possible. Expensive, but possible.

The money was more difficult than the bridge. The power would not put coin in a treasury without attending to what the coin would purchase elsewhere. It went through the municipal accounts with two clerks who became increasingly annoyed by its questions.

There were stores being heated that no longer needed heat. There were contracts still paying for maintenance on a cableway dismantled forty years earlier. There was equipment bought for three districts that did not yet exist, deteriorating in the weather while the working districts went without.

None of this paid for the whole connection.

Enough of it paid for a beginning.

The council argued, the railway amended the design, and the gardeners required larger openings beneath the approaches. Mara attended one meeting to explain why an empty stretch of stone was not necessarily unused.

The bridge took two years.

During those years, the power carried passengers when they asked and when the intervening hours allowed it. It did not make the offer conditional on their opinion of the bridge.

Mara accepted twice.

On the first visit, she and her daughter quarreled before lunch about how rarely Mara wrote. Afterward they repotted a tree together.

The power waited outside.

---

Iven was slower to ask for anything.

He worked beside the stranger for months. He learned that it would hold a pipe indefinitely without becoming bored, but had to be told if it was standing in the way. He learned that it did not mind being corrected.

One afternoon, when they were replacing a threshold strip, he said, “Can you tell where I am?”

“Here,” said the power.

“I know that.”

“Yes.”

He pressed a tack into the wood with his thumb.

“Sometimes I think there’s somewhere I haven’t got back from.”

The power sat down on the floor, leaving the doorway clear.

It could perceive more of Iven than a person could. There were hours still touching him that had never taken hold elsewhere. There was the warm stone at the foot of the vanished staircase. There were other things it could not yet distinguish without disturbing them.

“I can help you look,” it said. “I don’t know whether looking would help.”

“Could you stop it?”

“Some of it, perhaps. I wouldn’t do that without telling you what I could first.”

Iven worked another tack into place.

“Not today.”

“All right.”

After a while he said, “When I say we should go around, I’d like people to go around.”

The power asked whether it could speak to Mara.

“She already does.”

“Then whom?”

“The men from inspections.”

That was arranged. It required no alteration to Iven at all.

---

The power never acquired an office.

Letters for it were left on a shelf beside the laundry gate, between a chipped mug and a sock whose owner had moved south. Some asked for things it could give. Some asked for things it would not give. It answered those too.

It relieved pain where it could and where relief was wanted. It helped make rooms easier to reach. It searched for missing people, sometimes successfully. When it could not bring someone home, it stayed with those who had been waiting.

It learned to leave a certain pipe joint leaking until the gardeners had laid another route for the roots.

It learned that there were days when Mara wanted company on her rounds and days when she preferred to hear the animals.

At the final bench, it added a low rail beside the path. The first design crossed the white bird’s approach to the seam, so it moved two supports and tried again.

The mountain continued to grow.

There were still mornings when a cloud remained in a factory until the afternoon shift. The lamps still needed cleaning. The new railway connection was late often enough to become a familiar subject of complaint.

One day Mara came to the precipice with her daughter. They sat on the bench, drinking coffee from the lid of a flask.

The bird arrived and inspected them both.

Mara’s daughter had a new coat, sound at every seam. Mara’s old cuff offered a short blue thread.

She held her arm still.

Nearby, the power was tightening a loose bolt in the rail. It paused when the bird flew past, wool trailing from its beak, and watched it settle at the warm seam.

Then it finished the bolt and moved its tools off the path.
