The power did not begin with the man.

It noticed him, and the weight in his hands, but it also noticed the bird whose feet had not reached the stone. Beneath the granary it found the young mice. In the valley, farther than any person in the city could see, a root had begun to divide around a buried pebble.

There was no part of the stillness that was merely background.

For a while, the power attended without changing anything.

It followed the suspended water back through the jug, through the cistern where the jug had been filled, into narrow passages in the mountain. It followed the sound beneath the stair. The sound was not being renewed. What remained was a relation between water, stone, and air, held as closely as the woman’s fingers were held around her cup.

The people were not waiting in the ordinary sense. No long interval had accumulated inside them. The porter had not spent centuries straining against his straps. The sleeping woman had not dreamed the same dream until it became unbearable.

This mattered. There was no need to wake them violently from a suffering they had not endured.

But neither had they consented to be kept.

The power sought the edges of the condition. Beyond the valleys were other valleys, other settlements, an animal crossing a field with mud between its toes. Beyond the cloud were currents of air that could not arrive. There was no safe boundary at which to restart the city alone. A moving river could not be joined carelessly to an unmoving river. A bird could not be given back its wings while the air remained unavailable to them.

So the power continued outward.

It did not hurry. Nothing here could become worse while it learned.

It examined whether the stillness was holding off some catastrophe: a fall of stone, a sickness, a failure in the mountain. It looked for a purpose that might be injured by release. It found dangers, but they were the dangers of a living country. One retaining wall had a weakness near its foundation. A child would eventually need help. Winter would not be equally kind to every household.

None of this required the city to remain unfinished forever.

The power could have changed more.

It could have made the porter strong enough never to need the bench. It could have settled the dispute in the storeroom by placing the correct number in both minds. It could have removed the third fruit from the boy’s shirt and the hunger from the fox.

But it did not mistake every difficulty for a defect in its work.

The porter’s back deserved care; his pride did not require an invisible hand inside it. The account deserved to be understood by the people whose goods were at issue. And the fox was not an error made at the expense of the mouse.

There were things the power did not yet know how to make gentler without taking something away.

It left those questions open.

Where the stillness itself had made a danger, it was more exact. The held stream would not acquire the force of all the time it had missed. Smoke would not arrive at a chimney’s end in a single accumulated rush. No heart would owe a debt of beats. Nothing would be asked to catch up.

The mountain would keep its old fractures. The spoon would keep its repaired handle. The young woman would keep the sentence she had begun, including the part of it she had not decided how to say.

When the power was ready, it did not send a sign over the roofs.

There was no command for the city to hear.

The next moment came.

The bird landed.

Its claws closed on the irregular surface of the stone, and its body dipped once before settling. The hollow beneath the falling drop flattened into a ring. Six beads of overflow struck the basin in such close succession that they made one small, untidy sound.

The old man tipped the jug back.

A little water ran along its lip and touched his thumb. The young woman drew her cup toward herself.

“—and he says that is where we always keep it,” she finished.

Someone farther down the table laughed, not very kindly, and then looked embarrassed.

The weaver eased the tight thread. It resisted, then gave enough for her to continue. On the stair, the porter took another step. He glanced toward the bench and stopped beneath the wall, breathing through his mouth while he worked one shoulder free.

The goat shifted its weight. The loose roof plate knocked against its neighbor.

A voice came sharply from the doorway.

In the passage beside the granary, the fox heard the voice and paused. The mouse stayed inside the wall. For now, neither reached the other.

Smoke loosened above the chimney. The wool hanging lifted and fell.

The city did not know that anything had been restored. The interruption had contained no experience by which it could be measured. No one became grateful on command. No one acquired a story about having been saved.

The power was content with that.

It remained near enough to notice the retaining wall. Not to hide its weakness beneath a perpetual miracle, but to learn who maintained it, what materials they had, and whether help could reach them before failure did.

For the moment, it took a form that could walk up the stair.

The form was neither splendid nor pitiable. It wore a cloak appropriate to the cold. Its hands were empty. It could have arrived carrying grain, medicine, a thousand answers; instead, it came prepared to find out what was wanted.

Beside the porter, it stopped.

“Would you like help with that?”

The porter looked up, then at the stranger’s hands.

“Only getting it back on,” he said. “I’m sitting first.”

So the stranger sat at the other end of the bench.

For a little while they watched the contour road. A woman passed with a basket balanced against her hip. Somewhere below, the argument over the jars grew loud enough for a few words to carry, then quieted again.

When the porter was ready, the stranger held the frame steady. It did not make the sacks weigh nothing. It lifted enough, where lifting was asked for.

At the table, another person arrived. The place near the edge proved convenient. A child wanted the smaller bowl and was told that it had been put there for someone else.

The old man set down the jug and flexed his right hand beneath the table.

Later, when the stranger came into the court, the woman with the cup looked toward the unoccupied place.

“Are you eating?”

“If there is enough.”

“We haven’t counted you yet,” she said. “Sit down, and we will.”

The stranger sat.

Above the roofs, the cloud began to change its shape.
