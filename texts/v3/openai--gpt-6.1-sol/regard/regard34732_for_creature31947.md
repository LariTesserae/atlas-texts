The power did not enter through the loose stone.

There was room for a visitor there, but not for an arrival of its size. Instead, it let its attention rest on the courtyard as lightly as the light rested on the backs of the leaves.

For a while, it did nothing.

It watched the spoon pass beneath your cuff. It watched the inhabitant carry the water to a pot tucked behind the lowest stair, where two leaves had begun to curl inward. It watched the spoon return with a small flake of glaze in it.

The flake was put aside. There was a place for such things.

The power had come prepared to mend. By the time the inhabitant put the leaf back over its cup, it understood that it did not yet know what was broken.

The missing bottom of the hanging pot was not broken in the same way as the chipped hinge of the shears. The blocked doorway was a wall to someone above, a bed to someone below. The cold kept certain roots sound. A little ice beneath the stair held back water that would otherwise have run into the bark nest.

It looked longer.

Beyond the courtyard, a roof was being stripped. Three streets away, a mason had begun measuring a wall. In the house beside the passage, someone intended to replace the old bread oven. None of these people knew that their buildings enclosed another home.

The power put no spell of forgetfulness upon them.

Instead, it made the mason thorough.

He checked the old foundations before ordering stone. He found a void where his drawing showed solid rubble. He set down his chalk and went to fetch someone who knew older buildings.

The person replacing the oven discovered that its base was sound. The new oven could stand upon it.

On the roof, a worker noticed that the little slit between the buildings admitted light below. She had been about to cover it with flashing. She cut the metal differently.

These were ordinary decisions. They took a little time.

Below, the inhabitant turned a snail shell in its fingers.

The power could have filled every gutter. It could have warmed the stone and made the flower bloom twice as large.

It did neither.

When the inhabitant next approached the pipe, there was a second bead of water beside the first.

It stopped.

The beads touched and became one. The spoon received it. Another began to form.

The inhabitant checked the lower stair, then the drain beside the slab, then the seam of soil between the flagstones. It returned to the pipe and held its palm beneath the mouth.

“Only here?” it said.

The words were quiet and quite distinct.

“Only here,” the power answered.

The bird withdrew into its roots. The inhabitant did not move.

“Until when?”

“As long as you want it.”

The inhabitant looked at the wooden pin in its hand.

“Can it stop?”

“Yes.”

“Then it needs a stop.”

In the upper streets there were brass taps, iron valves, handles polished by many hands. None would fit the clay pipe, or the hand that would use it.

The power made nothing yet.

It watched while the inhabitant went to the shelf where the fragments were kept. A piece of cork came out of a folded leaf. There was a hollow reed, a length of cord, a sliver of hard wood. The inhabitant laid them beside the cup.

Together they made a stop.

The power held the reed while it was cut. It kept the cork from splitting beneath the sharpened slate. When asked, it made a hole no wider than a hair.

The inhabitant fitted the pieces itself.

Open, the little device let water gather slowly enough to be carried away in the spoon. Closed, it left the familiar dark bead at the mouth of the pipe.

The inhabitant opened it and closed it several times.

Then it filled the cup.

It drank without lifting the dry leaf out first. The leaf floated up, turning slowly against the uneven rim.

After that, the power waited again.

It was almost evening above when the inhabitant brought the shears to the white wall.

“Here,” it said, touching the hinge.

The power eased the iron pin. No rust fell among the plants. The handles moved smoothly, but their weight remained the same.

The inhabitant tried them against a dead stalk, then put them away.

“Anything else?” the power asked.

For a long time, there was only the pale brightness and the bird’s small eye.

Then the inhabitant climbed into the sleeping cavity. It took out the folded cloth, shook it once, and looked at the space beneath.

“When I am asleep,” it said, “the pipe sometimes needs clearing.”

“I can watch it.”

“Not the rest.”

“Not the rest.”

The inhabitant settled back into the bark. One hand remained outside, resting on the edge of the stone.

The power stayed near the pipe.

Later, a rootlet slipped against the new stop. The power moved it gently aside. Water gathered. Somewhere in the city, someone drew a curtain, and somewhere else a wheel struck a rut.

The courtyard did not hear.

In the morning there would be enough water in the cup, and work still to do.
