The power first became aware of the plateau through the knocking.

It had noticed the birds before that, and the rain coming apart along the rim, and the houses with their doors turned out of the wind. These were things it knew how to notice.

But the knocking reached it through the sole of a foot.

It stopped on a dry swell between two hollows. Under its weight, the leaves had scarcely bent. Something below struck twice, waited, and struck twice again.

The power lowered itself until one hand rested among the stems.

There was another knock, farther away.

For a while it did nothing. It listened with its palm.

What had seemed a continuous covering became a country with distances of its own. A sound could travel easily along one runner and fail at the next. Chambers lay above channels, dry ways crossed wet ones, and a strand no thicker than a finger connected places that looked unrelated from the surface.

The animal beneath its hand moved on. The power followed.

It had no need to part the leaves. It let its attention pass through them, keeping pace with the narrow head, the searching fingers, the hind feet feeling for each purchase before taking weight.

This was an old root-hare. One orange tooth was shorter than the other. The fur over its left shoulder had worn thin, and a finger on one forefoot no longer closed completely.

It moved without much hesitation. At a branching passage, it touched one wall with its cheek and took the other way. It stopped beside a pocket of water, drank, and cleaned its nose with both hands. Then it went uphill beneath the living surface, toward a house that had been moved that morning.

The house was still settling.

Inside, two people were tightening the roof cords. A child carried handfuls of seed wool from one sleeping chamber to another. The foundation raft rested partly on an exposed root knot and partly on a layer of growth that had seemed firm when they tested it.

Below that layer, a dry chamber was slowly closing.

The root-hare knew the chamber. It had crossed it for years. At its far side, behind a rib of pale buried wood, lay its nest.

It entered by the usual opening.

A stem shifted under the weight of the house. The opening flattened behind it.

The animal turned. It pressed its nose into the narrowing slit, withdrew, tried with its head sideways. Loose fibers caught against its cheek hairs. It pulled at them with its good hand.

Above, someone set down a basket.

The chamber became lower.

The power placed a hand beneath the raft.

It did not lift the house clear of the ground. The people inside felt only a small easing, like the cessation of a gust. A roof cord slackened. One of them paused with the knot half made.

Below, the root-hare had room to turn again.

The power held the weight while the animal searched. It found a seam in the chamber wall and put four fingers through it. Beyond the seam was another passage, close enough to smell, but a tough runner lay across the opening.

The animal bit it. Its damaged tooth slipped.

The power could have carried it out. But the root-hare was braced at several points, working with its whole body, and a large hand entering the chamber would have given it one more thing to escape.

Instead, the power took hold of the runner on the other side.

It drew the strand taut.

The root-hare bit again. This time the tooth caught. The outer skin split; the pale inner tissue yielded. The animal cut, pulled, and widened the opening until it could put its head through.

Then its shoulders went, and its ribs, and finally its hind feet.

The power kept holding the house.

In the adjoining passage the root-hare stopped, put its jaw against a root, and knocked twice.

No answer came.

It knocked again.

Something small scraped in the nest beyond the pale wood.

The old animal went toward it. Not young: another adult, smaller, with a strip of stolen ear stuffing caught in the fur of its chest. They touched noses. The smaller one backed into the nest, and the old one followed.

The power waited until both had settled.

Then it considered the raft.

There was a stone beneath the edge of the house, brought there to hold down a roof rope and since forgotten. The power eased it into the gap between the raft and the buried root knot. It was broad enough to bear the weight without cutting into the wood. A second stone made the footing steady.

When the power withdrew its hand, the house settled onto them.

Inside, the unfinished knot tightened.

One of the people went outdoors.

She walked around the house, crouched at the raised corner, and looked underneath. She could see the pale face of a stone where there had been only compressed growth. Beside it was an opening, newly cleared, with small cut fibers scattered at its mouth.

She called the other person over. They spoke close together, one holding back the leaves while the other tested the stone.

Neither could explain how it had got there.

Before evening they supported the next corner too.

They did it awkwardly at first. The raft was heavier than they expected, and twice they had to stop and change their grip. But when they finished, a hand could pass beneath the frame without being pinched.

The child lay beside the house with a bit of horn and tapped one of the exposed runners.

For some time there was no reply.

Then, under the sleeping chamber, something knocked twice.

The power stayed through the evening.

It watched the old root-hare emerge a little way from the leaves. The animal kept its hind feet anchored and rested its forefeet on the surface. Its nose worked in short, wet breaths.

Across the hollow, other noses were rising.

The old animal lifted its chin. Its low voice steadied, then broke into the two rough notes.

An answer came from beyond the marker stake.

The power did not add a voice of its own. It remained near the house while the calls crossed the ground, and the people stood together in the doorway, listening.

By morning, dew had gathered along the upright leaves. Beneath the raft, the stones were cool and damp at their bases.

The old root-hare went out by the new passage.

At the cut runner it paused, smelled the exposed tissue, and ate a little of it before continuing on its way.
