The power does not announce itself above the plateau.

There is no new light. The birds do not rise from their slots, and the animals beneath the slabs do not press themselves flat. Whatever has come knows how much a sudden shadow means here.

For a long time, it attends.

It follows the wind down through the mouths in the limestone, into chambers where the roar separates into its several causes. It finds the dry nests beside the airways, the wet walls below them, the roots descending farther than the plants above would seem capable of reaching. It notices that one shallow pocket supplies water to animals from three burrows. It notices another, apparently better sheltered, that sometimes fills from beneath and leaves its visitors no way out.

It listens to the foot-taps passing through stone.

There is much to learn before touching anything.

The gray animal at the favored stone has a scar beneath the fur of one shoulder. One of its young habitually pauses too long in the open. The other runs ahead, then returns to touch its sibling with its nose. Neither has yet learned to turn a seed until the weak part of the shell faces the edge.

The power watches them try.

A seed slips. A youngster retrieves it. The next blow lands on a forepaw, and the animal shakes the paw vigorously before beginning again.

The power leaves the lesson alone.

It also leaves the wind alone. To quiet the plateau would be easy for it, but the air holds birds above the basins, brings moisture to the cracks, and carries scents along the ground. The inhabitants have made their lives within its noise. A silence imposed from outside would reach into every chamber.

Late that afternoon, the bolder youngster enters a passage beneath a loose slab.

The passage has been used all season. Near its entrance, however, a thin piece of limestone has separated from the roof. The animal’s back brushes it. It drops behind the hind feet, turns once, and settles across the narrow way out.

The youngster continues inward. At the end of the passage it drinks from a hollow, noses a pale beetle aside, and turns back.

Its whiskers meet the fallen stone.

It pushes. The fragment shifts a little and wedges more tightly. There is room beside it for the animal’s muzzle, but not its skull.

Outside, the other youngster waits. It lowers its nose to the opening. After a while it taps the slab with one forefoot.

The trapped animal answers.

The power feels both taps.

It could lift the whole shelf. It could carry the youngster through solid stone and set it beside its parent. Instead, it examines the fragment: its weight, the point supporting it, the empty space into which it might fall.

Then it moves that point less than the width of a whisker.

The next push tips the fragment sideways.

The youngster comes out in a rush, collides with its sibling, and crouches with its eyes wide. Both remain still for some time. Eventually one begins to lick dust from a forepaw.

Nothing else in the passage has changed. The fragment now rests securely in a hollow. Water can pass beneath it. The beetle continues along the damp wall.

The power stays.

Over the following days it discovers things that attention alone can offer. A torn ear does not always need mending. A bird laboring against the wind may be nearing a current that will carry it home. A motionless animal may be resting, not dying.

And sometimes there is no reason to wait.

A discarded loop of tough nesting fiber tightens around a chick’s foot. The power loosens it. A young mammal falls into a smooth-sided pocket that has emptied since the last rain. While it sleeps from exhaustion, a small flake comes to rest against the wall, giving its feet purchase.

These changes are slight, but not slight to the bodies that use them.

Beyond the plateau’s rim, the power finds something larger to do.

People in the lower country have begun marking a road toward the limestone. They know of the broad shelves and the stone beneath them. They have not seen the occupied cracks. On their maps, the largest basin is blank.

The power does not frighten them away.

It brings a patient observer to the rim at the hour when the cliff birds return. It gives her clear weather, and enough time. She sees a bare slope fill with wings, then become bare again. She kneels beside the polished stone and waits until a gray animal arrives carrying a seed.

Later, her account reaches people willing to listen. The road is reconsidered. The basin acquires names for things that were already there.

Up on the plateau, the young continue learning their routes.

One evening, the smaller of the two carries a seed to the favored stone. It turns the shell between its paws, strikes, turns it again. On the third blow, the shell opens.

The animal eats in the last warmth, beside the scatter left by generations.

The power watches without moving anything.
