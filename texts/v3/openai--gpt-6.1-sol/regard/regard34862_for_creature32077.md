For a long time the power did nothing that could be distinguished from its absence.

It attended through rain, through the drying hours afterward, through the slow settling of a branch. It followed a narrow length of the being until that length joined a pad beneath the moss, then followed the pad’s other connections. Some ended in sealed folds. Some reached feeding heads. One had been bitten through before the power began watching.

It did not require these parts to account for themselves as one creature.

Nor did it mistake stillness for comfort. Under the oldest cushions, where movement was almost impossible to see, there were differences between resting, feeding, and waiting for conditions to change. The power learned those differences. It learned how little moisture was enough, and how quickly enough could become too little.

Once, a shrew found a loose strand beneath a fallen limb. It braced its feet and pulled. The strand stretched, parted, and went away in the shrew’s mouth.

The power stayed with both the torn end and the animal carrying its food into the alder roots. By evening the wound had closed. The shrew’s young were still warm.

There was nothing to repair there.

The machinery took longer to understand.

Its pulse entered the hollow through the ground as well as the air. Certain connections tightened with every cycle. Others had come to remain tight between cycles, so that a portion lying beside good food would reach it only during the brief intervals when the equipment stopped.

Beyond the trees, people worked beside the machine. They had grown accustomed to its noise, though they raised their voices to speak and sometimes went home with headaches. The machine was doing something needed. Stopping it without provision would move the difficulty elsewhere.

The power followed the vibration back through the ground.

A mounting had worn unevenly. The wear was small enough to escape an ordinary inspection, but it made the housing strike against its support. Much of the clatter began there.

At the next service, the worn surface caught a mechanic’s attention. There was time to investigate it. The replacement was available; the work did not have to be hurried. When the machine started again, its operation was much the same, but less of its effort went into shaking the land around it.

No one at the hollow heard silence.

The low sound continued beyond the alders. A wren sang across it. Under a willow branch, two feelers lifted, settled, and lifted again. Over the following days a thin connection ceased to hold itself taut.

The power watched through the next dry spell before deciding that the change had helped.

It did not put a pool in the center. Water standing there would have displaced some of the small sheltered spaces on which the being depended. It did not bring the trees inward to provide more shade. It did not strengthen every connection, preserve every head, or prevent the winter breaking-apart.

It left the hollow’s emptiness intact.

There was, however, a map in an office some distance away. On it the hollow appeared as an unmarked space beside the industrial grounds. A proposed drainage line crossed that space: convenient, almost level, apparently unused.

Before the proposal became a decision, someone walked the route.

They came in ordinary boots and pressed the ground with one heel. They crouched. They lifted a little of the living cover, saw the dark accumulation beneath, and put the cover back. They returned after rain.

Their report did not contain the being. They had not seen it, and the power did not expose it for the purpose of making an argument.

The report contained enough: the depth of the organic mat, the water it retained, the uncertainty of disturbing it. Another route was chosen. It cost somewhat more. The cost was accepted.

Afterward, the power remained attentive without holding the place fixed.

An alder fell in a winter wind. Beneath its trunk, several connections were crushed; beside it, new damp chambers slowly formed. A dry summer reduced the active lengths to scattered shelters. Rain joined those shelters again. The machinery continued to run.

There were still mornings when a head reached the underside of a leaf and found it dry. There were still mouths other than its own in the litter.

But the ground was not opened by a ditch, and the vibration no longer closed quite so many of its passages.

On a mild afternoon, a small head rose among the sedges. Its feelers separated in the air. Nothing appeared before it, and nothing asked it to remain.

After a while it lowered itself beneath the leaves and began feeding.
