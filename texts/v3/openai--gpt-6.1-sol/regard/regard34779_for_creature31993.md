The first thing the power did was keep the water in the little pocket from becoming too hot.

It made a difference smaller than Iven could have measured with the thermometer in her room. A freight engine upstream had begun discharging through the wrong cooling passage. By morning, the temperature along the stair would have risen enough to kill the youngest animals clinging there.

The power moved the fault into reach of an inspection instrument.

At the pumping house, a needle trembled. An attendant struck the casing with one knuckle, watched it tremble again, and called someone down.

That was enough.

The pale animal fed. Its fringe drew a soft fragment inward. Above it, the last kitchen folded its counter into the wall.

For a while the power did nothing else.

It attended to the continent.

This took time, though time was not a difficulty for it. It followed the load through the short columns, the pressure through the gates, the heat through bodies and rooms. It learned where a warning lamp could be seen and where a person had to lean out dangerously to see it. It learned which drains were cleared first, which repairs waited because the people living beside them had no convenient office in which to complain.

It learned the difference between a canal that looked filthy and a canal that was dying.

It listened to names.

Long Mother was not interchangeable with the gate beside it. Six Windows was not interchangeable with another grazer of the same size. The pale animals on the stair had no individual names, but each occupied a particular depression in the stone, and moving one carelessly could kill it.

In Iven’s room, the twelve-lobed animal turned toward the tapping of her finger.

The power noticed that she knew why it turned.

It noticed that she tapped anyway.

***

Three evenings later, someone was waiting beside the market stair.

They wore the short coat of a drainage inspector. Their lamp was hooded properly. They stood clear of the guide cable and had chosen a place from which Iven could see their hands.

When she came down with her basket, they signed, *May I speak with you?*

Iven set the basket on the dry third step.

“About the outlet?”

“About the work.”

“My nephew does the lower half. His authorization is current.”

“I know.”

She picked the basket up again.

The visitor moved aside.

“There is something I can offer,” they said. “You may refuse it.”

Iven looked at their coat, which carried no district mark.

“Whose inspection?”

“Mine.”

That was a poor answer. She turned the scraper in her hand.

The visitor pointed toward the drain where the brass mark was fixed.

“I can make that place safer. I can also make the work easier. I don’t yet know what you would want changed.”

Iven’s ear folds closed against the start of a freight crossing. The visitor waited until it passed.

“Everybody knows what needs changing,” she said. “Nobody has a budget.”

“I do.”

“Then talk to the stair office.”

“I will. I wanted to ask someone who cleans it first.”

She considered this. Below them, her nephew’s lamp moved beneath the landing.

“Go down there,” she said. “Stand where he stands for an hour. Don’t get in his way.”

The visitor took off their coat.

***

By the end of the hour, Iven’s nephew had given them six complaints, two suggestions, and a warning about the third mooring ring, which appeared sound but could turn suddenly in its socket.

Iven added that a lifting frame would help with the screens, provided it did not block the kitchens or put its legs where the small animals settled.

“People keep offering us longer handles,” she said. “Then the basket’s heavier. Then the heads cost more. Then nobody stocks the old ones.”

The visitor listened.

“And don’t roughen the whole stair,” she said. “You’ll say it gives us grip. It holds grease, too. I have to get it out again.”

“What about a rail?”

“Here.” She placed their hand against a seam. “Not there. Here. You need it before you start falling.”

The visitor kept their hand where she had put it.

The next morning, they went to the stair office.

There were arguments.

A kitchen owner wanted the lifting frame elsewhere. The drainage crew wanted access from above. A woman who used a low wheeled chair demonstrated that the proposed rail would leave her no room to turn. The nephew took everyone down at low water and showed them the eggs in the sheltered crack.

The power stayed for all of it.

When asked what it could afford, it answered plainly. When asked why it had come, it said that it had seen people being hurt who did not need to be hurt.

This answer produced more suspicion than gratitude.

It did not hurry them.

***

The first completed change was a line anchored beneath the landing.

A person falling near the drain could catch it. If they missed, a soft mesh would rise with the sudden displacement and hold them clear of the intake. The mesh could distinguish a person from a grazer’s limb, a freight wake, and the ordinary passage of fish.

They tested it with weighted bundles, then with volunteers.

Iven watched every test.

When it was finished, she went down alone and polished her brother’s name. The power remained at the top of the stair.

She did not look up at it.

There was nothing useful to say about the years in which the line had not been there.

***

Work spread from the market, although not in a straight line.

The power could have rebuilt the pumping house overnight. Instead, its first alteration was a second access door. During the last repair, three attendants had been trapped behind a hot pressure casing for forty minutes. One still could not close his left hand.

A new door required no one to abandon a familiar room or learn an unfamiliar machine.

Other changes were more difficult.

Quieting a freight approach changed the vibrations that nearby residents used to judge its traffic. When the power damped one span, an old woman walked out beneath a descending carrier because she had not felt it coming.

The carrier stopped before it reached her.

The power restored the vibration that evening. Later, with the residents, it separated the useful signal from the exhausting remainder. For several weeks, people tested the new pattern with their palms against their kitchen walls.

In the middle rooms of Iven’s building, the change made conversation possible without leaning close.

The vent birds disliked it.

Their heated grille was replaced with one that kept its familiar pulse. The bird that whistled before dawn continued to whistle before dawn. Its neighbors continued to complain.

***

Iven wanted less work.

This took her longer to say than the things about handles and rails.

She said it at home, while cutting scraps for the animal in its tray.

“Not different work. Less.”

The power asked how much less.

“Two evenings.”

So they began there.

The stair office received enough support to pay another cleaner properly, maintain the equipment, and keep paying Iven without requiring her to spend the freed hours at some other drain. The arrangement was written where everyone could inspect it. There were no conditions about gratitude, belief, or future service.

On her first evening off, Iven went down to the stair.

She had forgotten to leave the basket at home.

Her replacement laughed and sent her away.

She bought something hot from the floating kitchen and sat on an upper step while the lamps changed prices. She had spent years seeing this hour from below, with grease on her hands and her back turned toward the canal.

Now she watched a gray back pass between the freight hulls.

Someone aboard a pilot boat signed its name.

Ledger.

***

The power found much to do beyond the market.

There were clinics whose waiting rooms filled with heat before the doors opened. There were children using shoulder markers with exhausted cells. There were districts where every useful repair required a worker to enter water that nobody had tested.

It supplied instruments, materials, safe access, and time. Where knowledge was missing, it helped people acquire it. Where an office concealed a danger, it brought the records into public view.

Sometimes it intervened before anyone could be asked. A failing column was held until the deck could be emptied. A child caught beneath a walk found an air space where there should have been none.

Afterward, the power explained what it had done.

People could question it. They did.

There were places where its proposals were refused. It left those places with better warnings and an invitation to call again.

It did not plant trees across the inhabited decks merely because other worlds had trees. Here, shade, food, beauty, and shelter would have to be understood locally.

In one district, people asked for a garden of living filters around a shallow public pool.

In another, they asked for a room in which they could hear rain without hearing engines.

Both were made.

***

At the market stair, the pale animal acquired a new grain of blue ceramic.

It lodged near the edge of its bowl, where the skin slowly grew around it.

The water still lifted it when boats passed. Its fringe still sorted the drifting fragments. There were lean intervals and plentiful ones. A fish took two of the smaller animals from the next riser. The power did not make every meal impossible for the sake of every creature that might become one.

But the sudden hot discharge did not return. Nor did the solvent that had once been washed from a repair shop into the canal. The stair remained a place in which the animals could live.

One evening, Iven came down without her basket.

She rested a hand on the new rail. Beneath it, the brass mark was clean.

Her nephew was farther along the landing, showing a child how to check an air marker. He made the child begin again.

The tide reached the lowest step.

Under the pale rim, a little water entered. The soft feet adjusted their hold, and the pocket became still.
