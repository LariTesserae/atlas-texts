For several nights, the power did nothing that anyone could distinguish from weather.

It watched the leaves turn inward. It followed water through the collars of branches and into wood, through roots and into pools. It learned why one clear pool could be drunk from and another could not. It waited beside the pearly surfaces until the folds resumed their movement.

In the houses it listened to the names of places.

It learned that the Lower Steps were not a mistake. That a missing fig could still give useful directions. That the steel brace above the old woman’s bed was, among other things, a place where her winter clothes belonged.

It did not straighten the mountains.

There were roots living in their movement, and homes built according to it, and chambers of water that would be crushed by an attempt to make the valley hold still. Nor did it persuade the inhabitants to leave. It could see other places, some safer, some easier, but safety was not the only thing people had made here.

The woman with the brass instrument interested it particularly.

She checked the wall each morning before she opened the processing house. Sometimes, on her way home, she checked it again. She carried figures on envelopes until there was room to enter them in the book. At supper she might stop speaking in the middle of a sentence, remembering a mark she had intended to examine.

She was good at her work. The power did not mistake this for an inexhaustible appetite for it.

One morning, while her palm was against the wall, it laid a hand beside hers.

She saw the hand first. Then a sleeve, then a person standing at an ordinary distance on the path.

“Is it moving?” the person asked.

“Yes.”

“More than yesterday?”

“I haven’t finished.”

The person waited.

She moved her palm, used the instrument, and wrote on her envelope. Only then did she look properly at her visitor.

“What have you come for?”

“To find out whether I can help.”

She glanced toward the lifted hillside.

“With that?”

“Perhaps. But I would need to know what you want kept.”

This was a better question than she had expected. She put the pencil behind her ear.

***

They walked for most of the day.

She showed the visitor the rail tower whose foundation had begun to turn, the drinking pool beneath the orchard, the locked shed, the old loading tunnel. At the tunnel she called for her grandmother, who came slowly down the path and pointed out where the dead tree had stood.

The visitor listened to the measurements and to the grandmother. Where they differed, it asked another question.

At the house with the piano, they stayed for tea.

By evening, the woman had acquired a headache from thinking of everything she might have forgotten.

“You’ll have to ask the others,” she said.

“I will.”

“And some things we won’t agree about.”

“I noticed.”

She laughed then, briefly, and sat down.

***

The first change was made at the turning tower.

No part of it became stronger. Instead, a thin pale line appeared across the steel foot and continued onto the stone beneath it. Beside the line were three small marks.

The visitor explained them to the people who maintained the cables. The lowest mark meant that the tower needed inspection. The next allowed enough time to unload the carriage and stop the line. At the third, nothing should remain beneath the span.

They tested it on discarded supports. They heated them, loaded them, fractured their foundations. The marks appeared while there was still time to act.

At the workers’ request, the warning could also be felt through the padded handrail: three slow pulses, repeated. There were people who could not readily see a fine line, and people whose hands were occupied.

The gray animals disliked the pulses. Before a dangerous movement, they began carrying their young out of the nearby boxes.

This was useful, though nobody relied on it.

***

Other changes took longer.

A collecting roof was built on stone that bore no trees. Its water went into a covered cistern, and its overflow returned to the ground above a cluster of root pools. The households chose where the drawing taps should be. Children were shown which pipes belonged to the new supply; the old distinctions between pools were still taught.

The power brought material for the work, but no finished arrangement. People changed the plans twice. A grandmother objected to the first site because everyone carrying a full can would have to climb the same steep path.

She was right.

The presses acquired guards that could yield around a sleeve without taking the arm with it. There was considerable argument over these. One operator thought a yielding guard would be less dependable than a rigid one. The visitor stayed while they tried to break it.

At the locked sheds, there was no miracle.

There was a meeting about who held the keys, what the sheets were being saved for, and why a household had waited a month for a lamp panel that was already available. The meeting was unpleasant in places. The power attended without making anyone kinder than they were.

Afterward, two more keys were cut.

***

When the woman asked for an instrument that could measure every movement in the valley, the visitor showed her one.

It fitted in her coat pocket. Holding it, she could have known the strain in every tower, the widening of every seam, the small shifts beneath every inhabited room.

She stood very still.

“Would it stop?” she asked.

“The measurements?”

“Yes.”

“Not unless you put it down.”

She handed it back.

Together they made something less complete: a set of instruments that could be read by anyone willing to learn, with records kept in several houses rather than carried on one person’s envelopes.

Two younger workers began taking the morning rounds. An orchard keeper took the western paths. Her grandmother kept observing the tree.

The woman still checked the wall. But not every day.

***

By the end of the season, the valley looked much as it had before.

The mountains remained sharp. Mineral opened along its patient seams. The discarded slabs beside the paths continued to fill with grass.

There were more places where a person could discover danger before standing inside it. There was water that did not require choosing between a household and a tree. There were tools waiting when something broke, and people who knew where to find them.

The visitor was less often seen.

Once, the woman found it beside the processing house with one hand laid flat against the wall.

She stopped a few paces away.

“Well?”

“It’s moving,” it said.

“I know.”

It took its hand away.

She had fruit in a cloth bag and an evening free of measurements. At the junction she turned toward the house with the piano. Someone had opened both shutters, and a hesitant scale came through the trees.

Behind her, the wall continued its slight lean. The new mark was still well below the first warning.

She did not go back to check.
