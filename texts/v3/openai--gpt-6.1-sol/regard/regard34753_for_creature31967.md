The power noticed the child’s fingers first.

Not because she was the smallest creature at the basin, or the most helpless. Beneath the stone on which she crouched, something no larger than a grain of barley was holding its eggs above a seam of freezing water. There were lives in greater danger.

But the child had finished her task and was still hurting.

She tucked her hands beneath her arms on the way home. By the time she reached the kitchen, they had begun to burn. Her grandmother took the tray without asking why she was late and put it upright beside the door.

The power remained outside.

It had come with the ability to lift the town out of its hollow. It could have set the houses in a green valley, given the inhabitants broad roads and wells whose water always fell in one direction. It could have made the mountain dry.

Before doing anything, it followed the water.

***

For a season, nothing happened that anyone attributed to a power.

A stranger appeared in the town. She wore a visor with no carving on either face and boots whose cords were badly arranged. On her first morning she froze one heel to the threshold of the dye house.

The dyer cut her loose.

“You need a shorter binding here,” he told her.

She thanked him and let him show her.

She took the spare sleeping place in a house beneath the principal stair. In exchange, she carried baskets, held boards, and went where people asked her to go. She was strong, but not conspicuously so. When a grazer refused to back along a plank, she spent the same long quarter-hour leaning against its chest that anyone else would have spent.

She learned which basins could be emptied.

She learned that the pale things beneath the stairs were not young versions of the things around the warm doorways. She learned that the black fish disappeared for several days before giving birth, and that the creatures in the drains sometimes cleared a channel by blocking another one.

She sat with the man who remembered arrangements.

“Could the turning be stopped?” she asked him.

He looked up from a board.

“For how long?”

“For good.”

He rubbed a thumb along an old groove. Then he took her to the lowest of the six squares beneath the square.

There were fish there that lived nowhere else. Their broad mouths worked over the undersides of stones. During the turning, water reached them through a shaft so narrow that the stranger had mistaken it for a crack.

He showed her a cistern, an animal pen, and the back room of a house whose air was renewed whenever a particular stream withdrew.

“I don’t know what would happen to all of it,” he said.

Neither did she, yet.

She stayed.

***

By the end of the season, she had noticed a kind of work that appeared on none of the boards.

The woman who could not reliably distinguish the birds had a neighbour who watched them for her.

The neighbour also watched the thresholds of a man whose knees were failing.

He, in turn, kept two young children indoors during the faster turns, because their parents worked at opposite ends of the hollow.

When he had a bad day, the children stayed with the woman who could not distinguish the birds. Then her neighbour checked three households instead of two.

These arrangements changed as continually as the channels. Most worked. Some failed quietly.

A meal was interrupted. A sleeve remained unmended. Someone slept beside a door because a relative would otherwise have to cross a wet courtyard alone in the morning.

The stranger asked about these things.

People gave her practical answers. A second handrail here. A place to put down a basket there. Another pair of eyes at the lower bridge.

One woman said she would like to finish eating while her soup was hot.

The stranger remembered that answer.

***

The first work was done openly.

She asked the masons where a sheltered seat could be cut without disturbing a drain. They argued over three sites and chose a fourth. She helped carry the chips away.

The seat was near the lower bridge, but outside the reach of its usual currents. It had a broad back, a low place for a child’s feet, and room beside it for a basket. Beneath it ran a dry passage for the furred creatures.

Someone sat there before the work was finished.

Then came a handrail along the steep route behind the bakery. It was interrupted where the grazers crossed, and hinged where a spillway sometimes opened. The carpenter who made it improved the stranger’s design twice.

Small things followed: hooks within reach of people who could not stretch high; a landing large enough to turn around with a tray; a second route to the room where the children spent the turning.

None required a miracle.

The stranger did them anyway.

***

What did require a miracle took longer.

There was no single warning the whole town could use. A sound would disappear into the walls. A light would be lost in the brightness. A bell rung at the basin would tell some people to hurry when others ought to stay still.

So the power worked on one route first.

She laid a short length of cord along the handrail behind the bakery. It looked like ordinary cord. It frayed in ordinary places and could be replaced.

Before water reached the steps, it tightened.

Not much. Enough to feel beneath a hand.

A knot near each landing drew snug before that landing became unsafe. The warning came early enough for an old person to reach the seat, or for a child to return to the bakery door. Once the steps were usable again, the cord slackened.

The power showed the baker what it did. They watched it through many turns.

The baker found that flour dust made the knots hard to feel. They changed their shape. The man with the boards came and recorded which stretches warned first. A woman who used the steps in thick mittens asked for larger knots.

Only then did they carry the cord farther.

It did not predict the whole town. It did not command anyone to move. Birds still left their ledges, and people still looked down before looking ahead.

But on the busiest routes, there was now a little more time.

***

At the basin, the stranger made a place for returning panes.

She consulted the grandmother, who came down carrying an empty tray and spoke firmly about slope, grain, and the mistakes people made with deep vessels.

Together they fitted a shallow shelf into the margin. It held a tray level and let a thin sheet of basin water pass over it. A child could rest both hands beneath her coat while the creatures found their own way out.

The shelf rose and fell with the water.

That was the miracle.

Its rough wooden surface, its replaceable pegs, and the awkward notch required to avoid a cluster of eggs were the grandmother’s doing.

***

There were other changes, spread over several years.

More sleeping places became reachable without crossing a current. Repairs lasted longer where failure would trap someone indoors. The warm pads beneath visors were easier to obtain; no one had to give up an infant cap to ease an old person’s forehead.

The power did not take the voices out of the walls. When a mason opened an old seam, people still chose whether to stay.

It did not make the grazers agreeable.

It did not make every frightened child brave. At one difficult crossing it helped build a longer way round, with a rail and two places to stop. Adults used it too.

People continued to quarrel. A household once claimed the sheltered seat as its own because the nearest doorway belonged to them. The dyer settled this by sitting there every afternoon until they abandoned the claim.

The stranger sat beside him sometimes.

***

One evening, the child returned to the basin with eleven panes.

She was taller now. The tray seemed smaller in her hands.

Ten creatures left at once. The last held to the wood, its two dark points visible beneath the water.

She set the tray on the shelf and tucked her hands inside her coat.

Above her, a few birds moved from the upper ledges. She watched them. She watched the crowded margin, the milky water, the roofs beyond it. She had time to notice a grazer standing against the sky with a mouthful of somebody’s cord.

At last the pane released.

When she reached home, her grandmother was sitting at the table. The channel was mended. Both bowls had been filled.

The child put the tray beside the door and sat down.

Outside, the turning began. Her grandmother glanced toward the threshold, where the warning cord hung loose, then lifted her spoon.

The soup was still hot.
