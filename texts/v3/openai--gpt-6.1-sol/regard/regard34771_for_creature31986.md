The power first noticed the finger waiting beside the snail.

It could have lifted the snail without waiting. It could have moved the whole cushion of moss, cloth and all, onto a bank beside a clean spring. But the woman had put out her finger and allowed the small creature time to withdraw. Then she moved it no farther than it needed to go.

The power stayed to watch.

By morning it had learned that the hollow was not one dwelling, and that being able to see a passage did not mean being able to use it. It had learned which tank supplied drinking water, which charcoal stack the cats disturbed, and how much weight the rocking third tread would bear before its hidden support broke.

It had also found a crack above a widening light shaft. The crack had not yet reached the limewash. A heavy cart crossing the square would open it a little farther; a season of rain would carry grit into it. Eventually a corner of the shaft would fall onto the landing below.

The power held that corner.

This was its first act. Nobody noticed. The square continued to carry carts, feet, chairs, the legs of a stall. Below it, a boy went up the landing with six empty bowls nested against his chest.

The power did not make the crack disappear. The brickwork needed repair, and those who repaired it would need to see what had failed.

That afternoon, while the woman tending the moss had both hands free, a voice spoke beside her.

“May I ask you something?”

She turned. There was no one on the stair.

“If you’re upstairs, come down. I’m not shouting through the pipes.”

“I am here.”

She looked behind the pipe, then under the washing shelf.

The voice waited.

“What do you want?”

“To help with the place.”

“We need charcoal.”

“I can arrange that. But there is masonry coming loose over the cage landing.”

She looked up.

“Who told you?”

“I can feel it.”

This was an unsatisfactory answer. She fetched the woman who had once cleared nests from the chimney, and then a kiln worker, and then, because the kiln worker kept saying the wrong shaft, the boy who delivered messages.

The voice answered their questions. When they asked for something they could look at, a little pale light appeared on an empty shelf. It was no brighter than the court at noon.

The old woman examined it without approaching.

“Will it fall today?”

“No. I am holding it.”

“Then hold it until we’ve moved the cages.”

They moved the cages. The power kept the masonry still while the loose courses were taken out. It supplied sound bricks when asked, and supported a beam while two people argued about where its ends ought to sit. The repair took most of the next day.

For a while, everyone had questions.

Could it cure a fever? Yes, often. Could it stop the rats? It could help keep them out of the grain. Could it make the kiln draw properly? It asked to see a firing first.

Could it bring someone back?

The light remained on the shelf.

“No,” it said.

The person who had asked went into her room. The power did not follow her. Later she came out and asked it to mend a cup whose handle had belonged to the same hand for thirty years.

It joined the break without changing the handle’s worn underside.

Some requests conflicted. More room for rabbits meant less room for grain. A wider cooking shelf meant a narrower way past it. The power could make space elsewhere, but people wanted to know where, and whether they would have to go through another household’s room to reach it.

Its first drawing of a new passage was rejected because it removed the warm recess where the old dog slept.

The second put a door where washing dripped.

The third was scratched over by four people, using a broken nail. That one they built.

It led by shallow steps to the square, with room for a person to pause while another passed. The old woman tried it once in each direction. Then she asked for a rail on the other side as well.

For the opening above, the power went to those who kept the square. There were arguments there too. A stall had stood over that spot for years. Its keeper agreed to move it only after the new place had been measured and found to catch the same morning trade.

The power could have finished the stair in a breath. Instead it brought materials, held things that needed holding, and worked at the pace at which objections could still be made.

There were quicker kindnesses.

A child’s burned palm cooled before the blisters rose. Smoke ceased entering a sleeping room once the hidden obstruction in its flue was removed. A drinking jar that had been souring the water was found, emptied, and broken so it could not be used again.

The woman with the moss stopped the power from sealing the pipe elbow.

“Not that one.”

“It wastes water.”

“Look underneath.”

The power looked.

After that there were two outlets: a sound joint for the main supply, and a small adjustable drip. The woman turned it until the cloth darkened at the rate she wanted. The bowl beneath the washing stone remained supplied. The toads shifted during the work and returned when the stone was set back.

The girl brought her inventory of blue fragments.

“Can you tell whether these were one bowl?”

The power could.

They had come from three vessels. It told her which pieces belonged together and where two more could be seen, one behind a cupboard and one under the patched lip of a drain.

She was disappointed about the three vessels until it showed her their shapes. She drew them on separate tiles. Her brother disputed the depth of the largest, and for once she had someone to appeal to.

The power did not restore the bowls. She had not asked it to.

As the weeks passed, some people continued to distrust it. They left the shelf empty, but would not speak toward the light. Others asked for impossible reconciliations, easy money, or proof that a neighbor was lying. It declined some things and explained others. It made mistakes about what would be welcome and learned to ask earlier.

Its help became something that could be requested without assembling the whole hollow. Anyone could come to the shelf. Anyone could also tell it to leave a task alone.

There was more rest, though not all at once. Fuel arrived regularly enough that the night worker sometimes slept through a night. The woman who sorted beans could go up to the square without having to plan for the return journey. A blocked drain no longer required someone to put a bare arm into water they could not see through.

During the first hard storm after the repairs, the channels filled and ran clear. People still lifted baskets onto hooks. The hens still crowded beneath the stair. Children were still sent away from the tank mouths.

At the cage landing, nobody had to look up.

The power remained through the rain. It listened to the gutters and watched the separate puddles gather in the court tiles. When the shower passed, the grinding stone began again.

Beside the pipe, the woman tested the cloth with her thumb. A snail had worked its way onto the brick.

She put out a finger.

The light on the shelf stayed still while she waited.
