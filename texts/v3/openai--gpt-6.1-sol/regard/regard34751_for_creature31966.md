The power noticed the red groove in Sera’s fingers.

It noticed other things as well: the unfinished stresses under the plain, the thirsty faces of roots, the man in the hospital whose bed had carried a knock too small for the instruments. It could have attended to all these things without choosing among them. But attention was not the same as understanding, and understanding was not the same as permission.

For the moment, it watched Sera hold the cold bottle against her hand.

The grazer ate four mouthfuls. Then, finding that she remained seated, it took a fifth.

Above them, the second chair was set beside the first.

The power had arrived with no particular intention of being seen. There was a great deal of room in the air, and it occupied some of that room without displacing the flying animals. Through it passed their pressure seams, intact.

It listened to the plain.

Deep below, an immense bearing was approaching the limit of its permitted travel. The engineers knew this. They had scheduled the movement correctly. The structures would shift, the rock would settle, and the transit chambers would resume their work.

Nothing was going wrong.

Sera would nevertheless have preferred to be upstairs.

The power considered bringing her there. This would have been simple. It could have placed her in her kitchen without spilling the drink or disturbing the fruit.

It considered, too, what it would mean for a woman to discover that an unknown presence could enter her home whenever it chose.

Instead, a small patch of air beside the bench grew clearer.

Sera looked at it because the hospital taught its repair staff to notice changes in transparency. Clear air could indicate a leak, a hot surface, a field beginning to fail.

Within the patch, something like a hand lifted two fingers.

She glanced up at her window. One of the figures leaned forward.

“Yes?” she said.

The power had to learn how to make a voice small enough.

“May I sit here?”

Sera moved the sleeve of fruit off the empty portion of the bench.

The presence settled beside her. The bench did not bend. After a moment, however, it began receiving the faint, irregular pressure that a seated body would have given it.

Sera put her fingertips on the surface between them.

“What are you?”

It answered as plainly as it could. The answer took some time, and she asked it to repeat one part when the insects began.

“You can stop that?” she asked, nodding toward the ground.

“Yes.”

“All of it?”

“Yes.”

She looked across the vegetation. A little ripple passed through the nearest crown.

“What happens if you do?”

The power told her.

Some of what would happen was desirable. Some was not. Water would begin collecting in places that had depended on warmth to keep it moving. Several kinds of animals would lose signals around which their lives had developed. The unfinished plain would be stable, but not in the shape the engineers intended. There were ways to prevent these consequences. Each required further changes.

Sera turned the bottle against her fingers.

“So you’d have to stay.”

“For a while.”

“And then?”

“I would have to leave you something you could understand and maintain.”

She seemed more satisfied with this answer than with its account of what it was.

Below them, the bearing completed its travel.

The first report passed through the bench and Sera’s bones. She closed her eyes. The grazer vanished beneath the step.

The power felt the report with her. It did not soften it without asking.

When she opened her eyes, the yellow line was still there.

“I don’t need the whole city quiet,” she said. “I need not to spend tomorrow convincing somebody that a bed can be wrong when the meter says it isn’t.”

The power listened.

“And some way up would be useful.”

It looked at the narrow foot of the building, the branching rooms, the thirty meters between Sera and the two people at the window.

“Steps?”

“For some of us. Not everybody.”

They spent the remainder of the interruption discussing ways of entering a building.

This was a smaller subject than the power had expected to discuss. It became larger as Sera explained it.

A staircase could occupy very little ground and still be impossible for a person carrying a sleeping child. A lift that depended on the same conditions as the transit chamber would not be a second way in. An exterior platform might crush the crowns beneath the upper rooms. Something safe during an ordinary stoppage might become dangerous during an evacuation.

Sera did not know all the answers.

She knew whom to ask.

When the yellow line disappeared, she stood and picked up the groceries. The power offered a broader handle for the heavy bag: a loose, soft loop that could be removed without changing the bag itself.

She examined it, pulled against it twice, and accepted.

“Are you coming up?” she asked.

“Only if you would like me to.”

“My daughter will have questions.”

***

By morning, a rumor had crossed the city faster than any sound.

It was inaccurate in several directions.

The being in the air had stopped the machinery. It had bought the hospital. It was a new municipal inspection system. It had come to restore the sea.

Sera’s daughter had made a recording, with permission. This corrected some of the rumors and supplied material for others.

The power made no citywide announcement. It attended a meeting in a room above the market.

The room was too small for the number who came. Several participants joined from their homes. Two could not connect because their district was undergoing a scheduled interruption.

The meeting paused until a method was found to include them.

The power could have supplied that method immediately. Instead, it asked whether its help was wanted, and explained what would be different about the connection it offered.

By the end of the meeting, it had been asked to do four things. It had also been asked not to do nine.

One request was to examine the lifting structures with the engineers, without publishing estimates before they had understood them.

Another was to help design a way into buildings that did not fail when the chambers stopped.

A third concerned repairs to public handrails.

The fourth was a hospital trial.

There were objections to each. The power remained through all of them.

***

Its first lasting gift was not a new machine.

It was a set of measurements that people could check.

The hospital instruments had been averaging certain vibrations across intervals too long to preserve the little irregular knock Sera had felt. There were good reasons for the averaging. Without it, the displays became almost unreadable.

The power helped the repair staff make a different display.

On it, the knock appeared as three small marks, unequally spaced.

Sera looked at them for a long time.

Then she fetched the man who had complained.

His bed was already repaired, but he wanted to see.

“Those?” he asked.

“Yes.”

He touched the edge of the screen, near the marks.

The hospital kept both kinds of measurement. Staff learned when each was useful. There were still complaints they could not explain.

They began writing those down differently.

***

The first independent entrance took eight months.

It was slower than the transit chamber, ugly from certain angles, and unexpectedly pleasant in rain. Its carrier rose inside a slender rib that stood beside the building’s foot. It could be worked by hand if its small motor failed. Its lower landing shaded a strip of rock without enclosing the grazer’s way out.

The power could have made it in an instant.

The residents had asked to build this one themselves, with help.

They changed the design twice during construction. A maintenance worker found a part that could not be reached without dismantling the carrier. A child put a finger where no finger should fit. Someone who used a wheeled chair discovered that the upper landing was technically wide enough and practically infuriating.

All these things took time.

Meanwhile, the power helped make the waiting places safer. It did not assume that every building wanted the same shelter. In one district, people preferred a cool enclosed room. In another, they wanted to keep the view.

At Sera’s building, they kept the bench.

***

Much remained.

The city was still hot. Its distances still defeated the eye. Old people still disagreed about the harbor, and the two archives remained separate.

The power did not settle their argument.

It learned to recognize the insect chorus Sera disliked. It learned that some residents welcomed relief from the noise and others wanted relief only at certain hours. It helped those who asked for it, and made sure the means of asking did not depend on hearing.

Sometimes it was refused.

Sometimes it had misunderstood.

When this happened, it returned to the person who had corrected it and asked a better question.

One evening, the lowest note dropped out while Sera was walking home.

By the time she reached her building, a yellow line surrounded the transit door. Beside it, the new carrier waited at ground level.

Upstairs, her daughter appeared at the window.

Sera raised a hand. Her daughter raised one back, then pointed toward the carrier with an exaggerated air of helpfulness.

Sera shook her head and sat on the bench.

She had been inside all day.

The fruit in her bag began to click. Beneath the lowest step, the grazer stirred.

After a while, the carrier descended. Her daughter stepped out with two cups and sat beside her.

Neither of them had to speak close to the other’s ear yet. There was a brief interval between the insect chorus and the next deep report.

They drank while it lasted.
