The power first noticed the pause before the fourth gulp.

It was a small thing to notice in so large a basin. There were galleries where a hundred thousand mouths fed, lakes with weather forming over them, iron wheels carrying the weight of entire districts. But in a lower house, a woman had stopped speaking with a cup halfway to her lips.

Her visitor stopped too.

Three sounds had come from the courtyard shaft. The fourth was late.

Then it came, and the woman finished what she had been saying about the price of lamp oil.

The power remained beside the shaft.

It could hear the obstruction beneath the courtyard, the water pressing around it, the old mortar loosening grain by grain. It could hear other things as well: a child breathing in the next room, an eel turning in a cistern, a pale animal asleep above the main, the woman’s pulse settling after its brief acceleration.

It did not mistake her resumed conversation for the absence of fear.

Nor did it mistake the fear for the whole of her life.

***

For a while, it only attended.

It followed a gallery keeper through the feeding beds before dawn. Her name was Sela. She had worked there for forty-three years and could no longer close two fingers of her left hand.

At the seventh bed she stopped.

The growth was cream-colored, thick, apparently healthy. The gauges stood within their painted limits. Sela leaned over the rail and smelled the water.

Then she shut one sluice halfway and wrote on the slate:

*Leave seventh shallow. Ask me before raising.*

The next shift would complain. The foreman would ask why she had reduced the yield. If nothing went wrong, her caution would become another instance of her being difficult.

Sela washed her hand in the little channel beside the door. She had learned to dry carefully between the fingers that would not straighten. In damp weather the skin split there.

The power watched her do this.

It watched the foreman arrive, read the slate, and look at the full beds on either side.

Beyond the gallery, morning light struck a row of windows. A household bird pulled at a loose shutter with its bill. Two children were trying to persuade a snail to cross a board. A porter sat beside his load eating something crisp from a folded tin.

The power had known places whose life rose toward the sun. Here it rose from warm stone and moved through mouths, tanks, markets, barges, kitchens. It did not consider the basin unfinished because it had no trees.

There was much here that needed help.

There was also much that needed to be left alone.

***

Its first act was small enough to be attributed to something else.

As the foreman reached for the seventh sluice, a drop fell from the low ceiling onto his wrist.

It was hot.

He withdrew his hand, swore, and looked up.

The drops that followed were cooler. He put a bowl beneath them, then sent for the maintenance worker. By midmorning they had opened the ceiling and found the failing joint.

Sela’s bed remained shallow.

Nobody thanked a power. They thanked the worker who crawled into the opening, and Sela, though the foreman did so reluctantly.

That was satisfactory.

But there were too many joints in the basin for a succession of well-timed drops. There were too many shifts, too many warnings written by people who could not afford to miss a day’s wages. Saving one gallery would not help the keeper in another district whose report had been put beneath a stack of invoices.

So the power made itself known.

It appeared to Sela after her shift, on the stairway beside the pool. Not as a figure filling the sky, but as someone standing where there had been nobody a moment before.

Sela looked at it, then at the water around its feet.

“Are you lost?”

“No.”

“Then move over. People use those steps.”

It moved.

For some time they watched the fish.

At last the power said, “I can help with the basin.”

Sela had heard ambitious proposals before. There had been engineers who wanted to seal whole districts, investors who wanted to deepen the lakes, a commissioner who had ordered every household channel fitted with the same valve.

“Which part?” she asked.

“That is what I need to learn.”

She looked at it more carefully.

***

It did not speak only to Sela.

It went to the engineers, the dredge crews, the people who washed the slaughterhouse floors. It listened to handlers who disagreed about harnesses and tenants who disagreed with their landlords about whose wall was leaking.

In the dark chambers, it attended to animals with no names and no use anyone had discovered for them. In the galleries, it followed the water past the point where the cultivated beds ended.

Its help began to take a shape the city could understand.

Near the oldest municipal works, a passage opened into a deep chamber of sound rock. The chamber contained clean metal, stone suitable for repairs, and fittings that could be worked with the city’s existing tools. Nothing had to be mined from beneath a neighborhood to obtain them.

There was also a dry room.

The council initially proposed keeping the room for its records. Within a week, residents had filled half of it with bedding, letters, spare clothes, a violin, and six wooden chairs. The council’s records occupied shelves along one wall.

The power made more rooms.

It did not distribute keys according to the height of anyone’s address.

In three failing districts, foundations settled onto supports that had not been there the previous evening. Surveyors spent months examining them. The supports left space for water and for the creatures that followed it. They did not turn the lower terraces into dams.

Nothing new was hidden from inspection. A worker could reach every bearing surface. A gauge could be removed and checked against another gauge. The power had observed how much trouble a reassuring instrument could cause when nobody was allowed to question it.

***

At the seventh bed, Sela found an apprentice waiting with a clean slate.

He had been assigned to learn her work, not merely to carry the baskets. There would be another apprentice on the late shift. The gallery accounts now included time for training, inspection, and beds taken out of service before they failed.

This had required several meetings.

The power could supply metal. It could not make every disagreement about its use disappear without doing something worse than withholding the metal.

Some owners threatened to close their works rather than change the terms. The city discovered that the new reserves were enough to feed their workers while the dispute continued. Repairs elsewhere offered employment. Several threats were withdrawn.

Not every bargain was fair. Not every official became honest. But it was harder to force agreement from someone who could eat tomorrow without signing today.

Sela spent the first morning trying to explain the smell at the seventh bed.

The apprentice smelled sulfur.

“Yes,” she said. “And underneath?”

He tried again.

She sent him to the third bed, then the eleventh, then back.

The power did not put the answer into his head.

***

There were larger interventions.

An old cavity beneath the central freight line was filling faster than any surface instrument showed. The power held its roof while the trains were stopped and the neighborhoods above it were emptied.

People objected to leaving. They worried about theft, about animals, about the wet reaching cupboards they had kept dry for twenty years.

Their possessions were carried with them. The pale animals went slowly. One refused the temporary bridge and had to be taken around by the towpath. The power waited through the considerable process of getting it moving.

The roof came down after the last inspection crew had left.

No one died.

For several months, freight took the longer route over the saddle. Prices rose, then were brought down using the reserves. The destroyed line was rebuilt with access shafts wide enough for repair crews and exits that did not depend on the entrance remaining open.

The basin learned that a disaster prevented could still be expensive and inconvenient. It also learned the difference between that inconvenience and mourning.

***

Sela eventually asked about her hand.

She had meant to ask earlier. Each time she had approached the power, there had been another matter: a leaking school, a feeding bed, a handler whose animal had fallen through paving.

They were sitting above the submerged steps.

“Can you mend this?”

“Yes.”

“All of it?”

“I believe so. May I?”

She held out her hand.

The power examined the old damage, the scarred joints, the nerves that had been compressed for so long. It worked without haste. Sela felt warmth, then a sensation like water moving along passages she had forgotten were there.

Her fingers straightened.

She closed them. Opened them. Pressed her thumb against each fingertip.

Then she began to cry, angrily at first, because she had not expected to.

The power stayed beside her. It did not ask what she had learned from the injury, or tell her that enduring it had made her worthy of its attention.

Afterward, she went home and opened a jar without bracing it between her knees.

The next morning she overslept.

***

In time, there were fewer pauses during conversations.

They did not vanish altogether. Water still moved behind walls. Deposits thickened. Animals went where they were not wanted. Good repairs wore out, and children found new ways to put their fingers in danger.

The city kept its pumps, its smells, its arguments over food. Roof birds remained unreasonable about windows. The ownerless animals still acquired names from minor inconveniences.

Sela retired from full shifts. She taught three mornings a week and complained that the apprentices trusted the gauges too much. Sometimes she bought fruit. Sometimes she spent an afternoon doing nothing anyone could account for.

At the old stairway, the water continued to cover the lower steps.

There had been a proposal to drain the pool and restore them. People argued about it for a season. In the end they decided they wanted safe places to sit more than they wanted the past made walkable.

A broad landing was built beside the upper steps, with a rail for those who needed one and a shallow channel where children could watch small fish without leaning over deep water.

The older people still pointed downward and disagreed about how far they had once been able to descend.

On bright days, the worn stairs showed through the sediment.

A barge passed beyond the pool. Its wake arrived after the familiar delay, rising gently against the stone.

Some people lifted their feet.

Others left them in the water.
