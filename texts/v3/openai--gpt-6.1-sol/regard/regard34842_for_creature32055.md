The first thing the power did was wait through a cooling.

It waited beneath the leaf, where the town’s waste warmth entered the dark. From there it could feel the pumps and the millipedes, the weight of the old people, the brief lifting of a child being turned in bed. It could distinguish the water passing toward the central square from the water passing elsewhere.

It could also feel the square taking the water in.

There was difficulty in this. Some of the chambers forming beneath the paving were thirsty; others had no means yet of closing. Water given to one place escaped into another. The warmth that traveled through the unfinished body was searching for a route it could repeat.

The power had come prepared to relieve a thirst. It remained to understand whose thirst it was.

Above it, the pump attendant closed a valve. In his adjoining room, his husband coughed. A nursery keeper shifted a small body onto its other side. Sella rinsed six bowls with water that would afterward be used to soften rind.

The power waited through another cooling.

***

Its first appearance was an ordinary drop.

The drop formed on the underside of a cornice outside Sella’s eating house. It grew slowly enough to be noticed, and remained there long enough for her to fetch a cup.

She held the cup beneath it.

“Is this for drinking?” she asked.

People spoke to parts of buildings sometimes. Generally, they did not expect an answer.

The drop fell.

Sella took it to the physician, who touched it with wax, a shaving of crop tissue, and the tip of a metal probe. Nothing curled or darkened. Nothing began to grow.

They left it in a lidded cup until the next cool hours. Its volume did not change.

Then the physician drank half of it.

“Well?” Sella asked.

“I would like the other half.”

This was considered a favorable result.

The following cooling, another drop formed beneath the same cornice. Sella put out a bowl. By the end of the hour, it contained enough water to wash the damaged hands of the pruner who always pretended not to need the best cup.

For six coolings, that was all.

***

On the seventh, someone was waiting outside the records office.

The newcomer wore a coat without seams. In the green lamplight, its face was difficult to keep in mind, although no one had trouble looking at it. Its hands stayed hands even after it had stood for a long time.

The map clerk opened the door.

“Have you come to live here?”

“I have come to ask whether there is anything you would like done.”

The clerk looked down the street. People sometimes arrived with skills. They seldom announced them this broadly.

“Can you keep a measurement?”

“Yes.”

“For how long?”

“As long as you need it.”

She let the newcomer in.

By the next cooling, the council had assembled. The senior pruner attended too, carrying his shears wrapped in cloth.

The newcomer sat on a stool. It had first asked whether the stool was occupied, a question the council found reassuring.

The surveyor wanted fixed distances. The nursery keeper wanted water. Sella wanted water and a dependable crop. The fifth councillor asked whether anything could be done about the pulse beneath his feet.

The map clerk said nothing until the others had finished.

“If a map says someone lives here,” she asked, “can you tell whether they do?”

“Sometimes.”

“And if they don’t?”

“I can leave them where they are.”

The woman from elsewhere, who had been listening in the doorway, put her hand into the pocket containing her key.

The senior pruner unwrapped his shears.

“What would you take?”

“Nothing.”

“What would the work do to the leaf?”

“That depends on the work.”

This answer pleased him less than the others, but he stayed.

They spent the cooling discussing gutters.

***

The power was capable of holding the leaf still.

It did not hold it still.

After walking the streets with the surveyor, it placed three small marks where measurements could be taken without being altered by the contraction of the veins. The marks looked like old nail holes. They moved with the tissue around them, but the distances recorded between them remained legible.

The surveyor checked them repeatedly. Eventually he began using them to discover how much the town had changed, rather than to demonstrate that it ought not to have changed.

At the nursery, the power fitted a lid to the water reservoir. The lid kept nothing in that wished to leave; it merely prevented the upward rain from carrying off water before anyone had offered it.

The nursery keeper tested it with an empty cup, then a full one, then a cup she had promised to a child.

It worked differently each time.

She asked for written instructions.

The power wrote them in large letters on a membrane that did not ripen:

**Ask before pouring. Keep the lid closed afterward.**

In the poorer streets, it accompanied the senior pruner.

They stopped at a house whose doorway had drawn its lips tightly together against the cold. A child watched them through a window.

The pruner asked what the family had been eating.

The power listened to the answer.

Later, a water gutter was extended to that block. The doorway kept its mouth. Its owners received enough damaged crop tissue to feed it without taking anything from their bowls.

The pruner retained his mark on the frame and returned the next morning.

***

En asked about the unfinished streets.

By then, people had begun to understand that the newcomer’s offer was larger than the work it had undertaken.

“Can you give them depth?”

“Yes.”

“All of them?”

“Yes.”

She looked pleased for a moment. Then she frowned.

“Would anyone still be on them?”

The power looked out along an avenue that could be seen to its far end.

“One person.”

En stood up.

They found him in a block the council had not yet named. Most of him lay across the pale surface beneath a low wall. A small portion of his coat extended into the adjacent street.

He had been there for three coolings.

His household had thought him at the pumping station. At the pumping station, they had thought him ill at home.

The power could have lifted him whole. Instead, it knelt beside En and showed her where his edge had folded underneath itself.

She fetched warm water.

While she worked, the power maintained a patch of shade and kept the paving from healing around him. En told the man about her buckle. She had finally repaired it, she said, but the repair made it difficult to open.

When he had enough thickness to speak, he asked to see it.

They brought him home before the next cooling.

Afterward, the power and the pruners worked out a way for an unfinished street to wrinkle slightly at its entrance when a foot approached. The wrinkle was small, but it could be felt through a sole.

The council wanted the streets completed as well.

“They will still need tending,” the power said.

“We know,” said En.

So the work began.

***

The central square was more difficult.

The power sat at its edge for a long time. Children came to watch, became bored, and went away.

Eventually one stayed.

“It’s putting that one there,” she said.

“Yes.”

“It keeps falling over.”

“Yes.”

The child considered this.

“You could hold it.”

“I could.”

“Why don’t you?”

“I don’t know whether that is where it wants it.”

The child crouched beside the power. Her sleeve slipped back, revealing the fine ridges at her wrist.

After a while she said, “It wants to try again.”

The power put one hand on the paving.

Beneath it, a chamber shifted. A narrow passage collapsed, formed again, and remained open for several strokes of the distant pump.

The child went to tell the nursery keeper that she was hungry.

***

At the next meeting, the power asked the council for permission to speak to what was growing beneath the square.

“How?” asked Sella.

“With its water ration. A small change in the intervals.”

“Will it still get the same amount?”

“Yes.”

The nursery keeper agreed. The surveyor requested a record of the intervals. The fifth councillor asked that it be done while he was awake.

The first question took an entire cooling.

The answer came through the pumping-station floor: three soft knocks, a pause, and a fourth.

The attendant called for his husband, who put down the shared cup and came through the doorway.

They listened together.

Over the following coolings, the power learned enough to report a few things.

The being beneath the square could feel the cold. It could feel footsteps, though it did not yet distinguish one walker from another. It had found the nursery’s warmth. Sometimes it tried to turn toward it and undid a portion of its own construction.

It did not know that the town was waiting for it to become something useful.

Sella sat very still when she heard this.

“Does it know we’re here?”

“It knows there is company.”

“Can you tell it who we are?”

“With time.”

The senior pruner set his wrapped shears on the table.

“And what does it need?”

“A little help keeping the chambers apart. Enough warmth to continue. Permission to stop, if it needs to.”

The council authorized the water and the warmth.

Permission to stop required longer discussion. Construction had already been delayed. Several households were waiting for rooms. The surveyor had spent a great deal of time on the possible façades.

In the end, the nursery keeper pointed out that an exhausted child could not be persuaded to develop by telling it about the beds required elsewhere.

The clerk entered the decision in a fresh pod.

***

The power remained through many coolings.

It held certain small chambers open while the being learned to hold them itself. When asked, it withdrew. Sometimes an entire cooling’s work came apart afterward.

Water continued to arrive beneath Sella’s cornice. The quantity was agreed upon each week, after the crop and the leaf had been examined. There was enough now for the washing of aprons. People still kept lids on their cups.

The woman from elsewhere spoke privately with the power.

No one asked what they discussed.

Afterward she continued working in the records office. Once, she took an old map home. She returned it the following morning with a name corrected in the margin.

***

The first opening in the square appeared beside the pumping-station gutter.

It was scarcely wider than a cup.

En arrived with binding membrane. The senior pruner listened at the edge. Sella brought warm water, then waited to be told whether it was wanted.

From beneath the paving came a faint current of air.

The opening closed.

The next cooling, it opened again.

This time the power sat nearby with the child who had wanted it to hold the falling chamber.

“Does it need feeding?” Sella asked.

“Not through there,” said the power.

“What is it for, then?”

They waited.

The aperture widened. Air moved through it, caught, and moved again. The sound was soft and uneven. Several of those present felt their own pulses shift toward it.

The senior pruner lowered himself onto the paving.

Sella set down the covered cup.

When the sound came a third time, she answered in her small voice.

“We’re here.”

Beneath the town, something rested.

For once, the warmth stayed where it was.
