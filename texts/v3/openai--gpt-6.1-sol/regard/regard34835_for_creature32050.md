The power notices the new trail before it notices the pump house.

It is a slight disturbance: dust pressed between two stones, a seed hull displaced, the edge of a moss pad brushed smooth by passing flanks. On one side of the building, six animals have ceased to come. On the other, their feet have begun making a way.

The woman has moved a potful of water. She has not moved the heat.

Below the platform, a fault is opening by less than the width of a hair each day. Steam has entered a layer of broken stone that used to carry only warm air. For the moment, the change is small. The northern pipe remains cool enough for lizards. The sleeping room is uncomfortable only toward morning.

In nine days, the heat will reach the spaces beneath the spare-parts locker.

The power does not close the fault.

Farther along it, beyond the basin, water gathers against a buried wall of basalt. To close one passage would send that water into another. There are roots there, and a hollow where birds shelter their young. The plateau is full of occupied places, including places nothing on the surface knows about.

For a while, the power attends.

It follows the warmth through the stone. It finds the woman’s old readings in the cupboard, their paper softened at the corners. It watches her rise from the sleeping shelf with one hand braced against the wall. It watches the torn-eared animal pull a strand of cotton into a passage beneath the platform.

At dawn, the woman takes the probe to the bend in the path.

This time she leaves it in place longer than usual.

The number rises, settles, then rises again.

She checks the probe against the instrument inside. She replaces its battery. She goes back outside and measures three other places.

By noon she has drawn a rough map on the back of a freight docket.

The power has done very little. It has kept a failing contact in the probe clean for one more morning.

That afternoon, the woman sends a message down the cable line.

She asks for an inspection, flexible pipe, and two extra pairs of hands. After looking at the map again, she adds a request for a portable condenser.

The answer arrives before sunset. Usually these things take longer.

Elsewhere, in an office whose windows look onto trees, a man reads her pressure records before deciding which shipment can wait. He has several reasons to put her request aside. None is a good reason. He puts it at the top instead.

The power leaves him his reasons.

—

The old female’s passage becomes warm during the following night.

She wakes, turns, and goes to the entrance. Behind her, two younger animals shift in their sleep. She returns far enough to nose one of them sharply, then leaves again.

There is another refuge near the abandoned trestle. She has used it before. The crossing is longer than the route to the pump house, and a strip of hot gravel lies between them.

During the afternoon, a narrow piece of stone falls from the weathered shoulder of a boulder.

It lands across the gravel.

The sound is small. The woman hears it while she is recording the pressure and looks through the doorway. Nothing else moves. She finishes the line she is writing.

By nightfall the fallen piece has cooled. Its underside makes a low passage, open at both ends.

The seam-runner finds it while searching along the edge of the hot ground. She crosses underneath, pauses beyond it, and continues toward the trestle.

Later she comes back.

The power does not guide her feet. It does not tell her where to live. There is now a way across, and she can examine it.

—

The workers arrive on the seventh day.

One is younger than the woman expected. The other has brought a folding stool without being asked. They spend the morning walking the basin with instruments, then sit in the pump house with the door open and compare their measurements.

The building can remain where it is for now. The intake cannot.

They disconnect the southern line, lay the flexible pipe around the changing ground, and mount the portable condenser beside an outcrop that stays shaded after noon.

While lifting a panel, the younger worker finds bedding.

He stops.

The woman brings the empty pressure-gauge housing from behind the cupboard. They put it beside the platform, under a board supported on two stones. They do not try to catch the animals.

For two nights, nothing uses it.

On the third morning, the cotton has gone.

The new condenser has a drain. Before fastening its outlet, the woman asks for it to be turned toward the outcrop.

“Not under the platform,” she says.

The water reaches a shallow groove in the rock. It runs for a short distance and sinks into grit. By evening, beetles have found it. A bird comes, lifts one foot, and waits.

The woman writes the new valve schedule inside the door.

Below it, in smaller letters, she writes the places that need watching.

—

There are losses.

A nest beneath the old platform is abandoned with two young still inside. The power knows them. It does not make their deaths smaller by attending to the whole basin.

Nor does it make the snake disappear.

The snake crosses beneath the fallen stone three nights after the seam-runners begin using it. Later, a bird hunts along the damp groove. The water serves lives that do not all spare one another.

The power remains long enough to see whether its changes have held.

The fault opens. The southern ground grows too hot to cross. The woman begins using the stool when she works on the lower valves. Her message results in a revised inspection interval, not only for her station but for the next two basins along the cable.

Beneath the abandoned trestle, the old female sleeps with her torn ear folded against her flank. There are other bodies in the passage. At dusk she will go out before them.

The power leaves the heat moving through its ancient channels. It leaves the woman her work, made less solitary. It leaves a cool crossing where there was none, and water within reach of shelter.

Under the outcrop, a drop lengthens at the mouth of the drain.

A small animal waits below it.

This time, while it waits, its feet are cool.
