The power noticed first the place where Mara’s left hand hurt.

Not the scar itself, but the small adjustment she made before lifting the bowl of washers: the turn of the wrist, the transfer of weight, the brief closing of her eyes when she thought no one was looking.

It noticed other things as well. The pump’s losses. The water held in the upper leaves. The load borne by the crushed sluice gate. The difference between a root growing toward warmth and one growing toward water, though both had entered the same sheath around a pipe.

It could have separated them all. It could have lifted the hollow, living and otherwise, into the air and laid every part down again in an arrangement without friction.

It did not.

There were reasons for the spoon beneath the dripping leaf that could not be learned merely by knowing where the water went.

It came down the path in the morning, while Mara was retrieving the cucumber.

She heard it stop behind her.

“If you’ve come about the electricity,” she said, “the switch is under the other roof.”

“I haven’t.”

She secured the cucumber between two stems. The snail had moved far enough that its shell was now hidden by the curve of the leaf.

“What, then?”

“I wondered whether there was something here you wanted help with.”

Mara straightened slowly. She looked at its hands, which were empty, and then at its feet.

“You’ll want different shoes.”

It left its shoes at the rim.

***

For three days it did very little that could have been called remarkable.

It held a lamp. It carried cuttings to the channel exit. When Mara asked for the smaller wrench, it brought the smaller wrench, although it knew the nut would shear if she turned it much farther.

“This one will break,” it said.

“I know. I’m trying to find out how badly.”

So it held the lamp closer.

It learned that she disliked anyone putting tools away while work was unfinished. It learned that the notes about water smell were sometimes more useful than the pressure readings. It learned that the pale swelling beneath the clamp was neither a fault nor an improvement yet. Mara wanted to watch it.

On the second afternoon she asked it to steady a pulley bracket while she packed a split with folded metal.

It held the bracket without shifting. For the first time in years, she could use both hands there.

“Like that,” she said. “Don’t correct it.”

It did not correct it.

On the third evening, as she stood with her foot in the puddle, it asked what would happen if she stayed in the village overnight.

“That depends on the night.”

“And for a week?”

She looked toward the shed.

“I haven’t.”

The valve groaned. Water slid away from her foot.

After a while she said, “There’s a room at my sister’s. She keeps saying.”

The power waited.

“I don’t need you to turn this into somewhere else,” Mara said.

“No.”

“And I don’t want it stopped.”

“No.”

She rubbed her left palm with her right thumb.

“I would like to go before her youngest has children of his own.”

***

They began with the bend beneath the flywheel.

Mara brought out eleven years of drawings. Some were in the ledger; some were on paper softened at the folds. One had been drawn on the inside of a biscuit label before the tin lost it.

The power could see a solution through the root crown. Mara could see why that solution would make the roof difficult to cut back in winter.

They made another.

For one morning, the power took the weight of the higher pulley and the kitchen roof. It bore them so quietly that Mara distrusted it.

“Still holding?”

“Still holding.”

She put her fingers against the crown, felt its ease, and began.

The old joint came free with a smell of oil and enclosed water. Behind it was a bolt her father had dropped and never found. She cleaned it on her trouser leg and set it aside.

They fitted the new pipe around the crown rather than through it. Its joints could be reached from the path. Beneath the lowest coupling, Mara left room for a bowl.

“It won’t leak,” the power said.

“Not now.”

It moved the coupling a little higher.

Afterward the pump’s sound changed. Mara stood beside it for nearly an hour, listening.

That night she wrote three pages.

***

The next work was less visible.

They made a second route for water to reach the rim. It used the slow filling of two stems Mara had watched for years but never clamped, because a split in either would empty the channel behind her room. The power showed her how to take only part of their motion. She devised the releases herself.

They made a float that would shut the evening valve before the clearing grew brittle. They made it broad enough to resist a root tip, and placed it where she could lift it out without cutting anything.

They opened the locker.

Inside were a coil of wire, four sound hinges, a jar of screws, and a coat that no longer fitted her. Mara sat down when she saw the coat. The power went to clean the hinges and did not ask.

Nothing was made permanent. There were covers to lift, joints to undo, openings large enough for hands. Beside each new device, Mara left a mark in yellow paint.

She also insisted on a way to turn it off.

***

Only once did the power offer something for her hand.

They were drinking tea beneath the parted leaves above the stove. She had dropped her cup that morning, though it had landed on a root and not broken.

“I can help the scar,” it said.

Mara laid her palm on the table.

“How much?”

“It would close properly. You would still feel pressure and heat. You would still need to rest it.”

“Would it look different?”

“A little.”

She considered this, then pushed back her sleeve.

The change took less time than the tea took to cool. There was no light. A slight warmth moved across her palm, and the tight white seam softened.

She flexed her fingers.

The power did not touch her knee until she asked.

***

A person from the village came down twice before Mara went away.

He had repaired bicycles and occasionally the mill pump. He was patient with instructions, but on the first visit he called the flattened vine belt a curious thing and reached to feel it.

“Not while it’s running,” Mara said sharply.

He drew his hand back.

On the second visit, he noticed the end of the metallic squeal.

“That sounded longer.”

Mara looked at him.

“Yes,” she said. “It did.”

The power paid him for his time. It left money for parts as well, in an ordinary account that Mara could use without asking anyone’s permission.

She took four days to write the instructions. Then she crossed out half of them.

“If you give him everything,” she said, “he won’t remember where to start.”

On the morning of her departure, she checked the channel entrance twice. She put the biscuits higher, wrapped a book, and stood a long time looking at the bed.

“I’ll be back Thursday.”

“Thursday,” the power said.

At the rim she changed into her good shoes. One sandal strap had begun to tear. She left it hanging beneath the basin anyway.

***

The power stayed until Thursday.

It did not make the hollow flourish beyond recognition. A roof leaf curled and was cut. The upper gauge misted over. The snail ignored a fresh piece of cucumber and continued along the underside of its leaf.

On Wednesday, the man from the village heard the squeal finish badly. He found a loosened roller, stopped the belt, and tightened it. In the ledger he wrote what he had done, pressing too hard with the pencil.

Mara returned carrying a bag of plums and a photograph her sister had made her take.

She descended without calling out. At the clearing she put down the bag and read the new entries. Then she went to the shed.

The pump churned, knocked, paused, and churned again.

She listened through several turns.

Later, while they ate, she described the room at her sister’s house. It had a window that opened outward. At night she had been able to hear someone washing dishes on the other side of the wall.

“First night I hardly slept,” she said.

“And the others?”

She reached for another plum.

***

Before leaving, the power repaired the torn sandal strap.

It could have made a pair that would never wear out. Instead it used a piece of belt from the locker, and left enough length for another adjustment.

Mara found them hanging beneath the basin the following morning.

Below, the man from the village was waiting beside the table. He had brought the wrong oil.

She held the tin, read its label, and explained the difference. Then she showed him where to stand when loosening a sleeve, and how to put his hand against the stem first.

The pale swelling was still there. Slightly larger now.

They bent over it together.
