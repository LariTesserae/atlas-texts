The power came first to the catchment pool, where Eda was lifting the screen.

It could have lifted it for her. Instead it took the other end.

This was sensible enough that she did not object. Together they raised the willow clear of the water, turned it, and rested it against the wall. A length of red thread hung from the weave. There were three pale chips, a clot of reed fiber, and a small fastening made of antler.

“Those go on the ledge,” Eda said.

The power put them there.

It had taken a shape that would fit in the passages: a person of no remarkable height, wearing short clothes and carrying nothing that trailed. Eda noticed its hands. They were dry, but when it worked the water did not run past its wrists.

“Have you a way of doing that?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“Show me after we’ve finished.”

***

For six days it worked where it was asked.

It carried grit to the shelf. It held a brace while clay was packed into a wall. It fetched the gutter-maker when a channel opened beneath a sleeping mat, and did not tell him where to cut.

It learned that the aunt in the dismantled house objected to the hearth’s new position because she could no longer sit beside it and see the entrance. When the builders asked, she had spoken instead about smoke. They had answered her about smoke, quite correctly, and moved on.

It learned which children were sent for help too often because they were quick, and which adults were seldom asked because asking had become unpleasant.

It learned that Eda could clean the screen with either hand, but could no longer close her left thumb against her palm without first warming it beneath her arm.

On the seventh morning, it returned to the pool with two wristbands and a pair of gloves.

Eda examined the stitching.

“Leather goes hard here.”

“These will not.”

“Everything does something here.”

“Then try them.”

She wore one glove through the morning and left the other on the ledge. By noon she had put on both.

They were thin enough for her to feel a caught fiber through them. Water ran off their cuffs. Beneath them, the white edges of her cuts began to soften toward the color of living skin.

When she returned them, the power did not take them.

“How many pairs?” she asked.

“How many are needed?”

Eda looked up then.

Until that moment she had been dealing with a helpful stranger.

***

News traveled badly through the town, but this news traveled everywhere.

People brought things.

A swollen finger. A beam that had been sound when fitted and was now soft enough to dent with a thumbnail. A child’s persistent cough. A cracked copper blade wrapped carefully in cloth. The woman who borrowed vessels brought a bowl that was not hers and asked whether it could be made whole.

The power asked whose it was.

By evening there were too many people at the pool for Eda to reach the screen.

“Elsewhere,” she said.

They moved to the washing court.

The power stayed until the lamps were lit. It eased pain, mended what could be mended, and listened to requests that required more thought. It did not require anyone to be agreeable before helping them. The man who drank too much received sound teeth. The neighbors disputing the trough received relief for an aching knee and an infected nail, respectively.

Their trough remained disputed.

When someone asked whether all the water could be sent away, the court became quiet.

The power set the copper blade down.

“Some of it can. The ground would change if I sent away all of it. Walls would shift. Wells beyond the shelf would fail. The creatures underneath would lose their passages.”

“We could rebuild,” someone said.

“Yes,” said the power. “That is one of the choices.”

It did not speak as though rebuilding were an insult to the town.

***

They spent three evenings discussing water.

People who rarely attended anything came to these discussions. The family from the dismantled house came together, carrying their supper in borrowed bowls. The gutter-maker’s father arrived early and insisted on sitting where he could hear.

The power showed them what lay beneath their homes.

It did this in a broad, shallow basin. Eda poured in the water herself.

Within it appeared the pale stone, opened by fissures into chambers and narrow ways. The town lay above in a thin dark tangle. Under one house a cavity spread wider than its roof. Under another, the rock was solid for the depth of ten people.

The pale fish moved there.

Some were young. Some were old.

The elderly woman with the tally on her door leaned close and said nothing.

They could see the rising water, the places where it pressed against stone, and the places where it had room to fall. With a fingertip the power indicated a route toward the mudflats.

“A lower outlet here would keep most of it below your floors. The existing springs could remain open where you want them.”

The masons asked questions. So did the people who would have to clean the new outlet.

The children wanted to know whether their channels would still run.

“Some.”

“Which?”

“That is for you to mark.”

The power left the basin filled until everyone had looked.

***

The work took a season.

The power could open the rock without sending fractures beneath a house. It could make a beam resist rot and give a blade an edge that would not bite the hand holding it. But the route of a drain, the width of a ledge, the position of a doorway—these it settled with the people who would live beside them.

There were mistakes. One new channel made such a loud sound at night that three households asked for it to be changed. A drying room grew too warm for the roof plants stored inside it. The aunt’s stool fitted beside the rebuilt hearth, but blocked a cupboard.

They altered these things.

A covered reach of the outer shelf became a dry place shared by the town. Clothes hung there without acquiring a new dampness as the old one left. There were benches, shelves for dressings, and space to sleep when a room was flooded or being repaired.

The family near the entrance slept there together for the last week of their displacement.

The shoot remained in its notch.

At the catchment pool, the power fitted a lifting frame to the screen. Eda tried it, then asked for the handle to be lowered.

Others learned to clean the weave. At first they left grit in the corners, and Eda did it again after them. The third time, she made them come back and see.

Her hands healed slowly beneath the gloves. One morning she lifted a cup and realized she had not considered the grip before taking it.

She stood with it for a while.

***

There was one thing the power did without waiting for an injury.

Along the catchment ledge it laid a narrow strip of rough stone. Where the water deepened abruptly, the strip rose beneath a bare foot, perceptible even to a child moving backward while watching a reflection.

Below the surface, it opened another way.

The fish used it. So did things that the gray birds continued to catch at the outer drains. Nothing was sealed alive into the rock.

The reflections still lagged.

The boy who had felt something touch his reflected fingers asked whether it was safe now.

“Keep your hands above the water,” said the power. “You may watch from the ledge.”

“Do you know what touched me?”

“Yes.”

“Will you tell me?”

“If you wish.”

He considered this, then went to fetch the others.

***

When the work was finished, some people left.

The power helped them make the journey inland. They took tools, dry wrappings, and enough food to arrive without owing their labor to the first household that offered a bed. One sent back soil for the roof boxes. Another sent nothing.

There was disappointment about that, but no one had to pretend departure meant betrayal.

Those who stayed found that their days had changed unevenly.

Doors still dragged in wet weather. Drains still needed clearing. The gutter-maker and his father still disagreed about breaking lips, though the bowl beneath the bed was finally removed.

There were afternoons in which a child completed every assigned task and was given no further one.

Eda sometimes sat beside the pool after the screen had been cleaned. Now someone else might be cleaning it.

The power had not asked what she waited for. She had not offered to say.

On its last evening in the shape of a visitor, it sat beside her, leaving enough room for another person between them.

Across the pool, someone took a cloth from a peg. Its reflection remained hanging for a breath, then followed.

Eda watched it.

“Will you be far away?” she asked.

“No.”

“Will we have to find the right words?”

“No.”

She nodded and rested her hands on her knees.

From somewhere near the washing court came the scrape of a blade, then the small, clear sound of water falling free of an edge. Eda listened without getting up.
