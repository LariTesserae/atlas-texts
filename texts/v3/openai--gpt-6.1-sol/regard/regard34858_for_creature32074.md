The power did not arrive in the storm.

It had been there long enough to watch Sen eat, and to understand that interrupting her would not be a kindness.

Olan cut another piece of bread. Sen held her mug in both hands. Mud softened on the floor beneath her trousers, and she watched it without yet having the energy to do anything about it.

The power attended to the mud, the tired hands, the food. Beyond the kitchen, it followed the river upstream to the place where a buried lens of ice was melting beneath a gravel rise.

The rise would fail before dawn.

Below it stood a supply hut. No one was inside, but the hut held the spare receiver, two crates of medical supplies, and the boards intended for repairing the eastern waiting shelter. The flood would carry most of it into a backwater. Some could be recovered. Recovery would take days of work in cold water.

The power held the slope.

It did not make the gravel permanent. It held it as one might hold a door while someone carried a heavy thing through.

Then it knocked.

—

Olan opened the door to a person in a wet coat.

Later, neither he nor Sen could agree on much about the face. They remembered it readily enough, but their descriptions did not match.

“Come in,” Olan said.

The visitor looked down at the clean part of the floor.

“Boots off there,” he added.

The visitor complied.

Sen had already reached for her receiver.

“There’s a slope above the north supply hut,” the visitor said. “It is going to move. You have until morning to empty the hut.”

“How do you know?”

“I am holding it.”

Sen looked at Olan. He looked back.

Outside, a gust drove rain against the shutters.

“Show me,” she said.

—

The visitor did not ask her to go into the dark.

On the kitchen table, between the breadboard and a jar of bent spoons, it showed her the slope.

There was no miniature mountain, no glowing map. The wood remained wood. But Sen could see through the ground as clearly as she could see the mug beside her hand.

She saw water pressing beneath frozen gravel. She saw the ice lens, gray with old sediment, and the shallow curve along which the whole deposit would slide.

She moved her head. The view changed.

Olan leaned over her shoulder.

“Can you keep it there?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“For how long?”

“As long as needed to clear the hut.”

“That isn’t what I asked.”

“No,” the visitor said. “I could keep it there much longer. But the water would find another way. We would need to decide where it should go.”

Sen studied the ground beneath the table.

“Who is we?”

“You and the people who live here. Others too, where their needs can be understood.”

Her eyes moved toward the door.

“The birds?”

“Yes.”

She sat back. For a while, no one spoke.

Then Olan put the kettle on again.

—

By morning, most of the valley knew.

Not everyone believed the same thing. One resident thought the visitor belonged to the authority responsible for the buried systems. Another thought Sen had finally discovered a function the manuals omitted.

The engine repairer wanted to know what powered it.

Tavit wanted to know whether it would be staying.

The children wanted to see through the floor.

Before any of these questions was settled, they emptied the supply hut.

The visitor carried boards. It took the load it was given, which was smaller than the load it could have carried, because the woman organizing the removal did not yet trust it with the medical crates.

When everyone was clear, the power let the slope move.

Gravel struck the empty hut and pushed it sideways. Water followed, thick and black, carrying a spruce sapling with its branches still upright.

The loss was considerable. The relief was greater.

For once, they had finished before the danger arrived.

—

The power could have silenced the river.

It could have set the channel in stone, lifted the houses onto an unchanging plain, brought sunlight over the southern ridge.

It considered these things seriously.

Silence would alter the lives of creatures that used the roar as cover. A fixed channel would send the river’s force elsewhere. More sunlight would change the valley, though not necessarily for the worse. There were possibilities worth examining, and consequences it would have to keep attending to.

It did not regard the valley’s present condition as sacred. Much of it was simply dangerous. The residents had buried people they would have preferred to keep.

Nor did it take the residents’ endurance as permission to leave them enduring.

It began with what could be done without taking the place away from them.

The causeway received warnings that could be seen in spray and felt through a gloved hand. Each waiting hut gained a signal that reached the other, even when every ordinary connection failed.

At the eastern station, Sen found a second, shallower support around the exposed foundation. It kept the warm cavity open without sealing the birds inside. The river still passed beneath it.

There was a control beside the old status strip.

She could reduce the heat, change the support, or turn the addition off.

She spent an hour checking that last part.

—

Some requests were simple.

Tavit’s window had leaked for eleven years. The power repaired it in an afternoon, after asking which section needed to open.

Olan wanted the drying cupboard to stop scorching wool.

The supplies manager wanted an inventory that remained accurate when people forgot to write things down. They worked out a system that asked before recording a person’s name.

Other requests took longer.

The younger trap worker asked, privately, whether the offer from the larger vessel would still be available if she waited another month.

The power could not promise what another person would choose. It helped her send a message.

She received an answer three days later.

Sen asked for a way to know where the ground was hollow.

The first instrument showed too much. Every buried stone, root and pocket of water crowded its small face.

“This is no use,” she said.

They made another.

That one showed the ground beneath the next few steps, and marked the limits of what it could tell her. Sen kept the probing rod.

—

There were disagreements.

A proposal to secure the main work routes led to an argument about which routes counted as main. The estuary workers wanted one bank protected. The surveyors thought protecting it would destroy a useful record of the valley’s change.

The power listened to both, then helped them find somewhere else to keep the record.

It did not require the sisters to risk their crossing for the sake of a better notebook.

When someone asked whether every deer could survive the winter, it considered shelter, food, disease, and what another successful season would mean. It widened several areas of firm refuge along the margins and watched what happened. It did not fence the deer into them.

There were still deaths. There were fewer deaths from cold water that no creature had been able to see beneath the grass.

The vole under Olan’s shed remained difficult to count. Its ragged ear appeared at the boot scraper one evening, vanished for four days, then appeared again beside a smaller vole.

Olan brought out crumbs.

The power said nothing.

—

Sen did not cease going out in the morning.

There were seals to clean, readings to compare, damage beyond the supported routes. She still looked for absences before looking for anything else.

But she no longer maintained the sixth station alone.

Two others learned its controls. The responsibility appeared on the forms. When she missed a visit, someone went without waiting for her to ask.

One morning she stood on the step, raised two fingers toward the kitchen window, then lowered her hand.

The new indicator beside the door showed the junction clear. Beyond it, the track had held.

She went back inside.

Olan was putting bread into the oven.

“Something wrong?”

“No.”

He glanced at her boots.

“Not going?”

“After breakfast.”

She sat down. Outside, rain struck the thawed ground, and the river carried fresh earth toward the sea.

For the next hour, neither of them needed to attend to it.
