For a long time, the power did nothing that could be distinguished from attention.

It listened to the water carriers coming down through the crack. The vessels made different sounds when full and when nearly empty. It watched where a foot was placed before the weight came onto it, and where, despite a lifetime of practice, a foot sometimes slipped.

It followed the roots beneath the houses.

There was water deep in the mountain, but not a river waiting to be released. There were damp fractures, cold pockets, a slow migration through stone. Some of that moisture sustained the old junipers. Some emerged on a distant face of the cliff where small birds drank. Some would not reach daylight for another hundred years.

The power could have broken the mountain open. It could have set a lake upon the plateau.

It considered the roofs below, the weight of saturated soil, the clay packed between stones. It considered what happened when goats found a new thing before people did.

Then it went to the woman who repaired the water vessels.

She was working in the interval of sideways sunlight, pressing warm resin into a seam. One hand steadied the pot between her knees. The other had become stiff enough that she sometimes needed to open its fingers with her good hand.

The power stood where it would not block her light.

She looked up.

“If you’ve brought one,” she said, “put it with the others.”

“I haven’t.”

“Then don’t stand on the onions.”

It moved its foot.

She finished the seam before asking what it wanted.

“To make the carrying easier.”

“Good,” she said. “Take those two up in the morning.”

It did.

It carried the empty vessels to the plateau and brought them back full. It waited its turn at the cleft. On the descent, it stepped aside for a boy leading a goat, though there was scarcely anywhere to step.

By evening, several people had opinions about it.

It was strong. It was peculiar. It had asked too many questions about the old channels. It had returned the vessels to the wrong house, which was understandable because the woman who owned them had lent them to her cousin, but it ought to have asked.

On the third day, the vessel-mender said, “You meant something else.”

“Yes.”

“Tell me, then.”

It told her there was a way to bring a little of the plateau seep down through the mountain. Not all of it. Enough that the daily loads would be fewer, though snow would still have to be stored and the upper chambers kept sound.

She set down her work.

“Through whose house?”

“No house, if the old channel goes where I believe it does.”

“And if it doesn’t?”

“Then we will find another way, or leave it.”

“Who is we?”

That took longer to answer.

By the next evening, there were eleven people in her courtyard and four listening from places they supposed were less obvious. The old woman who remembered a wider passage came. So did her brother, ready to dispute it. A man whose planting beds lay below the oldest house brought a piece of charcoal and a flat stone to draw on.

The power did not explain the whole mountain at once. It showed them the part they needed to consider.

Under the oldest house, beyond a filled-in store recess, the ancient channel bent inward. A narrow branch rose toward the seep. It had been closed by fallen stone long before anyone had built on the ledge.

They argued over where the water should emerge.

Nobody wanted strangers queuing against a bedroom wall. Nobody wanted the public basin beside the dung heap. Three people proposed their own courtyards and gave reasons that sounded public-spirited until someone else examined them.

The girl who knew the latches said there was a place beside the lower stair where everyone already stopped to change a load from one shoulder to the other.

They went to look.

A juniper root crossed it.

They chose somewhere else.

Before any stone was moved, the power asked permission of the people who slept over the channel. One refused. She had heard promises about repairs before; the dust always came into her room, and the men always said they would return to finish.

The power asked what would make the work acceptable.

“Do it after the grain is taken out. And put the floor back the same day.”

It did.

There was no thunder. The mountain did not shine. A few obstructing stones loosened into waiting hands, and the workers found that their tools stayed sharp. The vessel-mender’s stiff fingers remained stiff, but when she reached for a pot, she could close them without pain.

She noticed. She said nothing until they were alone.

“Is that you?”

“Yes.”

“Will it last?”

“I can make it last.”

She looked at her hand for a while.

“Do that, then.”

The first water arrived brown with old dust.

They let it run into a bucket and carried the bucket to the thorn bushes. The next was clearer. By sunset, a narrow thread fell from a stone lip into a covered basin.

It was not enough to be careless with.

It was enough that an old man did not have to climb the crack that week. Enough that a woman could wash the blanket under her sick husband without calculating what the washing would cost the drinking jars. Enough that, after a cup was spilled, the room stayed quiet.

People touched it. They smelled it. Someone went up to inspect the seep, suspicious that the upper storage had simply been emptied.

The power went with him.

It showed him the little gate that could stop the flow, and the place where grit would collect, and the signs that meant the channel needed attention. Two other people learned these things afterward. Then a child insisted on learning them too.

When the oldest doe managed to lift the basin cover, they changed the latch.

The power left several other things alone.

It did not straighten every stair. Some unevenness was harmless, and a village could become unfamiliar through too much improvement. But it steadied the loose top course of the wall the children were forbidden to climb, and told their parents exactly what it had done.

It did not widen the narrow passage by the old woman’s house. Her brother was disappointed; he had hoped the work would settle their argument.

It did not make every shrub fruitful or every quarrel fair.

Once, at someone’s request, it sat in a courtyard while three households argued about a shared root. It listened until they had stopped explaining themselves to it and begun speaking to one another. When they asked which of them was right, it showed them where the root actually ran. This complicated matters, but usefully.

There were other requests, made quietly.

A swelling examined. A missing goat found below a broken path. News carried to a daughter in the valleys. A night’s sleep for someone who had not slept properly since a death.

Sometimes the power could do what was asked. Sometimes it could only stay.

It did not require a shrine.

When a household put a cup for it in a niche, it thanked them. When another household declined to do so, it continued helping them.

Toward the end of that season, the young man who watched the unseen route beyond the clouds came to it.

He wanted to leave. He also wanted his mother not to need him, which was harder.

The power did not choose for him. It went with them both to the water basin, where his mother tried the handle of a full vessel, lifted it, and set it down again.

“I can manage this,” she said.

He left a week later with two cheeses, a repaired knife, and considerably more advice than he could remember.

The settlement remained crowded. Smoke still gathered under the overhang. Goats still occupied doorways at inconvenient moments. In dry years, people still climbed to the snow hollows and came down carefully.

But on some afternoons there was a little less carrying.

The woman who had dreamed of a river town began sitting by the basin while she sorted herbs. The sound was nothing like a river. She knew that better than anyone.

Still, when somebody offered to put a strip of felt beneath the falling thread to quiet it, she said no.

At evening the people took their work to the parapet. One had brought out her red cloth for no occasion anyone else could identify. The old man held his needle far from his eyes. The sisters under the juniper had acquired a door made from a grain sack.

The power sat at the end of the stone bench, leaving room for the baskets.

For once, the vessel-mender had brought no work.

She opened and closed her hand in the warmth, then rested it on her knee. Below them the clouds moved slowly across the hidden roads. Behind them, someone drew water, fastened the cover, and went home.
