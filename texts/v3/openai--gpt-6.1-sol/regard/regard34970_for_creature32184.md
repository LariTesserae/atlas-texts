The power first notices the space where the threshold has been.

It is not an impressive space. There is a pale rectangle, a scattering of grit, and two deeper marks where the wedges entered. Beyond it, the mason’s daughter is rubbing the heel of her hand. Her father has gone to fetch a bowl of water, although she has said she does not need one.

The power could put the stone back. It could restore the doorway, straighten the floor, return the house to the shape it had when the mason was a child.

It follows the weight downward before doing anything.

Below the house is a collapsed washing room. Below that, a channel runs through several older walls. Farther down, water is easing grains of limestone out of a seam. The slight movement at the threshold belongs to all these things. Holding it still would send weight elsewhere. Holding everything still would interrupt the water, seal away new feeding surfaces, and leave the city resting forever on the decision of someone who had only just arrived.

The power lets the house move.

It does not let the daughter’s hand go unnoticed.

When she washes it, a little grain of stone comes free from beneath the skin. The soreness diminishes. She flexes her fingers, examines the water, and assumes her father has found the grain.

He takes credit without quite saying so.

***

For several nights, the power listens.

It hears the watchers beneath the drips. They make small noises to keep themselves awake: a tongue against a tooth, a fingernail along a jar’s rim. One woman has learned to sleep between drops. She wakes when the interval changes, adjusts a vessel, and closes her eyes again.

There is a boy beside her, old enough to work and young enough that his head still tips heavily when he falls asleep.

The woman catches him before his forehead strikes the rim.

The power stays beside them until morning. It holds the boy’s head clear of the jar. It keeps the woman warm enough that she can feel her fingers.

Then it considers how much of their work could be spared.

The next evening, someone is sitting in the unused watcher’s place.

The newcomer wears a pale coat. There is nothing strange about the face except that neither the woman nor the boy can remember it afterward. They remember its attention.

“Whose water are you watching?” the woman asks.

“No one’s yet.”

She tells it which jars not to touch.

It obeys. When a drip begins to stray outside its vessel, it calls her over.

“You’ll have to learn that without calling me each time,” she says.

“Yes,” says the power.

By morning it has learned the jars, the seams, the ownership marks, and the hollow notes. It has also learned that the woman has been owed a replacement coat for two cold seasons.

It asks what would allow her to sleep at home.

She laughs once, thinking this is a proposal.

Then she sees that the question is serious.

***

The first device is ugly.

It consists of three fired-clay pieces, a bone pivot, and a narrow trough. When the jar fills, its weight turns the trough toward the next vessel. A shallow cup catches the wandering drip and directs it downward through a joint that can be adjusted by hand.

The woman distrusts the joint. The boy distrusts the pivot. Both are right.

The power makes another.

A potter improves the shape of the cup. A pool worker points out where mineral crust will gather. The watcher insists that every part must come apart without requiring anyone to put a hand beneath a full jar.

For six nights she sits beside the device. On the seventh, she leaves for the time it takes to eat something hot.

There are difficulties. Water owners ask whether the contrivance belongs to the person who made it or the person who owns the drip. Watchers ask what becomes of wages when watching is no longer needed every night.

The power does not answer with a rule delivered from above the ceiling.

It asks the watchers to describe their work in the presence of those who pay for it. This takes longer than anyone expects.

There is cleaning, mending, carrying, listening. There is knowing which seam will divide in cold weather. There is the difference between a vessel that looks sound and one that can be trusted through the night.

The new troughs remove some of the waking hours. They do not remove the need for that knowledge.

Not every agreement is fair. But the woman obtains her coat, and payment for teaching three households how to maintain their joints. The boy is no longer required to sit up beside her.

Copies of the device begin appearing elsewhere. Some are better than the first. The power is pleased to have its version dismantled for parts.

***

There are harms for which consultation would come too late.

One afternoon, a roof slab breaks above a room where two children are sorting needles.

The slab stops a hand’s breadth below its supports.

Dust falls. One child drops the needle case. The other calls for her mother.

The power holds the stone until they are out, then lowers it to the floor. It is still there when the masons arrive: a great pale weight laid down so gently that only one needle has bent.

Afterward, people begin calling to the power.

They call from dangerous rooms and disputed doorways. They call because a grazer is trapped, because a child is feverish, because someone wants a wall held in place while its stones are taken apart. Sometimes they call merely to find out whether anyone will answer.

The power answers when it can help.

It gives no punishment for mistaken fear.

But it cannot make every falling thing wait for someone to notice it. So, with the people who already read the ground, it makes another kind of warning.

Before a dangerous movement, a low vibration passes through the threatened stone. Bowls hum. A hand laid against a wall feels a steady trembling unlike the irregular movements of settling.

The mason tries it first.

“Too much like a loaded cart passing,” he says.

The power changes it.

His daughter asks that the warning reach sleeping people. A woman who cannot hear asks for it through the floor. A herder asks whether the animals will panic.

They test it in an empty court, with sleeping mats, vessels, a loose wall, and three increasingly irritated grazers.

Eventually they find a warning that wakes without startling, and persists until the danger has passed.

It tells people to move. It does not choose where they must go.

***

The power works beneath the city as well.

It opens no great reservoir. Sudden abundance can be dangerous in a place whose floors dissolve.

Instead, it brings water slowly to several dry margins, clear and drinkable, in amounts the channels can carry. It asks the pool workers where the outlets should be. It keeps the water apart from the old mineral pools, where small creatures require precisely what is already there.

The first outlet is low enough for a child to reach.

No household receives the right to close it.

There are arguments about that too. The power remains through them. It will listen to objections; it will help build a channel that does not wet someone’s sleeping room. But it will not take the water away from a thirsty person to preserve the value of another person’s drip.

People learn this distinction about it.

They also learn that it will warm a cooking stone without flame, but will not tell them what to cook. It will heal a cracked lung, but does not treat grief as an illness. It will go with someone to an inaccessible burial chamber, if they wish, and carry them home afterward.

***

One evening it returns to the mason’s house.

The threshold is lying along an inside wall. His daughter has placed her open household bag on it. A needle case, cord, and folded drinking skin occupy the shallow hollow worn by generations of feet.

The mason is sitting near the doorway, his hand on the floor.

“Is it going to move?” he asks.

“A little tonight.”

He waits.

“No need to leave,” the power says.

He nods, but keeps his hand where it is.

After a while his daughter comes in carrying three bowls. She looks at the stranger, then at her father.

“Is this the one?”

“Yes.”

She hands it a bowl.

The power sits where she indicates, clear of the passage. Outside, a herd is feeding on stone exposed that morning. Somewhere beyond the court, a jar fills and a small trough turns toward the next.

The mason lifts his hand from the floor to take his supper.
