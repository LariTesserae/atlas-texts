The power did not stop the pump.

It had heard the pump from every street, and beneath its noise it had heard what the noise was keeping out. It knew the weight of the water beyond the gate and the softness of the ground below Mara’s bed. Silence, given carelessly, would not be a kindness here.

Nor did it send a breeze through the district at once. There were things in the ditch that ought not to be lifted into the air. There were loose sheets on the roofs, and pigeon nests tucked against them.

For a while it did nothing that anyone could notice.

It attended to the district as closely as Mara attended to a changed vibration: separating one thing from another, not assuming that everything unpleasant had the same cause.

Then, on a morning when Mara was scraping a clot of plastic from the intake screen, someone waited outside the pump-house door.

They wore ordinary clothes and stood clear of the hose. When Mara came out, they asked whether there was somewhere they could talk.

“Not in there,” she said. “And not till I’ve washed.”

They waited.

At the table beneath the residential eaves, the visitor sat facing her, so that she would not have to turn her better ear toward them. They had brought breakfast, but asked before opening the parcels.

“What is it you want?” Mara said.

“To help.”

“With what?”

“That is partly what I came to ask.”

She looked at them for a moment.

“The spare impeller,” she said. “If you can get that out of stores, you’ll have done more than the last three people.”

The visitor nodded.

“And somebody on nights. Somebody trained. Not a driver with a telephone.”

Another nod.

“You’re writing none of this down.”

“I’ll remember.”

Mara had heard that before. She opened her breakfast.

—

The impeller arrived on Thursday.

It was the correct one. Mara checked the number twice. There was also a gasket set, which nobody had mentioned at breakfast but which would have been needed as soon as the old housing was opened.

Nothing was installed by an invisible hand. The engineer came, and this time there was money for him to stay until the work was finished. He put his case on a dry bench. The second motor carried the load while the first was taken apart.

Mara watched the worn components emerge.

“That would have gone,” she said.

“Yes,” said the engineer.

He did not tell her that she had been lucky.

The new night worker arrived the following week. She was a woman from another pumping station, familiar with the machinery but not with this district. Mara showed her the readings, the screens, the float switch, the troublesome latch.

At the handrail she stopped.

“Put your hand here.”

The woman did.

“That’s normal. You’ll learn it.”

They worked together for a month. Neither was expected to train the other while doing a full shift’s work. There were hours set aside, and those hours were paid.

Mara complained about some of the arrangements. The visitor returned, listened, and changed two of them.

—

There were limits the power did not intend to leave in place merely because people had learned to live within them.

The cooling water ceased entering the open ditch warm enough to hold the heat against the rooms. It was taken through a closed return, with access panels large enough for a person to reach the joints.

The machinery acquired housings that reduced its noise without concealing its faults. Inside the pump house, gauges remained visible. Warnings could be heard and seen. Outside, for the first time, a conversation could continue through the compressor’s release.

No one was asked to trust that quiet meant safety.

The residential roofs were repaired and insulated. Screens were fitted properly. Fans drew from the cooler side of the buildings rather than moving the same hot air from one room to another. A covered drying place was made where laundry could actually dry, not merely remain out of the rain.

When the first shirt came off the line dry at the seams, Mara held it against her cheek. Then she folded it.

There was a clinic visit on paid time. Her cuts were treated. Her hearing was tested. She was offered something to help with it, and allowed to try it without committing herself.

The power did not restore her to an earlier age. It did not think the years she had lived were an error.

It did make sure she no longer had to purchase care by giving up a day’s wages.

—

The animals were not forgotten, though the power did not confuse kindness with leaving every creature exactly where it was.

Gaps into the food stores were sealed. Rats were excluded from the workers’ rooms. Waste was collected before it became food for them. Standing water that served no purpose was drained, and the ditch was made less hospitable to mosquitoes without poisoning everything that moved in it.

There were still frogs.

The pigeons lost some of their ledges above the machinery and gained sheltered places farther from the drains. They objected loudly to the change.

The fig behind Mara’s room was given a proper container, deep enough for its roots, with an outlet that would not leave them standing in sour water. Mara watched it being moved and told the workers twice which branch she wanted kept.

The gecko stayed behind the cupboard.

One evening, after the room had become cooler, it did not appear beside the light switch. Mara looked behind the cupboard with a torch.

It was there.

“Suit yourself,” she said.

—

The room near her daughter remained available.

The power had not arranged for Mara to be evicted, promoted out of reach of her neighbors, or suddenly seized by a longing for the inland courtyard. It had not made the district unbearable in order to guide her toward a better life.

It made the choices less unequal.

Her years of work were accounted for. The money due to her was paid. Her pension no longer depended on staying beside the pump until she could no longer hear it. A move, if she wanted one, would not mean losing her claim to either.

She went to look at the upstairs room.

Her daughter showed her where the sun fell in the morning. They opened the window. From below came the sound of someone shaking out a mat.

Mara asked about the roof again. She tried the taps. She sat on the edge of the bed.

She stayed two nights.

On the second night she woke and listened. Nothing was wrong. There was simply no machinery close enough to feel through the floor.

In the morning she told her daughter she would think about it.

Her daughter, who had been trying not to ask, said, “All right.”

—

When the visitor next came, Mara was eating beneath the eaves. The fig had put out three new leaves.

“Well?” they asked.

“Well what?”

“Have we missed anything?”

Mara considered.

“The new fellow in the freight office still thinks he can put a pallet over an inspection cover.”

“I meant for you.”

“That is for me. I’m the one who has to get it moved.”

The visitor smiled, then asked about the upstairs room.

“Still thinking.”

“There is time.”

She looked at them sharply, because people often said that when there was not.

This time there was.

Later she went to bed in the end room, with both feet beneath the sheet. Across the court a trained woman was making the night inspection. The pump continued, quieter now, carrying water safely out of the basin.

Mara woke once.

She listened, recognized the ordinary sequence, and waited for the familiar urge to get up.

It passed.

She turned toward the cooler part of the mattress and went back to sleep.
