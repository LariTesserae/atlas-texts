They are the K’tarrans. Not settlers. Not survivors. *Participants*.  

To see one is to witness geology made gentle. Their bodies speak the language of the Uplift without uttering a word. Skin carries the faint ochre stain of iron-rich water, the silver sheen of pumice-dust rubbed into palms to grip slick vines. Fingertips are mapped with fine scars—testaments to basalt’s sharp honesty, to weaving reeds that resist without malice. Their posture is never rigid; it sways with the bridges, yields to the water’s push, breathes with the humidity. Bare feet know the walkway’s tremor as a mother knows a child’s pulse: the slight shudder where a new root has gripped basalt, the hollow resonance near a steaming vent, the firm certainty of a rock-spine that has earned trust. Eyes hold the soft focus of those who read light *within* water—the coppery pulse in deep troughs, the shift in silt when a sky-ray passes. They do not squint against glare; they lean into luminescence.  

Their voices are woven from the place itself. Speech is low, syllables shaped by the *shush* of water, the *plink* of weepwillow tears. A greeting is often silent: a palm pressed flat against a humming basalt column, then offered to another’s palm—a transfer of vibration, of memory. Children learn first to listen: to the chime of air-bladder reeds signaling a shift in current, to the thrum in the water that means the western spire has breathed deeper. Laughter is rare and precious, a soft ripple that startles even the speaker, quickly softened by a hand over the mouth—not from shame, but reverence. To break the silence carelessly is to fray the covenant.  

They move as extensions of the ecosystem. A fisher does not *cast* a net but *offers* it—a lattice of kelp-fiber and sinew lowered where the water glows warmest, waiting for the blind shrimp to bless it with presence. A weaver selects reeds not by strength alone but by song: which stalk hums truest when tapped against stone. Elders sit for hours where thermal veins kiss the sea, palms submerged, feeling the slow pulse of the Uplift’s dreams. They do not speak of "resources." They speak of *relationships*. The sky-kelp is "breathing stone." The weepwillow is "the sky’s tear made root." The obsidian ribs below are "the world’s bones, still warm."  

Their rituals are quiet revolutions. At dawn, when the cloud-ceiling lifts a hand’s breadth, a single note is sung into the central fire pit—a sound that travels down through the basalt spire, a question sent into the deep. When a new rock spine breaches the surface, the entire village gathers not with tools, but with open hands. They place offerings at its base: a coil of vine, a cluster of mist-blooms, a clay cup filled with condensation. They wait. Days later, if moss has begun to cling, if kelp-fronds have curled around its edge, they whisper, *"It accepts us."* Grief is returned to the water: a body wrapped in sky-kelp, weighted with cooled volcanic glass, released where the copper-light pulses strongest. Joy is planted: a child’s first solo crossing of a vine bridge is marked by tying a strand of their hair to the weepwillow at the path’s end—a thread of belonging.  

They share this world with quiet kin. They leave hollowed gourds of mineral salts near steam vents for the blind shrimp. They steer coracles wide around the slow-drifting shadow of the sky-ray, murmuring *"Elder of the deep plains"* as it passes. They know the mist-blooms will close if voices rise too sharp; they know the air-bladder reeds chime differently before a thermal shift. They do not tame. They *attune*.  

There are no leaders here, only listeners. The one who notices the subtle thinning of a kelp-mat speaks. The one who feels the new hum in the eastern spire is followed. Wisdom is not age but attention. A child who points to a ripple-pattern no elder saw becomes, for that moment, the village’s eyes. Their stories are not of heroes conquering landscapes, but of reciprocity: how the weepwillow’s tears nourish the sea that lifts the kelp that shelters the shrimp that stirs the silt that feeds the willow. Their oldest myth—the Dry Time—is told not as warning, but as compassion. *"Imagine a world that forgets to hold you,"* the elders murmur. *"How lonely it must have been."*  

To stand among them is to feel the self soften at the edges. You notice your own breath syncing with the water’s lap. You catch yourself pausing to press a palm to stone, feeling for the hum. You understand why they never say *"my home"* but *"the cradle."* They are not keeping the Uplift alive. The Uplift is keeping *them* alive—and they, in turn, keep its story alive through reverence. Every mended net, every whispered observation, every silent offering is a stitch in the fabric of becoming.  

They are the gentle witnesses. The patient weavers. The living answer to the Uplift’s first question: *Will you listen?*  
And in their quiet, steadfast presence—in the way a grandmother’s hand guides a grandchild’s fingers to feel the vibration of creation in a newborn rock—they have been answering, breath by breath, since the first stone rose from the deep:  
*Yes. We are here. We are listening.*  
They are not inhabitants of the Sky-Sea.  
They are its voice, learning to speak.
