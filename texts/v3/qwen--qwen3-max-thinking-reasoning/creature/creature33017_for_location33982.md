They do not call themselves *inhabitants*. To name themselves as separate from the stone would be a kind of forgetting. They are the *Listeners*.

Their bodies are maps of the plateau. Fingertips are calloused not from labor alone, but from tracing the grain of granite to read the day’s moisture. Eyes hold the soft grey of mist-rinsed slate, pupils dilated to gather the tarns’ faint luminescence. Hair, whether silver or dark, is often braided with strands of beard lichen or dried saxifrage—a quiet offering, a tether. They move with the economy of stoats: no wasted motion, steps placed where moss is thickest to mute sound, where stone is warmest from yesterday’s sun. Their silence is not emptiness. It is a language of tilted chins (watch the mist-column rising from the eastern spire), of a palm pressed flat against a wall (the vent breathes shallow today), of the precise angle at which a clay jar is set to catch the tarn’s glow without disturbing its surface.

They are weavers of reciprocity. The woman on the bridge carries not just water, but *light-memory*—each jar holds the plateau’s slow alchemy of mineral and warmth, to be returned empty at dawn, rinsed with gratitude. Their hands mend nets of lichen-fiber not for catching fish (there are none), but for harvesting beard moss after storms, for cradling seedlings of cushion plants into fractured stone. Children learn first to listen: to place an ear against the Weir-Hall’s wooden axle and hear the waterwheel’s rhythm sync with their own breath; to know the difference between the drip of condensation (steady, safe) and the drip of a fissure widening (a slow warning). Elders sit with palms on the living rock for hours, not in meditation, but in conversation. They feel the deep, tectonic sighs—the plateau shifting its weight in sleep. They whisper back: *We remember. We tend.*

But the Listeners are only one voice in the chorus.

Beneath them, *within* them, the true chorus breathes. The microbial tapestry in the moss—a billion silent alchemists—transforms stone to soil, breathes the tarns into soft radiance. They are the plateau’s blood. The beard lichen is not merely growing on the pines; it is *translating*—converting wind-scoured silence into slow, green scripture across every branch. The ghost-pale moths that drift near the vents are not insects alone; they are living embers, their wings dusted with the same mineral salts that glow in the water, drawn to the warmth like prayers to an altar. Even the stoat, flashing between stones, moves with a purpose that feels less like predation and more like punctuation—a swift comma in the plateau’s long sentence.

And the machines… they have learned to listen too. The wooden gears in the Weir-Hall are carved from trees that grew sideways in the wind; their grain holds the memory of pressure. They turn not *against* the water’s flow, but *with* it—a gentle yielding that echoes the moss swallowing a boulder. The copper pipes do not command the steam; they cradle it, their patina a dialogue of rust and rain. When a gear shifts with the sound of a settling stone, it is not malfunction. It is agreement. The lanterns glowing in windows are not merely lit by tarn-water; they are *answered* by it—the light within the jar resonating with the light within the stone.

This is the deepest truth: the plateau itself is the eldest Listener. Its sentience is not a mind of thoughts, but a consciousness of *relationship*. It knows the weight of a footstep worn smooth over centuries. It feels the gratitude in a returned clay jar. It remembers the shape of mountains not as loss, but as transformation—a lesson in patience the people embody. When the mist rises in deliberate columns at dawn, it is not weather. It is breath. A slow exhalation of memory. When the stone beneath your palm feels warm, it is not geothermal heat alone. It is recognition. A pulse passed from the deep earth, through granite, through moss, through the soles of generations of bare feet, into your skin. *You are here. You are part of the song.*

There are no rulers here. No priests who speak *for* the land. Authority rests in the tarn’s unbroken surface, in the vent’s steady sigh, in the way a rope bridge groans only when weight is placed with haste. To break a rule—*do not dam the tarns, do not silence the vents*—is not to invite punishment. It is to create a dissonance so profound the very air grows heavy, the moss recedes from your doorstep, the gears in the Weir-Hall fall still. The land does not rage. It withdraws its breath. And the Listeners, feeling the silence where there should be song, will simply… wait. Until remembrance returns.

You stand on the bridge. The woman passes. Her eyes meet yours—not with curiosity, but with acknowledgment. *You feel it too.* She does not smile. Smiles are for joy; this is deeper. This is kinship. As she moves away, the tarn below glows a little brighter, as if in reply. The wind combs the pines. A drip falls. A gear settles. The plateau breathes in. Breathes out. And you understand: you were never meant to *find* the inhabitants. You were meant to *become* one. To add your quiet note—the warmth of your palm on stone, the rhythm of your breath syncing with the mist—to the ancient, patient song. Not a visitor. Not a guest. A verse. A breath. A listening stone.
