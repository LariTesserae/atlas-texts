They are not a people who arrived. They are a people who *answered*.

You see it first in the hands. Not large, not brute-strong, but precise—knuckles mapped with fine white scars from stone and ice, fingers that move with the quiet certainty of a loom shuttle. The woman passing you does not smile, but her eyes hold yours for a breath longer than courtesy requires: a recognition that you, too, are breathing this thin air, that you have felt the plateau’s teeth. Her basket holds not just moss, but *specific* moss—*Hylocomium splendens* from the north-facing clefts, gathered at dawn when the frost-glaze is thinnest, for poulticing wounds. Her boots, wrapped in smoked goat-hide, leave no mark on the stone. She has learned the language of silence so deeply it has reshaped her bones.

They call themselves the *K’tal*—a word that means both “the listening ones” and “the stone-rooted.” Their faces are landscapes. Deep grooves bracket mouths that rarely open wide; eyes the color of weathered slate, narrowed against glare and wind, hold a stillness that is not emptiness but *reception*. They do not speak of “conquering” the cold. They speak of *negotiating* with it. A child learning to carve a soapstone lamp is taught: *The stone will tell you where it wishes to be hollowed. Press too hard, and it fractures. Press with patience, and it yields light.* Their bodies are compact, dense with slow-burning heat. Lungs expanded over generations to draw sustenance from the thin air. Hair, often the color of granite or storm-clouds, is braided tightly with sinew and threads of silver saxifrage root—not for ornament, but to keep it from catching on rock edges. They move with a grounded economy: no wasted gesture, no hurried step. To rush is to invite a fall; to fall is to become part of the scree.

But to describe only the K’tal is to miss the symphony. Life here is a woven thing. The thick-furred mountain goats—*Aq’var*, “cloud-walkers”—are not livestock but kin. They choose their own paths along the ledges, their bone bells a soft counterpoint to the wind. A herder does not drive them; she walks *beside*, her hand occasionally resting on a withers, reading the tension in the muscle that says *ice here, avoid*. The gyrfalcon circling above is *S’krel*, “sky-eye.” When it dives, the city pauses. A successful hunt means meat for the falconer’s family; a missed strike is met with no frustration, only a murmured note added to the day’s tally of wind and prey. The cats—small, wild-eyed, tails thick as rope—are not pets. They are *K’tal-ven*, “cliff-keepers.” They tolerate human proximity for the warmth of grain stores and the respect shown to their kills. A child who startles a cat is gently corrected: *You broke the silence it was listening with.*

Even the smallest lives hold weight. The springtails skittering in the steam-vents are *Tik’ka*, “first dancers.” Elders point to their frantic movement before a storm: *See how they leap? The mountain breathes shallowly. Prepare.* The rock ptarmigan’s seasonal molt—from stone-gray to snow-white—is the calendar by which planting (of moss-beds in sheltered niches) and harvesting are timed. Nothing is beneath notice. Nothing is separate.

Their culture is etched not in scrolls, but in action.  
- *Water is memory*: An elder dips a cupped hand into the stone channel, drinks slowly, then pours the last drop back with a whispered phrase: *“You return to the deep, and we remember you.”* Children practice this ritual until it is breath.  
- *Warmth is shared*: At dusk, families carry heated river stones wrapped in moss to the dwellings of the infirm. The stones are passed hand-to-hand along the terraces—a silent, glowing chain of care.  
- *Silence is respect*: A cough is stifled into a sleeve. A dropped tool is retrieved without exclamation. They listen for the mountain’s subtle languages: the *ping* of expanding ice in a fissure (a warning), the change in wind-hum through a specific rock flute (a weather sign).  
- *Nothing is wasted*: A broken basalt hammer becomes a doorstop. A shed goat-hoof becomes a needle case. The breath of the dying is caught on a strip of hide held to the lips, then buried with them—a final offering returned to the stone.

Time lives in their bodies. The eldest, *Mara’el* (“she who remembers the steam”), has fingers that trace the lichen-growth on a sun-warmed wall and can say, *“This patch was the size of a seed when my grandmother was born.”* Children do not learn history from stories alone; they press their palms to the cliff face at dawn and feel the faint, residual warmth of yesterday’s sun stored deep in the rock—a lesson in patience, in deep time. They know the names of 47 distinct steam vents. They know which fissure weeps water sweetest for drinking, which vents steam too sulfurous for moss but perfect for hardening sinew cord.

At night, in the central cavern lit by moss-fat lamps, the drum speaks. Not words, but vibrations. A slow, deep *thrum-thrum-thrum* that echoes the pulse of geothermal blood beneath the plateau. Dancers move not with leaps, but with grounded, deliberate steps—heels striking stone in rhythms that mimic falling scree, melting ice, the patient creep of roots in cracks. Their songs are low, hummed more than sung, harmonizing with the wind’s sigh. They do not sing *about* the mountain. They sing *with* it.

They are not survivors in the desperate sense. They are participants. The city is not a refuge *from* the plateau, but a conversation *with* it. Every carved terrace follows the rock’s grain. Every smoke channel uses the mountain’s own breath to warm shared walls. When a child is born, a single drop of water from the main vent is placed on its brow. When someone dies, their body is wrapped in moss and laid in a high, wind-scoured niche—not as burial, but as return. *“Let the cold take what it gave,”* they say. *“Let the stone remember.”*

You feel it most in the small moments: the way a man pauses while chiseling stone to watch a ptarmigan land on his windowsill, then resumes his work as if granted permission. The way two women mend nets side-by-side without speaking, their needles flashing in unison like synchronized insects. The way the entire city seems to lean into a sudden gust of wind—not resisting, but yielding, then settling back into place.

They are the damp in the stone. The moss in the crack. The steam rising to meet the cold. They do not soften the plateau’s edges. They have learned to dwell within its sharpness. To be cold, and yet generate warmth. To be silent, and yet speak volumes. To be small against the Spine of the World, and yet hold the entire sky in their steady, listening gaze.

They are the plateau’s conscience. Its memory. Its quiet, enduring breath. And in the space between their footsteps and the wind’s sigh, you understand: this is not endurance. This is accord. A covenant written not in ink, but in frost, stone, and shared breath. A life that does not merely persist in absence—but finds its deepest meaning there.
