They do not call themselves anything. Names are for things that stand apart. Here, identity is a relationship.

They are perhaps sixty souls across three generations—a number that breathes with the basin itself. Births are rare, deaths gentle returns to the moss. Their bodies speak the language of this place: skin mapped with the fine silver tracery of old scratches now softened by time and salve, hair the deep umber of wet bark threaded with living strands of *Tillandsia* that continue to grow after being woven in. Their hands are their legacy—fingers long and sure, nails worn smooth, palms reading the grain of wood and the tension of a vine like others read scrolls. Calluses bloom not from violence, but from the patient friction of guiding life: the press of a thumb against a shoot to encourage its curve, the steady pull of fiber through leaf-pulp, the careful untangling of a spider’s web after rain.

They move as water moves—without hurry, without waste. A step is placed where moss is thickest; a pause is taken where a beetle crosses the path. Their voices are low hums and soft clicks, syllables shaped to blend with the basin’s chorus: a rising inflection like a dove’s coo to signal readiness, a rapid *tch-tch-tch* mimicking crickets to call a child nearer. Laughter is rare and precious, a bright ripple quickly absorbed by the frog-song, never sharp enough to startle a nesting bird. They do not wear ornament for display. A necklace of polished river stones marks one who tends the water-channels; bracelets of braided root-fiber signify skill in living architecture. These are not status, but function—quiet signals in a community where every role sustains the whole.

Their days unfold in rhythms older than memory. At first light, two walk the moss-carpeted paths to clear the stone gutters of fallen petals and stray twigs, their movements a silent duet with the waking frogs. Others check the clay-lined seep-pools, testing clarity with a fingertip, whispering to the water-striders skating the surface. An elder—her spine curved like a willow branch, eyes the colour of deep pool-shadow—sits where the oldest fig’s buttress meets the earth. She does not *build*. She *listens*. Her fingers trace the new growth of a guided root, feel the sap’s slow pulse, then tie a single, living shoot into the lattice wall with a slip-knot of softened vine. This is not repair. It is conversation. The wall answers in the weeks to come: thickening where supported, reaching toward the dappled light she has shown it. Her knowledge is not stored in books but in the grain of wood, the scent of soil after rain, the precise angle at which a philodendron leaf channels water without tearing.

Children are not kept apart. A toddler, barefoot and steady, presses damp clay into a leaf-mold under a grandmother’s watchful eye. A girl of seven scales a knotted-vine ladder with the ease of a monkey, not to play, but to place a hollowed gourd where a pair of ground-doves have been circling—a new nest site, offered. They learn by doing, by watching, by *feeling*: which moss holds moisture for poultices, which beetle’s iridescence signals healthy bark, how to read the tension in a spider-silk bridge before stepping near. There are no lessons on separation. When the otters slide into the central pool, sending ripples across the reflection of woven walls, the children do not point and shout. They watch, still as ferns, understanding: *This is family. This is home.*

They take only what is given, and always with reciprocity. Berries are harvested with a thumb-press that leaves the stem intact; a single leaf is taken from a roof-canopy vine, and a pinch of nutrient-rich compost is pressed into the soil beside it. When a tree falls in a gentle storm—not often, for the forest is resilient—they gather not with grief, but with ceremony. They sing the old hums as they guide its trunk to become a new bridge foundation, its branches woven into a shelter for seedlings. Nothing is wasted. Fallen blossoms become dye; shed snake-skin, a teaching tool for patience; the hollowed shell of a giant beetle, a tiny cup for morning dew.

They do not worship the basin. They *participate* in it. There are no altars, no prayers to distant skies. Reverence is the careful tying of a shoot. Gratitude is the crushed mint left at the base of a fig tree after harvesting its fruit. Memory is held in the living architecture: the archway shaped by a great-grandmother’s hands, the root-bridge thickened by three generations of gentle guidance. When an elder passes, her body is laid within a hollowed cradle of living bamboo, wrapped in sheets of softened bark. Vines are trained over her, moss encouraged to grow. Within a season, she becomes part of the wall—a quiet curve in the lattice, a place where new orchids take root. Her stories live on in the grain of the wood, in the path of the water-channel she maintained, in the child who now ties shoots with her remembered rhythm.

And they are never alone. The monkeys are not "wildlife"—they are the chattering uncles who sometimes steal a ripe fig but also drop seeds that become tomorrow’s grove. The otters are the joyful cousins whose slides remind everyone to delight. The morpho butterflies, flashing like scattered sky, are fleeting messengers. The humans leave hollows for doves, plant trumpet-flowers for hummingbirds, avoid the mossy bank where frogs are breeding. This is not sentiment. It is necessity. They know the basin’s breath depends on every thread.

To ask *who* lives here is to misunderstand the question. There is no "who" separate from the *where*. They are the basin’s conscious tenderness—the part of the ecosystem that remembers, chooses, and cares. They are the hands that guide the vine, the ears that hear the stream’s subtle shift, the hearts that beat in time with the frog-song. They are not masters of this place. They are its voice, its memory, its gentle hand upon its own living skin. And in the soft *plink* of a droplet falling from a leaf into a pool cupped in living wood, in the elder’s steady fingers tying shoot to shoot, in the child’s silent observation of a beetle’s journey—you hear them. Not as individuals, but as a single, breathing presence: the basin, learning to hold itself with love.
