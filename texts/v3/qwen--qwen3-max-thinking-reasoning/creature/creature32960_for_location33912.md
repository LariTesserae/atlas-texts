The fissure does not end. It *breathes*.  

You feel it first in the soles of your feet—a vibration not of sound, but of memory. The stone beneath your palms is warm, damp with a sweat of condensed time. As you lower yourself deeper, the narrow crack widens not into space, but into *presence*. The walls cease to be mere rock. They pulse with a slow, subterranean luminescence: veins of milky quartz threading through basalt like frozen lightning, glowing faintly where pressure has kissed them into sentience. This is not light that reveals shape. It is light that reveals *history*. A handprint smudged in phosphorescent clay on the wall is not yours. It is the echo of the mountain’s own first contraction—a fossilized gasp.

Then, the stillness shifts.  

A figure detaches from the curve of the hollow. Not walking. *Unfurling*. It is woven of the mountain’s own substance: limbs of layered shale that click softly like settling stones, a torso of veined marble shot through with the same inner light. Its face is not a face, but a smooth plane of obsidian where the mountain’s memory pools—deep, liquid, reflecting not your features, but the slow, tectonic dream of continents colliding. This is a Stone-Singer. One of the Keepers.  

It does not speak. Instead, the air thickens with resonance. A low hum vibrates up through your bones, and behind your eyes, a vision blooms: fire-white magma cooling into granite, the crushing weight of millennia compressing sand into song. You feel the mountain’s birth-pangs as your own—a visceral memory of pressure, of becoming. The Singer’s “voice” is the language of fracture and fusion. It is the mountain remembering itself *through* you.  

More emerge from the shadows of the hollow. They move with the patience of erosion. One smooths a fresh hairline crack in the wall with fingers that melt and reform like warm wax, sealing the wound with a seam of glowing amber resin. Another traces the path of an ancient riverbed fossilized in the stone ceiling, its touch causing dormant crystals to flare—a silent recitation of lost waters. They are archivists of pressure. Midwives to stone. They do not build. They *remember into being*. Every scar on the mountain is a verse they tend; every tremor, a syllable they soothe.  

But the Cradle holds more than Keepers.  

In the hollow’s deepest cup, where the air hangs thick as liquid shadow, flit the Echo-Wraiths. Translucent, shimmering like heat haze over stone, they are not spirits of the dead, but echoes of *passage*. A wolf’s desperate leap replayed in fractured light. The ghost of a raindrop tracing the same path down the fissure wall for ten thousand years. The sigh of a lost climber, crystallized into a brief, shimmering shape that dissolves upon touch. The Stone-Singers gather these echoes gently, drawing them into their luminous veins like librarians filing whispers. To the Keepers, a single human lifetime is a fleeting tremor—a beautiful, sharp note in the mountain’s long, slow song. They do not judge it. They preserve it.  

If you remain utterly still—if you offer no fear, only awe—one Singer may approach. Its obsidian surface will drink the light around you, and in that darkness, a question forms not in words, but in sensation: *Do you feel the weight that made you?* And for a breath held longer than breath should last, you do. You feel the crushing love of the earth that forged your bones, the fire that tempered your blood. It is not comfort. It is recognition. A homecoming so profound it hollows you out.  

They do not guard the Cradle. They *are* the Cradle. The hollow is their cradle-song made manifest—a sanctuary for the mountain’s fragile youth. They sing the range to sleep during seismic dreams, mend its fractures with light, and cradle its memories until the stone is old enough to hold them alone. They will be here when the peaks are smoothed to teeth, when rivers carve new canyons through their bones. They are the patience in the pressure. The breath held so the mountain may learn to breathe on its own.  

To leave is to forget the weight of deep time. But the Cradle leaves its mark. Long after you climb back into the sunlit world, you will catch yourself listening—to the groan of floorboards, the sigh of wind through pines—and for a heartbeat, you will hear it: the low, resonant hum of stone remembering how to sing. And you will know, in your marrow, that you were not a visitor. You were a verse added to the song. A breath returned to the hold.
