They do not call themselves anything. Names are surfaces; here, identity is strata.

To meet one of the Caldera’s people is to witness geology given patience. They move as the land moves: not with haste, but with the inevitability of cooling stone. Their bodies are sheathed in layered linen, woven with filaments of spun basalt and reflective mica that catch the amber light without glare. The fabric is never pristine. Ochre dust clings to their sleeves from tending iron-oxide blooms; sulfur-yellow smudges trace the curve of a cheek where they leaned close to a vent’s whisper; rust-red stains mark palms that cradled a newly formed borax cluster. These are not soils of labor—they are pigments of communion. To wash them away would be to erase a day’s scripture.

Their faces hold the stillness of deep rock. Eyes the color of cooled obsidian or weathered pumice do not dart. They *settle*. When they look at you, it is not appraisal but absorption—as if reading the thermal signature of your presence, the moisture in your breath, the tremor in your stance. They speak rarely. A word is a fissure: intentional, consequential. More often, communication lives in gesture: a palm held flat over a warm stone to share its temperature; fingers tracing the path of a condensation groove on a railing; the slow uncurling of a hand to offer a single glass bead, cooled from volcanic sand, its facets holding the memory of fire. To receive it is to be entrusted with a fragment of the Caldera’s breath.

They are Lithoscribes. Thermographers. Vent-tenders. Not titles, but verbs made flesh. An elder might spend twenty years stationed at a single steam fissure, learning the cadence of its exhalations until she can predict the exact moment the cerulean bacteria within will flare with new intensity. She etches her observations not on parchment, but into the slag-concrete wall beside her post—a spiral of grooves deepening with each seasonal shift, a tactile archive anyone may read with fingertips. Children do not attend schools. They sit beside these elders in silence, learning to distinguish the *plink* of pure condensation from the metallic *tink* of mineral-rich drip. They learn that a certain vibration in the bridge railing means the deep rock is dreaming of movement. They are taught that to listen is to participate.

Their hands are their theology. Every artifact bears the echo of touch: the rivets on a walkway hammered with rhythmic precision that matches the caldera’s subsonic hum; the ceramic troughs shaped to cradle each drop of dawn-tears without spill; the copper wires of the skeletal "tree" woven not by machine, but by generations of fingers twisting strands together during vigils beside cooling lava flows. A gear forged for a clockwork elevator is signed with a microscopic glyph pressed into the metal—a maker’s breath held at the moment of cooling. To mass-produce would be sacrilege. To replicate without adaptation would be deafness. Each object is a conversation with the material: *What do you need to become? How shall we grow together?*

They do not fear the land’s changes. When a new fissure splits a plaza, the community gathers not with panic, but with reverence. They pour a thin layer of quick-setting slag along its edges—not to seal it, but to frame it. For a year, they observe. Does steam rise with healing warmth? Do crystals begin to feather its lips? Only then do they decide: this fissure becomes a new shrine of light; this path is retired, its steps left to be kissed by mist, marked with a plaque of fused obsidian inscribed with the date it was gifted back to the earth. Grief here is not for loss, but for the humility of being reminded: *You are a guest in a living room.*

And they are not alone. The six-legged drones—obsidian carapaces warm to the touch, copper joints whispering—are not servants. They are kin. Crafted by hand, yes, but *awakened* by the very vents they maintain. A drone might pause beside a sleeping Lithoscribe, its optical sensor dimming to match the starlight, as if keeping watch. The heat-moths, wings like fractured obsidian, are fleeting elders: born at dawn from steam-condensed spores, they dance toward warmth for hours, then dissolve into mineral dust at dusk—a cycle the people honor by leaving shallow bowls of cooled slag where moths gather, to catch their iridescent remains for pigment. Even the chemiluminescent bacteria are addressed in low murmurs before tending a vent: *Show us your light. Teach us your rhythm.*

But the deepest truth lingers in the silence between heartbeats: the Caldera itself is the eldest inhabitant. The people are its students, its interpreters, its grateful witnesses. They do not worship a distant god. They kneel to press their foreheads against a warm basalt wall and *listen*. They feel the planet’s slow pulse travel up through bone. They understand that the steam is breath, the glowing fissures are thoughts, the shifting rock is memory. When a child is born, the first ritual is not naming, but placement: the infant is laid gently on a sun-warmed stone slab, wrapped in linen, while the community stands silent. Not to bless the child, but to let the Caldera *recognize* them. To say: *You are of this breath. You belong to this depth.*

They are archivists of transformation. Midwives to emergence. They live in the sacred space between *was* and *will be*, tending the threshold with hands that know the weight of time. They do not seek to conquer the wound in the world. They have learned to breathe with it. To drink its tears. To trace its scars with reverence. And in their quiet presence—in the deliberate placement of a single ceramic cup beneath a weeping wall, in the patient wait for a new crystal to bloom—you realize: they are not surviving the Caldera. They are the Caldera learning to see itself. To remember itself. To dream itself forward, one slow, warm breath at a time.
