They do not call themselves anything. Names are anchors, and anchors sink in drafting waters. To outsiders, they are the Drafters. To the land, they are simply *listening*.

They move as extensions of the plateau’s breath. Their bodies are lean and sinewy, shaped by terrain that shifts beneath them—not with urgency, but with reverence. Skin carries the map of this place: faint silver traceries where mineral-rich condensation has dried along wrists and temples; knuckles dusted with copper-flecked grit from handling cooling stone; eyes the color of deep moss or weathered iron, pupils wide to drink the gloom. They rarely speak above a murmur. Their language is woven from the land’s own vocabulary—the *crack-hiss* of settling rock becomes a question; the low thrum of a sky-ray’s passage, an affirmation. A tilted head listens for the groan of rising stone. A palm pressed flat against warm basalt feels the day’s mood before a word is formed.

Their clothing is grown, not woven. Cloaks of silver-veined creeper-vine, cured in mercury-water basins until supple as shadow. Trousers lined with the soft, moss-furred hide of the six-limbed grazers—animals they never hunt, only honor. When a grazer rubs its side against a copper post left for the purpose, they gather the shed fibers like sacred text. Boots are seamless, molded from the same resilient lichen that carpets the ironbark roots, gripping stone without scarring it. Around their necks hang *mood-stones*: not jewelry, but fragments of the plateau’s recent memory—a sliver of obsidian cooled in a spiral, a bead of granite threaded with copper, a droplet of solidified dew that holds both yesterday’s chill and tomorrow’s promise. To hold another’s mood-stone is to hold their silence, their grief, their wonder.

They do not build. They *collaborate*.  
A stone-shaper kneels before a rising spire of basalt, not with chisel and hammer, but with breath and copper wedge. She hums a low note matching the rock’s cooling frequency, guiding the flow like a midwife. A gear-tender anoints the seventeen-minute-turning cog with a paste of powdered lichen and condensed mist, whispering thanks for its patience. Children learn by placing small, smooth river stones (treasured relics from rare descents into the cloud-sea) at the base of nascent arches—not as offerings to a god, but as punctuation marks in a shared sentence: *Here. Now. Together.* Homes are hollowed not by force, but by invitation—a family waits for a cooling lava flow to form a natural curve, then lines its interior with woven steel-cable roots and membranes of sky-ray wing that glow with captured twilight. Doors are left open. Walls breathe. If the stone swells overnight and seals an entrance, they do not force it. They wait. They listen. They find another way.

They live in kinship, not hierarchy. The eldest are Rememberers—not rulers, but living archives of pattern. They recall how the cloud-sea thickened before the great copper-vein emerged; how the clockwork sparrows fell silent the day the shadow-bridge first appeared. The young are Noticers, tasked with watching for new moss-cradles, tracking the paths of the many-eyed drinkers, learning the subtle grammar of condensation. There are no locks. No borders. A basin of mercury-water belongs to whoever finds it thirsty. A warm copper gutter belongs to the sparrow that nests there. When a tool sinks halfway into stone by dawn, it is not mourned. It is acknowledged with a nod—a gift returned to the draft. They craft a new one from reclaimed timber and riveted iron, humming as they work, so the metal learns the sound of gratitude.

They move through the soft edges of reality with the ease of breath.  
They step onto a bridge of solidified shadow without hesitation, trusting the land’s fleeting architecture. They leave tools in niches carved by yesterday’s stone, accepting that some will be embraced by tomorrow’s growth. They do not fear the pooling of time. A worker mending a valve might pause, watching a cloud-form hang unchanged for hours, and simply sip water from a copper cup, content in the suspension. They mark moments not in hours, but in events: *after the great gear sighed*, *before the archway clicked shut*, *when the stained-glass wings vanished*. Their stories are not of conquest, but of co-creation: "The spire grew a hollow, so we lined it with moss for the grazers." "The sky-ray brushed the tower—tonight, we mend the western cable." "The basin held two dews. We drank sparingly, and left a polished gear in thanks."

At the dimmest hour, when the bioluminescent lichen pulses like a slow heartbeat, they gather in open cradles between structures. No fires. No songs of old triumphs. Instead, they trace patterns in the damp stone with fingertips—ephemeral maps of what *might* be. A child draws a spiral where a new spire could rise. An elder sketches a bridge where shadow might solidify. They speak in fragments of possibility: *"What if the creepers learn to drink steam?" "What if the sparrows sing the gear’s next turn?"* Their art is transient. Their legacy is attention. They are not preserving a world. They are participating in its drafting.

And the land answers.  
A sky-ray drifts low, its crystalline wing brushing a spire—a message received. The clockwork sparrows trill a precise sequence near a cooling seam, and the stone-shapers adjust their wedges. The six-limbed grazers nuzzle a child’s hand, their moss-furred hides damp with dew that tastes of iron and hope. In the deepest hollows, where the mercury-water shimmers with doubled time, the many-eyed drinkers unfold their stained-glass wings not in flight, but in silent communion—a ripple of color that says: *We are here. We are drafting with you.*

They are not masters of this place.  
They are its commas.  
Its breath-held pauses.  
Its gentle pressure on the pen.  
They do not seek to finish the sentence. They exist in the luminous tension of the draft—patient, present, perpetually becoming. And in their quiet hands, the Anvil Plateau does not feel inhabited.  
It feels *understood*.
