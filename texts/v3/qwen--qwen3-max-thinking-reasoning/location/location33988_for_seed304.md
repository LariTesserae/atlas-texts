The basin is no larger than a village green, cupped in the crook of a recent geological sigh. You step down into it—a single shallow descent of seven moss-slicked steps carved not by hands, but by the slow insistence of water finding its way. The air changes immediately: cool (0.6), damp against the skin, carrying the mineral scent of wet stone and the faint, sweet rot of drowned moss. This is a place that *holds* water. Not in lakes or rivers, but in its very bones. The porous, honeycomb limestone of the basin floor drinks the frequent rains and underground seeps, then weeps it back slowly. Every surface glistens. Droplets tremble at the lip of a carved channel, fall with a *plink* into a palm-sized pool. Rivulets, no wider than a finger, trace silver veins across the packed-earth paths. The light (1.5) is soft daylight, diffused by a high, thin mist that never quite becomes fog—a breath held in the cool air.

This is a settlement, but not of fields or farms. Scale (0.9) confines it: a single, cohesive organism of stone and intention. Density (2.7) is its truth. There is no empty space. Low, rounded structures—beehive huts of woven willow wattle daubed with clay and crushed limestone—press shoulder-to-shoulder. Their roofs are living tapestries of thick, spongy moss (the sparse vegetation, 0.7, concentrated here by human design), drinking the weeping moisture. Between them, the pathways are not paths at all, but a labyrinth of shallow, purposeful channels. Water flows everywhere, guided. It trickles from a spout carved into the face of a central monolith, spirals down a stepped cascade beside a dwelling, vanishes into a grate of interlaced roots before reappearing ten paces later. The built environment (2.0) is layered not in height, but in hydrology: every stone is placed to direct the flow. Primitive tech (0.8) is evident in the tools: antler picks leaning against a wall, a stone mortar stained green with crushed lichen, ropes of braided reed securing a thatch patch. Nothing is metal. Everything is damp, worn smooth by water and touch.

The terrain is the architecture. Erosion (1.2) is young, sharp. The basin’s rim is a jagged crown of fractured limestone, raw and unweathered, where water seeps visibly from cracks like tears. Within, the settlers have amplified this rawness. They haven’t smoothed the land; they’ve *listened* to it. A natural fissure becomes a main conduit. A cluster of sharp, newly broken stones is incorporated into a wall, their edges still keen. This is the weirdness (1.4), subtle but profound: the water does not flow *around* the settlement. It flows *through* it, *as* it. The boundary between geology and construction is porous, literal. In the central plaza—a space barely large enough for twenty people—the main feature is not a well, but a "breathing stone": a massive, porous slab where groundwater condenses visibly on its cool surface, beading and falling in a slow, rhythmic pulse. When the light catches the wet stone just so, faint, dendritic patterns—like frozen lightning or neural pathways—gleam across its surface. Natural? Or subtly encouraged by the carvers? No one speaks of it. They simply place a smooth river stone where a new branch of the pattern emerges each season.

Sound (1.1) is a language of absence and presence. Mostly quiet, yes—but the quiet is textured. The dominant note is the water: a constant, gentle percussion of drips (*plink… plink-plink…*), the soft whisper of flow in the channels, the hollow gurgle where a stream vanishes underground. Beneath it, the creak of wet willow withes settling. A low hum vibrates from the breathing stone—a resonance caused by air moving through its microscopic pores as the water condenses. Fauna (1.2) is scarce: a single, iridescent dragonfly hangs motionless over a channel, its wings catching the light; a pale, blind amphipod scuttles in a clear pool near a dwelling’s foundation. No birds call. No rodents rustle. The ecology is microbial, mineral, human. The rare life that exists here is adapted to perpetual damp: silver mosses, liverworts like tiny emerald scales, fungi that glow with a faint bioluminescence only in the deepest crevices after dusk.

The rules of this world are written in water. To build is to channel. To walk is to step on the dry(ish) stones placed across the flows. Waste is unthinkable; everything organic is composted in sealed pits lined with clay, its nutrients later used to feed the roof-mosses. Conflict is settled by the Flow-Readers—elders who interpret the speed, clarity, and path of the water in the channels after a dispute. A sudden murkiness near a dwelling signifies unrest; a new, persistent drip where none existed before is an omen to be discussed. The dynamic (1.7) is not dramatic weather or predator-prey chases. It is the slow, sure work of saturation: the way a clay daub softens and is re-packed at dawn; the way moss thickens on the north side of every roof; the daily ritual of clearing silt from the channels with hands and woven baskets. It is active in its patience. The community moves with a quiet purpose, their movements economical, their voices low murmurs that rarely rise above the water’s song. They are not fighting the wetness. They are in conversation with it. Every drip is a word. Every channel, a sentence. And in this small, packed, weeping basin, the conversation has been going on for generations—a cool, quiet, deeply strange covenant between stone, water, and hand.
