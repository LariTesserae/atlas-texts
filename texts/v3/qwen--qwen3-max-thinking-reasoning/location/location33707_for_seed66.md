The air tastes of wet stone and warm iron. You stand on the Rimstone Plateau—a continental shelf lifted millennia ago and never set down. Below, the world falls away into a haze of its own making; above, the sky is a clear, ordinary blue, sun casting sharp, familiar shadows. But here, at elevation, the light holds a liquid quality, refracting through perpetual low-hanging vapor. This is not mist from weather. It is exhalation.

The city is not built *upon* the plateau. It *is* the plateau. Layer upon layer of construction has fused with the bedrock over centuries: foundations of volcanic tuff, streets of fused slag-glass, aqueducts of corroded copper snaking up cliff faces like fossilized vines. Erosion has not worn it down but *integrated* it—wind-smoothed archways now cradle newer steel gantries; rain-carved channels in basalt now serve as conduits for steam vents. Nothing is ruin. Nothing is new. All is settled, patient, in a state of deep equilibrium. A single cobblestone might hold the ghost-imprint of a cartwheel from three generations past, softened now into a shallow basin where water gathers.

Water is everywhere, yet never free-flowing. At 1.7, it is the city’s quiet pulse. After the daily high-altitude drizzle (warm rain that vanishes within hours), the stone weeps. Condensation beads on every cold surface—pipes, railings, the curved backs of dormant machinery—and trickles into hairline fractures in the pavement. In shaded courtyards, moisture seeps upward from the aquifer beneath the plateau, pooling in shallow, mineral-stained basins. The air carries a damp warmth (1.5), thick enough to slick the skin, carrying the scent of hot metal cooling and wet graphite.

There are no plants. Not a blade of grass, not a lichen speck. Vegetation registers at 0.3—a profound absence. Where life *should* root, only mineral processes unfold: rust blooms in fractal crimson across iron beams; salt efflorescence feathers the undersides of bridges in delicate white crystals; in the deepest steam tunnels, a rubbery, sightless mold feeds on mineral deposits and thermal gradients—a silent, non-photosynthetic biology barely acknowledged. The ground is pure geology and industry: crushed rock, poured concrete, polished obsidian walkways slick with condensation.

And yet—*fauna*. At 2.7, it is overwhelming. Not in wilderness, but in density. This is the city’s paradox. Rats move not as individuals but as tides—shifting rivers of grey fur flowing through drainage grates and along pipe corridors. Pigeons do not perch; they *coat* surfaces. Every ledge, wire, and statue is a living mosaic of iridescent feathers, their collective coo a low thunder beneath the industrial hum. In the steam-heated ducts, blind cave crickets swarm in rustling clouds. At dusk, swallows stitch the sky in synchronized arcs, their wings slicing the vapor. They thrive not on plants, but on the city’s own output: algae-scum in condensation pools, insects drawn to thermal vents, scraps from the few human habitations. Life has rerouted itself entirely through the machine.

Sound (1.7) is a textured pressure. The deep, rhythmic *thump-hiss* of steam engines cycling in distant foundries. The metallic shriek of a cargo lift ascending a cliff-face tower. The constant drip-drip-drip from a thousand weeping surfaces. Beneath it all, the ceaseless rustle and chirr of countless small lives—the skitter of claws on stone, the flutter of wings against wire mesh, the low murmur of a million pigeons settling for the night. It is loud, but not chaotic. It is the sound of a system breathing.

Scale (2.6) reveals itself slowly. From any vantage, the city extends until perspective fails—spires piercing the vapor layer, bridges arcing over chasms so deep their bottoms are lost in cloud. Weather forms *within* the city: by midday, heat rising from machinery and warm stone coaxes the plateau’s moisture into localized fog banks that drift between canyon-like streets. You feel small, not by design, but by physics. This place is continental in ambition, planetary in patience.

Density (1.5) is in the layers. Walk one street level; beneath your feet, three more tiers of tunnels, pipes, and forgotten workshops hum with latent activity. Above, suspended walkways and cable cars trace paths through the vapor. Every cubic meter holds presence: a nesting swallow in a gear housing, a cluster of crickets in a warm pipe joint, the faint vibration of a distant piston. Nothing is empty. Nothing is wasted.

Technology (1.9) is industrial, but aged into elegance. No flickering screens or silent drones. Here, power is visible: copper boilers wrapped in sweating pipes, water wheels turning in artificial channels fed by condensation collectors, conveyor belts moving raw stone from quarries on the plateau’s edge. Electricity exists as occasional arcs between tram lines, or the warm glow of carbon-filament bulbs behind grimy windows. It is technology that sweats, breathes steam, and bears the patina of use. A world where engines are tended like sacred beasts, their rhythms dictating the city’s slow pulse.

Light (2.3) is ordinary daylight—golden at dawn, stark at noon, bleeding into violet at dusk. But it interacts with the city’s essence: refracting through hanging vapor into faint rainbows over steam vents; catching the oil-slick sheen on a puddle; glinting off a million wet pigeon feathers. At night, gas lamps cast pools of amber, and the city’s own heat-haze softens the stars.

Weirdness (0.8) lingers only at the edges of perception. The way pigeons avoid certain alleyways for no discernible reason. How the rats part around specific manhole covers without hesitation. The faint, rhythmic *thrum* felt through the soles of the feet—not sound, but vibration—that seems to sync with the steam cycles. It is unusual, not uncanny. A place that operates by consistent, observable rules, even if those rules are not yet yours to know.

Dynamic (0.8) is the final key. Nothing happens quickly. Rust advances grain by grain. Stone settles millimeter by millimeter. The rat populations shift territories over seasons, not days. A new layer of construction might take a decade to complete, its edges already softening before the mortar fully sets. Time here is measured in erosion, in the slow accumulation of pigeon guano on a gargoyle’s shoulder, in the patient drip that will, in five hundred years, carve a new channel through basalt. Change is not absent—it is deep, geological, inevitable. The city does not rush. It endures.

You breathe the warm, wet air. A pigeon shifts on a nearby railing, eyeing you with ancient indifference. Somewhere below, a valve releases a sigh of steam. The plateau holds you. The city breathes around you. It has been here long before you arrived. It will weep stone and hum steam long after you are gone.
