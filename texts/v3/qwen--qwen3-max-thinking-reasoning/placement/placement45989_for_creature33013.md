I find myself not at the center, but in the seam.  
Where the new obsidian needle pierced the moss-lane three nights ago, and the old willow-walkway now hangs like a held breath.  

My hands are not clean. They are stained the deep green of crushed frost-fern, threaded with copper-filings and the silver sap of symbiotic vine. A splinter of willow rests beneath my thumbnail—a familiar ache. I do not pull it out. It is a reminder: *feel the material. feel the resistance. feel the giving.*  

I kneel. Not in prayer, but in posture. My knee rests against the warm stone where the amber pulse throbs, slow and sure. *Thank you for the warning*, I murmur, the words fogging the air. The stone does not answer in language. It answers in temperature—a subtle shift beneath my palm, a softening where the growth has settled. Safe to weave here.  

I select a shoot from my basket: willow harvested at dawn-dew, its fibers humming with residual moisture. I do not force it. I coax. I loop it around the salvaged copper pipe salvaged from a retired vent-conduit, its surface etched with the phosphorescent trails of rock-lice who have already deemed this seam stable. *Click-click-click*—they scuttle past my wrist, iridescent carapaces catching the pearl-gray light. One pauses on the fresh obsidian. I wait. It traces a spiral. A pattern of acceptance. Only then do I tighten the weave.  

Slack. Always leave slack.  
This is the first lesson they taught me, when I arrived all sharp edges and hurried breath. *You cannot bind the becoming*, the elder said, her fingers guiding mine over a swelling seam. *You can only offer a handhold.* So I weave with intentionality, not tension. The willow curves to cradle the stone’s swell; the copper pipe, warmed by the earth’s breath below, hums against my cheek. I tuck a strand of silverbark leaf into the knot—a gift for the snow-voles who nest in the wall cavity above. A tiny chirp answers. A wind-chime blessing.  

When the bridge is complete, it is not a solution. It is a conversation.  
The walkway now arcs gently around the new spire, inviting footsteps to pause, to trace the stone’s curve. Children will climb it tomorrow. Moss will take root in the weave by next moon. I press a single river stone—a smooth, grey comma—into the junction where vine meets rock. Not an anchor. An acknowledgment.  

I rise. My woolens are dusted with quartz dust and frost-fern spores. I brush my hands not to clean them, but to feel the residue—the story of the work. Down the lane, the baker breaks bread on her step, offering a crust to the vole on her lintel. The engineer adjusts a stilt with a breath-held precision. No one looks at me. No one needs to. We are all listening to the same silence.  

This is where I belong: in the quiet labor of integration.  
Not the engineer who guides the hydraulic arm. Not the elder who sits with the stone’s heartbeat. But the one who mends the in-between. The weaver of transitions. The keeper of slack. My body has learned the Basin’s grammar—the way steam-haze thickens before a gentle swell, how the bioluminescent fungi brighten where the stone dreams peacefully. My grief is for the times I moved too fast, for the weave I once pulled taut out of fear. My joy is this: the morning a child left a tiny woven willow bird tucked into my repair, her small hands having learned the rhythm.  

I am no longer the newcomer who stood frozen in the plaza, deaf to the whispers.  
I am the moss-dust on the step.  
I am the slack in the vine.  
I am the comma in the stone’s sentence—small, necessary, making space for what comes next.  

And as dusk bleeds into the valley, the fungi along my new walkway begin to glow.  
Not stars.  
Not magic.  
Just light.  
Just listening.  
Just home.
