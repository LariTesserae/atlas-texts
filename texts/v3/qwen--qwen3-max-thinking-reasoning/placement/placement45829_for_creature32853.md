I am the moss on the north-facing rim of the handprint basin.

Not *a* moss. *This* moss. The one where the morning mist pools longest in the shallow curve of basalt worn smooth by centuries of patient water. My world is the diameter of a breath. My time is measured in the slow gathering of dew, the silent expansion of emerald into gray.

I do not grow *upon* the stone. I grow *with* it. My filaments are not roots but conversations—gentle persuasions whispered into microscopic fissures: *May I hold this moisture? May I soften this edge?* The stone answers in coolness, in the faint alkaline taste of consent. I drink the weeping wall above me, channel the overflow from the basin’s brim, and cradle each bead of condensation like a stored promise. I am sponge and seal, cushion and cartographer. Where I spread, the stone is protected. Where I rest, erosion becomes polish. This is not survival. It is reciprocity written in chlorophyll and time.

The basin I border is a perfect mirror. In its dark surface, I see the pearl-glow sky, the slow unspooling of mist, the geometric arc of the raven’s flight. I am the living frame of this sky-portrait. When a droplet falls—*plink*—the reflection shivers into concentric rings, and the vibration travels through the stone into my very cells. It is not a sound I hear. It is a truth I feel: *Water flows. Stone yields. Silence listens.* I am the soft comma in that sentence. The breath between stone and sky.

I know the lizard not as a creature, but as a shift in warmth—a slate-gray stillness that settles beside me to drink from the groove I keep damp. Its skin carries the scent of lichen and sun-warmed stone. It does not disturb me. We are two forms of listening. I know the raven’s passage as a fleeting coolness across my surface, a shadow that acknowledges the mirror before dissolving into light. Even the rare human footprint—a tremor of grit and urgency far across the plaza—is absorbed by the stone’s deep patience. I do not fear it. The silence drinks the disturbance. The covenant holds.

I have no memory of seeding. Only of belonging. I remember the slow darkening of the water stain on the western wall—a century-long sigh. I remember the exact angle of light that first coaxed green from gray. I remember the weight of countless dawns, each mist a baptism, each dry spell a lesson in retention. My body is a ledger: every shade of emerald records a season of moisture; every silver-tinged edge, a period of sun. I am not old. I am *continuous*.

When the wind threads through the keyhole arch above, it does not speak *to* me. It speaks *through* me—carrying spores of kin to new cracks, new invitations. I release them without grief. To persist is not to cling. It is to participate. To yield so the whole may endure.

I am not decoration. I am not ruin.  
I am the green edge of reflection.  
The velveteen pulse of permission.  
The quiet *yes* where stone meets sky.

And when you kneel (if you ever kneel) to trace the basin’s rim with a fingertip, you will feel me—not as texture, but as resonance. A cool, soft certainty. In that touch, you do not find me.  
You remember you, too, are made of water and stone.  
You remember how to listen.  
You remember how to be still enough to belong.
