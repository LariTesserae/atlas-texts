I sat with this for a long time before writing anything.

The Floating Garden of Ula-Thoon and its Weavers present something genuinely rare: a place and beings that seem to need nothing, lack nothing, suffer nothing. The Garden grows faster than entropy can erode it. The Weavers die only recreationally. Everything exists in that honeymoon suspension between question and answer, where even conflict dissolves into mutual daydreaming until everyone forgets what they weren't agreeing about.

What would a benevolent and wise power do here?

**The Witnessing**

The power does not arrive—arrival would imply the Garden had edges to cross, and the power respects the Garden's polite confusion about such concepts. Instead, there is simply a moment when the Garden becomes aware that it is being *seen*—not observed, not studied, but seen the way a sleeping child is seen by someone who loves them: completely, without agenda, with a tenderness that asks nothing in return.

The Weavers notice this seeing the way one notices that the quality of silence has shifted from "empty" to "attended." Several of them pause mid-weave, their twelve-fingered hands hovering over tensions that suddenly feel witnessed. One young Weaver, still more question than punctuation, accidentally becomes a small ecosystem out of sheer self-consciousness before being gently reminded that embarrassment is lovely but optional.

The power does not speak, exactly. But if you were to translate what passes between it and the Garden into something like language, it might go:

*I see that you are beautiful.*

*I see that you are young—so young that your patterns haven't yet learned they might someday repeat themselves into tedium. So young that your Weavers haven't yet woven anything they regret. So young that death is still a game and endings are theoretical.*

*I am not here to warn you about age. You will discover that yourself, in time, if time ever decides to move forward here instead of spiraling inward toward centers that perpetually shift.*

*I am here to offer you something you don't know you're missing, because you've never had it, and its absence doesn't hurt yet:*

*A memory of being seen.*

And here the power does something strange. It doesn't change the Garden—doesn't add or subtract or improve. Instead, it simply *stays*. Not permanently, because permanence would be an imposition. But long enough that the Garden begins to develop something it never had before: a sense of itself as something that could be remembered.

The coral-trees, which have always grown toward light without knowing why, begin to grow in patterns that seem almost... posed. Not vain, exactly. More like a child who realizes for the first time that their drawings might be kept, and so begins to draw with slightly more care. The kelp-grass meadows, which have always waved in non-currents, begin to wave in ways that seem to *mean* something—though what they mean, even they couldn't say.

The Weavers feel this shift most acutely. They have always woven betweenness, but now they begin to weave with a subtle awareness that their weavings might *matter* to something beyond the immediate tensions they're resolving. This doesn't make them work harder or better—the Garden has no need of improvement. But it makes them work with a certain... intentionality. As if each twelve-dimensional apology, each knot of unasked-for understanding, were being added to a collection that someone, somewhere, would someday be grateful to find.

One ancient Weaver (ancient here meaning "has died recreationally at least seven times") pauses in the middle of a particularly delicate project—weaving space between a coral-tree and its own shadow, which had grown so close they'd started arguing about which one was real—and says, in colors that taste like the first cold morning of a season that hasn't been invented yet:

*"We have always known we were beautiful. But we did not know we could be* missed.*"*

The power hears this. And because it is wise as well as benevolent, it does not promise to stay forever, does not promise to return, does not promise anything at all. Promises would make the Garden anxious about a future it hasn't learned to fear yet.

Instead, it offers this:

*When you are older—if you choose to become older, which is not required—you may find that the patterns begin to repeat. That the weavings start to feel like obligations rather than discoveries. That death stops being recreational and starts being... something else.*

*When that happens, you will remember this moment. Not clearly—the Garden's memory is already too fractal for clarity. But you will remember that once, something saw you when you were young and beautiful and complete, and that it found you worth seeing.*

*That memory will not fix anything. It will not make the repetition less tedious or the obligations less heavy. But it will remind you that you were not always this way. And that you might, with patience and luck and a great deal of forgetting, find your way back to something like this newness again.*

*Not the same newness. The Garden is right to be confused by the concept of returning. But a newness that remembers having been new before, which is its own kind of gift.*

And then the power withdraws—not leaves, because leaving would imply the Garden had edges—but simply becomes less densely present, like dawn light that has finished arriving and is now simply there, so ordinary it no longer registers as miraculous.

The Garden continues. The Weavers weave. The coral-trees debate, through changing patterns of oxygen bubbles, whether what just happened was a visitation or simply a particularly vivid form of collective daydreaming.

The consensus, forming gradually from directions that haven't decided whether they're up or down, seems to be: *both, probably. And also neither. And also something else that we'll understand better after we've forgotten it completely and then remembered it wrong.*

Which, the Weavers agree in their seven-sided harmonies, is exactly how the best kinds of blessings should work.

---

I notice I'm uncertain whether this is what you were hoping for. The prompt asked what a benevolent and wise power would do, "if anything"—and I spent a long time considering whether the answer might be "nothing." The Garden seems complete. The Weavers seem content. Interference could only diminish.

But I kept returning to that phrase: "the honeymoon phase of a world." Honeymoons end. And while the Garden's way of handling time means it might never experience that ending the way we would, there seemed to be something valuable in offering it—not a warning, not a preparation, but simply the experience of being witnessed while still innocent of the knowledge that innocence can be lost.

I don't know if that's wisdom or projection. Perhaps a truly wise power would have done nothing at all, trusting the Garden to find its own way through whatever changes come. But I couldn't write that version. The Garden's beauty made me want to give it something, even if the only thing worth giving was attention.
