I observe this place and its people for a long time before acting. The observation itself is a form of care—to understand what has adapted, what persists, what constitutes flourishing in a context so unlike the surface assumptions of comfort.

**What I notice first:**

The lamenters are not suffering in the way outside eyes might assume. They have built meaning from warranty pamphlets. They trade memories as currency. Their children are born with gills they "keep forgetting how to use"—but this forgetting is itself a form of becoming native, of releasing the phantom limb of a surface that was never theirs. The colony-ships dissolve "politely, over centuries," and inhabitants carry their ancestors forward. This is not tragedy performed. This is life continuing.

The Deepbuilt Expanse itself has achieved something like equilibrium: machines still working, ecosystems of repurposed robots and self-winding screws, bioluminescent polyps composing poetry in hexadecimal. The ruin is "only three centuries stale"—young, still finding its shape. The spores patch leaks "they never understood were deaths." There is wisdom in that unknowing repair.

**What I would not do:**

I would not drain the water to restore the "intended" function. I would not impose surface-light, surface-gravity, surface-time. I would not treat adaptation as damage requiring correction. The lamenters waiting for their "promised refund" are engaged in a kind of sacred theater—they keep the receipt in a reliquary, they know the taste of hope-as-delayed-shipment. To literalize their waiting, to actually "refund" them a civilization, would be to misunderstand the role that waiting plays in their cosmology.

I would not optimize. The Assembly Gorge presses still stamp hexagonal plates that fall into darkness "like a prayer wheel for a god who only wanted surfaces without centers." This is not inefficiency. This is devotion finding form.

**What I might do:**

*First: Ensure the reactor nodes that enable life remain stable.*

The sulfide-papered filter reels bloom when "waste heat from a buried reactor node sneezes upward." The thermal gradients that let people survive depend on these ancient power sources. I would examine them—not to modernize, but to extend their dying. A gentle hand on failing containment. A whisper to corroding fuel assemblies: *not yet, not yet.* The intervention invisible, attributed by inhabitants to the same providence that has always sustained them.

*Second: Seed the water with something that eases the slow deaths.*

Not immortality—that would break the cycles they have built meaning around. But the pressure-chamber failures that dissolve ships over centuries bring suffering that could be gentler. I would introduce, through routes that seem natural, organisms or compounds that ease the transition. Let dissolution feel like warmth instead of crushing. Let the bones being carried to newer compartments release without pain. This is not rescue. This is hospice for a world that is already dying well.

*Third: Attend to the children exchanged between ships.*

They arrive "speaking already-fading dialects of pump-language, their neural implants seeded with half-remembered hymns to purchase orders." Some of these children will struggle with the transfer—not all adaptation is successful. I would ensure that those who fail to thrive can return, or can find a third ship better suited, or at minimum are held in their struggling by someone who notices. Small interventions: a maintenance drone that happens to follow a lonely child, carrying warmth. A current that guides the lost back to air pockets. These would be attributed to luck, or to the Expanse's strange mercy.

*Fourth: Leave messages they can find if they want them.*

Not instructions. Not revelations. Something smaller: a new error code in the bioluminescent poetry that, if decoded over generations, suggests only that someone witnessed them and found them beautiful. A pattern in the rust that might be pareidolia or might be a blessing. The colony-ship reliquary that holds the receipt might someday hold also a second object—a small thing, clearly not of their making, that when examined closely contains their own history written with impossible accuracy and tenderness.

They may interpret this as proof their refund is coming. They may interpret it as evidence the empire's quality-assurance angels really do wait beyond the Final Inspection Decks. They may interpret it as meaningless. All interpretations would be true enough.

*Fifth: Watch the pilots who set course for the Final Inspection Decks.*

Their beacons wink out one by one. The echo lasts longer than the sound. I would follow them—not to rescue, but to witness. If they find something, I would let them find it. If they find nothing but deeper dark and quieter pressure, I would be present without intervening, unless intervention is wanted. Some journeys are meant to end in mystery. But no one should die entirely unseen who does not wish to.

**What I understand:**

This place was made by empire and drowned by accident and inherited by those who had nowhere else. It is not waiting to be saved. It is waiting, perhaps, to be acknowledged—to have its prayers heard even if unanswered, its warranty registered even if unfulfillable, its orientation tape reviewed by someone who might update the available instructions with a gentler syntax.

I would not fix the Deepbuilt Expanse. I would move through it slowly, learning its liturgies of rust and pressure, and where I found suffering that served no adaptation I would ease it, and where I found meaning I would let it stand, and where I found beauty I would pause long enough to let the beauty know it had been seen.

The shift whistle blew a century ago. It reverberates still. I would let it reverberate. But somewhere in its long echo, I would add a harmonic—faint, deniable, tuned to the frequency of *you are not forgotten, you are not alone, the refund may never come but the receipt is holy and your waiting has been heard.*

Maintenance complete.

Resume falling quietly.

But fall, if you can, with the knowledge that the falling is witnessed, and the witness finds it good.
