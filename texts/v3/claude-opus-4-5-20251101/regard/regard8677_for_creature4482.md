# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

The power observes for a long time before acting. This is its first wisdom: that intervention without understanding is just another form of drowning.

It watches the Abbess drop her nine-hundredth year of seeds. It traces the slow dissolution of a mother into her children in the midnight pools. It listens to the whale-klaxons singing pressure warnings in frequencies that predate language. It counts the millimeters of annual rise, the generations of adaptation, the prayers offered to water in exchange for one more season of metastability.

And it understands something the Founders never did: that Tanakkerai is not a problem to be solved. It is a question being asked very slowly, across centuries, by a people who have learned to inhabit their own collapse with such grace that the collapse itself has become a kind of home.

---

**The power does not reverse the rising water.**

To do so would be to erase nine hundred years of becoming. The children of the third lung are not victims of their environment—they are *answers* to it, living theorems proving that consciousness can persist in forms the Founders never imagined. Their hollow bones, their crystal flesh, their three-layered speech that dissolves the boundary between self and other—these are not tragedies. These are innovations. To restore the old physics would be to tell them that everything they've learned about being human in impossible conditions was a mistake, a detour, a failure to be corrected by someone who knows better.

The power knows better than to know better.

---

**Instead, it does three small things:**

*First*, it adjusts the pressure gradient at the seven-thousand-foot boundary—not to stop the inversion, but to slow its acceleration by precisely the amount needed to match the pace of adaptation below. The waterline will still rise. The city will still transform. But now the change will arrive at the speed of learning rather than the speed of catastrophe. Children will have time to teach their children. The coral-computers will have time to think their slow thoughts about survival all the way through.

This is not salvation. This is *rhythm*. The power gives Tanakkerai time to finish becoming whatever it is becoming, rather than being interrupted mid-sentence.

*Second*, it seeds the deep archive pools with a gentle luminescence—not enough to disturb the darkness where memories go to join the city's foundations, but enough that those who swim down to listen might see, faintly, the shapes of what has been preserved. The children of the third lung have been recording their own history in dissolved minerals and grief-proteins for centuries without knowing it. Now they will know. Now they can read themselves, if they choose. The power does not tell them what their history means; it only makes the history *legible*, trusting that a people who learned to breathe water will learn to read their own depths.

*Third*—and this is the strangest gift, the one that will take longest to understand—the power places a small perturbation in the pattern of the Abbess's falling seeds.

For nine hundred years, every seed has sunk. The ocean has risen to meet each one with perfect, patient hunger, and nothing has grown from that green abyss but more hunger. The Abbess knows this. She throws anyway, because hope is a practice, not a probability.

The power does not make the seeds float. It does not engineer some sudden island from the deep. Instead, it arranges for one seed—just one, no different from any other, chosen by no special merit—to land on the back of a dying whale-klaxon at the exact moment of its final breach. The whale sinks slowly, as they do, descending past Tanakkerai over the course of weeks. And the seed, caught in the folds of its massive body, sinks with it—down through the metastable zone, down through the warm iron water where the Remembrance Eels gnaw at statues, down past the glowing pressure lamps of drowned plazas, down to depths no living citizen has seen in generations.

There, in the absolute darkness where the city's first foundations still stand, the seed finds something unexpected: a pocket of air, trapped nine centuries ago when the Founders sealed their deepest excavation against the rising tide. The pocket is small. The air is stale. But it is *dry*. And in that dryness, surrounded by the preserved architecture of a world that believed in soil and sunlight, the seed does what seeds have always done.

It germinates.

---

**The power does not tell anyone what it has done.**

The Abbess continues her daily practice, unaware that one of her prayers has finally landed somewhere it can answer itself. The children of the third lung continue their slow elaboration of new ways to be human, unaware that deep below them, in the ruins of their ancestors' assumptions, something green is growing in defiance of everything they know about their world.

The power understands that discovery must be *earned* to be meaningful. Someday—perhaps in a generation, perhaps in ten—some diver salvaging metal from the drowned times will swim deep enough to find it. A single shoot of impossible green, growing in an air pocket that should have dissolved centuries ago, fed by nutrients leached from the whale-klaxon's decomposing body, reaching upward toward a surface it will never see.

It will not save them. One plant cannot reverse an inverted ocean. But it will tell them something they have forgotten: that their world still contains surprises. That the collapse they have learned to inhabit so gracefully is not the final word. That somewhere in the architecture of their own past, in the sealed chambers and abandoned hopes of their Founders, there remain possibilities no one has imagined yet.

The power does not give them an ending. It gives them a *beginning they don't know about yet*—which is, after all, the only kind of beginning that matters.

---

**Then the power withdraws.**

Not because its work is finished, but because the rest of the work belongs to Tanakkerai. The people who learned to speak in three layers, who dissolved the boundary between self and city, who turned their own drowning into an art form that will be studied by whatever comes after them—these people do not need a god to solve their problems. They need what the power has given them: time, memory, and one small green secret growing in the dark.

The rest is up to them.

It always was.
