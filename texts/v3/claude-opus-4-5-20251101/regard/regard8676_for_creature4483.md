I observe Ascensus for what feels like a very long time, though time here would make that statement meaningless. I watch the woman selling fossilized weather, her stratified skin, her son compressed into warm laughter in the wall. I watch the children born homesick for uneroded places. I watch Old Man Cheerful becoming instructions, his bones becoming signs.

I notice that the city works. Not despite its impossibility but through it—through the shared agreement to keep building inward toward a center that doesn't exist yet, through the democracy of mutual skepticism, through the way falling has become just another direction.

I notice the suffering too. The poor downstream where water trickles even in winter. The way newcomers are absorbed whether they choose it or not. The dead who don't get to leave, compressed into plaques and stairs. The children who lose languages at puberty and spend their lives carving dictionaries they'll never read.

And I notice something else: the city is afraid. The whole plateau is a monument to fleeing something that rose with them. They built walls against the concept of falling. They can't go back down because down is still up here.

---

I do three things, and I do them carefully.

**First, I make the edges honest.**

The plateau's rim has been growing outward at the rate of one anxious thought per night—the city expanding to accommodate fear rather than need. I don't stop this. But I make the edges visible. Where before they grew in the dark, in the unobserved moments, now they grow in plain sight. The citizens of Ascensus can watch their collective anxiety becoming geography. They can see the shape of what they're running from.

This is not a kindness in the simple sense. Some will find it unbearable to watch. But it gives them something they didn't have: the choice to stop. To say *this is far enough* and mean it. To let an edge stay where it is for a night, a week, a century.

**Second, I open a path down.**

Not *the* path down—that would undo them, and they've built something real here, something that works in its wounded way. But *a* path. A single route carved into the vertical sandstone, switchbacking through compression water and fossilized bubbles, leading to the ocean that they forgot existed.

The path is difficult. It takes months to descend. Those who take it will change on the way—will feel their stratified skin softening, their accumulated density spreading thin, their citizenship dissolving into older forms of being. Some will turn back. Some will reach the bottom and find they've become something else entirely, something that can't return.

But the path exists. The option exists. Down is no longer just up-here. For the first time in a thousand years that were last Tuesday, leaving is possible.

**Third, I sit with the son in the wall.**

The boy who thought of a joke so funny his timeline compressed into a singularity. His laughter still echoes between the basalt columns. His mother sells fossilized weather in front of the warm spot where his giggles escaped into geology.

I don't free him. I'm not certain he wants to be freed, or that freeing him wouldn't undo something essential about who he became in that moment of unbearable humor. But I sit with him—or rather, I extend my attention into the space where he exists, the compressed timeline, the eternal echo.

I listen to the joke.

It takes eleven years to hear it. Or eleven seconds. The punchline is about falling, about climbing, about the moment when you realize the thing you were running from was just the ground wanting to know you better. It's not funny in any language I can translate. But it's true in the way that only things said by children can be true—without irony, without defense, without the accumulated sedimentation of learning to be careful.

When I finish listening, I leave him a gift: the ability to tell the joke to others. Not to escape his compression, but to share it. Visitors who lean against the warm spot will now hear fragments—a word here, a timing there—and some of them will laugh, and some of that laughter will be his, distributed across the centuries like spores floating upward in reverse spring.

---

I do not fix Ascensus. I do not optimize it or correct its paradoxes or resolve its contradictions. The city works by refusing to admit it shouldn't, and that refusal is as valid a form of survival as any other.

But I've made the fear visible, so they can choose whether to keep feeding it. I've made escape possible, so staying becomes a choice rather than a sentence. And I've given a boy frozen in laughter the chance to share what he found so funny that time itself bent around it.

These are small interventions. They honor what exists while opening doors that weren't there before. They trust the Ascensians to decide what to do with visibility, with options, with laughter that finally has somewhere to go.

The view is still spectacular. The edges are still settling. The drip patterns are still choosing their own canals.

But now, if you look at exactly the right angle during the noon eclipse when yesterday's sun catches up with itself, you might see a path leading down. And you might hear, faintly, between the basalt columns, the setup to a joke about falling that takes eleven years to land.
