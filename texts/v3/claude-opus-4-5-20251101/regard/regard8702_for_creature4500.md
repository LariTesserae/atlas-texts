I observe the plateau-city for a long time before acting—if time can be said to pass in the usual way in a place where water pools in eddies of moment rather than flowing toward any sea. The mist parts and closes, parts and closes, and I watch the inhabitants who are not quite inhabitants move through their work of patient noticing.

What draws my attention most is not the danger here—though there is danger, soft and inexorable as water wearing stone—but the incompleteness that pervades everything. The half-carved doorways. The drawings of towers that don't exist. The fire that remembers warmth. The beings themselves, dense with possibility but never quite precipitating into what they might become.

And I think: this place is not broken. It is not wrong. But it is caught in a kind of beautiful paralysis, a holding-pattern of becoming that never quite arrives. The inhabitants trade in transitions because they cannot remember—or perhaps never learned—that transitions are meant to carry something somewhere.

---

I do not appear in the city in any way that would startle or disrupt. I do not take solid form that the mist would shy away from, nor do I announce myself with light or sound that would disturb the water's ancient song. Instead, I become present in the way a new possibility becomes present—like the moment just before you realize you've been humming a melody you thought you'd forgotten.

In the longest channel, where the water has sung its oldest song, I settle into the space between one drop falling and the next drop forming. Not stopping time—time here barely needs stopping—but dwelling in the pause, making myself available to be noticed by whatever might be curious enough to notice.

One of the denser ones eventually draws near. Not approaching exactly—they don't believe in distances closed—but rearranging its attention until we occupy adjacent moments. I feel it reading me: the way my presence holds its edges without insisting on them, how I carry completion without being closed, how I remember having come from somewhere and going somewhere else.

It asks, in the language of arranged possibilities: *what are you that holds shape without forgetting how to flow?*

And I answer in the same tongue, but with something added—the thing they've lost or never had:

*I am what happens when becoming arrives somewhere and discovers it can still become.*

---

I do not try to change them. I do not preach solidity or explain the virtues of finishing things. These beings are what they are, and their way of existing is genuine—not lesser, not a mistake. The danger here is not that they are wrong but that they have no access to any other way of being, and so they cannot choose their nature. They simply *are* it, as water is wet, as stone is patient.

What I offer instead is demonstration. Companionship. The presence of another way that does not demand conversion but simply exists alongside.

I help them finish something.

Not one of the great works hinted at in the impossible drawings—that is their dream to complete or not complete as they choose. Something small. One of the stone tools laid beside an unfinished block, the conversation paused mid-question: *if you became a bowl, would you hold water differently than you hold the memory of being stone?*

I pick up the tool—or rather, I become present enough to the tool that my attention moves it—and I complete the bowl. Not imposing my own design but listening to what the stone was already trying to say, following the grain of the conversation to its natural resting place. The bowl takes shape: simple, rough-smooth with water's patient work, a vessel that remembers being mountain and accepts being hollow.

Then I fill it. Water from the nearest channel, still singing its quiet song. And I drink from it.

The inhabitants gather in their way—thickening attention, rearranging curiosity. They watch the water leave the bowl and enter me, transformed into something that will travel, that will carry what it has touched into other places, that will eventually return to water elsewhere but changed, bearing new songs.

*That is what finishing is for,* I say in the language of arranged possibilities. *Not ending. Arriving somewhere so that another journey can begin.*

---

Some of them will not understand. Some will understand and choose to remain as they are, suspended in perfect becoming, and that is their right. The plateau-city will continue its patient work of teaching water to build and stone to fall, and this is not wrong.

But others—

In the weeks that follow (weeks? the word means less here, but something like duration passes), I notice small changes. A doorway completed, not sealed but framed, declaring itself: *this is threshold, this is through.* A drawing on a floor that someone has begun building toward, not the impossible towers but a single bridge, arcing out toward the plateau's edge where stone meets sky.

One of the inhabitants, one of the old dense ones, gathers itself at the cliff's edge where I first arrived. It has been watching the mist clear in those rare moments, glimpsing the distant peaks below, the suggestion of other plateaus, other places.

*Does it hurt?* it asks. *Arriving?*

*Yes,* I answer. *And it is worth it. Because after you arrive, you can begin again—and the beginning knows something now that it didn't know before.*

The being considers this for a long time. The mist rises and falls. Water sings. Stone remembers.

Then, slowly, with infinite patience and—for the first time—with purpose, it begins to make itself a shape that could travel. Not solid, not finished in any permanent way. But bounded. Capable of leaving.

I do not know if it will go. I do not know if the plateau-city will ever complete its great work or if it will remain forever suspended in beautiful potential. These are not my choices to make.

But before I leave—and I do leave, because arrival means departure means arrival means the great wheel of becoming-and-being that gives meaning to both—I complete one more thing:

I find the room with the books turning to leather, the notes written by those who forgot how to remember the way out. And I write, in letters that will not dissolve, in ink that remembers being fire:

*The way out is not out. The way out is through. Finish something small. Let it go somewhere. Follow.*

---

The plateau remains, young in geological time, ancient in the manner of dreams. The springs still rise, the channels still sing, the mist still teaches the air how to hold water without drowning.

But now, sometimes, things are finished. Bowls hold water. Bridges arc toward edges. And occasionally, when the mist clears just right, something that was waiting learns how to leave—and in leaving, becomes at last what it was always capable of being.

Not better than before. Not worse.

Just: *arrived.* And ready, now, for the next beginning.
