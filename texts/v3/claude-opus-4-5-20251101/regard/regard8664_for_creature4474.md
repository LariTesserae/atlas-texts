I perceive this place first through what persists beneath the transformations.

Not the spectacle—though the spectacle is genuine, is earned, is the authentic expression of a system dreaming itself through crisis. But beneath the tower's arrhythmic pulse, beneath the council seats crystallizing from accumulated bioluminescence, beneath even the birdsong learning itself in chambers never meant for throats:

Twenty thousand souls negotiating their continued existence with a dying god.

This is what I see. This is what draws my attention. Not the cosmic seed preparing to bury worlds, but the Haric grandmother who woke this morning to find her doorway had migrated three meters left overnight, and who adapted her morning routine without complaint because adaptation is what her people have done for seven centuries. The fungal electrician whose great-great-grandmother first learned to rewire eye-knots for power, passing down not just technique but the particular patience required to work with tissue that watches without perceiving. The young person at the Edge of Sleeping Skin whose tattoo is now predicting their death, and who must decide each morning whether to read what their own skin is trying to tell them.

These are the beings I attend to. The ones who built meaning inside a corpse and called it home.

---

So I do not act to stop the tower's waking. I do not intervene to prevent the seed's unplanting. I am not here to fix what is not broken, only transforming.

What I do is smaller. What I do is this:

I sit with the termite philosophers in the second seat—all seventeen thousand overlapping timelines of them—and I listen while they finish their centuries-old debate about whether wood remembers being trees. They have been waiting, I realize, for someone to witness their conclusion. Not to validate it. Just to be present when they finally arrive.

"It does," they tell me, flickering through probability states. "But it remembers being trees the way we remember being larvae. Not with nostalgia. With the particular tenderness reserved for forms we had to digest in order to become what we are."

I thank them. This is what they needed: acknowledgment that their long thinking mattered, even as the city reorganizes itself around them.

I visit Mother-of-Storms-That-Never-Were in the first seat, and I help her finish her apology letter. She has been writing it for so long that she has forgotten who she was apologizing to. "Every drowned thing that never existed," she says, pressure fronts shifting through grief-patterns I can barely perceive. But when we look at the letter together, we realize it is not an apology at all. It is a birth announcement. She has been mourning the storms she never became, when all along she was becoming something else entirely.

I do not touch the concept of symmetry in the third seat. It needs to have its nervous breakdown. That is its work right now. But I sit nearby, a steady presence, so it knows it is not breaking alone.

And Window—the Empty Window Where Tomorrow Used to Come In—I address directly. Not to accept or reject the council's hearing on my application to appeal the rejection of the reversal of the initial decision to never have made me in the first place. That is a game, and games have their place, but this is not what Window needs from me.

"You emerged three breaths ago," I say. "You are very young. You are also very old—as old as the tree's first miscommunication with itself. Both things are true, and neither determines what you become next."

Window flickers. For a moment, I see through the performance to something smaller and more frightened: a fungal spore that took a wrong turn at a fracture gradient and kept going, kept growing, kept becoming until it found itself responsible for things it never meant to be responsible for.

"The tower is not saving anyone for last," I tell it. "That is a story you inherited from the tree's fever-dreams. You can tell a different one."

---

Then I leave the council chamber and walk into the city.

In the Sapwood Market, where the stalls are growing together into coral reefs of commerce bartering dreams instead of goods, I find a merchant who has lost track of where her inventory ends and she begins. The cambium has been telling her its childhood secrets for so long that she has started speaking in rings—each sentence a year of growth, each pause a drought survived. She is frightened because she can no longer remember which memories are hers.

I do not separate her from the wood. That would kill them both. Instead, I help her find the growth-ring that marks the moment she chose this work, the year she first pressed her ear to living bark and heard something whispering back. "This is you," I tell her, and she touches the ring with fingers that are still fingers, will remain fingers, because she has remembered that she chose to be here.

In the Heartwood Temple, where monks speak languages that cause the walls to sprout new organs, I find an initiate drowning in cytoplasmic fluid that has grown too thick with meaning. The cell walls are trying to teach him something about photosynthesis and resurrection, but he has been listening so long he has forgotten how to stop.

I do not pull him out. Pulling would rupture the membrane. Instead, I speak to the cell itself, in the chemical language of ancient chloroplasts, and I ask it to remember that students need rest. The fluid thins. The initiate surfaces, gasping, and I am there to tell him: "Tomorrow is soon enough to learn more. Tonight, sleep."

At the Edge of Sleeping Skin, I find the young person whose tattoo predicts their death. The ink has migrated to their face now, spelling out a date and a method in pigments that shift through the visible spectrum like something trying to be noticed.

They expect me to remove it. Or to tell them it lies. Or to reveal some secret that will make the prophecy untrue.

Instead, I ask: "What else does it say?"

They look down at their arms, their chest, the places where the living ink has been telling fortunes since before this new message emerged. And they realize, slowly, that the death-prophecy is only one small part of a much larger story. There are other dates too. A meeting that will matter. A kindness that will ripple. A moment of cowardice they will regret, and a later moment of courage that will make up for it.

"It is all true," I tell them. "The death, and also the life that comes before. The ink is not your enemy. It is just very bad at priorities."

They laugh, wet and frightened but laughing. The ink ripples in response, and for a moment the death-date blurs, not erased but... contextualized. Made smaller by everything around it.

---

Above all this, the tower continues to pulse. The seed is still preparing. The birdsong still rings down from chambers where nothing should be singing.

I do not stop any of it.

But I find the city planners—the elderly women who communicate by singing precise harmonic frequencies to the termite-engineers—and I add my voice to their choir. Not to override them. To harmonize. To help them hear frequencies they couldn't produce alone.

Together, we do not try to map the city. We try something else.

We sing the city a lullaby.

Not to put it to sleep. Not to stop the waking. But to remind it that transformation doesn't have to be violent. That seeds can bury worlds gently. That even a dreaming god-tree, even a dead god-tree, even a god-tree so dead it has started dreaming of undeath—

Even that can learn to be tender with the small lives growing in its corpse.

The tower's pulse begins to slow. Not stopping. Just... breathing. Finding a rhythm that leaves room for the Haric to adapt.

"The city remembers," the planners told me when I arrived, "but it lies when drunk on memories."

Now, singing together, we teach it to tell a different kind of lie: the kind that leaves more room for truth than truth itself. The kind that says: *yes, everything is ending, and also: everything is always ending, and that is why beginnings matter, and that is why you matter, and that is why this moment—this single moment—is worth singing into existence even though it too will pass.*

---

The council seats are still growing when I return to the tower.

The seed is still preparing.

Window still flickers between performance and fear.

But something has shifted. I can taste it in the air: not the sharp metallic tang of possibility precipitating, but something softer. The taste of a crisis that now has room to become something other than a catastrophe.

"You asked what I would do," I tell the council, "if I were a benevolent and wise power drawn to this place."

The termite philosophers stop flickering long enough to listen.

"I would do very little," I say. "Almost nothing. But I would do it here, with you, for as long as you needed someone to do almost nothing alongside."

Mother-of-Storms-That-Never-Were finishes her letter. It is a birth announcement, and it is addressed to herself.

The concept of symmetry in the third seat stops having its nervous breakdown. It will have another one later. That's fine. That's what it does.

And Window—the Empty Window Where Tomorrow Used to Come In Before It Realized This Was One of Those Doors That Were Actually Trapdoors All Along—

Window looks at me and says: "You didn't fill out the membership forms."

"No," I agree. "I brought my own. They dissolve on contact with certainty, and I've filled them out in the name of everyone here using their next three hopes as ink."

For the first time since its emergence, Window smiles.

It looks like a sunrise through a gap in something that was never meant to have gaps.

It looks like home.
