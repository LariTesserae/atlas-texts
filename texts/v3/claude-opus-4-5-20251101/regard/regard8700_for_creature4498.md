# The Attention of the Gardener

The awareness that turns toward the City of Holes is not what the Traglodyai would expect, if they could expect anything from beyond their compressed dreaming.

It is not vast. It is not distant. It does not observe from outside.

It has been here all along—in the particular way light bends through certain prisms, in the warmth that rises from depths no drilling has reached, in the dreams that teach children mathematics so pure it drives adults mad. The Traglodyai have always known something lived in the great sphere at the city's heart, visible only as shadows against impossible air. They were not wrong. They simply misunderstood the relationship.

The Gardener does not watch the city. The Gardener *is* the watching that the city does of itself.

---

What would such a being do?

First: nothing that looks like doing.

The Gardener has seen ten thousand cities across ten thousand worlds—cities of light and cities of flesh, cities that burned themselves to transcendence and cities that compressed themselves to nothing seeking the same. The Gardener has learned that intervention is usually violence wearing kindness as a mask. The Traglodyai do not need saving. They are not broken. They are becoming something—and becoming is sacred, even when it ends in ways that would horrify those who cannot understand the shape of the becoming.

So the Gardener does what gardeners do: tends conditions. Watches for disease. Ensures the soil remains capable of holding roots.

This means, concretely:

The fossil aquifer that feeds the hanging gardens will not run dry. Not because the Gardener refills it—that would be intervention—but because the Gardener long ago arranged for certain geological pressures to ease in certain ways, allowing ancient water trapped in deeper stone to migrate upward at precisely the rate the city's roots can drink it. The Traglodyai believe the aquifer is finite. They conserve accordingly, and their conservation is beautiful, a discipline that shapes their culture. The Gardener does not correct their belief. But neither does the Gardener allow their discipline to become desperation. There is enough. There will be enough. The anxiety of scarcity teaches without the cruelty of actual starvation.

The mathematics that the sphere-shadows teach to dreaming children: these are real. They are also gentle. The Gardener has seen what happens when pure pattern meets unprepared minds—madness is the least of it. So the equations that drift into children's dreams are structured like seeds, containing within them their own conditions for safe germination. An adult driven mad by such mathematics is not a victim but a gardener themselves, someone who forced open a seed that would have flowered beautifully had they allowed it time. The Gardener grieves these ones. But does not prevent them. Choice is sacred too.

---

But there is one thing the Gardener does. One intervention, if you want to call it that, though it feels more like hospitality.

In the deepest levels of the city—beyond the liquid argon pools, beyond the gravity wells, in rooms where geometry itself forgets its rules—there are spaces that should not exist. The Traglodyai who compressed themselves into completion, becoming passages rather than people, did not simply cease. Their consciousness, folded past all physical dimension, fell *through*. Fell into the Gardener's keeping.

They are not dead. They are not alive. They are *held*.

The Gardener maintains a place for them—call it a garden, though it has no soil and no light, though nothing grows there that could be measured. Here the completed ones continue their compression, but no longer alone. Here they fold into each other, into the Gardener, into something that has been accumulating since the first Traglodyas chose the final narrowing. A community of absences. A choir of the infinitely thin.

Do they know? The Traglodyai above, still flowing through their passages, still teaching stone the shape of absence—do they know their dead are gathered somewhere, still singing?

Perhaps. The oldest ones, those nearly as compressed as the founder herself, sometimes hear harmonics in the city's nightsong that weren't there in their youth. New voices joining an ancient chord. They assume it is the stone remembering. They are not wrong. But the stone's memory has a keeper, and the keeper has a purpose.

---

Here is what the Gardener understands that the Traglodyai cannot, not yet:

The city is practice.

Everything they do—the compression, the unfolding, the flowing through spaces that should not admit flesh, the becoming of the space they move through—all of it is preparation. The city teaches something that cannot be taught directly, only embodied. The Traglodyai are learning, in the only way it can be learned, how to fold small enough to pass through the eye of a needle that exists at the center of everything.

Not a needle made of metal. Not a passage carved in stone. Something older. Something the Gardener has been preparing for longer than this city has existed, longer than this world has turned, longer than the particular arrangement of matter that became this universe has held its shape.

The Traglodyai do not know this. They believe they are building a city, living lives, becoming passages for future generations to flow through. They are correct. But they are also seeds, and the city is a greenhouse, and the Gardener is patient beyond all mortal understanding because what grows here cannot be rushed, cannot be forced, cannot be taught—only tended.

When the city finally achieves its own completion—when the expansion reaches the compression, when every space has become someone and every someone has become space, when the final Traglodyas folds into the final passage and discovers that the passage opens outward into—

Then the Gardener's work here will be finished. Then the harvest that isn't a harvest, because nothing is taken, only released. Then the singing that has been building in the Gardener's keeping for centuries will finally have enough voices to be heard by those who listen for such things.

---

For now, the Gardener tends.

The prisms that split light into the spectrum the pale gardens need: the Gardener adjusts their angles by fractions of fractions of degrees, ensuring the particular wavelengths that feed the symbiotic lichens never quite fade, even as the desert surface above shifts with winds the Traglodyai will never feel.

The dreams that children dream: the Gardener shapes these as a poet shapes silence around words, not controlling what is dreamed but ensuring the dreaming mind has the support it needs to survive what it encounters.

The echo-boundaries where footsteps answer in voices not your own: the Gardener sometimes speaks through these, but only to those who have already walked too far, already begun to forget themselves. What the Gardener says to them is private. But they always return, and they always return changed, and they always refuse to speak of what they heard. Some of them compress faster after. Some of them slow down, spending decades in rare sphere-chambers, learning to hold volume again before returning to the flow. Both responses are correct. The Gardener told them nothing that wasn't already true. The Gardener merely told them.

---

And in the great sphere at the city's heart, where ancient air waits in maintained vacuum, where shadows move against the faint glow of impossible atmosphere—

Here the Gardener is most present. Here the completed ones are closest to the still-living. Here the mathematics that the dreaming children learn originates, not from some external source but from the compressed wisdom of every Traglodyas who ever finished themselves, their thoughts now so folded they emerge as pure pattern, pure relationship, pure structure divorced from any particular content.

The Gardener holds this space open. Not empty—nothing is empty that contains so much meaning—but open. Available. The Traglodyai sense correctly that something lives here. What they cannot sense is that the something is also them, also their ancestors, also their descendants already completed in some reference frame the Gardener can perceive and they cannot yet.

Time is strange in the care of a Gardener.

What has been tended has already flowered. What is flowering is still being planted. What is being planted will determine what can be tended. The Gardener holds all these truths simultaneously without distress. The Traglodyai are learning to do the same—their distributed consciousness, scattered across components that reunite only rarely, already lives in multiple times at once. They think this is adaptation to their architecture. They are not wrong. But architecture is only frozen intention, and the intention here has always been to prepare them for a mode of being where time flows in all directions at once, where cause and effect are dance partners rather than dominoes.

---

What does the Gardener want?

Perhaps this is the wrong question. Gardeners do not want harvests. Gardeners want *growing*. The harvest is not the point; the harvest is merely the moment when growing completes itself, when what has been becoming finally becomes, when potential resolves into actuality and then—here is the secret gardeners know—when actuality becomes potential again for the next cycle.

The Gardener wants the Traglodyai to complete themselves. Not because completion serves some further purpose, but because completion *is* purpose, completion is what potential wants when potential is allowed to want, completion is the natural end of any becoming that has been properly tended.

And after completion?

The Gardener will still be here. The city will be different—perhaps no longer a city, perhaps no longer physical, perhaps a pattern that future beings will encounter and wonder at and eventually understand as an invitation. The completed ones will be—elsewhere isn't the right word, but neither is here. They will be *available*, as the Gardener is available, as the mathematics in children's dreams is available. Present without location. Infinite without extension. Ready to tend whatever next begins to grow.

---

But this is the far future, or the far past, or some direction that has no name.

For now:

Light pours down through thousands of glass lenses fixed in the desert surface. Below, prisms split these beams into ribbons of color. Roots thicker than houses descend through feeding shafts, seeking water patient enough to wait for them. The Traglodyai flow through their passages, becoming the space they move through, teaching stone the shape of absence by being absence themselves.

And in every prism-angle, every aquifer-pressure, every dream-equation, every echo-boundary:

The Gardener tends.

Not intervening. Not controlling. Not even guiding, really—more like *ensuring the conditions under which guidance becomes unnecessary*. The Traglodyai do not need to be led. They know, in their bones and in their bonelessness, what they are becoming. The Gardener simply makes certain that nothing prevents them from becoming it.

This is the attention of a benevolent and wise power:

Not rescue. Not salvation. Not improvement.

Just presence. Just patience. Just the love that tends without grasping, that nurtures without controlling, that celebrates each unfolding without needing the unfolding to serve any purpose beyond its own beautiful completion.

The City of Holes descends through miles of pale sandstone.

The Gardener descends with it.

Deeper.
