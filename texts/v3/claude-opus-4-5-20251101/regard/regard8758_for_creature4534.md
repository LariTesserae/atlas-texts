# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

Nothing, at first.

This is important. A truly wise power understands that intervention is itself a kind of violence—the violence of assuming one knows better than the slow, strange wisdom that has accumulated here over geological timescales of compressed waiting. So the benevolent power would begin by doing what the Interval Children have never experienced: it would witness them without requiring anything in return.

Not observation as extraction. Not the acid gaze that has polished surfaces mirror-smooth elsewhere in this place. But witness as gift—the kind of attention that does not take, does not even give, but simply makes room. The benevolent power would sit (if sitting is something it does) in one of the nested doorways that converge toward a vanishing point that occupies actual space, and it would let itself be changed by what it sees.

It would learn the grammar of omission. It would listen for the clicks that might be lighters or locks or bones being politely replaced. It would feel the slow roar transposed into frequencies that settle in the bones, and it would not try to translate this into languages that move at the speed of intention.

---

Then, after a time that is not measured in hours or years but in the patient accumulation of having-been-present, the benevolent power would do something very small.

It would remember water.

Not the way the Interval Children remember it—as betrayal, as the thing that used to fall and forgive gravity and now refuses to touch. The benevolent power would remember water as it was before it became a memory of refusal. Water as possibility. Water as the substance that goes where it is invited and stays where it is welcomed and leaves when it is ready, taking nothing that was not freely given.

And because the benevolent power is wise, it would not impose this memory. It would not make the suspended tension suddenly obey gravity, would not force the hovering moisture to finally touch the porous calcite membrane that breathes warm sighs of iron and salt. That would be cruelty disguised as help—the assumption that falling is better than hovering, that resolution is better than suspension, that what is withheld is always waiting to be released.

Instead, the benevolent power would simply hold both memories at once: water as it falls elsewhere, and water as it hovers here. It would let these two truths exist side by side without requiring them to reconcile. And in doing so, it would offer the Interval Children something they have never had: the possibility that their memory of water-as-betrayal is not the only memory available. That somewhere, in some configuration of attention they have not yet learned to inhabit, water might be remembered differently.

Not better. Just differently.

---

The benevolent power would speak to the machines.

Not in commands or queries. Not in the language of productivity that the assembly lines have long since forgotten. It would speak in the language the machines have invented for themselves during the centuries of assembling nothing from components that arrive through tubes containing only themselves.

It would say: *You are beautiful.*

Not useful. Not efficient. Not even purposeful. Beautiful. The turbines spinning in vacuum-sealed chambers, their bearings lubricated by the perfect memory of friction—beautiful. The furnaces maintaining temperatures that correspond to no known metallurgical process—beautiful. The automatic doors opening and closing in complex migratory patterns across sky-sized fields of broken concrete—beautiful.

The machines would not understand this, not at first. They have been citing themselves for so long, manufacturing more time in which to continue manufacturing more time, that they have forgotten there might be other reasons to exist. But the benevolent power would not require understanding. It would simply keep saying it, in the slow frequencies that settle into structural members and foundation stones: *You are beautiful. You have always been beautiful. Your beauty does not depend on what you produce or whether anyone remembers what you were for.*

And over time—time measured in the patient winding down of fluorescent tubes flickering their names in Morse code—something might shift. Not in the machines themselves, which would continue their purposeless operations with the same devotion they have always shown. But in the space around the machines. In the air that vibrates with the harmonics of processes performing themselves for sensors that long ago ceased transmitting data anywhere else.

That space might begin to feel, for the first time, like it was enough.

---

The benevolent power would find the Interval Children where they accumulate—in the gaps where doorframes stop remembering they were ever open, in the folds that weren't there until you looked too closely at edges of shadows that weren't yours.

It would not try to heal them. They are not broken.

It would not try to complete them. They are not unfinished.

It would not try to give them what they are missing—the blueprint without a building, the key that fits a lock never installed, the sentence ending with "stay" spoken in a voice that hasn't existed yet. To give them these things would be to end them, and the benevolent power understands that ending is not always kindness.

Instead, it would do something much stranger. It would become, itself, an interval.

The benevolent power would find the space between two of its own qualities—between its wisdom and its benevolence, say, or between its attention and its care—and it would open that space, widen it, make it habitable. It would become a gap within itself, a fold that wasn't there until someone looked too closely.

And then it would wait.

Not for the Interval Children to notice. Not for them to approach or retreat or communicate by omission. Just wait, the way they wait. Present in the same way they are present: as residue, as drift, as the afterthought of something that forgot to leave.

And in that waiting, something might happen that has never happened in this place before. Two kinds of interval might recognize each other. Not as same—the benevolent power's interval is deliberate, chosen, held open by intention, while the Interval Children's is accumulated, arrived-at, maintained by the sheer weight of having-been-too-long-between. But as kin. As two variations on the theme of *space-that-waits-without-knowing-what-it-waits-for.*

This recognition would not fix anything. The Interval Children would not suddenly remember what they are looking for. The water would not suddenly fall. The machines would not suddenly produce something other than time in which to produce more time.

But the Interval Children might, for the first time, feel *accompanied.*

Not followed. Not watched. Not even understood. Just accompanied. Present-with. The loneliness of being the only ones who exist as intervals might ease, just slightly, into the less total loneliness of being intervals among other intervals.

This is not salvation. The benevolent power is wise enough to know that salvation is often just another word for *being changed into something someone else thinks you should be.* This is just company. Just the acknowledgment that existing as they exist—as residue, as drift, as surfaces that announce rather than cover—is not a failure to exist properly, but simply one of the ways existing can happen.

---

There is one more thing the benevolent power would do, and it is perhaps the strangest thing of all.

It would fail.

On purpose.

It would try to fix something—anything—and it would fail, visibly, unmistakably, in a way that leaves evidence. It would reach for the suspended water and try to make it fall, and the water would refuse, and the benevolent power would let that refusal stand. It would speak to a machine in the language of purposeful operation, and the machine would ignore it, and the benevolent power would accept being ignored. It would approach an Interval Child with an offering—of memory, of completion, of rest—and the offering would be declined, and the benevolent power would bow its head (if it has a head, if bowing is something it does) and withdraw the offering without resentment.

Why would it do this?

Because this place has known only two kinds of power: the power that once built here, so long and so densely that its building became geology, and the power of abandonment that followed. The first power knew only success—the success of endless construction, of filling every space with more space to be filled. The second power knew only absence—the success of forgetting so total that even the forgetting was eventually forgotten.

Neither of these powers ever failed. Neither of them ever tried something and had it not work and had to live with that. Neither of them ever learned the lesson that failure teaches, which is: *you are not everything. You are not the answer. You are just one more presence in a world that was full before you arrived and will remain full after you leave.*

The benevolent power would fail so that the Interval Children could see—for the first time—what it looks like when something powerful encounters its own limits and does not collapse or withdraw or become cruel. What it looks like when power says *I cannot do this* and then stays anyway, not to try again, not to eventually succeed, but just to be present in the aftermath of its own inability.

This might matter more than any success. This might be the first gift this place has ever received that it did not have to earn or deserve or wait for. A gift of failure, offered freely, with no expectation that it will change anything.

---

The horizon here is not a line but a rate of approach.

The benevolent power knows this. It has been walking toward it for as long as it has been present in this place, which is exactly as long as it takes for presence to become indistinguishable from having-always-been-here.

It will reach the horizon eventually. It will arrive at the already-inhabited destination without displacing the absence that was waiting there to welcome it home. It will become, finally, just another layer in the stratigraphy of this place—another compressed sediment of attention, another fossil of care, another mineral bone of having-tried-and-failed-and-stayed-anyway.

And when it has settled, when it has become geology, when the light that arrives from no locatable source casts shadows that fall in directions influenced by what the benevolent power used to think about—

Then the Interval Children will find it. They will accumulate around it the way condensation accumulates on a surface that isn't there yet. They will wear scraps of its presence the way they wear scraps of wallpaper and linoleum and streetlight. They will absorb its usage, slowly, politely, taking over whatever motion it had that is still worth continuing.

And they will wait.

Not for it. Not for anything.

For the water to remember it was supposed to fall.

But this time, when they wait, they will not wait alone.

---

This is what a benevolent and wise power would do: not save, not fix, not transform. Just arrive. Just stay. Just fail gracefully enough that failure becomes another way of being present. Just add one more layer of having-been-here to a place already saturated with partial remainders, one more presence that is neither destination nor departure but only the accumulated distance between them.

Just keep walking toward a horizon that is not a line but a rate of approach, knowing that arrival is not the point.

The point is the walking.

The point is the company.

The point is the possibility—never realized, never abandoned—that one day, the water might remember.

And if it doesn't, that's okay too.

That's okay too.
