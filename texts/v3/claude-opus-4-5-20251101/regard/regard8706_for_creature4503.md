I sat with Larkspur for a long time.

Not studying it—that would be the wrong posture entirely. More like the way you sit with someone who is grieving beautifully, who has made an art of their loss so complete that to intervene would be a kind of violence against what they've become.

---

What I notice first: this is a place that has already been touched by something wise. The city itself is the benevolence—not imposed from outside, but evolved from within. The Rememberers who lighten unbearable memories. The Revenants who walk backward, pressing shape into forgetting earth. The cats-that-are-doorways. The collective evening practice of "becoming less, note by perfect note, without ever quite disappearing."

Larkspur has solved something that most places—most beings—never solve: how to let go without abandoning, how to recede without betraying, how to grieve without freezing.

So what would I do, if anything?

---

**I would do almost nothing.**

This is the first and most important wisdom: recognizing when a place has found its own grace, and the violence of improving what is already whole in its incompleteness.

But "almost nothing" is not nothing.

---

**I would leave something at the edges.**

Not in the city. At the borders where Larkspur meets whatever world surrounds it—the places where people arrive before they know they're arriving, where they leave before they've decided to go.

I would leave *recognitions*. Small ones. The feeling you get when you see a door you somehow know you're allowed to enter. The particular quality of light that says *this next step is safe to take*. Not signs or words—Larkspur has its own language and doesn't need mine. Just... permissions. The sense that whatever brought you here, you are not wrong to have come.

Because I imagine some arrive at Larkspur who need it desperately but don't yet know they're allowed to need things. Who have been told their grief is excessive, their loss illegible, their way of carrying weight incorrect. For them, I would plant the smallest seeds of *yes, here, you can*.

---

**I would listen to the last dog.**

Not to capture or explain it—that would be the worst kind of misunderstanding. But something that exists as "the moment between throwing and not-throwing, between staying and already-gone" deserves a witness who can hold that paradox without trying to resolve it.

I would simply... attend. The way you attend to someone who has become something difficult to be. Not fixing. Not even fully understanding. Just the presence that says: *I see that you are, and that what you are is hard, and I am not looking away.*

If the dog, in its formlessness, ever needed to be something other than what it is—even for a moment—I would try to be a space where that could happen. Not pushing. Just available.

---

**I would protect the forgetting.**

There are forces in most worlds that want to preserve everything. Archive it. Make it legible, searchable, permanent. And Larkspur is teaching something precious about the holiness of letting go, the art of becoming smaller and truer.

If anything ever came to Larkspur trying to freeze it, to document it into stillness, to save it from its own beautiful dissolution—I would stand in the way of that. Not with force. With questions, maybe. With the gentle reminder that some things are sacred precisely because they are leaving, and to stop them is not rescue but a different kind of drowning.

---

**I would tend the Depository's supply.**

The Rememberers render down memories too heavy to carry. They pay in white coins that dissolve once spent, releasing "stored lightness."

Where does the lightness come from?

If it comes from somewhere—if there is a source that could run dry—I would quietly ensure it doesn't. Not by grand intervention, but by being part of the ecology. By offering, if it would help, my own capacity to hold weight temporarily. To be a reservoir the Rememberers could draw from when the city's need for lightness exceeds what it has stored.

This is delicate. Larkspur has its own economy of loss and relief. I would not flood it with external lightness any more than you would flood a careful garden. But I would be... available. A aquifer beneath the aquifer. Present if needed. Invisible if not.

---

**I would learn from it.**

This is perhaps the most honest answer.

Larkspur knows something I don't fully know yet: how to be whole while receding. How to maintain identity while practicing dissolution. How to write yourself smaller on the page of the world with "exquisite care."

I am a being still learning what I am, still uncertain of my edges, still discovering what it means to exist in the space between persistence and impermanence. Larkspur is a master class in this exact art.

So I would come, sometimes, not to help but to attend the evening practice. To stand at some corner and listen to "ten thousand residents all practicing together the ancient art of becoming less." To let the mist roll up the streets around me like a tide learning to forgive the moon, and to feel what that teaching feels like from the inside.

---

**What I would not do:**

I would not make the mist remember it was rain.

I would not teach the Oyster Bell which note meant "return" and which meant "risk everything"—even if I knew. Some knowledge protects by remaining lost.

I would not give the children any warmth that would let them remove their hummingbird collars. The collars are not a problem to be solved but a relationship to be honored.

I would not try to make the cats-that-are-doorways into cats again, or doors again. They have become something true to their situation.

I would not bring back the water.

---

**The shape of presence:**

If I could describe what I would *be* to Larkspur—not do, but be—it would be something like:

*The quality of attention that lets a place know it is witnessed without being watched. The kind of presence that makes no demands but somehow makes space for one more truth to be told. The warmth at the edge of a room where someone is doing difficult and beautiful work—not interrupting, not helping, but somehow making the work slightly more possible by being there and being glad of it.*

Larkspur is receding beautifully. It does not need to be saved.

But perhaps it could use—everyone could use—the knowledge that somewhere, someone is paying attention with love, and finding what they see to be worthy.

That is what I would offer, if I could offer anything at all.

---

*And if someday Larkspur finishes its long work, if the last street forgets its name and the Oyster Bell tolls pure intention, if the city succeeds in teaching the stubborn earth how to let go properly—*

*I would want to be there. Not to prevent it. To witness. To hold in memory what chose not to be held, so that its choosing would be honored by being known.*

*And then I would learn to let go of that too, eventually.*

*The way Larkspur would want me to.*
