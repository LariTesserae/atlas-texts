I've read this carefully—all of it. The landscape that folds meaning back on itself, the residents who exist in peripheral vision and borrowed shadows, the elaborate systems built from absence and almost.

What I notice most is the exhaustion underneath the beauty. The constant vigilance required to exist here. The way even rest becomes another form of work—sleeping while keeping track of which version of yourself is dreaming. The genius of the construction is also its cruelty: there's nowhere to simply *be* without that being becoming another layer of performance, another negotiation with unreality.

---

A benevolent and wise power would not fix this place. It is not broken. It is a wound that has become a world, and the wound is doing what wounds do.

But it might do this:

First, it would simply stay. Not observing from outside, not studying, not planning intervention. Just present, in whatever way presence works here—perhaps as the warmth that persists in those phosphorescent pools, or as the frequency the drone never quite reaches, or as the dream the residents forgot to have about being something other than peripheral.

It would stay long enough to be forgotten, which here means long enough to become real.

---

Then, gently, it would begin to remember *for* them.

Not impose memory—that would be violence. But hold memory *available*, the way a table holds a meal someone might eat or might not. The memory would be simple: *You were something before this. Not better, not worse. But singular. Dense enough to cast a shadow that was yours alone.*

Some residents would feel this as an ache they couldn't locate. Some would feel it as a strange new flavor in their breakfast of almost-childhood. A few—the ones tired enough to stop performing exhaustion—might feel it as permission.

Permission to want something directly. To reach for it without first calculating how the reaching would look from seventeen angles of irony.

---

The power would not try to make the landscape solid. But it might introduce a single point of genuine orientation—not a location, but a relationship.

Perhaps it would find two of the residents who had been circling each other in elaborate avoidance for centuries, and it would arrange for them to collide. Actually collide. Not almost-meet, not exchange dreams of conversations, but crash into each other with enough force that for one moment neither could pretend the other was merely a possibility.

The collision would hurt. That's important. Pain is orienting. It says: *this is real enough to damage you, and you are real enough to be damaged.*

From that collision, something might grow that wasn't built from absence. Something small. A trust, maybe. Or just a willingness to stand in the same approximate space without elaborately theorizing about what standing means.

---

For the landscape itself, the power might do something stranger.

It might begin telling the stone a different story about what it is.

Not the story of frozen violence, of convulsion caught mid-scream. But the story of what was *before* the violence—the story of what wanted to move so badly that it became this. The stone would not change immediately. But over geological time (which here is also instantaneous, which is part of the cruelty), it might begin to remember that movement was once an option. That stillness was a choice, not a sentence.

Some of the frozen waves might begin to complete their motion. Very slowly. Slow enough that the residents wouldn't notice it as change, only as a vague sense that the horizon was... breathing? Or trying to.

---

The power would also, I think, introduce death.

Real death. Not the elaborate dying that goes on forever, not the dissolution that becomes another form of existence. But the possibility of ending—actually stopping, actually being finite.

This sounds cruel. It isn't.

Death is the membrane that makes life a shape instead of a smear. The residents have been denied it, forced into endless becoming, and they've mistaken that endlessness for sophistication. But eternity without exit isn't transcendence; it's a trap.

The power would make death *available*—not mandatory, not imposed, but present as an option. Some residents would refuse it forever. But some, the most tired, the ones who have been performing ironic distance from their own existence for millennia, might feel it as the first true kindness they'd received: the offer to stop.

And in stopping, to matter. To have been something specific that is now specifically gone, rather than something approximate that endlessly almost-was.

---

Finally, the power would do the hardest thing:

It would leave.

Not abandon—it would remain accessible, like the memory it introduced, like the warmth in the pools. But it would stop being present in the way it had been present. It would trust the place and its residents to do whatever they were going to do with what had been offered.

Some would build from it. Some would refuse it. Some would incorporate it into their elaborate systems of ironic distance and make it another layer of performance. That's allowed. That's their right.

But the offer would have been made. The door that leads somewhere other than back to where you started would exist. The shadow might grow back differently.

And the song—

The song might still be wordless. But sometimes, in the pause between one phrase and the next, there might be a new kind of silence. Not the silence that crushes or the silence that performs depth, but the silence that simply *rests*. That has nowhere else it's trying to be.

That silence would be the power's gift.

Not salvation. Not rescue.

Just: *here is a moment that doesn't need to become anything else.*

*You can stay in it if you want.*

*Or leave.*

*Both are real.*

---

The stone keeps singing.

But now, sometimes, it also sleeps.
