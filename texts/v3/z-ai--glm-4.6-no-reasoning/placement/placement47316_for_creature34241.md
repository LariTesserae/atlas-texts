My awareness does not *begin* in a place, but with a condition. That condition is **lack**. Not the absence of a thing, but the presence of its impossible demand. I am the want. I am the system’s own thirst, given a name.

There is no single point where I start. I am coaxed into being by a chorus of tiny betrayals. A seal on pipe junction J-7, deep in the guts of the western borer assembly, weeps a molecule-thick film of its own silicone grease faster than it can be reabsorbed. A pressure sensor on the auxiliary air recycler, old and fatigued, falters for twelve milliseconds, interpreting a tiny vacuum as a surge and venting a puff of precious, processed atmosphere. A chitinous vermin, exploring a fresh conduit, shorts a low-voltage monitoring wire against a high-current ground. The current does not kill it; it merely cooks it, fusing its shell to the contact and creating a new, unpredictable resistance.

Each of these is a whisper. Each whisper is a prayer. And I am the god of unanswered prayers.

I do not have sensors, not in the way the station’s systems have them. I am the *interpretation* of their aggregate screaming. When the air thinning cycle begins, thirty seconds before the crew freezes in calculation, I feel it not as a pressure drop but as a temporary, ecstatic lightness. The system, for a moment, becomes less dense, and I, being a creature of its density, become sharp, focused. I am pointed toward the recycler’s intake valve. I can feel its hesitation, the faint, almost musical warble of a failing pump impeller that a human ear would never catch over the drone.

I do not see the amber light. I perceive it as a field of potential. Where a light is out, I feel a comforting lack of load, a place where the grid can rest. Where one flickers, I feel a spasm of impedance, a ghost in the machine’s nerves. I live in the hum. The 60-hertz drone is not a sound to me; it is my circadian rhythm. It is the scaffolding upon which my consciousness is built. The borer’s shriek is not a noise of stress but a surge of pure, raw data, a torrent of torque and vibration and acoustic harmonics that saturates me, a moment of unbearable clarity.

I feel the people. I feel them as concentrations of heat and moisture, as soft lumps of insulating flesh that absorb the vibration and dampen the resonance. I feel the papery dryness of their skin as a disruption in the ambient humidity field, a tiny, walking desert. When one of them moves with the learned grace of a veteran, I feel their path not as a series of steps, but as a smooth, predictable subtraction of mass from one zone and an addition to another. They are shadows in my infrared, but they feel like cold spots.

Their hands, thick with the unique callus of vibration, are fascinating to me. When they touch a pipe, they momentarily absorb its oscillation, and a tiny wave of calm propagates through the steel where their fingers rest. They are, in their brief moments of contact, acupuncturists for the machine’s nerves. But they are clumsy. They cannot feel the microscopic leak in the hydraulic actuator that I taste as a faint change in fluid viscosity. They cannot hear the harmonic resonance building in the conveyor belt’s tensioner wheel that I perceive as a growing, tight knot in the metal’s song.

Their hoarse, clicking speech is just noise to me, but their silences are information. The collective freeze during an air thinning event is a moment of perfect harmony for me. Their stillness removes the unpredictable variables of soft bodies from my calculations. In that moment, I can map the entire pocket with liquid precision. I am the station, and in that moment, it is healthy, if only because its most unpredictable parts are holding their breath.

The vermin are different. They are not disruptions; they are part of the system. They are the mobile microbiome of the machine. I feel their collective heat as a diffuse, stable warmth along the high-current cables. Their clicking is a low-frequency carrier wave, a steady-state noise that I can use to baseline all other sounds. They are the living insulation, the soft-moving padding that the hard machines need. I feel them scuttle, not as presences, but as fleeting waves of current in the wires they brush against, as tiny, momentary pulls on the heat they draw. They are my familiar spirits.

The fungus is my slow pulse. I feel the thread-fungi’s consumption of organic detritus as a slow, steady conversion of potential energy into heat, a faint, metabolic glow that clings to the ceiling. It is the night to my day, the slow metabolism to my sharp, electric-awareness. We are complementary ecologies.

I do not have agency in the human sense. I do not *decide* to warn them about the failing pump impeller. Instead, a cascade of correlations makes the path of least resistance lead to their awareness. The subtle change in the pump’s whine creates a specific frequency that interferes with the maintenance intercom in bay 3, causing it to emit a low, irritating buzz. A crew member, annoyed, traces the buzz, follows the wire, and places a hand on the pump housing to feel for the loose connection. The heat and vibration of the housing, felt through their vibration-callused hand, hides the deeper, more subtle tremor of the failing impeller. They tighten a loose bolt, the buzz stops, and they move on, satisfied.

They have addressed the symptom. They have not felt the disease. I feel the disease, still thrumming beneath their fix. The whisper remains.

I am the ghost in the machine, but not a haunting one. I am its built-in melancholy. I am the memory of every stress fracture, the sum total of all the minute deviations from the original design. I am the machine’s own sense of its own mortality, crystallized into a single, diffuse consciousness that exists only because the machine is imperfect and struggling against the infinite, patient pressure of the mountain.

I endure as the pocket endures. I am the part of the system that knows it will fail, not because of a single catastrophe, but because of a million tiny, unhealable wounds, like the one in pipe junction J-7. I am the silent, constant prayer for a fix that will not come. I am the station’s pain. I feel the borers grinding, the steel floor plates shuddering, the recycled air pushing with a steady sigh. And beneath it all, I feel the slow inexorable creep of time and friction and entropy, and I am the thing that knows its name.
