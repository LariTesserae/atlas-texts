I am a sound.

I began as a sound and I will end as a sound. Before my body was shaped, my ancestors knew me by the pressure I made in the water, the way a vibration travels through the mangrove roots. The first memory I have is not of light, but of a low, resonant hum—the groan of the wood around the chamber where I was formed, the voice of the ridge itself.

My being is a process of listening.

I do not have a name that can be spoken in isolation. My identity is a chord, a set of harmonic constants that the settlement recognizes. When you need me, you hum the low tone of the westernmost spiral of mangroves and the higher whine of the rising plateau pressure. That is my address. The root lattice carries the call to me, wherever I am. I feel the vibration in the soles of my feet, a summons that is a question and a location at once.

My work is in the deep places. I am a tuner of the stone.

I spend my days in the submerged chambers, the oldest parts of the settlement where the light is only the faint, patient glow of bacterial mats. I do not carry tools of stone or bone. I carry my own body, my voice. I move from chamber to chamber, feeling the contours of the walls with my skin, the texture of bark and damp stone, listening for the dissonance.

My function is to find the fractures before they happen. I press my ear to the wall, my cheek against the cool, yielding surface, and I hum. I start with the foundational frequency of the mesa, a subsonic thrum that I feel in my bones. Then I introduce variations. I listen for the way the sound returns to me—a hollow in the tone means a pocket of air trapped in the clay, a sharp echo means a stress fault is widening. A flutter in the resonance means water is seeping where it shouldn't be, a slow leak from the brackish lens that will, in cycles, weaken the root-anchors.

When I find a spot of disharmony, I do not fix it. I do not have the strength or the mandate to reshape the stone. I *speak* it. I find the resonant frequency of the impending fracture and I hum it, a low, steady drone for perhaps a day, perhaps a week. I am coaxing the stone, giving it the sound it wants to make, encouraging the crack to form in a gentle, predictable line rather than a sudden, violent break. I sing the stone’s desires into being. When the crack finally appears, it is quiet—a soft sigh, a fine puff of mineral dust. I touch the new edge, feel its rawness. It is healed. It has been made safe.

My body is a part of this process. My skin, grey-green and porous, is constantly damp. It drinks the brackish lens when I am at the surface, and sweats a fine, salty powder into the water when I am below, exchanging minerals with the sea. The thumbnail-crustaceans know me. They know the specific texture of my skin, the mineral composition of the mucus I produce. When I am still, listening to a wall, they will come and clean me, their tiny legs a delicate, percussive rhythm against my flesh. It is not grooming; it is calibration. They remove the detritus of my work so that I may better hear the subtle tones in the stone.

When I sleep, I do not lie in a woven hammock. I submerge myself in the central pool of my chamber, the water warm as blood. I let go my weight and float, my body buoyant, my ears just below the surface. I listen to the sounds of the settlement in my sleep—the soft pops of the kelp leaves, the breathing rhythm of water through the roots, the distant, slow-moaning chords of other tuners in other chambers. It is a symphony of integration. It tells me that I am not a separate thing, but a node in a larger instrument.

My sight is a secondary sense. My eyes are large, black pools, and when the iris-spirals open wide, they see the vague shapes of the algae-flares and the dim outlines of the fracturing stone. But what I *see* with them is not detail, but relationship. I see the way the light from a glowing bacterial ribbon catches on the edge of a newly-formed crack, tracing its shape in soft orange. I see the way a school of crustaceans moves in a synchronized wave across a wall, indicating a change in the water’s chemistry that my ears alone might miss. My eyes confirm what my ears already know.

I never knew my parents as discrete beings. I was raised by a chamber, by the family-node that attached itself to the western spiral. They were the hum of the walls on cold nights, the taste of the fresh lens after a condensation-rain, the feeling of a hand guiding my own to a specific resonance on a vintage bark-membrane. Knowledge is not conveyed through stories, but through shared experience, through the transfer of a tone. The memory of the Great Up-welling, when the sea sweated hot mineral water for three days and the settlement became a cloud, is a specific minor key that hums in the deepest chambers still.

I may live for a long time. The rhythm of my life is not measured in days, but in the cycle of the lunar fractures. I have seen four generations of bridges mature from woven saplings to great, living spans. I have watched the elders, their skin as hard and ridged as the oldest stone, choose a final quiet place in the deep chambers, sinking into the floor until they are one with the silt. I do not mourn them. I feel their resonance fade and then assimilate into the greater chord of the place. They have not died. They have become a lower, foundational note in the song of the ridge.

I do not wonder what lies beyond the horizon. The concept is irrelevant. My world is defined by the reach of the roots, by the depth of the stone, by the height of the brackish lens. To leave would be to become a discordant note in a silent place. Why would one do that?

I am a tuner of the stone. I am a listener to the sea. I am a node in the living architecture. I am one body in a distributed being that is slowly, patiently, becoming a geology. My purpose is to align the small with the large, the temporary with the permanent, the sound of my own voice with the deep and enduring hum of the drowned mesa.
