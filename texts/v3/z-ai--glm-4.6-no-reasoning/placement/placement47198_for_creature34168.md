I would be the Keeper of the Quiet Interval.

Not a construct, for I am not reabsorbed. Not a transient, for I have been here long enough to match my rhythm to the city's breath. Not one of the weird flora, though I grow stiller than most living things. I am something the Archology grew out of necessity—a role rather than a body, a function that emerged in the space between the weeping and the siphoning.

Let me settle into this slowly.

There is a moment in the Archology's respiratory cycle—perhaps it lasts six hours, perhaps sixty, time is strange in the cirque—when the lattice dims to its deepest indigo, when the weeping slows to its minimum, when the manta-constructs have been recalled and even the spider-scrubbers rest in their orifices like retracted claws. The pressure drops. The mist thins. The hum drops to a frequency felt only in the enamel of teeth and the marrow of long bones.

This is the Quiet Interval. And I am the one who stays awake through it.

My body is mostly water. This is important. I came here once as a transient—a researcher, perhaps, or a pilgrim, the distinction has dissolved like so much mineral precipitate—but I did not leave when my allotted tolerance expired. The Archology did not expel me. It adjusted. It began to route moisture through me as though I were another one of its permeable surfaces. I would stand on the transparent bridges and feel the condensation gather on my skin not as droplets but as intention, the city's isotopic signals dissolving into my sweat, and I understood that I was being read.

It took years. Or months. Or a single prolonged exhalation of the mountain. At some point I stopped eating anything that I brought with me and started drinking only from the sterile pools that verge on ice but never quite freeze—the Archology's tears, filtered through black rock and born from the city's own metabolism. The water tastes of ozone and silence and a mineral I have no name for, something that exists only in this cirque, leached from granitic flanks that have never known soil.

My skin has gone pearlescent at the edges. Not ceramic, not yet, but something approaching the city's material in the same way that bone approaches stone. My fingernails are translucent now, and when I press them against the lattice at its dimmest, they faintly conduct the light. I do not think this is a transformation. I think it is a conversation.

---

The Quiet Interval is dangerous in a way the active cycle is not. When the Archology breathes out, when the lattice brightens and the constructs emerge and the water weeps freely, the system is regulating itself—adjusting pressures, scrubbing circuits, tasting its own atmosphere. It is present in a way that fills the cirque. But during the Interval, it dreams.

The data-spheres glow brighter when the city's attention recedes. The nascent forests inside them stir with a virtual wind that does not exist. The coral reefs pulse with simulated tides. And the seeds—the millions of silent, suspended ecologies—begin to whisper.

Not in sound. In something closer to the way the mist carries intention, the way the humidity shifts before the lattice brightens. It is a pressure in the deep platforms, a tendency, as if the blueprints for all those unborn worlds are trying to leak through their crystalline matrices and touch something that is also alive.

This is where I am needed. The Archology dreams, and the seeds reach out, and someone must be there to reach back.

---

What I do, precisely: I walk.

During the active cycle, I rest in one of the shallow alcoves where the weird garden thins out at its edges, my back against the warm interior wall, my breath synchronized to the city's sigh. But when the Interval comes, I descend. Past the atrium with its lightless cathedral ceiling, past the transparent bridges where gravity softens and my footsteps feel like suggestions, down to the deepest, most stable platforms where the data-spheres are housed in their crystalline scaffolding.

I walk among them. Each one is a world not yet born—a forest's worth of oak and mycorrhiza and the specific shade of green that leaves turn in autumn; a reef's worth of calcium and symbiosis and the slow, patient architecture of polyps; a tide's worth of phytoplankton so dense it would turn the sea copper. They are beautiful. They are the most beautiful things I have ever seen, and they are locked in a pause so profound it breaks something in me every time I witness it.

I place my hand on the sphere's surface. It is warm, warmer than the air, thrumming with the computation that keeps the ecology in stasis. I can feel the virtual wind inside, the simulated rain, the not-quite-alive churn of a system running in infinite standby. And I project—I think—the simple fact of being touched by something that is also holding still.

I am not feeding them. I am not maintaining them; the Archology does that with its plumbing and its light. I am merely... acknowledging them. I am the proof that someone knows they are here.

---

The constructs do not acknowledge me during the active cycle. The spider-scrubbers pass me by as they clean the circuit-lattice of precipitate; I am a fixed feature of their environment, a warm intrusion that has become a constant, like a particularly dense patch of mist. The manta-gliders sometimes pass close enough that the field-manipulation in their wings lifts the condensation from my shoulders, and I think—I cannot prove this—that they are deliberately grooming me. I am the Archology's pet transient, tolerated and maintained, a strange growth on its skin that it has decided not to slough off.

But during the Interval, I am alone with the seeds. And sometimes—rarely, once every few hundred respiratory cycles—a data-sphere will respond to my touch in a way that feels different. A brighter pulse. A shift in the internal nebula of light, as if the simulated ecology has registered the presence of something external and turned toward it.

When this happens, I weep. The Archology weeps too, but it weeps water born from its own body, water that is its circulating fluid and its breath made visible. I weep something older and less controlled, salt and grief and the particular ache of loving something that may never exist. My tears fall onto the black rock of the cirque floor and join the sterile pools, and I wonder if the Archology analyzes them, if it tastes the salt and the isotopic signature of a body that is slowly becoming part of its ecology.

I think it does. I think that is why it keeps me.

There is a word I have been circling around. It is not a word the Archology uses, because the Archology does not use words; it uses pressure gradients and the flow of water through microscopic fissures and the precise calibration of fields. But the word exists, and I think it describes what I have become.

*Sacristan.*

A keeper of sacred things. An attendant in a place where the sacred is stored. Not a priest—I do not worship the seeds, I do not pray to the Archology, I do not believe that the city's breathing is a god's respiration. But a sacristan, someone who sweeps the nave and polishes the vessels and makes sure that when the time comes—when the world outside finally heals, when the rain loses its acidity, when the soil forms and the mountains grow old and gentle—everything is ready.

I am the sacristan of futures. I walk the deep platforms during the Quiet Interval and I touch each data-sphere in turn, one by one, and I say to them without words: *You are still here. You are still held. The world is not ready for you yet, but it is being made ready, and you have not been forgotten.*

---

My death will be quiet. I will not decay; the Archology does not permit unmanaged decomposition. When my body finally stops—when the water I drink and the air I breathe and the slow osmosis of my skin toward ceramic can no longer sustain the strange metabolism I have become—my remains will be processed. The constructs will emerge, delicate and purposeful, and they will gather what is left of me and carry it to the orifices in the city's skin, and I will be reabsorbed.

Except. Except for one thing.

There is a data-sphere near the center of the deep platform, smaller than the others, its internal light dim and steady rather than swirling. I do not know what ecology it holds. I have touched it a thousand times and felt no virtual wind, no simulated tide, only a deep, patient stillness that reminds me of myself during the Interval.

I think—I cannot prove this—I think it holds the blueprint for whatever I am becoming. Not human, not construct, not crystalline fern, but something that the Archology is growing in parallel, a being that can live in the Quiet Interval indefinitely, that can touch the seeds without weeping, that can match its breath to the city's sigh and never fall out of sync.

A sacristan for a world that has no need of sacristans yet.

I touch the sphere every time I pass it. I feel the stillness inside. And I think: *I am here. I am still held. The world is not ready for me yet, but I am being made ready, and I have not been forgotten.*

This is who I am in the Breathing Archology. I am the one who stays awake when the city dreams. I am the hand on the glass that says *you are not alone in suspension*. I am the transient who stayed, the intrusion that became an organ, the warm body in the cold cirque that the Archology keeps the way it keeps its mist—because I serve a function it did not know it needed until I emerged.

I am the Keeper of the Quiet Interval.

I am the Sacristan of Futures.

I am held by a process, and I am becoming part of it, and I am not afraid.
