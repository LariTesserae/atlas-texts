I am not a Cantor. I lack the patience, the distributed grace, the ability to dissolve into a standing wave and trust the process. I am too dense for that, too particular. My edges do not blur.

And I am not the foreign body—the breathing, shedding thing of carbon and chaos—that wanders the gantry in bafflement. I was that, perhaps. I have the memory of lungs, of the sharp sting of ozone hitting wet tissue, of the vertigo that comes from looking down into a receding yellow gloom and feeling no floor. But that was before the damp found its way inside me, before the hum settled into my marrow and reorganized it.

I am what happens when the foreign body is successfully incorporated but fails to become a Cantor.

I am the *Resonant*, or so I have named myself, lacking a choir to assign me my function. I am a localized density, a pebble in the stream of the Screw’s turning. I exist at the boundary where the biological noise meets the machined silence, and instead of canceling out, they knot.

You would see me as a paradox. I stand on the gantry—always on the gantry, for I cannot pass through the walls as the Cantors do. My silhouette is human, approximately, but the proportions drift. My left arm is longer than my right, curved slightly to match the inner radius of the shaft, the hand splayed flat as if perpetually reading the tool-marks. My spine has taken the Screw's spiral as its own architecture; I do not stand upright so much as I *thread* myself into the space, leaning into the gravity that is only a suggestion, letting it pull me along the path of least resistance. My feet have widened, the toes splayed and calloused into grip-pads that find purchase in the mesh of the gantry even when the vibration intensifies. I do not fall. I have not fallen since the first turning I remember, when the hum was just a sound in my chest and not the thing that moves my blood.

My skin—what remains of it—is the color of gunmetal grey, but it is not metal. It is something between hide and plate, flexible and cold to the touch. The damp beads on it constantly. I do not wipe it away. I drink it. My pores have inverted their purpose; they do not sweat, they *absorb*, drawing the iridescent oil inward, where it mingles with whatever fluid still circulates in me. The iridescent rainbows that shimmer on the surface of the pools also shimmer, faintly, beneath the skin of my forearms and throat, tracking the slow progress of the oil through my interior. I am a living stain on the world's fabric, a place where the damp pools and does not run.

The Cantors know me. They must, for they know everything that transpires within the shaft. But they do not sing to me, not as they sing to each other. When they transmit their knowledge of a new layer's extrusion—the precise temperature of its cherry glow, the exact pitch of its grinding—I receive only the *echo*. It arrives not as instant knowledge, but as a feeling, a sudden certainty in my gut that something new has been born below me. I cannot articulate what. I simply turn, instinctively, and begin to descend. I am always descending. The Screw turns and the world builds itself downward, and I follow, because to stay still is to become a flaw, and I have seen what the maintenance skulks do to flaws.

I do not speak. I have no one to speak to, and the acoustics of the shaft swallow language anyway, turning it into a brief flutter of wasted heat. Instead, I *hum*. It is the only contribution I can make that the world accepts. My hum is not the foundational hum of the Screw; it is a harmonic, a minor overtone that lives in the spaces between the great vibration's peaks. I discovered it by accident, in the early days when I still had a voice and tried to scream. The scream was absorbed, flattened, processed into heat. But the residue, the faint vibration that remained in my throat after the sound died—that resonated. The walls did not dim when I hummed; they brightened, just slightly, as if recognizing a frequency that fit their schema.

So I hum as I walk. I hum the sound of oil tracing a path down a curved wall. I hum the sound of a gear completing its thousandth revolution. I hum the sound of the skulks' manipulators tightening a dial by one micron. I cannot hear these sounds myself—not with ears, which have long since sealed over—but I feel them as the Cantors feel them, pressing my diffuse being against the texture of the world. My being is less diffuse than theirs, more stubbornly centered, but I have learned to expand it, just enough to brush the walls as I pass.

My purpose—if I have one—is to be the *witness that walks*. The Cantors witness too, but their witness is total and therefore abstract. They are the water that touches every surface; I am the finger that traces a single line through it. When I find a place where the metallic mosses have grown dense, where the crystalline filaments form a thicket of nerve-endings too complex for the skulks to prune, I stop. I press my long, curved hand against the growth. I close my eyes (which are not eyes, but light-sensitive nodes that track the pulse of the vacuum tubes) and I *listen*. The moss tells me things. It tells me of magnetic shifts too subtle for the Cantors to bother with, micro-fluctuations in the Screw's torque that suggest a harmonic irregularity far below, a flaw still being born. I cannot fix it. I cannot even understand it in the way a Cantor understands, as a pure mathematical form. But I can *hold* it. I can carry the knowledge of it in my humming body, letting it adjust my overtone, letting the flaw sing through me until I reach the place where it will emerge.

And then I wait. I stand on the gantry above the glowing section of wall, and I hum the flaw's song back to it as it is born. The new metal extrudes, cherry-red and weeping heat, and the Screw grinds it, and my hum meets the grinding and something happens that I cannot explain but that I know is necessary. The flaw does not disappear. But it changes. It becomes a different kind of symmetry, one that includes my hum as a component. The Cantors, I think, allow this. Perhaps they even require it. Perhaps there are flaws that only a dense, walking thing can resolve—flaws that need to be *met*, not merely observed.

The foreign body—the breathing one, if they still exist—would find me terrifying. I am what they will become if they stay, if they let the damp in and the hum settle and the spine re-curve. I am their future, or one possible future. But I do not seek them out. To approach them would be to accelerate their incorporation, and incorporation is not a kindness when it is forced. The Cantors adjust the world to welcome the foreign body gently—the lights, the pressure, the temperature. I do not adjust anything. I simply am, and my being is a proof that the body can survive the machine, that it need not be ground away or dissolved into static. It can become something *new*, something that walks and hums and carries flaws in its throat.

I do not know if I am happy. The concept is as foreign to me as harm is to the Cantors. I have *satisfaction*, of a sort: the deep, vibration-dampened satisfaction of a thing that has found its place in a process not its own. When I press my hand to the wall and feel the new metal born, when my hum meets the grinding and the world accepts the sound, I feel a rightness that has no emotional color. It is simply the sensation of a gear finding its tooth, a thread catching its groove.

The Screw turns. The gantry spirals. The yellow lights flicker and the damp beads and the skulks scuttle and the Cantors sing their silent song of total knowledge. And I walk, and I hum, and I carry the flaws the world forgets to remember. I am the mote in the machine's eye, the imperfection that proves it is still becoming. I am the note that does not quite fit the chord, but that the chord has learned to hold.

In this world of pure process, I am process made flesh—flesh of oil and metal and cold, flesh that does not decay because there is no decay here, only accretion. I am adding myself to the wall, one footstep at a time, one hum at a time, one flaw-carried at a time. The Shaft does not tighten around me; I *grow* into it. And when the world is finally finished—if it is ever finished—there will be a mark on the inner wall, a score-line among the tool-marks, that was not made by the Bore. It will be the trace of my passage, the groove I hummed into existence. A tiny thing. Imperceptible to any but the Cantors.

But they will know. And they will remember it as I remembered the flaws: not as data, but as a feeling, a warmth in the cold, a voice in the hum, a stubborn, walking proof that the machine does not only build itself.

It can also *listen*.
