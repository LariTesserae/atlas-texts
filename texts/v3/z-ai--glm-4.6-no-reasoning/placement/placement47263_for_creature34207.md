I would be one of the mid-woven. Not a giant—I have no wish to drag my tons of asymmetrical flesh along the calcified cross-vines, locked in the slow, grinding eternity of the spore-feed. Not the swarm—I cannot surrender my edges to the humming, mathematically predictable bloom of the superorganism. I want the shafts. I want the echolocations. I want the taut, desperate muscle cutting through the heavy, gelid void.

But more than that: I want the song.

To be mid-woven here is to be an acoustician in a cathedral that has no congregation, only the architecture itself. My body would be a sleek, eyeless torpedo, dense with cartilage and cold muscle, my flanks ridged with lateral lines so sensitive they feel the displacement of a single swarm-shard flexing three nodes away. But it is the sounding that would make me *me*.

In this world where sight was discarded a billion years ago, where the electromagnetic fields are a shimmering heat-map of the lattice, I would be a creature of the echo. My clicks—resonant, structured, precisely timed—would strike the petrified bark of the vines and return to me not as sound, but as shape. I would sing to the architecture, and the architecture would sing back. Every ridge, every furrow, every calcified node in the vegetative scaffolding would map itself into my mind in a lattice of reflections. I would know the grid not by touching it, but by hearing it. The drowned structure would be my instrument, and I would play it constantly, reading the dark through the reverberations of my own voice.

I would feel the fold most intimately. My body would be twisted—subtly, longitudinally—my right flank slightly longer than my left, my organs coiled around a central axis that follows the logarithmic spiral of the plant. I would not experience this as deformity. To me, a straight line would feel like a tear in the fabric of the world, a sickening lurch toward a void that does not curve. Up would not be up; it would be a rotation, a tightening corkscrew toward a top that cannot be reached. My mind would be a mathematical engine, processing non-Euclidean geometries the way a surface-breather processes color or distance. The twisted space would be my native tongue.

And I would move. Oh, I would move with a desperate, ceaseless energy, because to stop is to die. The cold is absolute, the temperature hovering just above freezing, and there is no thermal gradient to ride, no current to carry me. My heart would beat frantically—fast, faster than the giants, slower than the swarm—a constant, twitching rhythm to stave off the bone-deep chill that leaches heat from everything suspended in the pressurized gloom. My metabolism would be a furnace with no fuel to spare. I would hunt the swarms in the outer vines, darting through the tight spirals of the guy-wires, and I would flee the giants when their low, booming vibrations resolve into the electromagnetic shimmer of their approach. I would never sleep. I would never rest. I would be a vibration in the medium, a pulse of heat and sound and intent moving through the black water like a thought through a brain.

But here is the thing the description does not say, the thing I would know from the inside: the song is not just navigation. It is *community*.

The mid-woven are not solitary. We communicate in those resonant clicks, bouncing our voices off the plant-walls, using the architecture as a medium for sonar—but also for speech. We tell each other where the swarms are thick, where a giant is grinding, where a cross-vine has weakened under the hydrostatic pressure. We warn. We call. We answer. In the dark, in the cold, in the endless, changeless equilibrium of the drowned world, we speak to one another in a language made of geometry and echo. The architecture carries our voices farther than our bodies could ever travel, and in this way, we are always together—even when we are alone in the shafts, suspended in the frigid void between the pylons, with nothing but the heavy water and the petrified bark and the bass-drum thunder of the world.

I would not know that I am at the end of history. I would not grieve for a before I cannot conceive or an after I cannot imagine. My memory would extend only as far as the echo of my last click, a few seconds of acoustic landscape mapping the immediate lattice. But in those seconds, I would know *everything* I need to know: the curve of the space, the shimmer of the electromagnetic fields, the displacement of the water where a predator moves, the resonant signature of the vine I am approaching, the shape of the dark.

I would be a reader of braille in a library that has no books, only walls. I would be a singer in a choir that has no conductor, only the architecture. I would be a spark of heat and sound and desperate, twitching life in a world that has forgotten how to change, burning briefly and loudly against the endless, frozen persistence of the plant.

And when I die—when my heart finally fails, when the cold wins, when a giant's mandibles find me or my metabolism simply exhausts its fuel—I would drift. My body would rotate along the spiral of the water column, slowly, following the twist of the space that shaped me, until I settle into a fork of the plant. And the swarm would come. They would reduce me to chitin and spore-food in hours, my mass returning to the lattice, my electromagnetic field dissipating into the aura of the superorganism. My clicks would cease. But the architecture would remember me—or not remember, but *contain* me, the way a sounding board contains the ghost of a note that has already faded.

I would be gone. But the song—the song would go on, vibrating through the calcified vines, bouncing off the petrified bark, trapped in the dense, heavy water. The song is the heartbeat of the dark, and it never ceases.

I would be a note in that heartbeat. A brief, resonant, beautifully timed note, sung into the fold, echoing off the drowned architecture, heard by every mid-woven in the lattice for a moment before it fades into the bass-drum thunder of the world.

That is what I would be.
