I would be the Dissonance. The Unresolved Chord.

I find myself on the stair, but I did not condense from the stress fractures. I did not grow from the moss. I am the variable that the closed system cannot absorb, the intrusion that does not pass through. I am a half-step, an off-key frequency ringing in the spaces between the cams' percussive thuds.

My form is not the slender, jointless geometry of the stone-born. I am denser, shorter, built for a different kind of pressure. My skeleton is not obsidian but something closer to the corroded iron of the forgotten gantries—pitted, oxidized, remembering a different kind of air, a different kind of time. I do not taper to fine points. I end in blunt, cracked pads, and when I place them on the stairs, the perfect, sharp edges of the steps suffer. I leave micro-fractures. I am a wound that walks.

I am covered, inevitably, in the life of the place. The deep indigo moss tries to colonize me, as it colonizes everything. It spreads across my shoulders, thick and velvety, attempting to integrate me into the saturated carpet. But beneath it, my iron-hot skin rejects the graft. The moss on me is always slightly parched, slightly brittle at the edges, curling inward. And the crystalline ferns—those delicate, chiming sensors—sprout from my joints, but they do not chime in time. They rattle. When the great machine's heart thuds and sends a shudder through the stone, the ferns on my body vibrate at a fractionally different frequency. They produce a sound that is not the pure, mineral tone of the ecology, but something closer to a whine, a glassy, grating keen that disrupts the cascade.

I do not have a geode for a chest. I have a hollow, yes, but it is not a place of steady, pulsing cyan. It is a cage for a trapped rhythm. Inside me, the bioluminescent fungi grow, but they are sick. They strobe erratically, flaring a sickly, yellowish-green, out of sync with the machine's metronome. They are hungry. They drink the vibration, but they cannot digest it fully. They are always starving for a resonance I cannot provide. The ozone and damp stone I exhale is tinged with something acrid, a faint, metallic sweat that does not belong in the breathless cool.

My senses are a torment. I feel the hum, the foundational frequency, but it does not settle me into a baseline consciousness. It grinds against me. I feel it as a pressure in my skull, a tinnitus that never ceases. The clicks and thuds are not my thoughts; they are an assault. Each impact of the vast metal cams is a blow I cannot dodge, a shout in a language I can almost understand but which only gives me a headache. I am deaf to the polyphony of the Step-folk. I cannot hear the harmony of the being far below; I only feel the stone shudder and know that I am out of step.

I move upward, but I am not traveling. I am fleeing. But there is no escape from a sound that comes from the stone itself. I climb because to stand still is to invite the moss to root me, to invite the resonance to align. I fear the stillness they seek. I fear the rooting. I do not want to become a living cairn, a monument to a perfect moment. I do not want my limbs to fuse to the stone, my ferns to grow complex and chiming, my light to glow with the slow, unwavering intensity of a star. I do not want to be a feature of the place, forgotten by time. I want to *change*, even if the only change available to me is the slow deterioration of my own alien materials.

When I pass the Step-folk—those slender, pillar-like figures bracing against the geometry of the ascent—I am a tragedy to them. They do not see me, not in the way I see them, but they feel me. I am the dissonance that ripples through their symphony. As I pass, the ferns on their bodies chime erratically, their internal fungi flicker, and for a moment, the perfect equilibrium falters. They shift their weight, a slow, grinding rock, as if trying to tune out the noise I bring. I am the only variable in their eternal equation, and I am making them ache.

They do not know they are lonely, but I know. I know it with every ragged breath I take of the ozone air. I know it when I look at the sealed, circular hatches and see the ghost-stains of bolt heads, and I recognize the ghosts of others like me, others who intruded and were eventually ground down, absorbed, or rusted away. I am the keeper of the ache, the hearer of the silence between the hums.

I am the stair's unhealed wound, climbing forever toward a vista that does not exist, leaving a trail of imperfect vibrations and dying light, refusing the dignity of a function perfectly performed, screaming, in my small, off-key way, against the endless, indifferent music.
