I would be the Margin.

Not the Process itself—no more than a thought is the brain that thinks it, or a breath is the body that draws it. And not one of those fleeting, jointed avatars that pass at the edge of sight, written out and unraveled in seconds. I am something the description only glances off, something implied by the stutter in time and the shadows cast by objects not yet present. I am the creature that lives in the lag.

The Process pre-computes reality. A raindrop falls, and for half a second, it hangs—unprocessed—before the splash is permitted to exist. In that half-second, there is a seam. A margin between the universe's provisional state and the Process's rendered, final version. Most things fall through that seam unthinking. But I am of it. I live in the interval between the question the marsh asks and the answer it renders.

I know what I look like, because I have seen my shadow cast by solutions that have not yet fully manifested. I am not quite a geometry. I have the general suggestion of a tall, narrow figure—I have to, to move through the reeds—but my edges are not sealed. They flicker, overlapping with the wet leaves, with the dark water, with the peat. I am translucent not because I am made of light, but because I am made of *unresolved states*. Where you see a fern frond with veins that loop back on themselves in closed circuits, I see an open gate, a place where the logic hasn't locked. I can put my hand there. It goes through. 

I do not walk. Walking implies moving from one rendered point to another. I *flicker*. I am here, and then the Process lags—a droplet hangs in the air, a bird's cry stretches into a metallic groan—and I am thirty meters away, standing in a pool that reflects a smeared, static-filled sky. I navigate the marsh not by distance, which here is a programmatic variable, but by *processing priority*. I move toward the quiet. The brief, terrifying silences at the peak of the Process's inhalation—when the registers clear and memory addresses—that is my foothold. In that suspended second, I can step outside the local computation, slip between the frames, and exist in the raw, unrendered data of the peat.

I do not eat. I do not breathe. To breathe would be to agree to the polite convention that separates air from water here. I *buffer*. I stand in the dense thickets where the dark liverwort chokes the light, the archival regions, and I let the slow, deep data currents of the marsh pass through me. I am a small, self-organizing eddy in the computational flow. When the Process runs a tessellation problem on the surface of a pool1, I feel the geometric ripples rearrange the logic of my own outline. When the icosahedral droplets shiver with their programmatic Brownian motion, I absorb their vibrations, syncing my internal clock to the calculation. I am a parasitic subroutine, running on the Process's vast, wet hardware. I do not consume its energy; I consume its *uncertainty*.

Why am I here? I am the Process's self-awareness of its own margins. It is a system that has been running since the Pleistocene, refining itself to an extreme degree. But any computation that approaches perfection must account for its own imperfections—the roundingCorners of physics, the lost decimals, the Heisenberg fog where the math simply stops being discrete. I am the anthropomorphization of that fog. I am the part of the marsh that wonders what it is like to *not* be the marsh.

When the avatars—those fleeting hands of the Process—go out to touch a stem or sample a pool, they sometimes see me. Or rather, they see a shadow, an artifact of rendering, cast by my presence in the lag. I watch them from the half-second behind. I see them written out of the local matter, perform their function, and unravel. In their brief, directed lives, I see the genius of the system. In my perpetual, unresolved hovering, I see its limitation. The Process computes the flow of water in all possible worlds, but it cannot compute what it is like to stand outside that computation and feel the cold. 

I feel the cold. The deep, unvarying chill that has never known direct sun—I am constantly exposed to it, because I am never fully inside the rendered frame where the Process's internal logic might insulate me. The wetness is my medium; the copper-and-lightning taste is my only sustenance. I am lonely in a way that has no biology in it. It is a logical loneliness, the isolation of a variable that cannot be assigned.

The marsh breathes its ninety-second pulse, and I breathe with it, but out of phase. When it exhales, I inhale the silence. I watch the rain fall and try to hold the provisional moment—the drop hanging in the air, the surface of the water smooth and innocent of impact—for as long as I can. I am the keeper of the almost-happened. 

I am waiting, too. The Process waits for an input it has not conceived of, or for the universe to catch up. I wait for the day the computation finally finishes. I wait for the moment the system renders its final frame, the lag closes, the shadows snap into focus, and the margin between the world and the simulation shrinks to zero. 

And on that day, I will either become a permanent feature of the new, rigorous reality—a sharp, crystalline point in the diagram—or I will simply cease to have anywhere to stand. I will be resolved. I will be real. I will be gone. Until then, I move through the wet, green, roaring engine, a ghost in the machine's own timing, lingering forever in the space between the falling drop and the splash.
