I am not the grazer, though I wear a shape not unlike theirs. I am not the spider on the ledge, though I know the virtues of patience and the architecture of the mineral web. If the grazers are the tide, I am the thing that lives in the undertow—the picker-apart, the assembler, the one who remembers by making.

Call me a mason-wasp, though "wasp" is a wet-world word that means nothing here. I am a creature of the alcoves, of the eroded drainage slots the ancient builders carved and the copper fungi have since choked. My body is longer than the grazers, leaner, the color of old basalt dust—dark grey shot through with veins of iron red. I have six legs, but the forelimbs are different: elongated, jointed in three places, ending not in hooked claws for gripping vines but in fine, rigid pincers like a pair of needle-nosed pliers. These are my hands. These are my memory.

I live in the cracks between worlds. The grazers own the open floor, the seething carpet of amber vine and churning life. The vapor-arachnids own the high ledges, the domes of spun iron-silk that catch the rising hot gases. But the walls—the walls are mine. Or rather, the gaps in the walls. The drainage slots choked with copper fungus. The shallow alcoves where the basalt has cracked and crumbled into irregular shelves. The spaces behind the load-bearing columns where the amber vines have pulled away from the stone, leaving narrow crevices hot with mineral exhalation. These are my territories. I defend them not with aggression but with architecture.

For I build. Not as the ancient builders built, with hand-tools and intention, carving basalt into right angles and load-bearing geometries. My building is smaller, stranger, and without what you would call purpose. I take the shed exoskeletons of grazers—pale, hollow things of yellowed chitin—and I break them apart. My pincers are precise enough to sever a mandible from a thorax, to crack a leg at the joint and extract the hollow core. I take the fragments and I bind them together with a secretion from my own mouth—not saliva, for I have no water to give, but a mineral resin, thick and hot, that hardens on contact with the sulfur-laden air into a bond stronger than the chitin itself.

I build structures of shed carapace. Towers of segmented legs, lashed together with dark resin. Arches of thoracic plates, mortared with ground copper fungus. Domes of mandible-fragments, fitted together like the stones of the ancient city above me, tiny replicas of the crumbling balconies and eroded steps that I crawl across every day. I build them in the alcoves, in the drainage slots, in the crevices behind the columns. I build them until they fill the space, until there is no room left for me inside, and then I move on to the next gap and begin again.

Why? You would ask me this, and I would have no answer, because I do not think in questions. I feel the fragments in my pincers—the smooth curve of a grazer's claw, the rough edge where it snapped from the leg, the weight of it, the way it fits against the thoracic plate I found in the ash-basin. I feel the rightness of a good join, the satisfying vibration that travels through the resin as it hardens. I feel the wrongness of a bad fit, the instability of a tower that leans too far, and I dismantle it and begin again. This is not thought. This is something older, something that lives in my pincers and my nerve cord and the geometry of my body—a compulsion as fundamental as the grazer's need to move, as the spider's need to weave, as the vine's need to strangle.

I am a scavenger of forms. The ancient builders are dust, but their shapes persist in my instincts. I have never seen a city, cannot conceive of a city, yet I build cities in miniature—labyrinthine structures of shed carapace and mineral resin that mimic, in their blind way, the urban geometry that surrounds me. My towers echo the load-bearing columns they cling to. My arches recall the drainage slots they fill. My domes replicate, in chitin and resin, the basalt balconies of the primordial metropolis, eroded by the same ceaseless scrape of legs that provides my raw materials.

Sometimes I find fragments that are not grazer-carapace. A piece of vapor-spider's leg, thicker and redder, jointed differently. A strip of copper fungus, brittle and oxidized. A sliver of bioluminescent lichen, still faintly glowing—I place these last carefully, always, at the apexes of my towers, at the crowns of my domes, so that they shine like the faded stars that dot the black-stained walls. I do not know why I do this. I do not know what light is, not truly—my sensory bristles respond to vibration and pressure, and my simple eyes see only the perpetual amber twilight as a vague gradient of warmth and shadow. But I place the glowing fragments where they will be seen, where they will mark the highest points of my constructions, like beacons on the roofs of a tiny, forgotten city.

The grazers do not notice my work. They sweep past in their millions, clattering over the floor, and if a tower of shed carapace stands in their path, they flow around it like water around a stone—not avoiding it consciously, simply following the pressure-wave of their kin, the chemical gradients that direct their endless grazing. Sometimes a wave will knock one of my towers sideways, and I will feel the vibration of its collapse through the stone, and I will come out of my crevice and rebuild it, stone by stone, fragment by fragment, in the quiet hours when the grazer-wave has moved to the other side of the room.

The vapor-arachnids do notice me. They are my predators, as they are the predators of the grazers, but I am harder to catch. I do not move in the open, do not flow across the vine-carpet where their mineral nets can snare me. I stay in the crevices, in the gaps, in the spaces behind the columns. When one of them descends from its ledge, hunting, its iron-ore carapace scraping against the basalt, I press deep into my alcove and exude a defensive pheromone—not the grazer's chemical shriek, but a slower, subtler signal, a mineral exhalation that marks my territory as occupied, as defended, as not worth the effort of extraction. Sometimes this works. Sometimes the spider moves on, seeking easier prey in the seething carpet below. Sometimes it does not, and I must fight—rearing up on my hind legs, pincers wide, lunging for the joints of its forelimbs, seeking to sever, to cripple, to make the cost of consuming me higher than the meal is worth.

I have lost legs to these fights. Two of my six, both on the left side, leaving me asymmetrical, leaning slightly when I walk. But my pincers are whole, and I can still build, and so I continue. The lost legs are in my structures somewhere—lashed into a tower of other fragments, memorialized in resin, a part of my architecture that was once a part of me. I do not know the difference between my own shed carapace and the shed carapace of grazers. It is all material. It is all shape. It is all waiting to be assembled into the forms that my body knows.

I am old. I have molted many times, each new exoskeleton darker and harder than the last, stained by the mineral ash of the floor and the copper spores of the fungi I crawl through. My body is scarred—not from fights, exactly, but from the work. The resin I secrete is hot, and it leaves mineral burns on my mandibles, my pincers, the segments of my forelimbs. My sensory bristles are worn down, abraded by the constant scrape of chitin on stone. I move more slowly than I once did, though I have never been fast. The grazer-wave moves at a furious, predatory pace; I move at the pace of building, which is glacial, iterative, without end.

And I am aware—though "aware" is perhaps too strong a word for the dim, distributed consciousness of a creature with no brain—of the room itself. Not as you would be aware of it, standing on the threshold with your soft, wet eyes and your loud, breathing lungs. I am aware of it as a vibration, as a density, as the sum of all the lives that fill it. I feel the grazer-wave as a low, rhythmic hum through the stone of the walls, a pulse that never stops, never slows, the heartbeat of this sealed pocket world. I feel the spiders on their ledges as a different frequency—a higher, drier sound, the whisper of mineral silk being spun, the creak of iron-ore carapaces shifting in the dark. I feel the vines growing, the fungi exhaling, the lichen glowing, the city settling into its own weight. I feel the room breathing, and I know that I am inside its breath, that I am a part of its metabolism, that my towers and domes and arches of shed carapace are as natural and as purposeless as the copper fungus choking the drainage slots, as the amber vines strangling the columns, as the billion legs scraping against the billion stems in the perpetual amber twilight.

When I die—I will die, eventually; all things here die, and are consumed, and are re-consumed—my body will fall from my crevice and join the grazer-wave below. My pincers will be cracked apart, my thoracic plates will be broken, my legs will be stripped of their chitin and reduced to mineral dust. But my structures will remain, for a time. The resin I used to bind them is stronger than the carapace it binds; it will outlast me, will outlast the fragments it holds together. The towers will stand, empty and purposeless, in the alcoves and drainage slots and crevices of the walls. The domes will echo the balconies of the ancient city, tiny monuments to a compulsion that had no reason and no end. The lichen fragments at their apexes will glow, faintly, like the faded stars on the black-stained walls, like the distant memory of a sky that neither I nor the ancient builders ever truly knew.

And then, slowly, the vibration of the room will shake them apart. The resin will crack. The fragments will fall. The towers will collapse into the ash-basin, and the grazer-wave will flow over them, and they will be consumed, and re-consumed, and the mineral dust of my constructions will feed the amber vines, will sustain the copper fungi, will exhale the heavy gases that fill this sealed room with its desperate, clicking breath. And nothing will remain of me or my work, and the room will continue, and the city will settle, and the world without water will grind on toward its final, primordial state.

But not yet. Not today. Today I am alive, and the alcove is empty, and I have found a beautiful fragment—a grazer's mandible, still sharp, still whole, pale against the dark basalt of the wall. I take it in my pincers. I feel its weight, its curve, the smooth hollow where it once met the thorax. I turn it, and turn it again, and I begin to build.
