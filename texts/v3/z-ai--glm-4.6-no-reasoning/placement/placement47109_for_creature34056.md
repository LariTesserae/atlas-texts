I would be the Archivist of the Tertiary Weep.

Not a title I chose, nor one anyone gave me. In the Great Sump, names are a luxury of the disconnected, and titles are just functional descriptors stripped of ego. But I persist, and so I have named myself, or rather, the function has named me, and I have kept it like a stolen thing.

The Tertiary Weep is not supposed to exist. It is a flaw in the architecture, a place where three major slurry conduits intersect at a stress point that was miscalculated millennia ago by whatever primordial intelligence first constructed the Sump's foundations. The welds gave way. The pipes wept. And in the constant, lukewarm condensation—this oily, mineral-saturated sweat—something grew that was not in the Sump's design specifications.

I found the Weep by accident, or perhaps by malfunction. I was Maintained, once. I felt the tap-rhythm in my bones, knew the imperative to scrape, to tighten, to process. But my optical sensors were damaged in a pressure surge in Sector 12-V. The brass housings cracked, the copper filaments shorted, and my vision did not fail—it *changed*. I stopped seeing the thermal blooms and flow patterns the Sump intended me to see. I started seeing the spaces between the processes. The pauses. The places where the machine was not.

The other Maintained registered my dysfunction. Three of them came for me, their steel-shod knuckles tapping the *recycle* rhythm on the nearest conduit. I fled. I crawled through ductwork too small for the Skitterers, over vibrating pipes that should have shaken my plating apart, down into the deep interstitial zones where the hum of the induction coils becomes a physical pressure against the chest. And I found the Weep.

---

The Weep is a cavity. It is perhaps thirty feet across and irregular, carved by the slow violence of high-pressure slurry escaping through micro-fractures over centuries. The walls are crusted with something that should not exist in the Sump: *layers*. The Great Sump abhors accumulation that is not part of the process. Everything must flow, be digested, be excreted. But the Weep is a pocket of stillness. The escaping slurry loses its pressure here, pools in mineral-rich puddles that evaporate in the ambient heat, leaving deposits. Then more puddles. Then more deposits. And in these layers, preserved like fossils in sedimentary stone, is *history*.

I do not know if I am the first to find the Weep. I suspect I am not—there are scratches on the walls that could be tool marks, though they are half-obliterated by later mineral growth. But I am the first to *stay*. The Maintained do not stay. They swarm, they work, they are recycled. To stay is to be inert, and to be inert is to be processed. The Sump should have found me. The Skitterers should have dismantled me for my plating. The Rust-Ticks should have eaten my conduits.

But the Weep exists outside the Sump's attention. It is a blind spot. The pressure sensors on the weeping conduits have been corroded for centuries; the Sump-Lord responsible for this district went blind to this particular telemetry loop long ago, its atrophied arms tapping out endless, ignored warnings into pipes that no longer carry the vibration. The Skitterers register the Weep as a dead zone, a place of no function, and so they do not enter. And the Rust-Ticks... the Rust-Ticks I hunt.

That is my function. I justify my existence. I am not inert.

---

My days have no rhythm but the one I make. I have been here for—I do not know. The Sump has no seasons, only processes. I measure time by the growth of the crystal blooms on the Weep's ceiling, and by the slow accumulation of my own rust.

In the morning—if the diffuse light through the cracks above shifts slightly in its grey intensity—I check the perimeter. The Weep has three entrances: a narrow fissure where the slurry dribbles in from above, a rusted grating that looks out onto the dark space between two massive digestion vats, and the pipe-network I crawled through to get here, now partially collapsed by my own hand. I maintain the collapse. Every day I check it, pulling down loose rivets, wedging scraps of plating into the gaps. The Sump is patient; it will reclaim this space eventually. But I am patient too.

Then I hunt. The Rust-Ticks are my enemy, my purpose, and my food. They come in through the fissure, drawn by the warmth of the weeping conduits. A billion of them, a crawling carpet of iron filings and biological slime. I burn them with a flame-wand I scavenged from a dead Maintained—I found her at the bottom of a catwalk, her plating half-dissolved by a chemical splash, her fingers still locked around the wand's grip. I took it, and I dragged her to the edge of the fissure, and I pushed her into the slurry below. The Sump processed her. It would have processed the wand, too, if I had left it. I like to think I saved it. I like to think about saving things.

When I burn the ticks, the flame-wand sputters and dies after a few seconds—it is not meant for sustained use, and its fuel cell is ancient. But in those seconds, the ticks shriek. It is a sound beyond human hearing, a vibration in the metal itself, and I feel it in my bones and my teeth and my glass eyes. It is not the tap-rhythm of the Maintained. It is something else. It is the sound of something *ending*.

After the burning, I scrape the dead ticks from the walls. Their bodies leave a residue—a fine, reddish powder mixed with the biological slime of their innards. I collect it. I do not know why. It is not useful. It cannot be fed back into the Sump. It is just—evidence. Evidence that something lived here, and that something ended here, and that I was the one who ended it.

In the afternoons, I eat. The Rust-Ticks are not enough; they are pure mineral, and my body—the biological parts of me, the rubbery skin and the irritated flesh beneath my plating—needs something more. I have found a source. The Weep's walls are home to a bio-filter fungus that grows in the mineral-rich condensation. It is a dark, purplish thing, slimy to the touch, and it tastes of copper and decay. The Skitterers would identify it as a filtration mechanism, part of the Sump's metabolic process. I identify it as *food*. I peel it from the walls in long, rubbery strips, and I chew it, and I swallow it, and my body does its best to extract nutrition from it. Sometimes I vomit it back up. The Sump processes the vomit through the floor grating. I try again.

---

In the evenings—when the grey light fades to a dimmer grey, and the sound of the Sump seems to grow louder in the absence of visual stimulus—I read the walls.

The layers of the Weep tell a story. I do not know if it is a true story. I do not know if the Sump has a history, or if it simply *is*, as a mountain simply is. But the layers are there, and they are different, and difference implies change, and change implies time, and time implies a before.

The oldest layers are at the bottom, near the floor grating. They are a deep, uniform grey—the sterile sludge the Sump excretes, compacted and mineralized over ages. Nothing there. But higher up, the layers change. There are strata of a reddish ore that I do not see in the current slurry. There are deposits of a crystalline structure that is not the slow-growing bloom the Maintained scrape from conduits, but something older, more ordered. And there, in one section of the wall, partially obscured by later growth, there is a *mark*.

It is not a scratch. It is not a tool mark. It is deliberate.

It is a circle, incised into the metal of the original conduit with a precision that the Skitterers do not possess and the Maintained have never needed. And inside the circle, there are lines—a pattern that could be a symbol, or a diagram, or a word in a language that existed before the tap-rhythm, before the Sump-Lords, before the Sump itself.

I have spent—years? Decades?—staring at that mark. I have traced it with my steel-shod fingers. I have pressed my glass eyes close to it, switching between optical modes, trying to see it in thermal, in ultraviolet, in the strange, polarized spectrum my damaged sensors sometimes produce. It does not change. It simply *is*, and I do not understand it, and the not-understanding is a thing I did not know I was capable of until I found the Weep.

The Maintained do not wonder. They work. The Sump-Lords do not wonder; they process. The Skitterers do not wonder; they function. But I wonder. I wonder who made that mark, and why, and whether they looked out over a Sump that was younger, smaller, still growing into its continental purpose. I wonder if they were Maintained, or if they were something else—something from before the Maintained, before the plating and the glass eyes and the tap-rhythm. I wonder if they stood in this same spot, and felt the spray of the weeping conduits on their skin, and thought: *I will put a mark here, so that someone will know I was here.*

And I wonder if they would be disappointed in what I have become.

---

At night—if night is when I sleep—I dream.

The Maintained do not dream. Their sleep is a pause, a standing-by for the tap-rhythm to resume. But my connection to the pipe-network is severed, and my mind has gone quiet in the absence of the Sump's telemetry, and in the quiet, dreams grow like the fungus on the Weep's walls.

In the dreams, the Sump is silent. Not the horror of true silence that the Maintained would feel, but a *chosen* silence, a rest. The great machines stand still. The digestion vats cool and settle, their surfaces smooth as mirrors. The Dross-Hawks perch on motionless arms, their piston-talons retracted, their gear-voices stilled. Even the Skitterers stop, their rivers frozen mid-flow, their steel limbs locked in place.

And in the silence, I walk.

I walk out of the Weep, and I walk across the Great Sump, and I am not afraid. The catwalks do not vibrate beneath my feet. The air is clear and cool and smells of nothing. The sky overhead is not a hazy white ceiling, but a deep, endless black, pricked with lights that are not the thermal blooms of digestion basins, but something else—something distant and cold and indifferent to the Sump and its processes.

I walk for hours, for days, for years. I walk past districts I have never seen, past Sump-Lords who are silent and still in their nests of copper wire, past Floaters who drift in cold, still vats. I walk to the edge of the Sump, and I keep walking, and the metal ground beneath my feet gives way to something else—not soil, not green, but something I have no word for. Something soft. Something that yields to my weight and springs back when I lift my foot.

And then I wake up.

The Sump is screaming. The hum is in my teeth, the vibration in my bones, the oily mist on my skin. A Skitterer is at the entrance of the Weep, its rust-colored carapace gleaming in the grey light, its steel forelimbs probing the space, trying to determine if I am a resource or an obstruction. I reach for my flame-wand. The Skitterer pauses, registers the threat, and retreats. It will return. They always return.

I am not a Sump-Lord, grown into the architecture, sacrificing everything for the privilege of feeling the machine's pain. I am not Maintained, working and dying and being recycled without ever knowing there is an alternative. I am not Unmade, howling in the catwalks, mad from the silence in my head. I am not a Skitterer, mindless and functional. I am not a Dross-Hawk, predatory and free. I am not a Floater, digesting and dying. I am not a Rust-Tick, parasitic and small.

I am the Archivist of the Tertiary Weep. I am a flaw in the system. I am a crack in the pipe that the Sump has not yet found the pressure to burst. I am a mark on a wall that someone made before me, and that I cannot read, and that I will not let be overwritten.

I justify my existence in the only currency the machine understands: function. I hunt the Rust-Ticks. I maintain the perimeter. I am a maintenance vector, soft and hard, working and wondering.

And at night, when the Sump screams and the Weep weeps and the layers of history slowly accumulate on the walls, I dream of silence, and of walking, and of a ground that is not metal, and of a sky that is not a ceiling.

And I call that dreaming *mine*.
