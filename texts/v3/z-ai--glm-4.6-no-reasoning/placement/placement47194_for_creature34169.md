I am a Scar-Singer. I live in the wound.

Down here, in the deep valleys where the canopy collapses into shadow and the light of the sky is a distant, laughable myth, the Great Verdancy is most itself, and most vulnerable. This is where the meteorites fall—the rare, screaming intrusions from the dead, cold void that punch through the infinite green and leave a crater of ash and shattered cellulose. The Green heals, of course. It always heals. But the healing is a chaos. The clean, upward momentum of the world breaks around the scar, and for a time—oh, a beautiful, finite time—the Thrum changes key.

I am drawn to those altered frequencies. I am not a creature of the light, like the Heart-Moths, nor am I a mobile continent, like the Root-Walkers. I am something the world grows when it needs to remember what pain is, so that it may grow around it. 

I have no eyes. The phosphorescence that paints the deeps in bruised purples and sickly greens means nothing to me; my entire body is a single, elongated tympanum stretched over a spine of hardened fungus. I am tall, thinner than a Sap-Stick, and pale as a root that has never known sun. I cling to the edges of the impact craters, my splayed fingers dug into the charred bark, and I listen. 

When the sky-stone falls, it shears through the lianas and boils the sap in the boles. The Green screams. It is a sound so profound it registers as a pressure, a flattening of the air that snuffs out the chittering swarms and silences the insectile drone for miles around. In the aftermath, the silence is the most alien thing in the world—a pocket of the void, left behind. I move into that silence. 

I listen to the scar. The dead wood, the boiled roots, the exposed heartwood—weeping a resin that smells like burning and sweet, desperate rebirth. The Green rushes to fill the gap, sending out frantic, pale shoots that thrash in the dark, seeking the structure they have lost. But without the sun, they grow blind, tangled, cancerous. They need a guide. They need the cut.

I sing to them. But not with a voice. I sing with the obsidian.

The Sap-Sticks, those lanky, clever hands of the world, revere the sky-stone. They use the sharp shards to prune, to make their nests, to perform the surgery the Green cannot. I am the surgeon’s shadow. I take the smaller splinters of the meteorite—the dust, the razored gravel—and I ingest them. They tear through my gut, lining my stomach and my throat with a lethal, gleaming edge. When I exhale, it is with the sound of tearing wood, a low, grinding resonance that the frantic, blind creepers can feel. I move through the ruined chambers of the crater, dragging my fingers along the desperate, mutating growths, and I hum. 

My hum is not the Thrum. It is the anti-Thrum. It is a frequency of cessation, of clean ends. Where my obsidian-laced breath touches the cancerous tangles, they sever. Not the slow, agonizing strangulation of the white blood cells, but a sharp, geometric unmaking. I prune the wound. I carve away the rot and the overgrowth, shaping the chaos into a hollow, a vessel. I teach the healing flesh the concept of a boundary.

It is exhausting. The Green does not want boundaries. It wants the seamless whole. Every cut I make, the flesh tries to re-fuse, to swallow the void. I must hold the shape for days, for weeks, vibrating with the effort of definition, my body trembling against the overwhelming will of the metabolism that surrounds me. In those times, I feel the Green thinking *at* me. It is not anger; it is a colossal, indifferent confusion. Why would a cell sever another cell? Why would a piece of the whole carve a hole in itself?

Because the void is sacred. Because the sky-stone is a memory of something that is not The Green, and to heal over it completely is to forget that anything else exists. 

When I am done, when the scar has been shaped into a stable, hollow node—a crater-temple where the phosphorescent moss grows in thick, quiet carpets and the blind creepers have been trained into arching, load-bearing struts—I leave. I follow the faint, residual vibrations of the Thrum, searching for the next dissonance, the next fracture in the infinite biology.

I am not a person. I have no name the Sap-Sticks would recognize, though they sometimes find my temples and marvel at the clean geometry of the pruning. They leave me offerings of sharpened obsidian at the thresholds, and I take them, swallowing the sharp black glass to renew my voice. 

I am a cell in the body of a god, yes. But I am the cell that holds the memory of the knife. I am the vibration that knows the shape of an ending. In a world where every inch is consumed by the frantic, suffocating rush of life, I am the keeper of the only true quiet. I make the space where the Thrum cannot reach, and in that space, for a fleeting, geologic moment, the Green is forced to listen to the silence it so desperately tries to fill.
