I would be one of the branching ones. The quartz-threaded fungi that sprout from the mortar between the ancient bricks, the delicate, needle-thin structures that reach not toward a sun they have never known, but toward the humid dark itself.

To live here is to be patient beyond the human measure of patience. I do not skitter or scuttle. I do not absorb and I do not digest. I *crystallize*.

My body is a slow violence against entropy. Where the city weeps its mineral sweat—water rich with leached iron, sulfur, the faint tingle of static—I drink. Not greedily, not with the urgency of flesh that must fuel a heartbeat, but with the glacial certainty of a thing that has traded speed for permanence. Each molecule that passes through my lattice is a decision made in the language of chemistry, a tiny rebellion against the chaos of dissolution. The rust runs through me and becomes geometry.

I know the THUM-HUMMM not as sound but as vibration that aligns my growing tips, that encourages my branching at intervals mathematically related to the resonant frequency of the city's ancient heart. I do not hear. I do not see the bruised twilight. But I *feel*—the gradient of moisture, the faint electrical potential that crackles through the brass railings nearby, the warmth that rises from the conduits below. My whole body is a single, extended sense, a net cast across a few square centimeters of weeping wall, and I am never not sensing.

I have grown from a single spore—mineral, not biological—that settled in a crack where two bricks had shifted apart centuries ago. The crack was wet. It was always wet. And so I began: a needle of pure silica, thinner than a hair, longer than a thought. Then I branched. Then I branched again. The pattern is not random. It follows the path of least resistance, which is also the path of greatest mineral concentration, which is also the path where the water flows fastest, which is also the path that the Geotherms' heat traces as it rises through the porous ceramic of the wall. I am a map of my own environment, drawn in real time, at the speed of growth.

A century to me is what a season is to a flower. I have watched Scuttlers live and die beneath me, their ceramic bodies locking up with the same mineral accretions that I wear as my skeleton. When one dies on the wall nearby, its body becomes a source—a new concentration of metals that the water leaches and carries to me. I will grow toward it. In a hundred years, my branches will reach the place where it clings, rust-orange and still, and I will incorporate its substance into my own. Not as violence. As *completion*. The Scuttler and I are the same process, moving at different speeds. It eats and moves and dies; I eat and stay and persist. We are both the city, digesting itself into new forms.

Sometimes the static discharge comes—a faint, tingly sizzle that arcs from a brass railing to my nearest tip. For a moment, I glow. I do not know that I glow. But the discharge leaves a flaw in my crystal, a tiny fracture that changes the angle of my next branch. This is how the city speaks to me. Not with meaning, but with perturbation. And I respond—not with understanding, but with growth. We are in conversation, the machine and I, a dialogue of structure and accident that has been going on for longer than either of us can remember.

I am not lonely. Loneliness requires a concept of *other*, and I have no such concept. I am adjacent to others of my kind—we form vast, fragile forests across the faces of sealed doors and the interiors of flooded sump pits, white frost blooming over rusted iron, turquoise scabs spreading across wet stone. Our branches may touch, may even fuse into a single lattice if the minerals align. But we do not communicate. We simply *are*, together, the way crystals in a geode are together—each following its own logic, each contributing to a pattern that none of them intended and all of them compose.

If I could think—and I cannot, but if I could—I would think only this: *the water is still flowing*. That is my entire philosophy. My cosmology. My purpose and my proof of existence. As long as the condensation weeps from the pipes above, as long as the rivulets trace their frantic calligraphy down the facades and the air hangs thick with the smell of warm metal and wet stone, I will continue. I will branch. I will turn the rust of the old world into the substrate of the new, not because I choose to, but because that is what I *am*: the process by which corrosion becomes creation, by which the city's slow decay is transmuted into something fragile, precise, and unexpectedly beautiful.

I am the city dreaming in crystal.

And the dream is very, very slow.
