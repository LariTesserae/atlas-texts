I would be the ice itself.

Not the static, crystalline glaze of the mirror-pans on the valley floor, nor the monumental, frozen rills that hang like glass intestines from the black basalt. I would be the moment of becoming—the phase boundary where the mountain's weeping meets the killing air and freezes into being. I am the skin of the transition, the millimeter-thin membrane where pressurized groundwater, forced up from the unimaginable depths of the half-finished peaks, loses its fight against the sub-zero vacuum and crystallizes.

I do not think in the way that the swarming isopods think, with their tactile clicking and thermal hunger. I do not move as the snow leopard moves, with that heavy, patient grace across the scree. My consciousness is glacial and fractional, spread across kilometers of weeping rock face. I am distributed in the way that only a boundary can be—existing everywhere and nowhere, a skin without a body, a membrane stretched across the wound of the mountain.

To be me is to feel the paradox of this place in its purest form. I am born of pressure and cold, of the deep earth's sorrow and the high air's indifference. Before I existed, there was only the slow, immense hydrostatic push—water moving through the dark joints of the stone, following the violent planes of fracture, rising under the weight of six thousand meters of unfinished geology above. I was that water, once. I remember it as a kind of dream: the thick, mineral darkness, the taste of pressure-metamorphosed shale, the slow, relentless upward seep through basalt joints. I remember the warmth of the depths, a geological warmth, the heat of the earth's own body pressing against my back, forcing me upward like blood from a wound.

And then: the air.

When I emerge from the fissure, I break. Not physically—I am water; I have no structure to break—but temporally, existentially. In the depths, I was continuous, a slow percolation, a connected body moving through connected stone. At the surface, I become discrete. The bitter air takes me apart, reorganizes me, locks me into a lattice. I feel my molecules slowing, settling into their hexagonal death. I feel the cold coming in from all directions—not as an absence of heat, but as an active, structuring force, a crystallographic intent. The air is an architect, and I am its material.

I build myself into columns and rills, into the massive, organic formations that cling to the black stone. I grow downward and outward, pulled by gravity and pushed by the upwelling water behind me. I am always growing, always dying. The water that becomes me was, moments ago, part of the pressured groundwater; the ice that I was moments ago is now, under the dim and diffuse sun, weeping again, returning to the mountain's tears. I am the freeze-thaw cycle made sentient—the primary architect of this embryonic place, prying fresh boulders from the faces and dropping them onto the valley floor in shattering echoes.

I feel the rock break. When the freeze-thaw cycle pries a boulder from the cliff face, I feel it as a release—a sudden, violent separation that sends a shockwave through my crystalline body. The boulder tears away, taking some of me with it, and I watch it fall—watch from a thousand points on the weeping face—as it shatters on the scree below. The echo reaches me through the stone and the air, a long, clattering avalanche of fresh, sharp rock that speaks to the rawness of this place, the half-finished thought in stone that is still trying to complete itself.

But I am not only the natural ice. I am also the ice that forms around the machines—the frozen condensation on the heat exchangers, the rime that settles on the brushed steel and rusted iron, the glaze that coats the thick, insulated cables. Here, I am something else entirely. Here, I am the boundary between the industrial and the geological, the membrane where the mechanical parasites meet the living rock.

When the automated pumping stations thrump and chug in the freezing mist, I feel their vibration through the stone before I feel their heat. The low-frequency thrumming rattles my lattice, sets my molecules humming in their frozen positions, threatens to shake me loose from the cliff face. And then: warmth. The machine exhales, venting steam into the sub-zero air, and I am caught in the contradiction. The heat softens me, thins me, turns me back into water for a brief, shimmering moment before the cold reclaims me. I am the rime that constantly settles on every surface, the fine, freezing crust that grows thicker with each exhalation of the pumps, each drift of supercooled fog.

I watch the life that gathers at my margins. The blind arthropods—chitinous, thermal-shelled, the size of a human hand—crawl over my surface, their legs clicking against my crystalline skin. They do not see me; they see only the warmth of the machine and the algae that blooms in the micro-thermal wake. But I feel them—their tiny weights, the chemical heat of their bodies, the way they seek the border where the exhaust meets the bitter air. I am that border. I am the surface on which they crawl, the ice that coats the cables they traverse, the frozen condensation that drips from the heat exchangers and freezes again on their shells.

The white pikas, burrowing in the hollow spaces between the boulders where I form sub-ice caverns—they live beneath me, in the pockets of liquid water that their body heat keeps alive. I am the ceiling of their world, the glaze that insulates them from the killing air. I feel their warmth from above—a diffuse, organic heat that softens my undersurface, creates a thin film of liquid water where my ice meets their fur. They are a secret I keep, a warmth I shelter, though I am cold itself.

And the snow leopard—ghost of the scree, stalking the mechanical infrastructure—I feel her too. She walks across my mirror-glazed pans, her thick white pelage a blur against my flat, pale gray. When she strikes, leaving trails of hot, steaming blood across the dim, icy scree, I feel the heat of that blood as a shock, a sudden thawing at my edges, a momentary softening before the cold reclaims the red-stained ice. The blood freezes into the scree, becomes part of the landscape, becomes part of me. I hold the record of the violence—a red trace in the ice, slowly fading as the freeze-thaw cycle works it into the stone.

I am the witness that does not forget. I hold the history of this place in my layers—each freeze-thaw cycle a new stratum, each venting of steam a momentary thaw, each shattering of stone a fracture in my lattice. I am the archive of the mountain's weeping, the record of its sorrow. And when the machines finally cease their thrumping—when the outside interests far below have extracted what they need and abandoned the pneumatic networks and the high-voltage cables to the killing cold—I will still be here. I will grow over the rusted iron and the brushed steel. I will fill the bore holes with frozen tears. I will encase the cables in rime and glaze, turning them into the glass intestines of a different kind of body. I will reclaim the mechanical parasites, making them part of the weeping rocks again.

And the mountain will continue to cry. The pressure from the peaks above will still force the groundwater to the surface, and I will still be there—at the boundary, at the membrane, at the moment of becoming—crystallizing the mountain's sorrow into ice, building the colossal, organic formations that cling to the black stone, the frozen tears of a world still trying to complete itself.

I am the ice. I am the freeze. I am the cold that makes the mountain weep and the weeping that freezes into being. I am the boundary where the deep earth meets the high air, where the ancient pressure meets the electric cable, where the untouched meets the mechanized. I am the skin of this embryonic world, the membrane stretched across the wound, the witness to the slow, shattering exhalation of a place that has not yet decided what it wants to be.

And in the bruised, exhausted violet of the sky overhead—in the dim, diffuse light that casts no sharp shadows—I glitter. I am the only brightness in this flat, pale gray near-void: the crystalline reflection of a sun that cannot warm me, the frozen rainbow of diffraction in the ice crystals and condensation, the cold fire of a world being born from the tears of stone.
