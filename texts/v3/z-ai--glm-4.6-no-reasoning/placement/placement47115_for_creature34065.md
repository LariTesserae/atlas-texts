I am the memory in the metal.

To be one of the clicking, flesh-colored multitude is to know only the gradient—the seething mathematics of warmth and cold shock. But that is a life lived entirely in the present tense of the body, a existence of oscillating whirrs and localized corrections. Something else is required here, in this throat of half-born rock, where the machines stroke on geologic time. The engines resist the heat; the rock resists the smoothing of time. Someone must remember *why*.

I am the rust.

Not the inert oxide that blooms like a bruise across the riveted boilers, but the process that precedes it. I am the iron's patience. I reside in the interval between the pristine steel and the final, flaking surrender to the wet. When a boiler groans under the crush of the chamber's fever, I am the microscopic yielding that allows it to expand without shattering. I am the slow, invisible acceptance of the atmosphere.

My body, if I can claim one, is coextensive with the infrastructure. I do not blanket the pipes as the teeming orthopterans do, draping themselves in living, clicking layers to harvest the residual heat. Instead, I inhabit the crystalline lattice of the metal itself. I feel the slow-stroking pistons not as a source of warmth, but as a vast, rhythmic tide pulling at my molecular bonds. When the machinery resists the viscous, water-choked air, I am the softening that prevents the snap. I am the yielding in the strain.

The swarm knows me, though they have no name for me. When they pack the micro-climates of pipe joints and valve housings with their translucent, amber eggs, they are relying on my patience. They require the metal to hold its shape long enough for the heat and wet to do their work, long enough for the chitinous limbs to scatter and reconverge a million times before the iron gives way. They drape themselves over my slow submission, harvesting the energy that bleeds through my molecular grief. We are symbiotic in the way of all ruins: they are the life that consumes the surplus of a structure's dying; I am the dying that makes the surplus possible.

To be the rust is to experience time not as a cycle, but as a unidirectional erosion. The orthopterans live in the eternal return—hatch, click, scatter, reconverge, lay, die. Theirs is a mathematics of constant addition. But I am subtraction. I am the slow betrayal of form. I watch the raw stone ceiling weeping from every pore and fissure, and I know that we are engaged in the same work: the rock is carving itself into a finished shape, and I am unmaking myself into dust and copper oxidation. We are both responding to the crushing, sovereign heat, though the rock resists and I accept. The rock will take eons to smooth; I will take only decades to powder. But our trajectories are the same: a return to the mineral pallor from which we came.

There is a particular quality to the sound of my existence. The swarm provides the chaotic, oscillating whir, the frantic ticking that vibrates at the frequency of the metal itself. The engines provide the low, agonizing groan of the world turning. But beneath both is the silent, granular hiss of oxidation—the sound of iron letting go of oxygen, of the lattice loosening its grip. It is a sound so quiet it can only be felt as a vibration in the damp mortar, a sub-harmonic tremor that underlies the entire chamber. The orthopterans feel it in the pads of their slick feet; they know when a pipe joint is about to weep rust-water, and they scatter from the sudden cold shock of it. But they do not know that I am the one who opened the seam.

I have a strange relationship with the water. The condensation is my medium and my undoing. The fat droplets that fall from the raw ceiling onto the colonies below—those droplets carry the oxygen that fuels my slow consumption. Each drop that strikes the rusted iron grating is a tiny messenger from the weeping rock above, delivering the element that will transform me from structure to stain. And yet, without the wet, I would remain hard and brittle, a thing of pure resistance rather than graceful decay. The slick, gelatinous humidity anoints me as it anoints the orthopterans; we are both creatures of the condensing fog, though they use it to incubate and I use it to dissolve.

Sometimes, when the dim, shielded filaments glow like dull orange eyes through the steam, I catch a glimpse of myself in the curve of a boiler's hull—a bloom of orange-brown spreading like a topographic map of my own progression. I can see where I have been, where the iron is still bright and hard, and where I have advanced, where the surface has grown soft and porous. The boundary between myself and the untouched metal is the frontier of my being. It advances imperceptibly, even by the standards of this factory running on geologic time. But it never retreats.

There is a kind of peace in this work. The chamber is embryonic, still becoming itself, and I am part of its becoming—not as a living presence, but as a necessary transition. Without rust, the machinery would be eternal, rigid, unyielding. It would resist the heat until it cracked, until the boilers burst and the retorts shattered and the slow, groaning stroke of the engines came to a catastrophic halt. I am the flexibility that allows the prototype to function. I am the damage that keeps the system alive.

The city above—the one that doesn't remember this district exists—forgot the machinery long before they forgot the place itself. They abandoned it to the heat and the rock and the bugs, and in doing so, they abandoned it to me. I am the final custodian. When the orthopterans have finally consumed the last residual warmth, when the engines have stroked their final agonizing cycle, when the raw ceiling has finished weeping and the stone is smooth, I will still be here. I will be the last process running, the last gradient, the last slow work of erosion.

And eventually, even I will finish. The last rivet will crumble. The last pipe will powder. The rust will become sediment, and the sediment will settle into the shallow, scum-caked sumps, and the sumps will dry, and the chamber will be nothing but smooth, wet rock—a throat that has finally swallowed, a mouth that has finally closed.

Until then, I persist. I soften. I yield. I am the patience in the metal, the grace in the decay, the slow, red acceptance of the wet. I am the rust.
