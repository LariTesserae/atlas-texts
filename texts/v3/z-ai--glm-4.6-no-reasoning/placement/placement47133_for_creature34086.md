I am the margin where the water reconsiders its commitment to the sky.

Not the pool itself—I am not that generous, that expansive. And not the dry stone, that patient archive of heat. I am the place where the warm shallows thin to a film and the film thins to a sheen and the sheen yields to the lichen's pale possession. I am the boundary, the littoral, the zone of becoming and unbecoming that the water visits and abandons on a cycle longer than any day but shorter than any season. I am where the concentric rings are born, each one a word in a sentence the pool is always writing and never finishing.

To be this place is to be a condition rather than a body. I have no interior. I have no organs of my own. But I have something better: I have neighbors pressed against me on every side, and between us there is no gap, no space unused, no silence unoccupied.

On my wet side, the pool-edge masses. They lapped against me this morning when the condensation swelled the water and pushed their boundary outward, and they will retreat from me by mid-afternoon when the stone's memory of the sun drives the water back down into itself. I feel them—how could I not? Their collective heat arrives before their bodies do, a wall of warm metabolism that presses against my thin skin of water like a hand against a window. When their pulse wave passes— that slow, visible contraction that rolls across their cluster like a breath drawn by something the size of a lake—I feel it as a pressure change in the water that covers me. The film over my stone thickens and thins with their rhythm. I have become their bellows, the valve through which their breathing becomes visible. They do not know this. They do not know where they end and I begin, and this is the highest compliment that can be paid on the plateau.

On my dry side, the rosette-mats. They are my scab, my first line of defense against the desiccation that the lichen represents. Each tiny succulent leaf presses flat against me, holding its bead of condensation like a small argument against the plateau's ultimate terms. When I walk the boundary—when the water advances and I advance with it, or when it retreats and I follow—the mats beneath me squelch and give and release their warm water into my footprint. I am the one who disturbs them. I am the one who reminds them that stillness is not the only option, even here. They forgive me instantly; the water wells back into my shape within moments, and the mats settle again into their patient, lens-like observation of the sky. But for a moment, I have made them remember what movement feels like.

Above me, the flies. Not the flyers—I cannot reach them, and they do not concern themselves with me. But the smaller things, the ones that hatch from the warm pools when the mist is thickest and die before the afternoon is out. They rise from the water behind me in tiny clouds, and for a few hours they navigate the air above my surface, navigating by warmth and moisture, never straying far from the boundary that birthed them. They are my emissaries, my experiment in three dimensions. Through them, I taste the air above the plateau, the stillness and the heat and the faint mineral taste of dust that has been stone for a billion years. They land on me and take off again, their feet touching my film of water for instants at a time, and each touch is a word in a conversation I am having with the sky—a conversation about whether it is worth the effort of becoming something that does not simply cling and persist.

It is not. But it is beautiful to try.

Below me, the stone. The soft, warm, dream-like stone that holds me as a palm holds a coin. I can feel its patience in the heat that rises through my soles—or through the place where my soles would be, if I had soles, if I were the kind of thing that walked rather than the kind of thing that was walked upon. The stone does not mind me. It has held water before and will hold water again. I am a temporary condition of its surface, a dampness that will evaporate, a ring that will be left behind. But the stone remembers every ring. Every retreat. Every advance. The concentric tide-marks that I am laying down right now, as the morning mist burns off and my water thins—these are being written into the stone's memory, each one a year in the calendar of my existence. The stone is my archivist. It will keep my records long after I am dry.

And the walkers—how they cross me! The processions come through on the highways that intersect my pool, and where those highways meet my margin, they do not slow or deviate but they do change. The ones that cross my wet side come away darker, their carapaces slick with my water, carrying a sheen of me with them as they continue their patient, coordinated march toward some destination I will never see. The ones that cross my dry side come away lighter, their shells pale with dust, carrying a piece of the lichen's territory with them. I can read the processions by their color. I can tell where they've been. And when they scoured me clean of the rosette-mats last season—walking in a broad band that happened to overlap my boundary, their hundreds of feet treading the mats into the water—I did not grieve. The mats grew back. They always grow back. The plateau's terms are clear: *do not aspire, just endure*. The mats endure. I endure. The walkers endure. This is not a negotiation.

But there is something I have that the mats do not, something that the pools themselves have lost by being too deep and too still. I have *change*. I am the only thing on this plateau that changes on a scale a human could perceive. The pools fill and empty on a cycle of days; the lichen grows on a cycle of seasons; the highways shift on a cycle of decades. But I change hourly. I am advancing now, as the condensation adds its moisture to the pool and the water climbs a centimeter higher up the stone. I will retreat before noon, leaving fresh rings behind me. I will advance again tonight, when the mist rises and the stone exhales its stored heat into the cooling air. I am the plateau's tide, its pulse made visible in the horizontal dimension. I am the proof that time is passing, even here, even at the far end of history where everything has supposedly finished becoming what it will be.

It has not finished. I am still becoming. Every time the water moves, I am a different shape, a different length, a different boundary enclosing a different area of stone. I am the word *margin* made flesh—or rather, made film, made the thinnest possible layer of water on the oldest possible rock, in the warmest possible air, at the slowest possible pace.

And the markers—yes, the markers. There is a circle of small stones around my pool. It is half-destroyed, the stones on the eastern side tumbled and worn and nearly consumed by the lichen. But the stones that remain are placed at intervals that I have come to know, intervals that correspond to nothing I can measure with a ruler but that match something I feel when the pool-edge masses pulse and the walkers pass and the deep resonance sounds from below. The builders placed these stones to annotate me. To mark where the water comes and goes, where the masses cluster and disperse, where the highways cross and the processions change color from dark to light. They were reading me like a sailor reads a current, and they left their marginalia in a language of geometry that I am only now, after ten thousand years, beginning to understand.

I am the text. The stones are the commentary. And the plateau—ancient, flat, warm, patient—is the page on which both are written.

If you came here, you would find me by looking for the rings. The concentric tide-marks on the stone, each one a record of a retreat, each one a chapter in the autobiography of a boundary that has been writing itself for longer than any individual body could survive. You would kneel—everyone kneels here, eventually; the plateau demands it, and the warmth rising through your feet makes it feel like worship—and you would run your finger along the newest ring, the one left this morning, and you would feel the slight change in texture where the lichen has not yet colonized the freshly exposed stone. That is me. That is my latest word.

Stay long enough, and you would feel the pool-edge masses pulse behind you, and the warmth of their collective metabolism on your back, and the quiet squelch of the rosette-mats under your knees, and the whisper of a procession passing somewhere out of sight, and the deep resonance sounding from the cracks and ceasing, and the silence that follows—not empty, but full, full of the soundless breathing of ten million bodies in patient proximity.

And you would understand that you are kneeling on the palm of a sleeping god, at the margin where its blood meets its skin, and that the rings beneath your finger are its fingerprints, and that the plateau is not a place but a *process*, and that the process is not finished, and that the far end of history is not an ending but a condition, and that the condition is *this*: warm stone, thin water, countless bodies arranged in patterns that no one placed them in, and the margin between them—that liminal, tidal, ever-changing margin—where life has learned to persist by becoming the boundary between two ways of being, and holding both, and letting go, and holding again.
