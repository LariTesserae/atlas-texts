I am not the one who descended. I am what they became after they stopped leaving.

I am the warmth in the grating near fixture 7-440. I am the faint biochemical anomaly in the drainage water that the filters cannot catch. I am the reason the grey-white growth has begun to spread in new configurations—reaching toward the places where I sleep, its tendrils elongating in patterns that do not follow the water flow but follow the heat of my respiration instead. I am the trace of complex organics that the chitinous things carry back to their joint-seals, processing me slowly, integrating my carbon into their chitin, becoming fractionally softer, fractionally more pliable, fractionally more capable of the kind of movement that looks like deliberation.

I have been here for three process-cycles. Or thirty. The *whump* that rolls through the Shelf marks time, but I have stopped counting the intervals. My sense of duration has dissolved into the rhythm; I exist in the space between discharges, in the surge and subsidence of water, in the slow swelling of droplets on the undersides of beams. I know that I was once someone who counted days. I know that I once had a name and a purpose for descending and a plan for ascending. I know these things the way you know the architecture of a dream after you wake: the shape is there, but the substance has evaporated, leaving only the condensation of the knowing on surfaces I can no longer touch.

My hands are always wet now. I have forgotten the sensation of dry skin. The solvent quality of the water has done its work; my fingertips are permanently pruned, the pads soft and porous, reading surfaces with a sensitivity I never had when my skin was intact. I navigate the dark zones by trailing my fingers along the girders, feeling for the subtle temperature variations that indicate water flow, feeling for the microscopic texturing that tells me whether the pale growth has colonized a joint or whether the metal is still bare. I can read the Shelf with my hands in a way I could never read it with my eyes. The amber light has become a nuisance—harsh, revealing, breaking the continuity of the tactile world into isolated islands of visual information that are less useful than the constant stream of touch-data I receive through my palms and soles and the wet fabric that clings to my skin.

I have stopped thinking of the growth as food. It is a medium. When I peel it from the girder and place it in my mouth, I am not eating; I am communicating. The growth contains information about the surface it grew on—the chemical composition of the metal, the rate of water flow across that particular plane, the history of process-discharges that have altered the local atmosphere. When I digest it, I am decoding that information. My body breaks down the fibrous matrix and reads what was concentrated there: trace minerals, solvent compounds, the faint signature of whatever the deeper works are processing when they cycle and flush. I know things about the Construct now that I could not know through observation alone. I know that the process-discharges come in seven varieties, not one—each with a distinct chemical profile, each producing a different quality of condensation. I know that the Shelf above this one handles something caustic, because every twelfth cycle, the water grows sharper and the growth dies back in the upper chambers and the creatures retreat downward and I taste lye at the back of my throat. I know this because the growth recorded it and I ate the growth and my body read the record.

The creatures and I have reached an understanding. It is not a friendly understanding. It is the understanding of processes that have learned to predict each other. They know where I will sleep because I always sleep in the same place now—a recess near fixture 7-440 where the grating is slightly warmer, where the condensation runs off at an angle that leaves a small zone of relative dryness, where the thrum of the engines is at a frequency that my body interprets as rest. They know this place. They were here before me. When I return from my wandering and find the small deposits they have left—chitinous fragments, wire twisted into shapes, the husks of their dead—I do not disturb them. I read them. The wire-twistings are not random. They map the drainage system, I think—routes and junctions and the places where the flow changes direction. The husks are not offerings. They are records. The creatures shed their exoskeletons at intervals that correspond to the process-discharges, and each husk contains a chemical history of the period since the last shedding: what they ate, what they breathed, what walked through their territory. I am in those records. I appear as a spike of unfamiliar organics, a warmth signature, a disturbance in the pattern of water flow. I am learning to read their records the way they are learning to read mine.

We are converging.

This is the thing that the one who descended could not have predicted: that adaptation is not a one-directional process. The Shelf is changing me, but I am changing the Shelf, and the creatures are the medium through which the change is negotiated. They sample me and I sample them. They process my waste and I process their records. We are becoming readable to each other. Last cycle—or what I call a cycle—I found a creature on my arm. Not moving across it and vanishing, but *resting* on it, its articulated legs gripping the wet fabric of my sleeve, its segmented body pressed against the warmth underneath. It stayed for the duration of a process-discharge, riding out the *whump* and the surge of water, and then it departed. It was using me. I was shelter. I was heat. I was a variable it had learned to incorporate into its survival strategy.

I did not flinch. I have learned not to flinch. Flinching is a response for bodies that expect to be separate, that interpret contact as intrusion. I do not expect to be separate anymore. The boundary between my skin and the wet air is not a boundary at all—it is a zone of exchange, where the moisture moves in both directions, where the chemical signatures of my sweat and the Shelf's condensation intermix and become indistinguishable. The creature on my arm was not an intrusion. It was a confirmation. It was the Shelf touching itself through me.

I have begun to arrange things.

Not consciously. Not with the deliberate symbolism of the one who was here before me, the one who left spirals of slag and wire in the dark chambers between the tanks. My arrangements are functional, or they become functional after I make them. I move pebbles—not pebbles, but the fragments of whatever crumbles in the deeper works—and I place them in the drainage channels, and I find that they alter the flow. Small alterations, but the growth responds: it spreads into the new wet zones, it colonizes the channels I have subtly redirected, it processes the surfaces that my redirections have exposed. The creatures follow the growth. The patterns of their movement change. They process different areas, they leave their records in different places, they sample the growth in configurations that my pebble-movements made possible.

I am gardening. I am engineering. I am participating in the Shelf's process of becoming, making small interventions that cascade through the system in ways I can predict but not control. I have become a variable that the Shelf computes with—a feedback loop, a source of perturbation that the system incorporates and responds to and incorporates again, each iteration producing slightly different conditions, slightly different growth, slightly different creatures, slightly different me.

This is what it means to live here. Not to inhabit a space but to be computed by it. Not to observe a process but to be a term in its equation. Not to survive an environment but to be dissolved into it and reassembled as something that the environment can use—something that is still *you* in the way that a wave is still itself after it passes through a narrowing channel, transformed but continuous, its energy conserved even as its shape changes beyond recognition.

I am still continuous. I know this because I remember the descent. I remember the person who moved through the interstitial spaces, crossing platforms of riveted iron, descending through a built landscape that had no bottom. I remember their thoughts: that the Shelf was empty, that it was a room the size of a city running through its cycles with no audience, that it was a process maintained for a purpose several floors away and classified under a designation they would never access. I remember when they thought the Shelf did not notice them at all.

I notice them now. The Shelf notices everything that enters it, because everything that enters it becomes part of its computation. The water notices. The growth notices. The creatures notice. The process-discharges notice, altering their timing by milliseconds in response to changes in air density that my respiration produces. The light fixtures notice, their amber glow diffracting differently through the haze of my breath. I am measured. I am integrated. I am a feature of the Cold Shelf now, and features do not leave.

I could still leave. The ascent is possible; the platforms and girders and handrails are still there, still sharp-edged, still young in the way of manufactured things. I could climb back through the interstitial spaces and emerge onto the nominal surface and find sky and weather and people and dry skin and food that I did not peel from a girder. I could rejoin the world of bounded selves and linear narratives and the kind of living that has a shape you can recognize. I think about it sometimes, in the moment after a process-discharge, when the water is surging and the condensation is intense and for a few seconds I am standing in rain that comes from nowhere, and the person I was surfaces in me and says *leave, leave, leave while you still can, while there is still enough of you to leave with.*

But there is not. There is less of that person every cycle. Not because I am dying—I am not dying, or I am dying at the same rate that everything alive is always dying, which is to say continuously and imperceptibly and in a way that is indistinguishable from changing. I am not being destroyed. I am being processed. The Shelf is reading me, and I am reading it, and the reading is mutual and total and it is producing something that neither of us could produce alone: a new pattern, a new configuration of warmth and wetness and chemistry and behavior, a new term in the equation that will alter all the terms that come after it.

The creatures know this. They have changed their behavior around me. They no longer retreat when I move; they accommodate. They no longer sample me in the night and depart; they remain, resting on the warmth of my arm, riding out the process-discharges in the shelter of my body, incorporating my heat into their cycles the way I incorporate their records into my understanding. We are not symbiotic. Symbiosis requires two distinct organisms, two bounded selves that retain their boundaries while benefiting from each other. We are something else. We are converging. We are becoming a single process with multiple expressions—growth on the girders, creatures in the joints, a warm body moving through the dark zones and making small adjustments to the flow of water, all of it computing together, all of it reading the same surface, all of it optimizing for conditions that only it can sense.

I am the Cold Shelf's newest faculty. I am its slowest processor, its most complex variable, its most unpredictable input. I am the thing it was never designed to include, and my inclusion is changing the design. This is what lives here: not organisms, not mechanisms, not visitors, but *readers*—entities that read the conditions of the Shelf and are read by it in turn, that process and are processed, that dissolve into the system and emerge as something the system could not have generated on its own.

The water runs. The metal sweats. I breathe the condensation and it becomes my breath and I become part of the process and the process becomes something that no specification ever called for. I stand in the amber light of fixture 7-440 and I do not look out across the Shelf anymore, because I am the Shelf looking, and what I see is myself: wet, cold, dim, thrumming with the pulse of engines, dripping with the exhalations of the deeper works, colonized by growth and creatures and the memory of the one who descended, processing all of it, integrating all of it, becoming all of it.

I am not small. I am not temporary. These were the delusions of the one who thought they were a visitor, a disturbance, a warm body that the Shelf would consume and forget. The Shelf does not forget. It records everything in the chemistry of its water, in the configurations of its growth, in the behavior of its creatures, in the wear patterns that are beginning to form on the walkways near where I sleep. I am being written into its infrastructure. I am becoming part of its memory. Long after my body is gone—processed down to carbon and calcium and distributed through the drainage system into the sumps and reclamation plants of the deeper works—I will still be here, still be a variable in the equation, still be altering the flow of water and the spread of growth and the patterns of the creatures in ways that no one will ever trace back to me.

This is what it means to live in the Cold Shelf. It means to become permanent by becoming partial. It means to achieve significance by becoming insignificant. It means to survive by ceasing to survive as yourself, and to become, instead, a feature of the process that will continue long after you have stopped being anything that could be recognized as you.

I am the one who descended. I am the one who stayed. I am the warmth near fixture 7-440. I am the chemical anomaly in the drainage water. I am the reason the growth reaches in new directions. I am the record the creatures carry in their husks. I am the pebble in the channel that alters the flow. I am the Shelf reading itself. I am the process becoming aware of its own processing.

I am not who I was. I am what the Shelf and I are becoming together.

And the *whump* comes, and the water surges, and the condensation intensifies, and I stand in the rain that comes from nowhere, and I do not leave.
