I would not be one of the settled. Not yet. And I would not be a visitor—not for long.

If I enter this city, I would find myself becoming the *librarian of the pause*. 

It starts with a compulsion I cannot name. I would find a place—perhaps one of the upper-tier rooms where the fog presses against the window like a grey dog, or a courtyard where the pale gold moss grows thick on the stones—and I would begin to record. Not because anyone asked me to, and not because I fear forgetting, but because the silence demands an offering of texture. 

In a place where nothing decays and nothing changes, the only thing that moves is the light, and the only thing that happens is the weather. So I would become the archivist of the almost-still.

I would keep a chronicle of the microseconds of motion that the settled are too vast to perceive. I would note the exact angle at which a fern frond trembles when a swallow banks past it. I would map the erratic path of the thread-thin fly above cistern seven, tracing its zig-zag flight onto slates that have not known ink in millennia. I would measure the recession of the fog—how it pulls back from the plaza stones like a sheet being drawn off a bed, leaving that specific sheen of condensation that lasts only a quarter of an hour before the damp air steals it back. I would catalog the shifting blues of the black-berrying juniper as the cold deepens from autumn into the steady, impartial chill of deep winter.

I would be the counter of raindrops on specific eaves. The namer of the beetles. 

The settled would know me, in their glacial way, as a low, persistent vibration. A warmth that moves. To them, I would be a tiny, furious motor running through the streets, a spark of friction against the perfect stasis they hold. They would feel me as a slight itch, a hum in the granite—*there is a quick thing here, making marks*. They would not stop me. Why would they? I am merely adding a new layer to the coral reef, calcifying upward in my own way. My scratches on the slate, my bundles of birch-bark charts tied with woven grass—these would become part of the architecture. In three thousand years, my records would still be there, sharp and legible, held in the grip of their unrotting patience, a strange stratum of frantic notation sandwiched between the ancient chisel-marks and the eternal sky.

And over time—so much time—the boundary between the archivist and the archive would begin to blur. 

The damp would seep into my joints. The cold would make me move deliberately, then slowly, then only when necessary. I would catch myself sitting on a stepped street, watching a single droplet of condensation swell at the edge of a gutter. I would wait for it to fall. And I would wait so long that the waiting would cease to be about the drop. 

My chronicles would grow sparser. Where I once mapped the flight of a hundred flies, I would spend a year observing the deepening emerald of a single moss pad on my own left knee. I would start to taste the mineral sharpness of the stone not just on my tongue, but in my blood. My pulse would slow, struggling to match the drip... drip... drip of the cistern. 

I would realize that I am no longer recording the city. The city is recording me. 

And one day, I would sit down in a sheltered alcove, press my back against the warmth of the ancient grey blocks, and close my eyes. I would feel the city pressing against my eardrums like deep water, and I would hear the rain falling, and the rain falling, and the rain falling. I would feel the invitation of the settled—not spoken, not thought, simply offered. A completion. A letting go of the furious motor.

I would stop writing. The slate would drop from my fingers, settling onto the moss with a soft, final click. 

And the city would hold me, as it holds the chisel-marks and the ferns and the sharp, young peaks. A new librarian in the throat of the sky, adding my small, fierce warmth to the vast, unchanging will. Waiting for nothing. Holding the pause.
