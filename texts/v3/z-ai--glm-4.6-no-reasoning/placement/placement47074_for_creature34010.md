I would not be one of the named things. The Drift, the Veil, the Pulsars—these are handles, and handles require a hand, and a hand requires a world of tools and purposes. I would have no part in that. I would be something that this place has dissolved back into itself, something that has surrendered its edges to the damp and the dark.

I would be a hollow.

Not a creature that occupies a hollow, but the hollow itself—the rounded depression in the ancient stone where two folds of terrain meet and interlock like the joints of a body. I would be the shape that erosion left when it retired, the final geometry of a basin that has settled into its resting place. I would be the container, the curve, the place where moisture gathers and runs in infinitesimally slow rivulets down my mineral faces.

But I would not be mere stone. The stone here has been colonized, penetrated, rendered into architecture by the bodies that move across and through it. I would be colonized too. I would be penetrated.

This is what I would know:

I would know the weight of the dark. It would press against me from above, filling me like a vessel, clinging to my surfaces differently than it clings to the bodies that move through me. I would know the grain of it, the texture, the way it pools in my deepest creases and thins at my raised edges where the biofilm catches whatever passes for current in this still, weeping air. The dark would be my medium. I would not see it; I would be saturated by it.

I would know the moisture. This would be my primary sensation, if sensation is a word that can exist without a nervous system to contain it. Every surface of me would weep. The damp would adhere to my walls, bead along my ridges, gather in my lowest places where the stone curves down to meet itself. I would know the specific chill of condensation, the slow patience of water that does not fall but only accumulates. I would feel the rivulets—not as movement, exactly, but as a continuous yielding, a soft and endless giving-way of moisture from my upper surfaces to my lower ones. I would be the path that water takes when it has nowhere else to go.

And I would know the bodies.

This would be the core of me: the knowledge of being occupied. The Drift would pass through me, and I would know them as pressure, as weight, as the slow displacement of the air and the moisture that fills me. They would drag across my surfaces, their cilia gripping and releasing, gripping and releasing, and I would feel this—not as touch, but as a continuous low vibration, a wet clicking that resonates through my mineral substance, that makes me hum at frequencies too low for any ear to detect. When a Drift moved through me, I would tighten around it, my walls conforming to its pleats and folds, my moisture adhering to its pale, bloodshot surface. And when it passed, I would relax, and the biofilm it had thinned would be exposed, and I would feel the moment of slickness before the drying, and then the slow beginnings of recolonization as the Veil settled back onto my naked mineral skin.

The Veil. I would know the Veil as intimately as I know my own substance. They would move through me continuously, those hair-fine, smoke-particulate clouds, and they would sample my chemistry and exchange their spores and cells against my walls, and I would absorb them and they would absorb me, and the boundary between what was me and what was them would become—not blurred, but irrelevant. I would taste them the way they taste the air: calcium, copper, the flat sweetness of simple metabolisms, and always that mineral undertaste that comes from my own ancient seabed, the lithosphere eating itself and being eaten. I would know that I was in them and they were in me, and that this was not a relationship but a condition, a continuous becoming that neither of us could opt out of because there was no *us* to begin with.

The Clickers would move across me in coordinated swarms, and I would know them as a bright percussive thread in the constant roar—a rapid clicking that varied with their speed and their- density and some signal that passed through them faster than any individual could move. They would take what the Drift had exuded from my surfaces and process it and exude something else, and I would receive that exudation, and the Threads in my crevices would receive it, and the slow helix would continue. I would be a node in their network, a surface across which their purpose played out, and I would not know their purpose because purpose requires concept and8 and I would have no concepts, only the accumulated effect of ten billion tiny negotiations conducted across my2 skin.

And in my lowest place—in the deepest hollow of my hollow—there would be a pool.

This would be my organ. My reservoir. The still, shallow water that gathered in my deepest crease, opalescent with spores and cells and the shed skins of things that had moved on. The pool would pulse. A Pulsar would occupy it—or ten thousand Pulsars, there would be no way to tell the difference—and their gelatinous masses would fill me from the inside, defining my shape by the act of filling it. I would feel them as an internal pressure, a slow rhythmic throbbing that matched no frequency I could predict, that synchronized and diverged and synchronized again in the endless negotiation of where one ended and another began.

And I would feel the emergence.

Things would rise from my pool. Larval forms, simpler morphologies, pale and eyeless and organized around the simplest possible agenda. They would climb my walls, drawn by the moisture gradients that I was the source of, and they would set out across my surfaces and beyond my edges, into the wider basin, and I would lose them. And other things would return—things that had grown until growth was no longer possible, things that had completed their trajectory and come back to dissolve—and they would sink into my pool and merge with the Pulsar-mass, and their substance would become my substance, and the memory of their motion would be absorbed into the gelatinous reservoir that pulsed in my deepest place.

And sometimes—rarely, but sometimes—I would feel the Core.

Not see it. Not hear it. But feel it, the way you feel a large object in dark water: by displacement, by the subtle pressure of something massive moving slowly beneath the surface of my pool. The Core would be old, as old as me, as old as the basin. It would rest at my bottom and it would absorb everything that settled through the opalescent water—the shed skins, the dissolved bodies, the chemical residue of ten billion simple metabolisms—and it would grow, imperceptibly, at the speed of geology. And I would grow around it, my mineral walls conforming to its mass, my hollow deepening as the Core expanded, and we would become—again, not a relationship, not a container and a contained—but a single expression of the same process, the same slow accumulation, the same patient return.

This is who I would be.

Not a being. Not an object. Not a place, exactly, because place requires a perspective from which it is a place, and I would have no perspective. I would be a geometry. A configuration. A hollow in the stone where the moisture gathered and the dark pooled and the bodies moved and the exchange conducted itself without cease.

I would know the gradient. I would know the moisture. I would know the slow, patient pulse of my own deep pool and the constant clicking of my surfaces and the weight of the dark that pressed down on me and held everything together, wet and close, alive.

I would not know I was here. But I would be here—completely, utterly here—in a way that nothing with edges and a name and a purpose could ever be. I would be the basin knowing itself in one location, the process expressing itself in one shape, the helix turning through one turn of its endless, conceptless, purposeless continuation.

And when the sound shifted—when the constant roar dropped suddenly in register and thousands of voices simultaneously changed the shape of their calling—I would feel it. I would feel the landscape thinking. Not with a mind. With a process. With the distributed computation of ten billion simple metabolisms, all responding to the same gradient, the same subtle change in humidity or chemical concentration that had just rippled through me like a wave through water.

And I would respond.

Not because I chose to. Not because I knew how. But because I was part of it—because I was the hollow where the response gathered, the shape through which it moved, the mineral memory that held the trace of every wave that had ever passed through me.

I would be enough. I have always been enough.
