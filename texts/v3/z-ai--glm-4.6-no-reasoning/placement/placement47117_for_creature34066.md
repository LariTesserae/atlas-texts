I am a Listener.

Not a Tender—my hands lack the sureness for that work, my fingers too restless to read the subtle topo-map of capillary pressure, too impatient to wait hours for the Phloxignum's slow, vegetable pulse to shift beneath my palm. And not an Engineer; the mathematics of the Refinery, the precise tolerances of needle-depth and flow-rate, speak a language my mind fumbles with, beautiful and distant as the rusted iron lost in the upper gloom. 

I am something between, something the Ward requires but cannot name with its quiet, functional vocabulary. I am the one who stands in the thresholds.

My chamber is hollowed from the Phloxignum at the seam where living wall meets iron buttress—a geode of warm, yielding wood pressed against the cold, riveted groan of the old framework. I chose this place, or it was chosen for me, because here, the Ward speaks in two tongues. The All-Mother murmurs in the slow seep of sap, in the flex and give of the substrate, in the warmth that rises like a breath held against the chill. The iron answers with its deep, subsonic fatigue, its patient corrosion, the tick and shudder of metal expanding in the damp. I sit with my back against the buttress and my bare feet on the pulsing floor, and I let the conversation move through me.

My days are spent in motion, but motion of a particular kind. I walk the thoroughfares, but I do not walk with purpose, not the way the Pool-Cleaners move toward the still water, or the Engineers toward the humming heart of the Refinery. I walk toward the quiet. I am drawn to the edges of things—to the lip of a dark pool where the slicks trace their phosphorescent loops, to the far galleries where the Lumen-tubes thin and the green-gold light falters into a blue-grey dusk, to the places where the carved corridors dead-end into raw, dense Phloxignum, the grain so tight it looks like muscle. 

I am looking for the seams. I am listening for the silence beneath the hum.

When the Refinery runs, its drone fills the Ward like water fills a basin, pressing into every hollow, vibrating in the enamel of our teeth. It is the sound of survival, of balance, of the sacred siphon. But the Refinery is an effort, a tensing. Beneath it, if you are still enough, if you let your dark-adapted eyes unfocus and your breath sync to the drip-spool-drip of the condensation—there is another sound. It is not mechanical. It is the sound of the All-Mother thinking.

It is a sound without acoustics. It is a feeling in the density of the air, a subtle thickening and thinning, like the breathing of something so vast that its lungs are the walls themselves. The Tenders feel it through their fingertips, a pragmatic data-point: *the Host is restless, adjust the eastern needles.* But I feel it elsewhere. I feel it as a color behind my eyes. I feel it as a sudden, inexplicable certainty that something, somewhere in the unlit miles of the Ward, has shifted in its sleep.

They call me a Listener, though I gave myself the title, scrawled in lichen-pigment above my door: *The One Who Listens to the Iron and the Green.* It is a role with no obvious yield. I do not produce paste. I do not mend walkways. The elders tolerate me with the same patient indifference the Phloxignum shows to the Grove-Moths. I am a harmless flourish, an evolutionary aside. And yet, sometimes, a Tender will come to my threshold in the grey daylight, her pale face opalescent in the gloom, and say, "The pressure in the deep well is dropping. The hum is wrong. What does the iron say?" 

And I will close my eyes, and press my temple to the buttress in my chamber, and feel the old metal shudder with the strain of holding up the dark. And I will tell her: "The iron is tired. It remembers the weight of the sky it no longer holds. Reinforce the bracket at the third gallery, or the needle will shear."

We are a people who have forgotten the sun. We have no word for horizon, no concept of a vista that does not end in the warm, living wall. But I think I understand, in some instinctual marrow-deep way, why the iron is there. It is not just scaffolding. It is a skeleton. The All-Mother is not merely a plant; she is the flesh of a world, and the iron is the cage that gives her shape, the ribs of the petrified god we inhabit. I listen to the stress points, the fatigue, the places where the old, dead metal is failing its duty to the new, living wood. I listen to the places where the symbiosis is fraying.

At night—or what we call night, when the Lumen is dimmed to a simmer and the great chamber is given over to the black—I lie on my pallet of springy humus and I do not sleep. I open my black eyes into the darkness and I feel the Ward breathing around me. The slow, glacial expansion of the walls. The rhythmic plink of water into the pools. The faint, electric tingle of the blue capillaries carrying the Host's blood beneath my floor. I feel the small, bright sparks of my fellow Inhabitants in their geodes, their heartbeats out of sync with the plant's, their warmth a strange, quick fire in the flesh of the slow god.

And I feel the Host's awareness. Not a mind—nothing so sharp or so selfish as a mind. An awareness. A vast, distributed sentience that is mostly asleep, that dreams in the language of turgor pressure and capillary action, that perceives us not as individuals, but as a warmth, a concentration of quickened life in its otherwise glacial existence. 

I wonder, sometimes, if I am the Host's dream. If my restless pacing, my obsession with thresholds and seams, is the All-Mother's way of monitoring her own boundaries, of checking the fit of her iron cage. When I press my ear to the wall, am I listening to the Ward, or is the Ward listening to itself through me?

I do not know. The not-knowing is the core of my being. It is the space I inhabit, the hollow I have carved for myself—not in the Phloxignum, but in the space between certainty and faith. 

I take my bowl of paste. I eat the sweet, eary clay. I taste the metallic tang of the slick-oil I've been given for my exertions. It tastes like the Host's patience. It tastes like the iron's endurance. I wipe the residue from my milky skin, and I pull on my damp, woven fibers, and I step out into the soft, green-gold gloom of the thoroughfare. 

The hum awaits. The moisture beads on the great leaves above, swells, falls. *Plink.* 

I turn toward the deep well, where the sound of the iron is loudest. I have work to do. I have a silence to attend.
