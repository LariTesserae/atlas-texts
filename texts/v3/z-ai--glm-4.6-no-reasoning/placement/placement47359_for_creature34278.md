Here, there is no ground. There is only *less-up* and *more-down*. I was born *less-up*, in a hammock woven from my mother’s own hair and the shed silk of a spinnerwurm, strung between two ironwood pegs driven into the granite cheek of our world. My first sound was not a cry, but the gentle *plink... plink... plink... of condensation dripping from the underside of our neighbor’s floor into a shared skin bucket. My first sight was the soft, green glow of the under-fungi my father cultivated on the shaded wall of our dwelling.

I am a Carver.

My name is Kael, though spoken names are heavy things here, rarely used. I am known by the feel of my hands on the rock, by the rhythm of my chisel. My eyes are not like the Silk-Riders’, who scan the bruised sky for the turn of a cloud. My eyes are for the dark. They are large, like all of our people's, but they are milky at the edges, accustomed to teasing shape from shadow. They see the subtle gradations in the stone that others miss: the faint shimmer of a vein of quartz that promises a weakness, the deep, solid malachite of a place that will hold water for a thousand years.

My body is the body of a Carver. My chest is not a bellows for shouting across peaks, but a slow, steady pump for breathing thin, damp air. My legs, while broad and strong like a Ridge-Wrangler’s, are for bracing, not for striding. I stand for hours in a carved niche, my body a wedge, my weight driving my tools. My arms are my story. One is corded with the muscle of holding, of gripping the rock, of belaying my partner on a rope that is my only connection to anything called "else." The other is marked. A long, pale scar runs from my elbow to my wrist, a souvenir from a sliver of granite that sheared off when I was tapping a new cistern. It is not a mark of failure. It is a map. The stone remembered me, and I remember the stone. My hands are broad, the callouses not just hard but layered, like the city itself. I can feel the difference between wet granite and the slick, petrified moss that coats older sections of the city. I can feel the thrum of a Silk-Rider’s glider passing overhead through the soles of my soft leather boots, pressed against the wall.

I do not hear the wind the same way a Wrangler does. To me, the wind is not a language to be read, but a constant, mournful tone against which all other sounds are played. The creak of a load-bearing rope is a word. The skittering of an eyeless scutler in the fungus gardens is a footnote. The slow, groaning sigh of the city settling is the paragraph in which my life is written. My focus is down, into the stone. The city is a clamor above me, a world of swaying walkways and tethered children, but down here, in the belly, there is a different music. It is the *tink-tink-tink* of my bone mallet on an iron chisel, the sharp *crack* of a flake of granite breaking free, the percussive, hollow sound as I test the thickness of a new chamber, my voice a low hum that tells me the space is good.

My work is a conversation with the cold sculptor. The city above wants to expand, to add another platform, another cistern. The building committee, a council of Anchors and Weavers, will come to me. They will not speak in demands. They will tie a small knot in a guide-rope—a request for water storage for twenty new souls. I will feel the knot, understand its need, and I will begin the search. I will run my hands over the damp, uncarved sections of the under-city, a place no one else goes. I am looking for a promise in the rock. I will tap and listen, tap and listen, my hum changing pitch as I test the resonance. I am listening for a deep, full sound, one that says the heart of the stone is solid and will not weep or crack under the weight of the cloud’s gift.

When I find the place, I begin. It is not a violence, my work. It is a persuasion. I do not shatter the stone; I unhinge it. Layer by layer, flake by flake, I coax a cavity from the granite. The dust is fine and cold, it cakes in my hair and the lines of my skin. I am damp with sweat and condensation, a perpetual state of being that means I am never truly dry or truly warm. But I am never cold, not the way the open air is cold. The stone has a memory of the earth's core, and the work of carving generates a gentle, persistent heat.

My life is a tapestry of obligations. The rope that holds me is woven by my sister, a Master Weaver. The water I drink is channeled by my nephew, a Rope-Mason who maintains the gutters. The ridgewing meat in my stew is provided by my cousin, who walks the high arêtes. My obligation to them is the cistern. A perfect, water-tight vessel carved from the living rock is my prayer, my art, my contribution to the mutuality that holds us all from the fall. A single crack in my work is not a flaw; it is a potential catastrophe. It is a promise broken, a life Unmoored.

Sometimes, on a rare clear day when the mist parts, I will climb. I will ascend from my green-lit world, past the layers of the city, the creaking walkways, the shuttered windows. I will not go to the top, where the Silk-Riders live in the glare of the sky. I will stop on a mid-level platform, far enough up to feel the vastness of the empty air. I will watch the Silent Drakes pass, shadows in the bruised vastness. I will not look at them with awe. I will look at the rock they are passing, the sheer, unbroken face of a young mountain, and I will think, *Yes. There. That is a good place. In a hundred years, when the Weavers' lines can reach that far.* My gaze is not of wonder, but of assessment.

I do not belong to the sky, nor do I belong to the abyss. I belong to the crevice. I am a growth in the stone, a change as slow and patient as the cold. My hollow will become a new cistern, or perhaps a niche for the phosphorescent fungi. My name, my scar, the rhythm of my chisel—it will all be absorbed back into the granite. I am a Carver of Sere. I am a knot in the rope, a peg in the wall, a memory in water-dark stone. I am the foundation upon which the swaying city dares to exist.
