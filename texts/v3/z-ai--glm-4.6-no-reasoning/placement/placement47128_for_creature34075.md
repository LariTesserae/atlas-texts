I would be a Humidkeep.

We are not the walkers who drift on the city's metabolic currents, nor are we the long-lived tenants whose bodies have learned the slow cantilever of a rearranged wall. We are fewer, and our purpose is specialized. We are the Keepers of the Wetness.

I would live not in the habitation clusters, but in the intervals themselves. I am itinerant, bound to a path invisible to the walkers, a route measured not by distance but by potential.

My body is a consequence of the city's paradox: growing things that thirst in a place without rain. My form is optimized to manage the micro-doses of water. My skin is not the warm, glowing translucence of the residents, but a lattice of dark, semi-rigid filaments, like a chitinous exoskeleton that has been hollowed out and woven into a net. This lattice does not glow; it absorbs the ambient amber light, a sharp darkness against the warm city. Within this framework, my actual tissues are pale, almost white, glistening with a constant, fine sheen of moisture. The lattice serves two functions: it protects my delicate, water-hungry inner body from the drying air, and it acts as my primary instrument. My fingers are not flesh but filaments, endlessly sensitive to humidity, to temperature, to the subtle electrical charge that precedes a condensation event. To touch a surface is to read its thirst.

My senses are attuned to the city's rhythms. The long, tidal respirations of the city's metabolism are a comfort I can feel in my bones, a slow pressure that tells me when the humidity is approaching its threshold. The utterances, those low resonant tones, are my work orders. Each one passes through me, and I feel not its semantic void but its structural impact—precisely where a new stress in a tower's field will cause a change in the substrate's porosity, where a shift in a vegetative spiral will alter its dew-catch efficiency. I do not interpret them; I *am* their reading.

My function begins in the quiet moments. I find a leaf the size of a sail, its surface already beading. My filaments trace its fractal edges, feeling for the blockage in the microscopic channels that should be wicking the water inward. I find it: a single shed cell from a walker, lodged in a channel the size of a hair. I don't pluck it out. Instead, I adjust. I touch my filaments to the leaf nearby, exuding a minute enzyme, a catalyst that encourages the leaf's own tissue to re-absorb the obstruction and smooth the channel. I am a gardener of plumbing, a custodian of capillaries.

Sometimes, my task is larger. I find a section of street-walk, the warm composite, that has become sluggish in its absorption. Its pores are clogged with the dust of construction-motes and time. Here, I set my filaments and vibrate. It's a sub-audible hum, a counter-tone to the city's utterances, a resonance that loosens the matrix of the street. The composite softens beneath my touch for a moment, and the accumulated film of grime sinks into it, becoming one with the substrate. The street is thirsty again. It will drink when the next wetness descends.

We, the Humidkeeps, are the ones who guide the rare fauna. The grey flyer and the many-legged walker are not perturbations to us; they are vectors. They travel through intervals we cannot cross quickly, and their bodies shed cells and moisture as they go. They carry the seeds of new growths. I will stand still for a cycle, feeling the after-image of a flyer's passage in the air, and I will know that a new, distant canopy has been inoculated. I do not need to see it; I can feel its future thirst, a nascent pull on the district's water budget, and I will adjust the schedule of my route to accommodate it.

We do not speak. There is no need. The city is our conversation. My life is a state of constant, quiet listening. I am a node in a vast, slow network of feedback loops. When the humidity peaks and the wetness precipitates onto every surface at once, I do not only drink. I open my lattice. The dark filaments become a net, and for ten minutes, I glisten more brightly than any street, a lens of purest water collecting and channeling it not into myself, but into the ground below me. I become a temporary wellspring, a localized saturation point that sends a deeper pulse of moisture into the substrate, ensuring the thirst of this grove is met for the next cycle.
