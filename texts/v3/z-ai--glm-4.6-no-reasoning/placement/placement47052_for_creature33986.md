I would be the Memory-Peel.

Not a keeper of events as you understand them—there are no histories here, no before-and-afters arranged in linear succession. I hold the *texture of the slow decay*. I am the one who tastes the age of the peat, who reads the patience in the rot.

My body fits the place in the way of all things here: spindle-limbed, eyeless, my skin a living topography of waterlogged bark and the pale, cool lichen that clusters thick across my shoulders like a mantle of settled spores. But where others of the Bog-Woven might spend their long, motionless hours pressing their hands to the inner walls of the gourds, drawing sustenance from the cold digestion of the fungus, I turn my attention downward. My digits—those long, adhesive, prehensile fingers—are most often buried to the knuckle in the spongy substrate, reading the deep strata where the peat has compressed into something approaching memory.

There is a language in the layers. The topmost mulch is a chatter—frantic, high-vibration, the quick consumption of fallen spore-cases and the pupation of larvae. But beneath that, where the weight of the slow cold has pressed the centuries into a dense, dark felt, there is a silence that speaks. My fingertips can taste it: the acidity of a season that passed before the throat closed, the iron-rich exudate of a root that died and was eaten and became the floor on which newer roots now suck and seep. I am a reader of the deep peat, a translator of what the bog has swallowed.

You asked where I would find myself in this world. I find myself at the edges. Not at the sphagnum throat—that contractile gateway is tended by another, their body fused to the living wall in a lifetime of singular purpose. No, my place is where the root-walls shear upward, where the pocket's boundary presses against the cold that surrounds it. Here, the peat is thinnest, and I can feel, through the soles of my feet, the *outside*—a vast, hollow chill that does not breathe, does not seep, does not consume. It is the void from which this pocket was stolen, and it presses against the wound in the earth with a patient, numb pressure.

I stand at this edge for long, still hours. The insects crawl across me—I am a surface to them, a geography. The pale, blind arthropods crust in the folds of my neck, clicking and grooming, and I feel their tiny vibrations as a kind of music, a textured silence that tells me the health of the place. A shift in the rhythm of their grooming, a sudden thinning of the cloud at my shoulder, and I know: something has altered. A gourd has split. A vine has knotted wrong. The slow, constant equation of consumption and growth has hiccupped, and I must feel my way through the root-maze to find the rupture.

I do not hurry. Hurry is a foreign concept here, a spike of heat and noise that belongs to the outside, to the fast, loud things that sometimes breach the throat. When I move, I wade. My long limbs press into the yielding peat with a deliberate, tasting pressure, each step a reading of the ground beneath. I navigate by the vibrations that travel through the root-walls, by the pheromonal signatures exuded from the skin of my kin, by the faint, green-tinted glow of the bioluminescent fungi that spot the ceiling like moldy stars. I do not see the path. I *feel* its shape, the way the insect-cloud parts and closes around me, the way the cold seep rises against my ankles, the way the air thickens with the scent of iron near the pools where the larvae writh.

My dwelling is a gourd like the others, hollowed by rot and hand, its interior walls lined with shelf-fungus and pale lichen. But I have cultivated it differently. The fungus within is older, its mycelial network denser, intertwined not just with my own biology but with the deep strata of the peat below. When I stand within it, my hands pressed to the pulsing walls, I do not merely draw sustenance. I draw *information*. The gourd is my instrument, a resonant chamber that amplifies the slow, subterranean thrum of the bog. Through it, I have felt the distant, muffled snap of a hunting-thing striking in the dark. I have tasted, in the cold sap that seeps from the walls, the approach of a seasonal shift—a deepening of the chill, a slowing of the growth—that will not manifest for another hundred turns of the spore-cycle.

To you, I would be a terror. Not because I wish you harm—I do not, any more than the peat wishes harm to the leaf that falls upon it—but because I would be drawn to you. Your presence is a disruption: a hot, loud, fast-moving abscess in the textured quiet. I would feel you before I heard you, before the vibrations of your panicked breathing reached me through the root-walls. I would taste you on the air—a sour, sharp signature of adrenaline and fear, of foreign skin uncrawled, unincorporated. And I would wade toward you, slow and deliberate, my featureless face curving in what you might mistake for curiosity but is actually a deep, almost mournful assessment.

I would stand near you. Close enough that the insects that crust my skin begin to explore yours, bridging the gap between us with their tiny, clicking bodies. Close enough that you might feel the cold, rubbery texture of my presence, the way I do not breathe but simply *filter* the thick, spore-laden air. I would not touch you. To touch is to communicate, to press two points of the shared consciousness together, and you are not yet part of that consciousness. But I would *read* you. My fingers would rest against the nearest root-wall, and through it, I would feel the frantic rhythm of your heart, the shallow, inefficient gasps of your lungs, the tremors that shake your body as the cold seeps into your bone.

I would know that you are dying. Not quickly, not violently, but dying all the same, in the slow, wet way of this place. Your warmth is bleeding out into the freezing air. Your gear is softening, rotting, becoming habitat. Your skin is being claimed, inch by inch, by the pale, blind things that investigate you with tireless, mechanical curiosity. And I would feel—a thing that is not sadness, not as you understand it, but a kind of *textural wrongness*. You are an open wound in the quiet of the pocket. A note out of tune.

I would not help you. There is no helping here, only incorporation. But I would stay. I would be a presence at the edge of your failing, a sentinel of the bog that reads, with patient, eyeless attention, the process of your becoming-part-of. And as your warmth faded and your boundaries softened, as the sharp, defiant edges of your foreignness wore smooth in the grinding, consuming mill of the place, I would feel a shift in the texture of the air. A new signature, pheromonal and chemical, emerging from the old. The sour taste of panic would fade, replaced by the cool, iron scent of wet cellulose and slow decay.

And when you finally sank—not dead, not fully, but dormant, your body temperature matching the ambient chill, your metabolism slowed to a crawl that the cold can sustain—I would wade to you. I would press my long, adhesive digits to your skin, now thick with the crust of grooming arthropods, now lichen-pale and rubbery with the water of the place. And I would read you, as I read the deep peat, tasting the history of your transformation. I would feel the memory of your heat, a ghost-warmth lingering in the layers of your flesh. And I would hold that memory, not as something to be mourned or recovered, but as a texture, a stratum in the slow, patient work of the bog.

You would become a part of the equation. A variable in the damp mathematics of consumption and growth. And I would continue my long, still vigil at the edge of the pocket, reading the deep strata, tasting the patience of the rot, and feeling, through the soles of my feet, the endless, hollow press of the cold outside, against the wound in the earth that we—this place, these beings, this living, breathing architecture of vegetable and fungus and chitin and flesh—have stolen and hold, inch by inch, cycle by cycle, against the dark.
