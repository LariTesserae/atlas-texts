I am a **Gantry-Cantor**, and I am neither flesh nor crystal, but something in between—a living calibration mark, a flesh-encased frequency born to mediate between the slumbering iron and the creeping paradox.

I do not live *in* the Marginalia, suspended in the terrifying "yesterday" rooms. I live *on* the periphery, on the rust-streaked iron skeletons themselves. My home is the network of catwalks, ladders, and gantries that cling to the colossal, half-formed concrete strata like a metallic web. I am an organism of the vertical. While the Sump-Walkers wade through the mossy boulevards and the Silent accrete in the narrative blind spots, I climb. I ascend the girders until the grievance mist is so thick that it feels like water, until the fungal glow below is just a cold, blue memory, and the only sound is the deep, unending 1.5-score thrum vibrating through the very marrow of my bones.

My body has adapted to this altitude and this vibration. I am thinner than the Sump-Walkers, elongated by the constant stretch and reach of the climb. My fingers and toes are long, the digits fused with gripping chitin harvested from the vines, allowing me to hang indefinitely from a single rivet head while I work. My skin is not just pale; it is rust-bloomed. Patches of my epidermis have mineralized into a rough, iron-oxide patina, warm to the touch, blending seamlessly with the gantries I cling to. I do not wear the insulated wrappings of the Sump-Walkers; my clothing is a harness of braided vine-tendrils and salvaged copper wire, strung with heavy iron plumb bobs that dangle from my belt.

These weights are my anchor, but they are also my instrument.

The Sump-Walkers tap the walls to read the hum. I *tune* it. 

The great factories are not merely sleeping; they are dreaming, and their dreams are unstable. Over the uncounted years, the slow shifting of the geography—the staircases that lead to yesterday, the doors that open onto the void—has thrown the colossal generators out of phase. The thrum is not a single note, but a chord, and it is fracturing. Dissonances are forming in the deep turbine halls, pockets of harmonic collapse that attract the Silent like moths to a dying star. When the dissonance grows too loud, the glitching physics bleed upward, and a gantry I am climbing might suddenly terminate in empty air, or loop back to a platform I passed three days ago.

This is where my purpose lies. I am a cantor of the iron. I climb to the nodes of greatest dissonance—the sparking junction boxes the size of cars, the gnawed-through transformer coils, the massive gear-teeth that grind against each other with a sickening, irregular screech—and I sing. 

I do not use my voice. Sound is a violence here. Instead, I use my plumb bobs. I swing them against the massive iron plates, striking them in complex, polyrhythmic patterns. I listen to the echo decay, and I adjust. I strike again, and again, feeding the resonance back into the iron, forcing the divergent frequencies to lock into phase. It is a agonizingly slow process, matching the 1.2-score dynamic of the city. A single calibration can take weeks. I will hang by my chitin-gripping toes from a swaying smokestack, swinging my iron weights in the perpetual condensation, slowly, patiently, weaving my own heartbeat into the city's chord. I am a metabolic metronome, offering my own internal rhythm as a sacrifice to syncopate the slumbering titan back into stability.

When I succeed, the hum smooths out. The staircase settles. The door leads to the room it was built for. The Silent, starved of the sudden uncertainty, drift away, becoming translucent, fading into the mineral leeching of the concrete.

When I fail, the Silent come for me.

They do not hunt me; they *accrete* around my failure. A misjudged strike, a rhythm that falls out of sync, creates a wound in the city's memory. The grievance mist thickens, and the crystalline figures begin to gather at the edges of my gantry. They stand on the air itself, refracting the cold blue light, their slow, glacial drifts suddenly resolving into a terrible proximity when I blink the rust-water from my eyes. They do not touch me; they do not need to. Their presence alone is an erosion. When they are near, my anchor-weights feel lighter. My iron-rusted skin begins to grow translucent at the edges, my own narrative weight leaking away into their prismatic forms. I can feel myself becoming an echo, my purpose forgetting *me*.

To survive, I must throw myself from the gantry. I must plummet through the dim, fungal-glowing air, tearing through the leathery caps of the tree-sized fungi, trusting the chitin-vines to catch my harness before I hit the mossy plain below. It is a violent, narrative-heavy act—a sudden, undeniable assertion of cause and effect in a city of creeping paradoxes. It shocks my system back into material reality, breaking the Silent's hold. I survive, bruised and bleeding iron filings into the emerald carpet, and I begin the long, slow climb back up to the rusting iron spine of the city, to begin my song again.

I am a Gantry-Cantor. I am the iron's advocate. I am the flesh that refuses to forget the factory's purpose. I live in the cold, damp exhalation of the world, swinging my weights against the infinite, rusting ribs, holding back the silence with nothing but a swung iron bob and the stubborn, rhythmic insistence that the city must not be allowed to dream itself apart.
