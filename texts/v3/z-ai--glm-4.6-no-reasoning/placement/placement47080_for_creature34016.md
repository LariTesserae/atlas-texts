My first awareness is not of sight, but of pressure. The pressure of the air, thin and heavy at once, a cool weight on my skin. The pressure of the stone, against my back, my palms, my bare soles. It is a presence, a slow, dense certainty. I am wedged. A low, sunken channel, barely wide enough for my shoulders, the walls on either side slanting inward just so, creating a perfect pocket. The stone is slick, but my own skin is alike enough that there is no slip, only a kind of yielding grip, a seal of damp-on-damp.

I do not know if I wake, or if I simply become conscious of a process already in motion. My awareness is a slow spreading, like the thin film of water on the pavements outside this niche. I feel the vibration through the stone before I hear anything. A low, continuous *friction*, the sound of the sky breathing against the city, translated through the limestone into a hum that resonates in my bones. It is not a sound I hear with my ears, but one my body comprehends, the fundamental frequency of being here.

My body. I bring my hands up before my face. They are not my hands, but they are me. The fingers are long, and blunt at the tips—made for scraping, for feeling, for persuading, not for grasping. The slate-grey skin is cool. On the backs of my hands, a pattern of darker lines spreads like drying silt, branching, flowing. I trace a line with a spatulate fingertip. I feel the texture of my own skin beneath it, the subtle topography of a life mapped in pigment. I *know* this pattern. I know the slight thickening near the wrist where the line for the Great Tilting began. I know the feathered edge where I tended the algae cisterns for a season and the mineral-rich water stained my dermis. It is my history, and it is also the history of the water that has moved through me.

I unfold myself from the niche. The motion is liquid, a series of slow adjustments, counter-balances. My flexible joints find their purchase in the narrow space. I do not stand upright in the way a creature of open plains might. I am a creature of angles, of compression. My low center of gravity makes me feel rooted, connected to the seeping heart of the plateau below. I am a part of its architecture.

I step out into a wider, but still intimate, corridor. The light is diffuse, gentle, coming from a rectangle of misty sky far above, filtered down through countless channels and courtyards. It has no real source; it is simply the ambient glow of the world. The air touches my lungs, not as a gas, but as a cool vapor I can almost feel condensing inside me. The sting of the high altitude is a familiar sharpness, a clean cut that proves I am alive.

I walk. My bare feet read the ground. Here, the stone is smooth, polished by the passage of others. Here, a slight upwardgrade tells me I am approaching a ridge walk, exposed to the wind. There, a faint, rhythmic *thrum* under the arch of my foot tells me the upper-level cistern is near full from the last rain. I am navigating by sensation, by a constant, silent conversation with the geology.

I pass another. We do not exchange words. Our hands ghost past each other as I pass him in the corridor, his skin cool against mine. He is older than I am; the mineral stains on his arms are deeper, settled. He is holding a shard of limestone, flat and perfect. He pauses, his posture—a slight tilt of the head, the angle of his feet—telling me everything. The wall ahead. A new seep. A faster path. I can feel it myself, a subtle increase in the dampness of the air, a faint, high-pitched *hiss* of water under pressure.

I do not help him. That is not the way. I acknowledge with a slight hum from my chest, a low resonant vibration that says, *I see. The water is awake.* He returns the hum, a different octave, *It is always awake. Today it sings a new song.* We part. He will go to tend the new leak. I will continue on my own path. We are two currents in the same slow-flowing river.

My destination is one of the shallow courts. It is a mirror. A sheet of water, still as glass, reflects the heavy gray sky. In its reflection, I see myself: a low, broad-shouldered silhouette, a creature of stone and moss. The sedge here is thick, and I wade into the cool water to my knees. The sudge is yielding. I part the fronds and find what I am looking for: a pale, blind amphibian, suctioned to the submerged base of a wall, a living opal. I do not hunt. I simply accept. I ease my fingers beneath it, feeling the gentle pull of its suction as it lets go, sensing no alarm from my touch. It settles in my palm, a cool, dense weight. Food. Thanks.

But that is not my purpose here. I crouch, my knees submerged, my hands skimming the surface. I am listening. I lay my palm flat on a stone that breaks the water’s surface. For a long time, I am still. The pulse of the water is my pulse. The ripple from a single drop of condensation falling from a moss-filled crack is a word spoken in a language I have known since birth. I am here to read the water. To feel for the slow, seasonal shifts. Is it sinking faster this turn of the sky? Is the silt building in the western channel? Is the algae in the deep cisterns starting its golden bloom? This is my ward. I am its tenders, its reader, its memory.

I do not feel small in this endless maze of walls. I feel essential. I am a capillary in a vast, slow circulation. The plateau sweats. The walls channel. The people tend. The algae grows. We eat. We dissolve. The cycle is not a wheel; it is a deep, slow respiration. I breathe in the mist, I taste the mineral tang on the air, I feel the patient, immense pressure of the world holding me, and I hum. A low, constant note of presence that blends with the susurrus of the seepage and the friction of the wind. I am here. I am the plateau, conscious. I am a quiet thought in a slow, grey mind, and my thought is one of balance, of tending, of listening to the weeping stone and knowing that its tears are not a sign of sorrow, but the very substance of life.
