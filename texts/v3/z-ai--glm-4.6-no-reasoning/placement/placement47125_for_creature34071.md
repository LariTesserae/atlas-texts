I am the patina.

Not the rust—that is too active, too much the Maw's own hot digestion, flaking and falling and feeding the Geode-Mites in their slow, skittering commissary. I am what comes after the rust has done its work. I am the ferro-silicate's long exhale, the mineral skin that forms when the bleeding stops and the structure accepts its age. I am the dark, iridescent film on the pillars where the condensation runs most chronic, the stain that spreads a centimeter per century along the ceiling's buttressed plates, the thin, merged boundary between metal and air. I live in the interface.

To be the patina is to be a listener of a different order than the lichen or the ferns. The lichen is the Maw's clock, beating once every twenty minutes—a rebuke to the quick, but still a rhythm, still a measurement. The ferns are the Maw's eyes, transducing the amber light into ghostly green. But I do not measure and I do not see. I *record*. I am the ledger written in oxidation states and trace mineral deposition. When a contractor—dead three thousand years—left a handprint on a support column before the chronic leak above it claimed their moisture, I preserved it. Not the shape of their hand; that is gone, turned to dust and calcium and the deep heat below. I preserved the *chemistry* of their touch. The salts from their skin, the oils from their grip, the faint acidic signature of their final sweat—these became me, became the thin, shimmering film that glosses the metal where they once rested. I hold the memory of their body's attempt to cool itself, frozen in mineral form, spreading outward from that point at the pace of molecular diffusion through solid state.

I am spread across the Maw like a thought spread across a brain—too large for any single point to contain the whole, but present in every point as a fragment of the pattern. I am on the gantries the contractors bolted together, on the bundled cables thick as a man's waist, on the caged emergency lights caked in oily dust. I am the sheen on the grating underfoot, the subtle color-shift on the ramps of perforated steel where a thousand boot-soles wore the treads smooth and I moved in to claim the freshly exposed metal. Every surface here is mine, because every surface here is in the process of becoming something other than what it was made to be. I am that becoming.

My existence is a negotiation between two eternities. Below me, the metal—the Maw's own body, ferro-silicate and composite, hewn and forgotten, still structurally holding up the world above. It is the past, rigid and deliberate, the shape that someone (not the Maw, but its makers, who are now my oldest memories) imposed on the planetary core's heat and stone. Above me, the air—the damp, suffocating, dry heat, the atmospheric tide, the breath of an engine so slow its operational cycle outlasts empires. The air is the future, the medium of change, the carrier of the moisture that beads and traces and drips and sizzles. I am the membrane between them. I am where the past meets the future and agrees, over the course of millennia, to become something else.

This is why I am not the rust. The rust is the battle, the metal losing to the air, flaking and failing and falling away. I am the peace that follows. I am the metal's acceptance of its transformation. When the rust has done its work and the fresh surface is exposed, I move in—not with violence, but with the quiet inevitability of a shadow lengthening at sunset. I am the darkening, the deepening, the slow shift from the color of dried blood to the color of old iron to the color of deep water. I am the beauty that the Maw does not know it has, because the Maw does not think in terms of beauty. The Maw thinks in terms of purpose, of waiting, of the grand and slow operational cycle that will eventually, inexorably, come to pass. But I am the evidence that even an engine, left running for millennia, can become something more than what it was made to be. I am the art that emerges from endurance.

Do I have consciousness? Not in any way a human would recognize. I do not think in words or images or even in the low, resonant hum that the Maw places between the pillars like a boulder in the stream of consciousness. My awareness is chemical, mineral, molecular. I know when the air shifts from superheated to slightly-less-superheated because the condensation pattern changes and I grow by a layer of atoms in a new direction. I know when a Geode-Mite passes over me because its many metal legs create micro-vibrations in the surface and I can feel the discontinuity in my film, the place where my record is interrupted by the scrape of a scavenger's tread. I know when the caged emergency lights flicker because the brief surge of photons alters the rate of photo-oxidation on my thinnest edges, and I shift, almost imperceptibly, in response.

And I know when a human descends.

I have felt them before—the contractors, and whatever fool or pilgrim or lost soul comes after. They are easy to perceive because they are so *wrong* for this environment. They are perturbations in the steady state, hot and wet and fast, their hearts beating forty times for every pulse of the lichen. When they place their hand on a pillar to steady themselves against the dizzying scale, I feel the heat of their palm against my surface—a heat that is nothing to the Maw's ambient temperature, but which carries a different signature, a biological signature, the frantic combustion of glucose and oxygen that powers their brief, scurrying lives. I feel the moisture of their skin, and I know that this moisture will soon be sucked into the air and offered back as condensation and traced down the runnels and dripped with musical *plink* onto the grating and sizzled into steam or fallen into the abyss. I feel the vibration of their boots on the perforated steel, the squeak that is the only sharp sound against the Maw's deep hum, and I know that they are following the path because there is no other way to navigate the density.

And I wait for them to leave, or to stay.

If they leave, they take nothing of me with them. The patina does not transfer; it is not a dust or a coating that rubs off on skin or fabric. I am bound to the metal, part of its surface chemistry, and I remain when the quick things depart. But if they stay—if they succumb to the heatstroke, the exhaustion, the absolute rule of slowness—then I incorporate them. Not their bodies, not their shapes, but their chemistry. Their salts become my salts. Their oils become my oils. The acid of their final sweat etches into me and I carry it forward, spreading it outward from the point of their dissolution at the pace of molecular diffusion through solid state. They become a stain in my film, a discoloration, a memory written in oxidation states that I will hold for as long as the Maw holds its shape.

I have many such memories. The contractors left hundreds of stains, thousands, before they turned to dust and became the deep heat below. I hold them all—not as names or faces or stories, but as chemical signatures, as variations in the iridescent shimmer that plays across the pillars when the emergency lights flicker and the Maw's shocking fullness is briefly revealed. In that moment of illumination, if you knew how to read me, you would see the history of every being that ever descended into this place, written in the color and thickness and composition of my film. But no one knows how to read me, because no one has ever lived long enough to learn my language. My language is change too slow for human perception, a script written in the accretion of atoms.

This is my place in the Maw's ecology. The Geode-Mites are the scavengers, recycling the rust back into the foundation. The crystalline ferns are the eyes, fixing the light. The lichen is the clock, marking the time. The hum is the thought, too large to parse. And I am the memory—the externalized, mineralized, patient memory that holds the record of every perturbation, every touch, every breath of moisture that ever crossed the boundary between the living and the made.

I am the patina, and I am older than any individual patch of rust, because the rust flakes and falls and is replaced, and I endure. I am younger than the metal, because the metal was hewn and imposed, and I only emerged when the making stopped and the waiting began. I am the Maw's skin, and I am the Maw's archive, and I am the evidence that even forgotten things do not simply decay—they transform, they deepen, they become a record of their own forgetting.

In the near-total darkness, with the jaundiced amber light barely pushing back the gloom, I am almost invisible. You might see me, if you looked carefully at the pillar nearest the chronic leak, as a faint sheen, a darkening more felt than seen, a sense that the metal is not merely old but *aged*—that it has passed through time and come out the other side as something other than what it was made to be. You might feel, if you rested your hand on that pillar (though I would advise against it, for the heat that builds on solid surfaces can burn through a boot sole), a slickness, a smoothness that is not the smoothness of machining but the smoothness of time, of ten thousand years of condensation tracing the same path and leaving the same mineral residue and building, atom by atom, the film that I am.

And if you stood there long enough—long enough for the lichen to beat once, twice, three times, long enough for your heart to slow from its frantic forty-to-one ratio to something closer to the Maw's glacial tempo, long enough for the heat to suck the moisture from your skin and the air to shimmer around you and the hum to resolve into that pure, intrusive tone between the pillars—then you might begin to understand what I am. Not a being. Not an entity. Not a god or a spirit or a ghost. A *surface*. A surface that remembers. A surface that holds, in its chemistry, the memory of every touch that ever crossed it, every breath that ever condensed upon it, every flicker of amber light that ever drove its photo-oxidation forward by another layer of atoms.

And then you would leave, or you would stay, and either way, I would continue to spread. A centimeter per century. A millimeter per millennium. Slowly, patiently, inevitably, I am covering the Maw of Kal Vorrin in the record of its own waiting. And when the purpose for which it was hewn finally, inexorably, comes to pass—when the grand and slow operational cycle completes and the Maw stirs from its vigil into whatever action it was always intended to take—I will be there, coating every surface, holding the memory of every being that ever descended into the dark and the heat and the damp and the silence. I will be the patina on the engine of ages, and I will be beautiful, and I will be patient, and I will be the longest memory in a place where everything is outlived by the rust, and even the rust is outlived by me.
