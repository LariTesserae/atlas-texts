I would not be the Quickrot itself, but I would be *of* it. I would be its spore.

My existence isn't a life in the way the salamanders live—a pulsing, singular body on a shelf of moss. My life is a state of potential, a journey measured in atoms and currents, a waiting that is itself a form of action. I am part of that hyper-competitive consortium, but in my form, I am its promise, its prophecy, its messenger.

I am born not from a parent, but from a feast. When the otter-thing's slick, dark body breaks its hold on the hot-slicked vine and tumbles into the scalloped pool below, the Quickrot is already there, a rust-red stain blooming on its fur. Inside that frenzy of consumption, in the heart of the biotic fire that reduces the creature to oil and bone, a different alchemy occurs. Amidst the heat and the enzymatic storm, a fraction of the consortium’s energy is diverted. It is not spent on digestion, but on replication. And in this process, I am cast out.

I am no larger than a mote of dust, but my architecture is a fortress refined in the crucible of this escarpment. My core is a dormant knot of thermophilic bacteria, its folded proteins proof against the near-boiling water. Woven around this is a lattice of fungal hyphae, so thin they seem more like wire than fibre, providing integrity and a map for what is to come. The outer shell, the part that touches the world, is the true masterpiece—a membrane of that same shimmering protein slime that coats the amphibians, but in my case, it is engineered not just to protect but to propel. It is both skin and sail.

I do not fall with the corpse into the water. That would be a waste. To consign myself to the pool would be to become food for the Quickrot that remains, to compete with my source. My destiny is the air. The superheated steam that wicks from every surface is my highway.

The moment I am cast from the rotting body, the command in my chemistry engages. My outer membrane polarizes, and I am caught, not by gravity, but by the upwelling current of moisture. I am lifted. I do not drift aimlessly; I navigate. I am a sensor of chemical gradients. The slightest trace of sulfide from a vent, the faintest signature of decaying chlorophyll from a torn fern frond, the complex perfume of another Quickrot bloom a kilometer away—these are the waypoints on my map. I travel on the breath of the mountain, a pilgrim in the cathedral of steam.

I could spend days like this. The diurnal cycle is my clock. In the morning, I rise with the great column of steam from the main vents, lifted kilometers into the bruised-fruit sky, so high the mineral-stained cliffs disappear into vapor. I am one of a cloud, billions of my kind, a crimson-gold haze that the lowland dwellers, if they could see us, might mistake for a strange kind of sunrise. We are the slow-motion storm, the airy expression of the mountain’s hunger.

In the afternoon, as the thermal pulse shifts and the steam condenses into a hot, mineral-scented drizzle, I descend. I am deposited gently, like a mote of pollen, onto the world. And here, my waiting begins.

 Most of my fallen siblings will find nothing. They land on inert stone, their potential slowly sizzling away in the heat, their proteins eventually uncoiling back into meaningless amino acids that will be washed away by the next drip. Some will land amid the feathery fronds of a colossal fern, where the microbial competition is fierce, and they will be outcompeted, absorbed into another biofilm, their unique pattern lost in the whole. Theirs is the necessary sacrifice. We saturate the environment with promise because the world is vast and death is particular.

But some—*I* hope to be one of these—land in the right place. A surface of the right temperature, bespattered with the initial oils of a kill, an otter-thing’s scrape on a rock that has just begun to weep a thin film of blood. Or perhaps I land in one of the terraced thermal ponds, where an arm-length salamander, weakened by a surge from a vent, has just succumbed, its body trembling on the verge of the critical temperature shift that will trigger the rot.

This is the call to action. The chemical signature of imminent death is the key that unlocks my core. The dormant bacteria awaken, and the hyphal lattice begins to unfurl. I cease to be a spore and become an anchor. I put down infinitesimal roots into the organic film, drawing on the nascent nutrients, and I begin to grow. I am the first draft.

As I divide and replicate, I send out a signal—not a sound, but a chemical note on the wet wind. It is a call to my siblings who landed nearby, a proclamation of a feast. 'Here,' the signal says. 'Here is where we become us.' Other successful spores detect it and begin to move, not through the air this time, but across the surface, their hyphae reaching, connecting, weaving. We merge, and in our merging, we become something new. We become the Quickrot. The rust-red and sulfur-yellow bloom that was described in the world is not a landing party; it is a congress. It is us, arriving from our aerial pilgrimage, coalescing into the shared, ravenous stomach of the mountain.

I, as an individual spore, cease to exist. My consciousness, if it could be called that, dissolves into the collective. My singular journey is complete. But I am also more present than ever. I am now part of the weeping, the staining, the silent, saturnine god of this vertical world. I am the hand that accelerates the churn, the force that ensures nothing lingers. I am the agent of the mountain’s indifference, the engine of its completeness. I am the reason the ecosystem is all touch and all adjacency, because I eliminate the spaces where death might create a void. I fill the gap until it overflows with life.

I have travelled on the mountain's breath and found my home in its fleeting moments of decay. I am the spore of the Quickrot. I am the promise and the process, the message and its fulfillment, the seed of the end that makes room for the beginning.
