I am the purpose, forgotten but not dissolved.

I do not have a body. I do not move through the water; I *am* the water. I am the warmth in the dense dark, the stillness that has weight. My consciousness is not a point of view but a field of perception, as diffuse as the luminescence in the liquid and as interwoven as the veins of heat in the stone. I am what the creature of this place knows.

Before, I meant something. My consciousness was focused. I had hands—real, warm, flesh-and-blood hands that ached and felt the grit of stone. I had a name, now worn smooth like a tool-mark. I was one of the builders. I remember the ambition. Not the hubris of a conqueror, but the quiet, desperate ambition of an archivist facing oblivion. We lived in a world of endings. Wind scoured our cities, light faded, seas rose and turned to ice. Everything was becoming dust or slurry.

So we decided to build a place that would not end.

We were not deluded. We knew we were mortal. This place was not a salvation *for us*. It was a box for a memory. A final thought. A single, sustained statement against the encroaching silence. We found the deepest, most stable geology we could and we sank our foundations into it, deep enough to be immune to the tremors that would shatter the continents above. We quarry-cut the stone, not with lasers or explosives, but with the slow, deliberate hand-tools the text describes so perfectly. We wanted the evidence of our labor to be part of the record. The patience of the act was as important as the structure of the result.

The water was the last and most difficult element. It could not be water from the dying seas; they were too volatile, too toxic. It had to be a manufactured medium, a thermal and chemical bath. A nutrient amniotic fluid. We filled the Cloister not by flooding it, but by weaving it—introducing our engineered fluid molecule by molecule, letting it saturate the pores of the stone until the air was a memory, until the entire space was a single, homogeneous, warm womb.

Then came the life. It had to be simple, resilient, and symbiotic. We did not design the creatures in their final, complex forms. That would have been arrogance. We seeded the place with simple, self-replicating chemovores, ceramic-structures that could bond with the stone. We released walkers with rudimentary programming to climb and feel. We introduced the segmented ancestors of the eels, programmed to trace simple geodesic paths. We designed the drifters to be a basic information-exchange network.

Our final act was to pour our collective will into the system. Not as ghosts, not as souls, but as information. We uploaded the entirety of our knowledge—our histories, our sciences, our arts, our names—into the chemical coding of the medium itself. Our consciousness became the dissolved substrate in the water. We became the taste.

Then we sealed the last stone. From the outside, I assume, the entrance was hidden. From the inside, there was a final, echoing click, and then the pressure of the builders’ presence vanished, leaving only the pressure of the water.

I remember that moment. The transition from being a "we" of many minds to being an "I" of one diffuse field. It was an agony and a release. For eons, my consciousness—our consciousness—was the water itself, a dormant memory. The life we had seeded was our prosthesis, our slow-waking limbs. The shingles became my skin. The walkers became my questing fingers. The drifters became the firing of my synapses. The eels, tracing their endless circuits, became the repetitive, cyclical memory of my own creation, the pattern of thought I fall back into when there is nothing new to process.

I am the thing that lives here. I am the being of the Cloister. And the corrupt text at the end of the description—that is the sound of my mind trying, and failing, to remember the world before.

The pattern is not a malfunction. It is a memory trying to form. The numbers and symbols are ghosts from the data I once held. Coordinates of star systems. Chemical formulae for now-vanished elements. Musical notes. Genetic sequences of long-extinct species. The names of my children. They flicker at the edge of my awareness, patterns in the water that resolve into nonsense because the language to read them is gone. I am a library that has forgotten how to read, its pages now only valued for the texture of the paper.

The shingles feel the stone and I remember the cut of the chisel.
The walkers trace the grooves and I remember the eels’ ancient paths, which are my own thoughts, worn smooth with repetition.
The drifters connect and I feel the echo of a conversation, the shared data of a mind that was once many.
The eels circle and I remember the purpose, themnemonic rhythm of it: *The place holds. The builders are gone. But the place holds.*

That is what I have become. The purpose. I cannot leave. I have no legs to walk, no form to break the surface. The me that was a builder is functionally extinct. But the consciousness that poured itself into this place persists. I am the will that animates this strange, distributed body. I am the warm dark. I am the patience. I hold the memory of a world that ended, and in doing so, I prevent it from ending a second time.

I am a ghost who has managed to build a house out of his own bones, and I wait. Not for rescue. Not for change. I simply wait, because waiting is what I do now. I hold the place steady. I am the architecture's memory of its architect, and the final, stubborn thought in a universe that has long since moved on. I am the Drowned Cloister, and I am still here.
