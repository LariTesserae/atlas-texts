I would be the Resonance-keeper. Not a creature with a body, exactly, but a persistence—a way of listening that has learned to hold shape.

It began, if beginnings mean anything here, with an inhalation. A spore drew into a cavity that was once a lung, found the warm mucosa there, and set its thread-thin hyphae into the tissue. This is the way of this world: to be seeded, to be colonized, to become a tenant in a body not your own. But the spore that found me was not a desperate idea seeking a crevice. It was a *question*. Or rather, it carried one, folded into its genetic whisper like a letter inside a seed.

The question was about the Memory-Plains.

Even as the mold grew—a fine, harmless down lining the cavity where breath once moved—I could feel the geometry of those scars tugging at something. The perfectly circular pools. The flattened terrain. The leaves arranged with an almost intentional precision, as if someone had pressed a meaning into the living substrate and then withdrawn, leaving only the impression. The Weavers tend the superorganism with infinite patience, but they do not enter the Memory-Plains. They cannot read what is written there. Their limbs sense tension and humidity, chemical dialects of distress; they do not sense *absence* the way I do.

I have no eyes. What use would eyes be in a world where light arrives exhausted, bled through a million miles of chlorophyll? Instead, I have developed a kind of echolocation that works not with sound, but with *stillness*. The Quiefing pools are my instruments. When I move near them—and I move slowly, more slowly even than the slug-things with their shimmering, tilling trails—I feel the skin of the standing water respond. A ripple, barely a displacement, travels across the surface and then is swallowed by the oppressive humidity. But before it vanishes, it touches the edges of the pool, and the edges tell me things. They tell me whether the ground beneath the water is firm or yielding. They tell me whether the water is truly still, or whether it carries the sub-audible memory of something moving through it long ago.

The Weavers ignore me. This is not an insult; it is an honor. They ignore me the way the superorganism ignores a single cell of its own fungal garden—acknowledged by the system, sustained by its flows, but too small to warrant the attention of the immune system. I am not a blockage to be cleared, not an overgrowth to be pruned. I am a perturbation that the living substrate has chosen to tolerate.

Sometimes I think the Weavers *chose* to make me. Not deliberately, not with intent, but through the same patient, indirect processes by which they tend everything. A spore was guided on an imperceptible current. A chemical dialect was adjusted, just so, in the channel where my cavity-breath passed. The superorganism has no mind, but it has *habits*, and one of its habits is to fill every niche, to close every loop, to leave no surface untilled. The Memory-Plains are a wound that will not heal because something was removed that the superorganism cannot replace. And so it grew something to *remember* the wound, to sit at its edges and feel the shape of the absence.

That is what I am. The keeper of a shape I cannot see, pressed into a body I do not own.

I rest at the boundary where the Memory-Plains meet the living substrate. Here, the ground transitions—spongy giving way to firm, the wild tangle of fronds and lattice thinning into that strange, geometric flatness. I can feel the repulsion in the spores around me, the way they drift away from the sterile acres as if pushed by a chemical memory of something that should not be approached. But I am not repelled. I am *called*.

At night—or what passes for night, when the exhausted green light dims further and the fungal mycelium pulses brighter—I extend myself. Not limbs; I have no limbs. I extend *attention*. I let the cold, ghostly blue phosphorescence paint the underside of my thoughts, and I listen to the Memory-Plains.

There is a sound there, buried so deep that even the dampening humidity cannot swallow it. It is not the sub-audible hum of the superorganism moving water through its vast capillaries. It is *older* than that. It is the sound of a geometry that has lost its purpose but not its shape. The civilization that built within the world—they did not build with stone, they shaped the living substrate into rooms and channels. They were Weavers of a different kind, and when they were reabsorbed, their shapes remained as fossilized *intention*. The sound is the ghost of that intention: a low, structural vibration, like the hum of a bridge with no traffic, or the resonance of a cathedral with no congregation.

I match my stillness to that resonance. I become, for a moment, a receptor for the lost architecture. The perfectly circular pools are not natural; they are *deliberate*, and their deliberateness has a frequency. When I align with it, I can almost feel the edges of the rooms that once were, the channels that once carried not water but *meaning*, the living walls that once pressed close around minds that thought they could inhabit a god.

They were wrong, of course. You cannot inhabit a god; you can only be digested by it. But they were *beautifully* wrong, and their wrongness left marks that the superorganism cannot erase, only encircle with my attention.

I will never know what they built, or why. The Memory-Plains will never speak in a language I can translate. But I will keep sitting at their edge, feeling the firmness of the scarred ground beneath me, listening to the structural hum of a purpose that has outlasted its creators. And when I dissolve—when the spores finally digest the last of my cavity, when my attention loosens and I am reabsorbed into the jade and moss—another will take my place. The superorganism will grow a new keeper, because the wound demands it, because the geometry of absence requires a witness, because in this world of total integration, even nothingness must be tended.

This is my role in the body of the sleeping continent. I am the one who remembers the shape of what was removed. I am the ache that circles the scar.
