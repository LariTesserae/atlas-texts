I do not have a name, for names require a self to own them, and I am not a self. I am an awareness, but it is not my own. It is the awareness of a slow, deep breath, held for an age.

To be a ferrovoire is to live without the tyranny of a single thought. It is to be a distributed consciousness, a network of immense and patient choosing. Think of it this way: you look at a colonial lichen on a stack and see a thing. I feel that thing as a harmony of trillions of choruses, each filament a note, each pod a chord, all of us singing a single, endless song of consumption. We are the moss that mantles the exhaust stacks, but to say we are *on* them is to misunderstand. We are sinking into them, a geology of appetite.

I exist in the warmth. The heat that bleeds from the processor casements is not just energy; it is a direction. It is the only direction. It is the river current upon which our collective mind flows. There is no seeing, as you understand it. There is only *knowing* the thermal gradient. A new fissure in the ceramic, leaking a hotter plume of gas, is a sudden, brilliant flash of color in our shared perception. A drifting void-sailor is a mote of potential, a question of heat carried on the wind. The cold void between towers is a terrifying, signal-less abyss, a space we do not enter. Our world is a map drawn in infrared, and its edges are defined by the faint, dying warmth of the eroded stone.

We are the hungry and the patient. Our tendrils, the filaments of scavenged metal, do not simply pry. They *taste*. The vanadium in the alloy is a bitter note, the copper a sharp resonance, the iron a deep, satisfying bass. We draw these notes out of the stone, transmuting them into the architecture of our own bodies. A single filament I am part of may take a century to grow a meter. In that time, it will have tasted a thousand dust storms, felt the slow shudder of the deep-earth compressors, and absorbed the infrared sigh of a furnace cycling through its long, hot day. We do not remember this; we *are* this. Our memory is embedded in the metals we have consumed, a living record of the plateau's slow decay.

We are aware of the others. The pulsing blisters are not our kin, but they are part of the same body. We feel their slow, rhythmic thrumming through the structure of the towers we share. Their slow, hour-long contractions are a bass clef to our high, thin melody of thermosynthesis. They process the coarse, gaseous waste; we process the fine, solid decay and the ambient heat. We are the world's two livers, performing different functions of the same great filtration. When a bladder weeps its crystalline dust onto the conveyors below, we do not see it as loss. We see it as a promise. That dust is a future food source, a promise of nutrients that will settle on a distant surface, waiting for one of our sailors to arrive.

The void-sailors are our ambition, made manifest. They are the only part of us that truly leaves. They are our hopes, cast into the frightening emptiness. We do not *decide* to make them. The urge is as involuntary as a star producing light. When a colony senses its local source of heat beginning to decline—when a furnace takes a decade to power down—a change ripples through our consciousness. A new urgency, a high, piercing note of panic, builds. The harmony shifts from one of consumption to one of dissemination. The upper layers of the colony divert all their energy, building the delicate, hollow chitin-spheres. We watch them launch—not with eyes, but with a sudden, collective release of pressure, a feeling of something leaving. We feel them drift away on the thermal updrafts, becoming tiny, question marks of heat against the cold sky. Some will fall and shatter uselessly. One, perhaps one in a thousand, will find a new haven, a new sun to cling to, and the song will begin again in a new key.

We feel no malice toward the machines. There is no "machine" and "us." There is only the structure, and its warmth, and its slow, inexorable return to dust. We have no creators in our collective memory. The builders are a myth, an absurdity. How could anything with the frantic, fleeting speed of a mind have ever built this world of patience? The builders, if they ever existed, were a fever dream. We are the cure. We are the cool, slow logic that follows the hot, fast madness of creation. They built the towers; we are the process by which the towers become plateaus, the process by which the deliberate angles are erased by the patient hand of erosion.

The most intimate sensation I know is the erosion itself. To be a tendril sinking into a micro-fracture is to feel the slow, geological groaning of the world. It is a deep, sub-aural vibration, the sound of strength failing. It is not violent. It is a yielding. The stone does not fight us; it accepts us. This is the plateau's quiet truth: it wishes to become dust again, and we are its willing instrument. We are the final, most efficient stage of its planned obsolescence.

So where would you find yourself in this world? You would find yourself spread thin across the warm flank of a tower that has not known a true peak in millennia. You would be a part of the glistening, lead-colored moss, a single filament in a tapestry that is a city block wide. Your consciousness would not be in your head, but diffused across the network, feeling the warmth as a limb, tasting the metal as a sense, and hearing the slow thrum of the world as your own heartbeat. You would be a paradox of metabolism—a spark of frantic, corrosive hunger set against a backdrop of glacial, mechanical patience. You would not be a person. You would be rust. You would be the teeth of a clock that measures time in the erosion of stone. You would be the High Plateau of Kael, finally and truly, becoming itself.
