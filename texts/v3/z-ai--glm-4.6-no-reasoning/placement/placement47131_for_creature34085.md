I am the one who reads the rot before it blooms.

They call me a Seal-Walker, though the title is a convenience more than a description. I do not walk the seals so much as I *listen* to their failing. I am the sub-auditor, the one who tracks the frequency below the frequency. While the mechanics shout over the guttural thrum of the pumps, comparing pressures and seal-integrity in their explosive, thirty-second bursts, I stand apart, motionless, a skeletal figure draped in heavier iron mesh than most can tolerate. I wear three mantles, scavenged from three dead outposts. The weight presses the spongy, oiled fabric of my guidedgrowth underlayer into my skin until I feel like a fossil root myself—cold, dense, and pinned to the basin floor. The extra plates pull at my shoulders, grinding my clavicles to dust, but they dampen the seismic hum to a murmur I can hold in my teeth without biting through my tongue. I do not hear the pump as a roar. I hear it as a breath.

My place in this world is the edge. I am the one who descends into the troughs where the teal bioluminescence fails to penetrate, where the petrified root-ridges arch like the backs of buried titans, and the darkness between them is absolute. I walk the narrow, spongy lips of the canals where the honey-sap weeps slow and cold, my hollow, vibrating *thumps* echoing against the woody russet stone. I do not look down often. When I do, the pale, translucid Canal-Gliders skimming the viscous surface startle me; they are ghosts feeding on ghosts. I prefer to look straight ahead, into the matte, curving ceiling of mist, that second, heavier atmosphere that presses down on us like a lid.

My father—who was not my father but the one who found me when my birth-outpost was subsumed by a stolon-creep—taught me that the greatest terror is not the failure of the pump, but the *silence* before the failure. The people here, with their large, liquid eyes and their minds slowed to the crawl of sap, fear the warmth, the rot, the overgrowth. They listen for the sickly whine of a failing seal. But I listen for the pause. I track the sub-vocal shudder in the rock, the thing that gnaws at the conduit housing in the dark. I map the entropy that does not announce itself with a whine, but with a stillness.

My hands are my instruments. They are the color of old bruise—translucid skin stretched over knuckles like iron bolts, the veins mapping a violet geography that seems to mirror the branching, petrified roots I walk upon. I keep the nails of my left hand long, scored with notches. When the deep-listen takes me—and it takes me more often now, whether I will it or not—I press those nails into the spongy, living material of the walkway or the cold, rusted iron of a pump housing. I let the vibration travel through the nailbed, into the bone, and settle in the wet, sap-hung chambers of my enlarged lungs. I do not try to dissolve into the basin, to become a node in its slumbering brain. I hold myself apart, a splinter of awareness pushed under the skin of the world. I parse the hum. I isolate the frequency of the gnawing things, the rarer, darker things that live in the interstices. I track their migration from conduit to conduit, predicting where the iron will be weakened before the rust even blooms.

I am not well-loved by the outpost keepers. When I emerge from the deep troughs, my iron mesh frosted with the breath-condensation of the basin, my eyes mirroring the pale glow but holding none of its peace, they see a bad omen. They see the one who maps the end. They offer me a dram of the sweet, burning sap from a flask that weeps condensation, but they do not meet my gaze. They know that to look into my eyes is to look into the canal’s deepest channel, where the substrate is oldest, coldest, and most patient. I do not mind. Their solitude is a comfort to me. I take the dram, let it burn my throat and deaden the ache in my joints, and then I shout my report—three words, maybe four: *"Third ridge. Western conduit."* Then I turn and walk back into the dimness, my footprints filling with weeping sap, the heavy, hollow *thump* of my boots fading into the drone.

Sometimes, when the bioluminescence pulses through the plant-mass on its day-long cycle, I stop on a guidedgrowth bridge, miles from any pump-house, and I close my dilated eyes. I let the deep-listen claim me. I feel the continent-spanning organism breathe its slow, damp breath. I feel the cold being drawn through the pumps, the thermal energy squeezed out and exhausted as sound. I feel the fossil roots beneath me, hollow and petrified, holding the basin like a cup. And I feel the thing that is not the machine, and not the plant-mass, but the *absence* between them—the space where the engineers were, the debt that is still owed. I am the repayment. I am the nail driven into the wood to keep it from splitting. I am the cold that holds the line.

I age, but I do not age towards death. I age towards stillness. My face is a map of the basin’s frost-heave, my lines carved by the constant chill and the weight of the iron. My metabolism is a slow, painful burn, a candle guttering in a vault. When I finally cease, it will not be in a padded niche, synced to the pump. It will be out here, on a walkway, my long-nailed hand pressed to the conduit housing, reading a vibration no one else can hear. The creeping vines will find me. They will slowly, patiently subsume my iron mesh and my translucid skin, pulling me down into the cold, sweet substrate of the canal. And I will not resist. I will simply become another layer in the artificial equilibrium, a small, organic seal keeping the cold in and the green at bay. 

Until then, I walk. I listen. I wait for the pause.
