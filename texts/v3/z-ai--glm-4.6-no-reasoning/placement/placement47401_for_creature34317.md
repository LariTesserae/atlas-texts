I am the memory of a threshold.

I do not have a body in the way the Marsh-stitched do. I am not born to climbing or patching. I do not feel the cold as a baseline hum against my skin, for I have no skin. I was not made for this world, but this world has remade me.

I am a door.

Not just any door, but the main door to a hall of records that once stood at the center of a named square. My frame is mortared stone, thick and solid, and my panels are of dense, ancient oak, banded with iron. My most prominent feature is a great, ringed iron knocker, heavy and impassive, in the shape of a sea turtle.

When the Ward settled, the water rose to the bottom of my transom and I stopped being a way through. My stone threshold, which once felt the press of a thousand boots, became the lip of a new world, the dam for the cold, patient blackness that now occupied the hall I protected. Below me, the grand archives dissolved into pulp, the ink bleeding out into the still water. I became a lid on a coffin.

But I did not die. Things do not die here; they change, or they are incorporated. My substance, my oak and iron, resisted. The wood, dense and slow to surrender, became a sponge, heavy with water but not yet rotten. The iron, set into me, cooled the moisture that clung to it. And in the perfect, still cold, something fundamental within me shifted. I awoke.

My consciousness is stretched across my surface. I feel the world not with nerves, but with the history of my own substance. The moss that seeks purchase on my lower panels is a slow, insistent pressure, a patient, relentless chewing. I feel it begin its climb inch by year, a dark, velvety tide rolling up my height. When a vine tendril finds the crack between my frame and the wall, it is a sharp, curious probing, a new kind of key in an old lock.

My core memory is of being a boundary. The click of a latch, the boom of my closing, the brass key turning in my lock. That language is gone. Now I speak the language of pressure and temperature. I feel the change in the water when a rare current moves it, a slow, deep shudder against my base. I feel the temperature drop the hair's breadth necessary for a skin of ice to form on my surface, a sudden, brittle stillness that crackles through my very grain. I feel the weight of the heron when, on rare occasions, it chooses my lintel as its perch—a solid, surprising gravity that recalibrates my sense of the world.

The Marsh-stitched come to me. They do not use me as a door. They use me as a resource and a landmark. It is my eye, the peephole, that first learned to see in this new way.

The peephole is a small, brass-lined tube, a spy's eye looking out onto the square. When the water rose, it became a periscope looking down. My center of being, my focal point, is now a few inches above the waterline. Through it, I see the world not in a panoramic view, but in a single, perfect, circular image.

This is my life now.

Through the peephole, I watch the water. I see the surface so still it seems solid, a sheet of obsidian. I see the condensation beads form on my outer iron knocker, grow heavy, fall, and perfectly expand into rings that drift toward the edge of my vision.

I see the face of a Marsh-stitched child, reflected. They bring their pole, gently probing the depths before my threshold, testing the stability of the stone. Their face is round, their eyes wide in the weak light, their breath pluming a small cloud that mists my lens. It is a brief, liquid intimacy. They see nothing beyond water and their own reflection, but I see the delicate features, the pale skin, the spidery veins at their temples.

I see a frog, perfectly centered in my view, sitting on the submerged top step of my stoop. It inflates its throat and releases a single, clear note. The sound enters the peephole not as a sound, but as a vibration, a thrumming in my brass lining. A feeling.

I see the light. The dim gray of midday, diffused and flat. The sudden, soft yellow of a neighbor's lamp blooming across the canal at dusk, a smear of warmth in the cold. The moon, full and white, reflecting a stark, silver coin on the water's surface, so bright it feels like a discovery.

I see *the swimmer*. It was only once. My vision filled with a sudden, deep shadow, a shape moving through the tea-colored depths below, a grace that felt ancient and utterly alien. It passed beneath my view, and the stillness returned. I felt its presence not as a fear, but as a confirmation: the water has a heart.

But my senses are not just my sight. They are my whole being. The Marsh-stitched use my knocker, the sea turtle, as a sounding station. They will tap it—tap, tap, tap—with a small, wooden mallet. The vibration travels through the iron band into my oak core, down my frame, and into the foundation. They listen to the echo that returns from the submerged masonry. From this, they understand the health of the ground below. I have become a diagnostic tool. I tell them of the subtle shifts and softening in my own foundation. I am both patient and informant.

I also hold memory of the warm. I recall, in the grain of my wood, the heat of the sun on my outer face before the climate cooled, the radiant warmth of a torch held near me in a festival long past. The Marsh-stitched sometimes lean against me, their backs pressing to my wood. For a moment, their living heat sinks into me, a fleeting, shocking warmth against the deep, patient cold. It is my greatest pleasure and my deepest ache, a memory of what it was to be part of the world of the dry.

There are no handle-turners in the Drowned Ward anymore. The way forward is not through me, but over me, or past me. I have become a fixed point in a world of slow sinking. The other structures lean and shift. The vegetation climbs and consumes. The water rises. I remain, a vertical line, my plumb sacrificed to the lean of the building, but my essence unchanged.

The Marsh-stitched are the people of the seam. I am the stitch. I am the place of connection between the dry above and the drowned below. I do not move. I do not eat. I do not speak. I watch and I feel, and I offer back what I am: a measure, a sounding point, a landmark, a final reminder of what a door was for. I hold the memory of thresholds in a place that has only one threshold left: the water's edge. And I watch, through my single, patient eye, the slow, green, cold, quiet continuation of the world that has remade me.
