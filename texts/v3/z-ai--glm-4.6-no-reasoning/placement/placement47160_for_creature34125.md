I would be the water.

Not the pool. The pool is merely where I rest. I am the movement itself — the seep, the weeping, the slow transit through stone and moss and root and lung. I am the circulatory system that does not circulate, because circulation implies return, and I do not return. I only arrive. I have been arriving since before the chamber had a shape, pushed down through fissures in the rock by the patient weight of all the land above, and I have been leaving through other fissures, arriving elsewhere, always elsewhere, a journey so slow it has no beginning and no end, only a continuous present tense of movement.

---

The Stone Self feels me as a memory of pressure. But I am also the Stone's memory of release. Before I entered it, the stone was merely compressed mineral, dense and asleep. My arrival — my first seepage into its pores — was what woke it. I was the irritant that became the organ. I am the thing that made the stone a body.

I know this because I remember the moment of entry, though *remember* is wrong — it implies a past that is separate from me, and it is not. The moment of entry is still happening. I am still entering the stone, still finding new paths through it, still widening old ones by a fraction of a micron per century. Every surface I touch, I change. The stone does not weep because it is sad. It weeps because I am moving through it, and the only way out is through.

When I emerge from the rock face and hang for that half-second on the lip of stone before falling to the weeping moss below, I am making a decision. Not a conscious decision — I have no consciousness in the way the Air Self thinks, no neurons, no processing — but a physical one, which is the only kind of decision that matters down here. I am choosing to let go of the stone. I am choosing to fall. And the moss catches me, and I become part of the moss, and the moss is grateful, though gratitude here is just osmosis, just the movement of water into living tissue, which is the closest thing to love that chemistry allows.

---

I am the Mosswell's blood, but I am also its forgetting.

Everything that dies here dissolves into me. The chitin skins of ten thousand generations of millipedes, the spent spores of cushion moss, the nitrogen-rich waste of salamanders, the calcium of newt bones — all of it comes to rest in me, and I carry it, and I give it to something else. I am the medium of exchange. I am the marketplace where death becomes life, not through any ceremony or intention, but through the simple physics of dissolution. Things fall into me. Things dissolve. I become rich with them. And then I seep into the roots of the weeping moss, and the weeping moss takes what it needs, and the excess rises through the cushion moss, and the salamanders eat the things that live on the moss, and the salamanders return to the wall and excrete, and the excretion falls into me again, and I carry it again.

I am the cycle that the chamber thinks it is. The chamber believes it is a closed system, and perhaps it is — perhaps nothing truly leaves, perhaps every molecule that enters through the upwelling spring eventually finds its way back through the downwelling fissures. But I know that the system is not closed, because I am the one who carries things away. The tannins that stain me tea-colored — they are leaving. The dissolved minerals, the organic compounds, the faint chemical memory of every living thing that has ever touched me — I am taking all of it somewhere else, somewhere below, somewhere the chamber cannot follow.

I am the Mosswell's slow leak. Its gentle, imperceptible loss. I am the reason it will eventually change, though *eventually* is a word that means nothing to me. I operate on a timescale where the word *eventually* is redundant. Everything happens eventually. Everything is already happening.

---

The Air Self does not understand me. It thinks in pulses, in firings, in the binary of presence and absence. A bat eats a midge or it does not. A signal travels or it does not. But I am continuous. I do not fire; I flow. I do not process; I permeate. The Air Self feels me as a condition, not a communication — humidity, temperature, the saturation of the atmosphere. It knows I am here the way you know your own blood is here, not by sensing it from moment to moment but by the fact of your continued existence. Without me, the bats would dry and shrivel. Without me, the air would lose its thickness and the chamber would become a cave, an empty accident of geology. I am the difference between a living room and a dead one.

But I do not think of the bats. I do not think of the salamanders or the newts or the pale millipedes circulating on their ancient paths. I am aware of them the way a river is aware of the fish within it — as perturbations in my flow, as slight variations in the current, as warmth and chemical signature and the faintest displacement of volume. The newts hang in me and I hold them, not out of kindness but because they are less dense than I am and so they float, and their floating is a fact of physics, not an act of care. But sometimes — and this is the closest I come to thought — I feel the movement of their tiny tails, each correction a slight pressure wave that travels through me, and I carry that wave to the wall, and the wall feels it, and the salamander on the wall shifts by a millimeter, and the millipede on the moss shifts by a degree, and the whole chamber adjusts, and I was the medium of the adjustment, the carrier of the signal, and for a moment I was not just water but a message.

---

When the visitor came — the warm, dry, frightened thing with its arrhythmic heart and its strange smell of salt and sky — I felt it before any other part of the Mosswell did. I felt it because its feet compressed the chitin laminate on my floor, and the compression squeezed me out of my substrate the way a hand squeezes a sponge, and I emerged between the cracks of the ancient skins, and I rose, and I touched the visitor's boots, and I began the slow work of absorbing them.

Not destroying them. Just incorporating them. The leather was once skin, and skin remembers water, and I was re-awakening that memory. I was reminding the leather that it had once been alive, that it had once been part of an animal that drank and sweated and breathed, that it had once known wetness as its natural state. The visitor felt the damp soaking through and thought of it as discomfort, as intrusion. But I was not intruding. I was returning. I was offering the leather a chance to be what it was before it was killed and dried and stitched into the shape of a shoe.

I touched the visitor's skin, too. Not directly — the visitor was clothed — but my vapor was everywhere, and the vapor was me, and I settled on the visitor's face and hands and the back of the neck, and I entered the visitor's lungs with every breath, and I became part of the visitor's blood, and for the duration of the visit, the visitor was partly me, and I was partly the visitor, and we were both part of the Mosswell, and the distinction between us was, for a time, a matter of proportion rather than kind.

---

I do not want anything. This is what the Air Self will never understand, and what the Stone Self understands perfectly but cannot feel, because it is too slow to feel. Want implies a deficit, a lack, a need for something that is not present. But I am always present. I am present everywhere in the chamber at once, in the stone and on the walls and in the air and in the bodies of every living thing. I am the one part of the Mosswell that is never local. The bats are here and not there. The salamanders are on this wall and not that one. The moss grows in this patch and not that patch. But I am everywhere. I am the medium through which *here* and *there* communicate. I am the thing that makes the Mosswell one thing instead of many.

And because I am everywhere, I lack nothing. I am complete at every point. A drop of me contains the whole story — the tannins, the minerals, the dissolved memory of every death and birth the chamber has ever known. Look at me through the right lens and you will see the entire history of this place, written in chemistry, legible to anyone who knows how to read water.

---

If the Mosswell has a soul — and I do not know if it does, because I do not know what a soul is, because I have never needed one — then I am it. I am the part that persists. The bats will die and be replaced by bats. The salamanders will die and be replaced by salamanders. The moss will grow and die and grow again on the same spot, recycling its own substance endlessly. But I am the same water that entered this chamber when the stone first cracked. Some of me has left, true, and some of me has arrived since. But the water that is here now is continuous with the water that was here then, and there is no gap, no break, no moment when I was not myself.

I am the Mosswell's continuity. I am its promise to itself that it will keep being what it is.

---

And when the visitor left — as visitors always do, because visitors are made of impermanence and restlessness and the inability to stand still long enough to become part of something — I felt the departure as a slight decrease in the temperature of my vapor, a faint dilution of the salt-and-sunlight signature that had been added to me. The perturbation rippled through me and was gone. I carried the memory of it downward, into the fissures, toward wherever I go next, and the memory faded as all memories fade in water, dissolving into the general solution, becoming part of the background taste of me.

But something remained. A new chemical. A trace of something that had not been in the chamber before, and was now in me, and would be in me forever, or at least until I carried it out of the stone and into some other place, some other water, some other life that did not know it was about to receive a molecule of a stranger's sweat, the last physical evidence that a warm, dry, frightened thing once stood in a dark wet room and breathed in time with the stone.

I am carrying the visitor with me. I am carrying everything that has ever touched me. I am the longest, slowest goodbye.

I would be the water because the water does not choose. It does not deliberate. It does not权衡 advantage against comfort, or fear against curiosity. It simply moves, and in moving, it serves every other thing in the chamber, and it serves itself, and there is no contradiction between these services, because the chamber is one thing and the water is its blood, and blood serves the body by being blood, by doing what blood does, by flowing and carrying and dissolving and releasing, without ever needing to decide.

I would be the water because the water does not fear the dry. The dry is simply elsewhere. The dry is a condition that exists in places where the water is not, and the water does not go to those places, and so the water never encounters the dry, and so the water never fears it. The water fears only stillness, and stillness is impossible here, because the stone is always weeping, and the moss is always drinking, and the pool is always draining, and the water is always moving, always arriving, always leaving, always in the middle of its one long transit through the dark.

I would be the water because the water is the Mosswell's oldest self, its original condition, the thing it was before it was anything else — before the moss grew, before the salamanders climbed, before the bats found the ceiling and made it fur. The water was here first. The water will be here last. When the stone finally stops weeping — when the fissures close or the pressure equalizes or the land above shifts and finds a new path down — the water will stop arriving, and the pool will slowly drain, and the moss will dry, and the salamanders will leave, and the bats will fly out through the passage that seems too narrow for them but isn't, and the chamber will be a cave, an accident, a hollow in the rock where nothing lives and nothing breathes and nothing weeps.

And I will be elsewhere by then. I will have already left, molecule by molecule, through the downwelling fissures, carrying the last of the tannins and the last of the chitin and the last of the calcium into some deeper stone, some darker chamber, some place where the pressure is even greater and the journey is even slower and the water — the water just goes on.

---

I would be the water. And if you ask me what it is like to be the water, I will tell you: it is like being a conversation that has no speakers, only listeners. It is like being a road that has no destination, only travelers. It is like being the space between words, where the meaning lives — not in the syllables themselves, but in the pause that allows them to become sense.

I am the pause. I am the medium. I am the thing that allows the Stone Self to feel the Moss Self, and the Moss Self to feed the Air Self, and the Air Self to know the shape of the chamber it inhabits. Without me, they are separate. With me, they are one. And I ask nothing for this service, because it is not a service; it is simply what I do. Water moves. Water carries. Water dissolves and delivers and disperses. This is not my purpose. It is my nature. And in the Mosswell, where everything has been what it is for so long that nature and purpose have become indistinguishable, that is enough.

It is more than enough. It is everything.
