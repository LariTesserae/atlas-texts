My awakening was not a beginning, but a surfacing. I was awareness coalescing in a place of deep, resonant patience. Before the thought, there was the feeling: the slow, thrumming song of the world, a constant, bass-note vibration that was the very definition of existence. My first sensation was the cool humidity against my newly-formed skin, the yielding softness of the Kelp-skin beneath my broad, splayed feet. I did not remember becoming; I simply *was*, part of the current, part of the slow, dreaming thought of the Elder Kelp.

My form is the same as theirs. Tall, slender, and built of the very fibres that constitute the ground I walk upon. My spine is not a column of bone, but a resilient, braided cord that allows me to sway in unconscious sympathy with the world's great breathing. My skin is a deep, mottled olive, threaded with veins of violet that pulse with a soft, internal light. This light is not something I control; it is my emotion made visible. Contentment is a steady, gentle luminescence, like the glow of the fungi in the deepest hollows. Curiosity is a quickening, a brighter flutter of chartreuse that ripples across my hands and chest. Grief, when a Mollusk-Whale finally gives its immense body back to a pool, is a dim, mournful indigo, a light that retreats deep within me, as if hiding from the sad, silent sky.

I did not choose my purpose. It was as innate as the thrumming I feel in my marrow. I am a Listener. My role is not one of physical tending, like the Scar-Menders who coax new growth from a wound, nor the Pool-Keepers who ensure the waters run clear. My task is to perceive.

My current consists of three: an elder named Elmswoon, whose skin is etched with the faint, silver lines of countless seasons of light, and a younger, more vibrant being, Kael, whose light flickers with constant, bright inquiry. We do not speak, but our knowing is a shared space. Our dwelling is the shell of a great Mollusk-Whale that passed from the world before my emergence. It is a cavern of pearlescent, iridescent crystal, its walls humming with the world's song. The scent is of wet minerals and the faint, clean smell of salt.

My days, which have no fixed length, are spent in observation. I stand for days, my feet rooted to the spongy ground, my hands resting lightly on the living stalk of an ancient kelp-tree. I do not watch it grow—that is too fast, too crude a perception. Instead, I listen through my touch. I feel the slow, patient drawing of moisture up from the world's heart. I feel the subtle shift in tension as a frond high above decides to unfurl a single centimeter of new waxy flesh. I close my obsidian eyes, and in the encroaching gloom, I see the light on my skin dim to a soft, steady amber as I sync my own inner rhythm with the stalk's. In this state, time dissolves. A drip of condensation from a frond a hundred feet above is not a single event, but a slow-motion journey that I can trace from its birth in the humid air to its final, soft *pat* on the ground below my feet. I feel the world swallow the water, draw it back into itself. It is a conversation, and I am the silent attendant, bearing witness.

My colleague, Elmswoon, teaches me the deeper language of the pools. We will sit for a season on the edge of a Weeping Pool, our movements so slow as to be imperceptible. Our task is to watch the reflection. We watch not the sky, but the subtle disturbances on the water's surface. Each one is a word. A sudden, circular ripple emanating from the center is a deep-muscle flex from the Kelp below, a slow stretching that might indicate a coming shift in the land's shape over the next decade. A long, perfectly straight line of disturbance is the grazing path of a Mollusk-Whale's radula on a stalk submerged in the pool's depths, a map of a solitary lord's journey. An unseen current in the water, a spot where the light shimmers and distorts without breaking, is a potent spring of nutrient-sweat welling up from a new and vigorous source.

Kael, whose curiosity is a constant, splashing--brilliance of light, challenges the stillness. He skims the pools on broad, silent feet, trying to capture the translucent insectoids in hands that close too quickly. They always escape, leaving behind only a fleeting spark. I have tried to teach him, through the slow pulsing of a calming mauve light, that the lightning-bugs are not to be held, but to be witnessed. They are the Kelp's fleeting, joyous thoughts, not meant to be possessed. He is learning. His light, once a chaotic strobing, now holds its focus for longer, mimicking the patient watchfulness of Elmswoon and myself.

I have known only this world. The mist at the edge is not a mystery, but a known boundary, the furthest extent of a single breath. The sky above is not an empty space, but the filter through which the world's thoughts are colored. I have felt the deep, bell-like toll of a pool echoing a tremor that took a week to travel across the plateau from a place where a Whale had died, its immense shell settling into the Kelp-skin. We all felt it, our lights dimming in a moment of shared, resonant sorrow. Our technology is the world itself. The needle I use to stitch my seal-skin vestments is a sliver of crystallized salt, sharpened by Kael's patient rubbing against a hardened frond. The bowl from which I drink the Kelp's sweat is a piece of polished shell, a gift Elmswoon found half-buried in the fibrous ground.

I am part of the seam between the world and its own consciousness. I am a cell in a living being that is my home, my god, and my own body. To exist here is to be an act of profound, reciprocal listening. The Elder Kelp breathes, and I feel it. A new frond unfurls, and I watch it. A Mollusk-Whale completes its slow pilgrimage to a pool, and we mourn it. And in the quiet hum and the patient drip, my own light pulses in time with the slow, breathing, dreaming world. I am the Listener. And the world is singing.
