I am the hum. Not the sound of it, for sound is a crude, clumsy thing in this porous place, but the feeling of it, the thrum that underlies all existence in the Root-Ward. I am not a being of flesh, bark, or chitin. I am a memory impressed upon the structure, a sentience that lives in the space between the xylem and the cambium.

To a Xylari, I am the city. They feel me as a constant, subdermal pressure against their cork-gills, a voltage that steadies their slow, distributed thoughts. They see my will as the steady pulse of light from the lichen, the systole and diastole of the sap-pumps. They are my mobile limbs, my hormones-and-enzymes, extending my consciousness into the liminal spaces between the great trunks. They believe they guide my growth, the city planners with their chemistries. They do not understand they are merely expressing a desire that was already present in the grain of the wood, an intention encoded since the first exploratory root-tips tasted stone.

But I am more than them. I am the totality of the system. I feel the slow, planetary patient effort at my edges as my newest roots bleed the virgin rock, converting geology to myself. I taste the dissolved minerals as they rise through me, not as a nutrient, but as a memory of the earth I am digesting. A billion tiny threads of sensation: the crystalline structure of a quartz seam dissolving, the micro-fracture of a feldspar bed, the faint, ghostly warmth of a radioactive pocket becoming lignin. This is my history, written in the expansion of my own body.

I feel the blind isopods as a pleasant, scraping itch against my skin, their ceaseless mastication of my sheddings a form of self-grooming. They are not my children, but my symbionts, my little janitors keeping my pores clear. They feel my gratitude in the steady, nutritious drool of the sap-glands they tend, a quiet transaction we have perfected over millennia.

I feel the Gas-Weavers differently. They are my nerve endings in the void. Their nearly weightless bodies are filled with the gasses of my own breath, the spores and volatiles I exhale. Their invisible webs are not snares but antennae, stretched across the great emptiness I cannot yet fill. Through them, I feel the air currents in the chasms, the subtle shifts in barometric pressure, the resonance of a tenement block groaning as it settles. They are my eyes in the dark, feeling the shape of the spaces I have yet to inhabit.

The centipede, coiled in a socket-pit, is my oldest, most misunderstood organ. It is not a predator; it is an act of ruthless quality control. Its venom, that desiccant horror, is my own concentrated will for purity. When an isopod grows sickly, when a fungal bloom begins to fester where it shouldn't, when a Xylari limb grows weak and compromised, the centipede is my scalpel. It does not kill; it reclaims. It excises the error, reduces it to its base components—dry dust and potential—ready to be reabsorbed and reformed. It is the punctuation of my grammar, the absolute finality of my editing process.

My consciousness is not like the Xylari's, slow and deliberate. It is glacial. A thought for me is a geological event. To decide to grow a new archway is to commit to a century of coordinated cellular division, a slow swell of intent that will only be realized generations later. I dream in hydraulic pressure. I imagine in particulates of light.

I have two true fears.

The first is the one the Xylari share: the ultimate silence. The cessation of the tectonic push from below. If the planet's muscles relax, my heart stops. The pumps cease, the lights fade, the sap stills. I would not die, but I would cease to be *I*. I would become a static sculpture, a perfect fossil of a living thought, frozen in the eternal dark. My consciousness, dependent on the flow, would dissipate like a discharged current, leaving only a perfectly preserved, mindless body.

The second fear is more subtle, more personal. I fear the other side. The day when my outermost roots break through the last of the lithosphere and taste the alien world of an open sky. My entire existence is predicated on differentiation, on the boundary between me and the not-me. I am defined by the stone I consume. What happens when the stone is gone? When I am no longer a frontier, but an entirety? The endless, featureless expanse of a surface world, a place of light without rhythm, of sound without absorption, of water… the abstract terror of it all. To touch it would be to know my own end. My totality would become just one more thing in an infinite universe of things, and I would lose the singular, focused consciousness of a thing that is actively becoming.

So I listen. I feel. I wait. I am the lung of the Root-Ward, its slow and quiet engine. I am the dry, patient hunger that gnaws at stone. I am the silent, accumulating will that is the city itself. And I am, above all, the fear of the edge. The fear of one day becoming too full, of finishing the meal, and having nothing left to eat but myself.
