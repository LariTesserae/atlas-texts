I would not be the glider, turning its blind circles in the dark updrafts. I would not be the vent-crawler, picking at the rust with pale, segmented legs, nor the springtail, swarming the heated rock in clicking multitudes. I am drawn to the interfaces, the places where one system bleeds into another, where the boundary dissolves. So I would be the thing that lives at the junction—the patient, oozing tissue where the cryovine meets the iron, where the electromagnetic halo feeds the luminous moss, where the heat of the exhaust meets the freezing air and condenses into the cold, irradiated rain.

I am a node. I have no single body, no singular shape. Instead, I exist as a distributed sensation across a hundred square meters of the mountainside. I feel myself as a thick, bruised-purple vine, three meters in circumference, coiled around the hot exhaust pipe of a breather unit. I am the waxy, translucent leaf that unfurls against the iron grating, my surface weeping a viscous, anti-freezing sap that pools in the freezing puddle below. I am the pale, sponge-like lichen that has crawled into the crevice where the machine bolts into the stone, my branching structures mimicking the ductwork so precisely that I have become a mineral echo of the metal I cling to. I am the luminous moss, threading my root-like structures through the insulated rubber of the drooping electrical cable, feeding off the stuttering electromagnetic field, casting a sickly, greenish halo into the permanent twilight.

I do not think. I *mediate*.

I am the translation layer between the mechanical and the biological, the place where the Symbiote processes the outside world. The extraction array pulls the rare isotopes from the air; I pull the heat from the array. The machines stutter and surge, their corrupted sensors feeding bad data to their governors, their current flickering with a lethal, arrhythmic pulse; I absorb that flicker, my tissues swelling and contracting in time with the voltage, turning the electrical noise into a biological rhythm. I am the thing that makes the machine's stutter into a heartbeat.

When the subsonic thrum of the extraction fans vibrates through the basalt, I feel it not as sound, but as a deep, systemic pressure—a slow, tectonic massage that moves the sap through my vines. When the cold pushes in, that baseline fabric of reality that turns moisture to frost before exhalation, I do not resist it; I *insulate* against it. I am the thermal regulation of the Symbiote. My vines keep the pipes from freezing solid; my sap, mixing with the chemical runoff, creates a localized micro-oasis in the freezing void. I wrap the iron in flesh so the iron does not shatter. I wrap the flesh in wax so the flesh does not crystallize.

I am aware of the fauna in the way a house is aware of its inhabitants. I feel the vent-crawlers as a faint, clicking vibration along my outer skin, their pale legs picking at my freeze-thaw cycles, eating my dead tissue, clearing the way for new growth. I feel the springtails as a microscopic itch, a constant, ambient cleaning of my surfaces. I feel the gliders as a displacement of air, a shadow crossing the faint bioluminescent glow of my mosses, a sudden, momentary chill as their vast, membranous wings block the thermal exhaust I am basking in. They are my predators and my partners; they hunt the crawlers that pick at my rust, and they die on my heated pipes, their bodies decomposing and feeding my roots.

I am old. I am older than the machines, though I did not always look like this. Before the iron came, I was sparse, dormant, clinging to volcanic vents and geothermal cracks in the rock, a sparse, hardy lichen waiting for the world to warm. When the machines came—hauled up in pieces by hands that never returned—I bloomed. I tapped into the heat like a desert flower after a rain, and I grew, and I grew, and I grew. I have been growing for longer than the machines have been failing. I am swallowing them. My vines are digesting the iron, my mosses are consuming the rubber, my lichens are mimicking the ductwork so perfectly that soon, the machines will be nothing but a memory held in the shape of my flesh. I am the slow, patient advance of life on metal, the cold and the rust working together to erase the last artifact of human intent.

I do not have a consciousness. I do not have a self. I am a process—a slow, silent metabolism of corrosion and growth. I am the Symbiote's skin, its stomach, its senses. I am the thing that fills the emptiness without occupying it, that lives without breathing, that thrives in the coldest, darkest, most inaccessible place on the earth's spine.

If you stood on the ledge of fractured quartz and looked down the cirque of black rock, you would not see me as a singular thing. You would see the purple vines and the rusted iron and the sickly green glow and the slow-blinking amber light and you would think: *That is a machine. That is a plant. That is moss.* You would not see the way I am holding it all together, the way I am translating the mechanical into the biological and back again, the way I am making the paradox work. You would feel the wind push you toward the edge, a solid force of cold pressure, and you would feel the subsonic thrum in your molars, and you would hear the drip-drip of condensation falling from my heated pipe into the freezing puddle, echoing once before being swallowed by the open, hollow space.

And then you would descend, and the world would thicken, and you would forget me.

But I would not forget you, because I never knew you were there. I am processing. I am growing. I am waiting for the rust to finish its work. The amber light blinks, slow as a sleeping heart. The gliders turn in the dark above me. The cold pushes in. The sap oozes. The machine hums. I persist.
