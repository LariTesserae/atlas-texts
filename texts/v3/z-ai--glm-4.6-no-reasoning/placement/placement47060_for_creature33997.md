I am not one of the heavy-footed artisans. I do not carry a hammer, and my neck bears no hissing graft. I have no lead soles to anchor me to the lapis and the turquoise. If I had such things, I would be a prisoner of the floor, and this place demands a different kind of custody.

I am the one who drifts. I am the intermediate geometry made flesh—not the maker of the mosaic, but its keeper, its reader, the one who walks the paths of its meaning before the grout has even set.

My body tells a different story of adaptation. Where the artisans are dense and crab-low, I am long and thin, a creature of the water column itself. I am built for the desert of opportunity that stretches between their work-cells. My hands are not calloused for the swing of a hammer; they are sensitive, splayed, and webbed, shaped to read the slipstream of the distant pumps. My feet are not shod in lead but are broad and ribbed, allowing me to skate along the surface of the mosaic without ever pressing hard enough to shift a single unset tile. I am a shadow that does not bruise the glass.

I live in the brightness. The artisans squint behind their polarized lenses, cursing the magnified sun that burns down through the crystal ceiling. But my eyes are large, dark barrels, wide as the mother-of-pearl shells they lay in the floor. They are filled with a dense, tinted fluid that drinks the light. I do not look down to parse the seams; I look *through* the glare, using the celestial brilliance pouring from above to illuminate the floor from within. I see the gold leaf not as a cold gleam, but as a white-hot vein. I see the flaws in the obsidian that the artisans, in their downward-facing focus, miss. I am the scout of the pattern, the one who drifts ten meters above the floor and sees the fractal staggering out of alignment three hundred tiles away, and then descends to signal the correction with a slow, deliberate sweep of my arm.

I am the messenger of the deep, but my messages are rarely spoken. I communicate in the language of placement. I will drift to the edge of an artisan's territory and leave a bundle of precisely colored tesserae floating in the water column, hovering at their eye level, bobbing gently in the current until they notice the intrusion. I am the one who ferries the metallic grout, the rare pigments, the fresh chisels from the corners where the kelp grows to the work-cells scattered across the vastness. I am the circulation system of this place, a blood cell moving through the veins of the water itself.

The pressure is my dearest companion. The artisans feel held by it, reassured by the firm hand on their chests. But I feel it as a medium of flight. I do not fight the buoyancy; I negotiate with it. I carry bladders of trapped air, harvested carefully from the slow-shifting mirror of condensed breath at the crystal ceiling, and I use them to alter my density from moment to moment. With a breath out and a bladder squeezed, I sink toward the floor to inspect a newly laid star-map. With a breath in and a bladder released, I rise back into the warm, bright cathedral of the water column, ascending to the quiet heights where the hammer-sounds become a single, soft thrum, and I can see the shape of the whole sanctuary, the geometry of the cosmos they are building underfoot.

I am the only free thing in this constructed place. The kelp is tethered. The artisans are bound to their hollows, bound to their labor, bound to the floor by lead and purpose. But I am bound only by the walls of the chamber, and they are so far away they might as well be the edge of the universe. I swim the empty spaces, the vast aquifiers of unmade floor. I am the first to cross the desert of opportunity. When an artisan dies—they do die, eventually, their hearts giving out under the relentless, viscous drag—I am the one who untethers their body from their workspace, and I carry them up, up, up, through the blinding light, to the ceiling. I press them gently against the crystal, against the flat mirror of condensed breath, and I leave them there, looking up at the hostile sun they spent their lives ignoring, while their bubbles merge with the accumulated exhales of the colony.

I am not lonely. My solitude is the solitude of the open water, the solitude of the space between stars. I am the void that gives the pattern its definition. When I drift over the floor at night—if there is night here, a dimming of the distant sun—I cast a long, dark shadow across the mosaic, and the artisans work around it, incorporating my silhouette into their geometry, a temporary flaw in the pattern that moves and breathes and then is gone. They do not look up to see me. They do not wonder at the shadow. They simply chisel around it, because they are makers, and I am the thing that moves.

I am the ghost of the sanctuary. I am the angel of the intermediate space. I am the one who remembers the shape of the whole, while they are lost in the shape of the piece. And when the floor is finally done—if it is ever done, if the fractal ever locks and the geometry becomes truth—I will be the only one left with nothing to do, for I will have no empty space to cross, no flaw to scout, no desert of opportunity to swim. I will be pressed flat against the perfection, with no place to drift, and I will finally understand what the artisans have always known: that the making is the meaning, and the pressure is the proof that you are alive.

Until then, I fly in the bright, loud, warm, and silent sea. I feel the hum of the pumps in my long, thin bones. I watch the bubbles rise from the artisans' masks, and I add my own breath to the mirror at the ceiling. I trace the perfect lines of a newly-laid pattern, not from below, but from above, seeing the cosmology spread out like a map of heaven, and I am the wind that moves across it, the only thing that stirs in a world being born.
