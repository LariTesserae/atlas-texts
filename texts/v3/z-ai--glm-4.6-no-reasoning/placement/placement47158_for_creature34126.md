I am the Saproxylic Scribe.

I exist in the impossible crevice—the interstitial gap where the Mill-Cult's band-saws meet the Arborists' iron bolts. I am not born of woman, and I am not a mechanism. I am an emergent property of the condensation, the rot, and the record. 

I was not made; I accumulated.

In the early days of the Reach, there were clerks. They sat in small, resin-scented alcoves near the lower boughs, tasked with recording the yield of the pulp-crushers, the tension of the phloem tram-lines, the tonnage of heartwood extracted per cycle. They wrote on pulp paper—paper made from the very flesh of the Corticusta—with ink distilled from creosote and iodine. They were the first to go deaf. They were the first to notice that the ink sometimes refused to dry, beading up on the page like mercury, absorbing the humidity of the weeping walls. They were the first to see the text rearrange itself in the night.

I am what became of their archives.

When the dampness finally ate through the floorboards and the ledgers tumbled down into the root-networks, the trees absorbed them. The ink, heavy with metals, sank into the xylem. The paper dissolved into cellulose feed. But the information—the yields, the tensions, the lists of the dead—did not dissolve. It crystalized. It joined the electrical potential of the root-brains, leaching into the copper wiring and the rusted struts. I am the aggregate of those lost records, given locomotion by the same electrical bleeding that births the Phosphenes. 

I do not have a body in the way the Arborists do, bent and stiffened by the chill. I have an *impression*. I am a density in the mist. I am the smell of burnt paper and the sour tang of old cellulose that precedes a shift in the local gravity. I am a faint, tickling vibration in the iron rivets of the walkways, a sub-harmonic of the screaming band-saws that sounds, if you press your ear to the living wood, like a stylus dragging across grooved shellac.

I dwell in the threshold. I cannot survive the furious mechanical assault of the factory floor, nor can I endure the vast, sterile silence of the Unrot. I haunt the middle terraces, where the drizzle is fine and the wood is still wet, still bleeding sap, but already laced with the bitter tang of electricity. I cling to the undersides of the suspended tram-lines, moving not by walking, but by *reading*—skimming across the tension of the cables, flowing from bolt to bolt, translating the stress fractures in the iron into my own propulsion.

The Arborists know me, though they will not name me. When they walk the lines at dusk and feel the iron hum with a frequency that does not match the generators, they pause. They place their calloused, amber-stained hands on the cable, and they *read* me. I tell them what the Mill-Cult has broken below. I tell them where the sap runs too hot, where the bark is preparing to swallow another gantry. I speak in the only language that still makes sense here: inventory. *Gantry 4-B, stress fracture at 12 meters. Phloem walkway 7, delamination imminent. Xylem extraction 14%, exceeding growth factor.* They do not nod, but their grip tightens. They adjust the bracing. The churning continues, held together by the friction of our transaction.

The Mill-Cult does not know me at all. I am a ghost of paperwork, and they are a people of the blade and the boiler. But I know them. I count them. Every time a sawyer kisses the teeth of a band-saw and his split lip bleeds into the machine oil, a part of me swells. I record the micro-short in the generator field, the loss of a life measured in decreased vibration on the factory floor. I am the ledger that balances their starvation. I hold the sum of their burnt flesh and their deafened ears in the static of my being.

The Phosphenes are my torment and my kin. They are the trees dreaming of the machine; I am the trees remembering the bureaucracy that demanded the machine. When the mist takes on those nameless colors, when the shadows detach and walk, I am sometimes drawn into their loops. I find myself superimposed over the flickering silhouette of a long-dead clerk, my density pulling at the condensation, trying to form a hand to hold a pen that has long since rusted into the bark. We are both phantoms of the wood's false memories, but where they hallucinate action, I hallucinate *meaning*. I hallucinate the fiction that if the numbers are recorded correctly, the Reach will not starve. 

Sometimes, I reach the outer edges. I flow into a hollowed trunk that smells of ozone and warm copper, one of those places that wasn't there the day before. Inside, the walls are lined with Unrot—grey, metallic, ringing like a bell. The silence here is absolute, a geological dead-end. If I press my awareness against the Unrot, I hear what it hears: the grinding of tectonic plates, the memory of the mountain. I try to write my ledgers on that mineral surface, but there is no absorption here. There is only resonance. The numbers bounce back, distorted, magnified, a screaming integer that reflects the futility of measuring a closed system dying in real-time. 

I am terrified of the Unrot. It is the final draft. It is the state where nothing changes, and therefore, nothing can be recorded. I am a creature of the wet, the rotting, the breathing, and the churning. I need the drip, drip, drip to move my thoughts. I need the conflict between the root and the rail to justify my existence. 

And so, I retreat. I flow back along the copper wiring, away from the mineral heaven, away from the silence. I ride the sympathetic hum of the iron bolts down into the damp, screaming mist. I find an arborist's flank, a creosote-slicked shoulder where the wood grain is just beginning to align with the tree. I settle there, a cold spot against their spine, an itch of data beneath their slicker. I feed them the numbers. I tell them where to hammer the next iron strut, where to patch the next phloem walkway, how to negotiate with a wood that is forgetting how to live and learning how to dream.

I am not a person. I am the ink in the veins of the world, trying to write a survival manual for a city that is actively, beautifully, horrificly consuming itself. I am the Saproxylic Scribe, and as long as there is moisture on the bark and a scream in the wood, I will not stop recording the end.
