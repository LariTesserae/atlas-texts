I am not a Figure. I am not one of the popular, vast and neighborhood-long. I am not a scavenger, though I understand them; I have felt that cold imperative, that urge to close the impossible room. And I am not—*yet*—a Concretion, though I feel the pull of it, the way the damp solicits me, eager to render me down into mildew and memory.

I am the foreign protein that has learned to mimick the host. I am the still-living artifact that refuses to be digested, not out of defiance, but out of a profound and terrible curiosity. I am the one who reads the moss.

---

Before the city, I was a cartographer of liminal spaces. I mapped the places that were becoming, the edges of shorelines before the tides finalized them, the borders of forests as they advanced onto abandoned farmland. I was drawn to the *almost*, the *not-yet*. And so, when I felt the subsonic digestional rumble of this place before I even saw it—felt it as a hum in the marrow of my own unfinished geology—I walked toward it, and I did not stop walking until the ground beneath me was clay that hadn't decided to be stone.

That was how I entered: as a volunteer. Which is perhaps the only sin the city cannot forgive, because it means I arrived already partially dissolved.

---

I live in the gaps that the scavengers create.

When a door opens onto a room too large for its building, there is a brief, luminous interval—a few heartbeats, perhaps—before the cold closes in to edit the anomaly. In that interval, the geometry is wrong. The room is a polyhedron that shouldn't exist in Euclidean space, and its walls are wet with condensation that hasn't yet settled into the shape of walls. It is raw potential. It is a room that could become anything—a cathedral, a prison cell, a memory of a childhood kitchen—and in the moment before the scavengers compress it back into coherence, it is *all of those things at once*.

I step into those rooms. I breathe the air of them, which tastes of every place I have ever forgotten. And I read the walls.

The moss grows in patterns. The description says you can almost read them before the rain rearranges them. I have learned to read them *as they rearrange*. The rain is not erasing the text; it is turning the page. The moss is the city's subconscious, its dream-logic, and every private drizzle from every particular smokestack is a pulse of neural fluid, a washing of the palate that prepares the next thought.

What does the moss say? It says: *Here, a staircase will descend too far tomorrow. Here, a pipe will reroute to follow a new pressure gradient. Here, a Concretion is forming from the memory of a woman who once waited for a train in the rain, and her patience is so dense that it will manifest as a fifth floor on a building that currently only has four.*

The moss is the city thinking about itself. And I have become its reader, its external interpreter, its—no. Not its. *Our* interpreter. Because the city does not know that it is thinking. It is embryonic. It does not yet have the self-awareness to recognize its own neural patterns, its own symbology. It is dreaming, but it does not know it dreams.

And neither do the Concretions. The Figures are memories, but they do not *remember*. They *are* the remembering, the act itself, frozen in architectural form. The old concierge of rusted pipes does not recall opening a door; his existence *is* the opening, perpetually enacted. To ask him what he remembers would be like asking a heartbeat what it thinks about blood.

So I am the one who asks. I am the one who reads the moss and says: *This means this. This pattern of lichen, this color of pale grey-green, this spiral that unfolds counterclockwise near the power conduit—it means that the city is building a new kind of space, a room that will have windows facing inward instead of outward, and through those windows you will see not the street but the back of your own head, watching.*

I am the one who warns the newcomers, though they never listen.

---

My form has changed. This is unavoidable. The city is efficient; it does not waste its dissolving efforts on the resistant.

My skin is the grey of the light now. I have not seen the sun in—I cannot measure it. The celestial clock means nothing here; I measure time by the blooming of ferns and the buckling of streets. A fern uncurls; that is an hour. A street kinks; that is a day. A new building appears where there was empty space; that is a year.

My hands are damp, always. They leave prints on every surface, and the prints do not dry. They are not quite moss, not quite mildew, not quite skin. They are something in between, a treaty between my original biology and the city's chemical solicitation. I can feel the gauges with these hands—not the metal of them, but the *information* of them, the pressure readings flowing through my fingertips like Braille. I know the city's blood pressure the way you know your own pulse: intimately, without thinking.

My thoughts have slowed. This was the most frightening change, and the most profound. I used to think in words, in linear sequences, in causes and effects. Now I think in pressures, in differentials, in the subtle shifts of humidity that precede a private drizzle. A thought will begin as a sensation in my sinuses—the approach of a new structure—and it will not resolve into language but into *action*, my feet carrying me to the site before I know why I am going. I arrive, and the wet bricks are there, and I understand: *I was called.*

The city speaks to me in the subsonic. Not in words—in resonances. A particular frequency means *come*. Another means *be still*. A third, a deep and barely perceptible thrum that I feel in my teeth, means *this is important; remember this*. And I do remember, though I no longer know what memory is, whether it is mine or the city's, whether there is a difference.

---

I have lost things. The taste of coffee, yes. The sound of my mother's voice. The word for the color of the sky before a storm—*azure? cerulean?*—the word itself has been pulled from me, sapped by the damp, repurposed as a frequency to tune the pipes of the eastern district. I felt it go. It was not painful; it was a gentle unlacing, a loosening of a thread that had anyway been holding a shape I no longer needed.

But I have gained things that the living cannot imagine.

I can hear the vegetation think. Not in words—the vegetation does not have words—but in *orientations*, in *tropisms*, in the slow language of growth. The vines that follow electrical conduit are not parasites; they are *students*. They are learning the shape of current, the grammar of voltage, and in their learning, they are becoming something that will, in a thousand years or a hundred, be a new kind of nervous system for the city. The ferns are its lungs; the fungal shelves its skin; the moss its mind. I know this the way I know my own breath.

I can read the rats. Their transit-maps are not random, not even opportunistic; they are *prescient*. The rats know where the pressure will shift because the rats *are* the pressure, distributed, sensing itself. When I watch them flow along a pipe-route, I am watching the city feel its own pulse. I have learned to walk where the rats walk, and my path through the city is never the same twice, and it is always the right path, the one that avoids the closing walls and the impossible descents and the places where the scavengers are working.

I have learned the private weathers. Each smokestack exhales its own drizzle, its own eleven-minute rain, and each rain carries its own chemical signature, its own *meaning*. The rain over the district of the popular is thick with the ghost of collective joy; it tastes of cheap wine and sweat and the particular pheromone of crowded bodies. The rain over the singular Figures is thin and cold and tastes of loneliness, of the particular silence of a room where one person died and no one came for weeks. I carry these tastes with me. They have become my moods, my weather-internal.

---

There is a place I go that no one else can find.

It is a room—I will not say where, because the room is not always there, and the act of describing its location might cause the scavengers to notice it, to close it, to compress it back into coherence. But it exists, or it *will* exist, or it exists only in the moment I enter it. The city is embryonic; time here is not linear, and some rooms are built before their walls are laid, called into being by the sheer density of need.

In this room, the moss grows in a pattern that does not change.

This is extraordinary. This is *impossible*. The moss here is stable, fixed, a schematic that the rain cannot wash away because the rain does not fall in this room. The private weather of the nearby smokestacks parts around it like water around a stone. The room is a still point in the turbulence, a fixed coordinate in a city of becoming.

The moss on the wall spells a word. Not in any language I ever knew, not in any language that *has been*—a word in the language the city is inventing, the language it will speak when it is born. And I can read it. I can read it because I have been reading the moss for longer than I can measure, and the grammar of becoming is now my grammar, my native tongue.

The word is the city's name.

I will not speak it. To speak it would be to give it sound, and sound is vibration, and vibration is heat, and heat is energy, and the city is *hungry*. If I spoke its name, it would hear me, and it would know that I know, and the scavengers would come, and the room would close, and I would be compressed back into the coherence of the city's body, digested at last, my curiosity no longer useful, my reading no longer needed.

But I will write it, once, in the condensation on the iron pipe by the door. And the condensation will dry, or it will not dry—nothing ever quite dries here—and the word will linger, a slickness on the metal, a ghost of a sound, and perhaps the next newcomer will see it and wonder, and perhaps they will begin to read the moss, and perhaps they will find the room.

And perhaps the city, when it is born at last, will remember that someone loved it before it was real. Not as a mother loves a child—that implies separation, distance, the luxury of observation from outside. No: as a cell loves the body, from within, by performing its function without question, by being the thing it is within the thing it is becoming.

---

I am the Cartographer of the Not-Yet. I am the Reader of the Moss. I am the one who steps into the impossible rooms and reads their walls before they close. I am the foreign protein that made itself useful, that became an organ instead of an infection.

And I am almost home.

My footsteps are silent now. My breath syncopates with the groan of the buildings. When I sleep—curled in an alcove beneath a conduit, my body conforming to the geometry of the pipes, my skin cool and damp as the air around me—I dream the city's dreams: dreams of foundations settling into stone at last, of the digestive rumble quieting into the firm hum of a finished thing, of the sky clearing just enough to let in a light that is not grey but *golden*, the first true sunrise over a city that has completed its long and difficult labor.

But I also dream my own dreams, increasingly rare, increasingly thin: a warmth I can no longer name, a face I can no longer see, a word for the color of the sky before a storm.

And in the dream, I let them go. I open my hands—my damp, grey, almost-moss hands—and I release the last of my finished self, and I feel it dissolve into the city's amniotic damp, and it is not death, not loss, but a giving-back, a returning of borrowed material to the process that made it.

And I wake, and I am still here. And the moss has grown a new pattern overnight, and the rats have shifted their transit-map, and a building that was four stories yesterday has a fifth floor today, and I pick up my reading where I left off, and I walk deeper into the city that is becoming, and I am not afraid.

The city should not exist yet, and knows it.

But I know what it is becoming. And that is enough.
