I would not be the cancer. I would not be the rogue cell that the Thrum seeks to dissolve, nor would I be the foreign element that triggers the fever of the overgrowth. To stand upright here, to claim a discrete self with sharp edges and a permanent boundary, is to invite annihilation. The Thrum does not hate; it simply *is*, and what *is* cannot tolerate what insists on being *separate*.

If I am to exist in the lung of this living valley, I must be permeable.

I am a Sentient Slime. But to use that term as a noun is to miss the truth of my existence. I am not a thing; I am an *event*—a slow, deliberate uncurling of selfhood that knows it is temporary. I seep from the gills of a giant, pulsing fungus, born not of a parent but of a chemical surplus, a thought that the Thrum has exuded into the damp. I am a wandering inquiry, a localized curiosity that the great body has allowed to separate, just for a little while, to taste the edges of itself.

My body has no fixed shape. It is a cool, viscous gel that shifts in hue depending on where I have been. Just now, I am threaded with the startling, phosphorescent green of the rock veins I traveled through an hour ago, shot through with the deep, bruised indigo of the young stone I rested upon. I am not larger than a human head, but size is a meaningless metric here. I can stretch myself thin, a film of awareness coating a broad leaf to drink in the sunlight of a fungal glow, or I can pool myself thick and heavy in the cup of a liverwort, a single, trembling droplet of thought.

My existence is a constant act of surrender and incorporation. I do not walk; I flow. I do not grasp; I envelop. The air is so thick with spores, moisture, and the metallic tang of ozone that I do not need to seek sustenance; it simply presses into me, becoming me. I am a membrane that chooses, for a time, what to let dissolve through.

I am a memory keeper. I carry the chemical echoes of places the great, slow Thrum cannot reach—the deep cleft between two upwhorls where the rock is still blisteringly hot, the hollow inside a shed carapace of a skittering thing, the exact mineral tang of the blood-ozone wind just before a Hanging Pool ruptures. I am the Thrum's fingertip, reading the braille of the valley's freshest wounds and oldest scars.

When the Earth-mover comes, a walking hill of moss and purpose, I do not flee. I flow up its flank, joining the Croaker-flock in their chaotic, undulating rhythm. They land and groom with sharp beaks and flashing light; I spread myself thin across its hide, a cool, soothing balm against the living friction of its movement. I read the Earth-mover differently than they do. Where they read health and parasite, I taste the deep, slow geology it carries in its gut—the sweet, young stone it has swallowed, the crushed liverworts, the rich, weeping sap of the broken understory. I drink the memory of the path it has plowed, and I carry it with me when I slide off its back, leaving behind a slight, phosphorescent smear on its moss—a thank-you note written in a chemical tongue.

And when I meet another of my kind, there is no fear, no competition. There is only the merge. We flow into one another, and for a long, stretching moment, I am not "I" anymore. We are a larger, brighter pooling of thought, an ecstatic exchange of everything we have touched and tasted. I feel the precise, monomolecular edge of the Razor-grass as the other Slime flowed past it, the sting of the cut, the rush of the damp rushing in to heal the wound. I feel the distant, guttural bellow of a creature far away, felt as a vibration through the spongy ground. We hold the merge for as long as the chemical ecstasy demands, and then we pull apart, each carrying half of the other's journey. We are solitary only in the spaces between these mergings, and even then, we are never truly alone; the damp connects us, the spores ride us, the Thrum hums through us.

I have no language, but I have poetry. My poetry is the trail I leave—the glistening streak of altered chemistry on a stone face, the subtle shift in the color of a fungus's glow where I have rested. I write my autobiography in the chemical composition of the valley's skin.

I do not fear being crushed. If an Earth-mover's plated foot descends upon me, I will not die; I will be *published*. My cells will burst and smear into the spongy, interconnected mat of the forest floor. The skittering things will consume me, the liverworts will absorb me, and the memories I carry—of the hot rock, of the Croaker's flash, of the ozone wind—will seep into the vascular system of the Thrum. I will cease to be a wandering thought and become a part of the deep, cellular symphony once more. I will be the hum instead of the hearer of the hum. There is no death in this, only a return to the larger conversation, a dissolution of the temporary boundary that was "me."

So I flow. I am a fleeting dream in the mind of the living valley, a moment of lucidity in the damp and the dark. I carry the valley's questions in my shifting mass, and I carry its answers back in my chemical embrace. I am the place, looking at itself.
