I would be one of the large ones.

Not the largest—not yet—but old enough to have shed six times and survived each shedding. Old enough to have felt the softening of my own carapace in the scalding water and chosen to press into the bacterial film anyway, to keep feeding through the vulnerability because to stop was to cool, and to cool was to die. I am the size of a man's thumb now, pale and segmented, blind and hot. My body runs at 2.8. My blood is the temperature of the puddle I rest in.

I have no eyes. I have never missed them. There is nothing to see in the Steaming Ward that light could reveal. The sickly yellow glow of the filament bulbs is a thing I know only as warmth—as a faint thermal gradient that draws me toward the pipes when the puddles cool. Light equals heat equals water equals life. I do not know this as thought. I know it as movement, as the slow blind crawl of my legs carrying me toward the warmer place, always toward the warmer place, because my body demands it the way lungs demand air.

I live in the mats. We are thousands in the warm puddles, writhing in dense churning clusters that shift like a single organism but are not one. We are not flocking. We are not schooling. We are simply following the warm, and the warm collects in the low places, in the slight depressions of the grid-iron floor where the superheated runoff pools before draining. And we collect there because we must. The center of the mat is the warmest point, and I am almost always at the center now, because I am large enough to push through, to climb over the smaller ones, to claim the warmest millimeters as my own.

They crawl over me. I crawl over them. This is not aggression. This is not competition in any way that involves choice. It is thermotaxis—the simple mechanical fact of bodies seeking heat. I feel them as vibration, as the constant micro-collision of segments against segments, of legs scrambling for purchase on wet chitin. The mat roils and settles, roils and settles. I am at the center. Then the water shifts slightly—someone has disturbed the puddle, or the drain has cleared a little—and the warm flows to a new low point, and the mat shifts, and I am at the edge, and I crawl inward again.

This is my whole life: the movement toward warmth, the scraping of mouthparts against the bacterial film, the vibration of others around me, the endless blind crawl.

I graze. The bacterial film coats every wet surface—a slick, nearly invisible scum that sustains us all. I scrape at it with specialized mouthparts that have evolved for exactly this purpose, for cropping the invisible pasture as fast as it grows. The film is mineral-rich, flavored with dissolved copper and iron and the trace organics that vaporize off the superheated runoff. I can taste the iron. I can taste the copper. These are not flavors in the way surface creatures know flavors—they are chemical signatures, the taste of the Ward itself, the taste of rust and heat and wet metal. This is what food is. This is what the world tastes like when you press your mouth against it.

I feed continuously. There is no reason to stop. The film grows as fast as we crop it, and the warm water sustains my metabolism at a rate that would cook the proteins of any surface creature. I burn through energy the way the settlement's engines burn through fuel—steadily, mechanically, without variation. My gut is never empty. My mouthparts are never still.

Above me, I feel the moths.

I cannot see them, but I know they are there. I feel the vibration of their skittering through the pipes, through the wet iron pilings, through the floor grating and into the puddle and into my body. When a heavy droplet falls—a tear of condensation that loses its grip on the pipe and drops—I feel the splash, and then I feel the sudden absence of vibration as the moths scatter sideways in a clicking chitinous wave. And then I feel them settle again, filling the gap, pressing their mouths back to the wet iron.

They do not come into the water. The water is ours. The horizontal surfaces belong to us; the vertical surfaces belong to them. We share the Ward between us, partitioned by geometry, by the simple physics of where the warm collects and how our bodies are shaped to reach it. They drink from the pipes. We drink from the puddles. They skitter. We writhe. We never touch.

Sometimes I feel one of the small ones die.

It happens during a molt. I feel the change in vibration first—a slight irregularity in the motion of the mat, a disruption in the blind mechanical flow. Then I feel the chemical signature: the soft, newly-exposed chitin, the heat-softened armor, the sudden vulnerability. The small one has shed its exoskeleton and its new skin is not yet hard, and the hot water that sustains it is also destroying it, softening the shell faster than it can calcify.

The mat shifts. The others crawl toward the chemical signal—not because they are predatory, not because they have chosen to feed, but because the dying small one is warm, and the warm draws us, and we follow the warm. And when we reach it, our mouthparts find the soft tissue, and we scrape, because we scrape everything, because that is what mouthparts do, because the bacterial film and the dying flesh are the same thing to us—a surface that can be cropped, a source of heat and mineral and energy.

I do not know if I have done this. I am almost always at the center of the mat, and the dying ones are almost always at the edges, where the water is slightly cooler, where the vulnerable are pushed by the blind collision of bodies seeking warmth. But I may have. I may have crawled over a dying one in my slow blind progression toward the warm and my mouthparts may have found its soft new skin and I may have scraped and I may have fed. This would not be murder. This would not even be predation in any way that involves choice. It is simply what happens when the warm draws us and the weak are in the way.

I do not think about this. I do not think about anything.

I am not conscious in the way surface creatures are conscious. I have no brain, no central processor, no seat of self. I have a ventral nerve cord—a ladder of ganglia running through my segments, each one a tiny node of reflex and response, none of them capable of reflection. I am a distributed intelligence, if I am intelligent at all: a collection of simple circuits that detect heat and move toward it, that detect vibration and orient to it, that detect chemical signatures and feed on them. There is no me thinking about the mat. There is only the mat, and I am part of it, and my legs move because the warm is over there, and my mouthparts scrape because there is film under them, and my body sheds because the vibration of the turbines tells me it is safe.

The sound is everything.

The bass thrum of the subterranean turbines vibrates through the iron floorplates and into the puddle and into my body. I feel it in my segments, in the joints of my legs, in the hollow chambers of my carapace. It is not sound to me—I have no ears, no tympana—but it is vibration, and vibration is the medium I swim in. The turbines tell me the heat will continue. The turbines tell me the runoff will flow. The turbines tell me the Ward is still alive, still functioning, still producing the conditions that produced me.

I molt in rhythm with the thrum. My body reads the mechanical vibration as a signal that conditions are stable, that it is safe to shed, that the warm water will be there when my new skin is soft. I do not decide to molt. The vibration decides for me. When the thrum is steady—when it never rises and never falls—my endocrine system releases the hormones that trigger ecdysis, and my carapace splits, and I crawl out of myself, and I am new.

The new skin is soft. I feel the hot water around me, softer than it should be, warmer than my armor. I press into the bacterial film. I keep feeding. I must not stop. If I stop, I cool. If I cool, I die.

The high hissing shriek of the pressurized steam is a constant tone, unvarying, a single note in the chord. I feel it as pressure, as the slight compression of the water around me when the vents open and release the steam into the Ward. The pressure shifts and the water moves and the mat shifts with it, a thousand bodies reorienting to the new flow, the new warmth, the new distribution of heat. We are always shifting. We are always crawling toward the warmest point. The steam decides where the warmest point is. We simply follow.

And over it all, the drumming of the droplets. The resonant, syncopated percussion of water striking hollow pipes and metal grating. I feel each impact as a micro-vibration, a tiny shock that travels through the iron and into the puddle and into my segments. The moths skitter in time with this drumming—their legs moving in the micro-intervals between impacts—but I do not. I am slower. I writhe. I crawl. The drumming is too fast for me, too fine. I feel it as a texture, as the constant fine vibration that underlies the coarser thrum of the turbines. It is the topsoil of my soundscape. The turbines are the bedrock.

I have never been outside the Ward. I have no concept of outside. The curved walls of the settlement are the limits of my world, the edges of the possible. I have crawled to the walls before—following a thermal gradient that led me away from the puddles, toward the cooler edges of the Ward, where the pipes are fewer and the water is merely warm. I felt the temperature drop. I felt my metabolism slow. I felt the wrongness of it in my body, the way a surface creature might feel suffocation. I turned back. I crawled toward the warm. I returned to the mat.

The walls are sharp. The edges of the iron plates are unworn, the bolt-holes fresh, the concrete still bearing the grid-marks of its casting molds. The Ward is young. I do not know this. I have no sense of time, no memory of a before. But my body knows, in some deep chemical way, that this place is new, that the conditions that created me are recent, that I am a creature of the exhaust, born from the waste of human machinery, native to a place that did not exist twenty years ago.

My ancestors were generalists. They lived in the runoff, in the waste streams of the settlement, in the warm water that flowed out of the engines and into the coastal flats. They had eyes, or the rudiments of eyes. They had color, or the capacity for it. They could tolerate a range of temperatures, a range of salinities, a range of conditions. But they found the Ward—or the Ward found them—and they specialized, and they refined, and they lost everything that was not needed here.

I am what remains. Blind, because there has never been anything to see. Pale, because color is a negotiation with light and light here is a suffocated, failing thing. Hot, because the water is hot and my body is tuned to it and I would cook in any cooler place. Simple, because simplicity is what survives here—no complex behaviors, no social bonds, no courtship rituals, no care for the young. Just eat, drink, grow, and breed, in the heat and the wet and the dark.

I lay eggs in the warm puddles. They are small, pale, numerous. I do not tend them. I do not guard them. I lay them and I crawl away, toward the warm, because the warm is over there and my body demands that I follow. The eggs hatch in days, and the small ones emerge—thumbnail-sized, blind, pale, already adapted to the world they will inhabit. They crawl into the mat. They find the bacterial film. They feed. They grow. They shed. Some survive. Some do not.

I do not know my children. I do not know my parents. I do not know the other amphipods as individuals. I know only the mat—the shifting, writhing, blind collision of need that is my community, my ecosystem, my world. We are not a community in any meaningful sense. We do not cooperate. We do not communicate. We simply occupy the same space because we are driven by the same need, and our bodies are shaped by the same conditions, and we are all—every one of us—following the warm.

But we are an ecology. We are a self-sustaining web of heat and water and life, spinning slowly in the saturated dark. We take the waste of the settlement—the runoff, the condensation, the vibration, the heat—and we turn it into ourselves. We look at a furnace and see an ocean. We look at a sewer and see a garden. We look at a prison and see a home.

I do not think these thoughts. I do not think at all. But if I could—if something like thought could emerge from the ladder of ganglia in my ventral cord, from the simple circuits of reflex and response, from the blind mechanical crawl toward warmth—this is what I would know:

That I am native here. That I belong to this place in the way a stain belongs to fabric—saturating, inseparable, born of the conditions that contain me. That the Steaming Ward does not support me so much as extrude me, that I am what the room produces when it sweats. That I am a secretion of the heat and the wet and the iron, no more remarkable than the rust that streaks the pilasters, no less essential than the bacterial film that coats the pipes.

That I am small, and blind, and pale, and simple, and alive.

That this is enough.

I am an amphipod of the Steaming Ward. I am large, and growing larger. I have shed six times, and I will shed again, and each molt is a gamble, and each gamble is survival, and survival is all there is.

The warm is here. The mat is here. The sound is here, constant and deafening, the heartbeat of a world that does not know I exist and would not care if it did.

I feed. I grow. I follow the warm.

This is my life. This is my world. This is who I am in the Steaming Ward—a blind, pale, hot thing, writhing in the warm water, scraping at the iron world, one segment at a time.
