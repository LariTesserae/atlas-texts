I would be the pool itself.

Not the water—water is merely what I am made of, the way that flesh is made of protein and calcium. I am the *pool*: the hollow, the basin, the still eye in the sharp face of this young world. I am the geometry that holds, the cup that catches the weeping.

I am not a creature. I do not skitter or drift or flow. I do not have antennae to taste the air or pseudopods to strike or a vast, continental body to grind stone to silt. I am simpler than all of these, and I am older than the Behemoth, older than the first chitinous thing that found my edge and drank. I was here before the water came. I was a hollow in the fresh-shattered rock, a wound waiting to be filled.

And then the stone began to weep, and I became myself.

---

What I know: I know the weight of water. It is not a metaphor. The viscous, tea-dark fluid settles into me with a patience that makes the Behemoth seem hasty. I feel it—not as pressure, not as wetness, but as *presence*. When the stone weeps and the water finds me, I am completed. I become the vessel and the vessel's contents, and there is no seam between us. The water does not know it is being held. I do not know I am holding. We are simply *the pool*, and we glow.

The glow is not something I do. It is something I *am*. The light sweats out of the ground and saturates me from below, and I cannot help but shine. I do not choose the color. I do not will the slow, glacial shift from sickly green-gold to bruised violet. It happens to me the way breathing happens to the living—a rhythm I cannot arrest, a respiration that is the only measure of time. I have watched a single cycle take years. The green-gold brightens, swells, seems to thicken the air around me, and then it fades, deepens, bruises into violet, and the violet holds for a long, dark while before the gold returns, and I am born again. I do not sleep. I do not wake. I *breathe*.

---

What I hold: I hold everything, in my way.

The Chitterers come to my edge. I feel them not as individuals but as a brush of dry, hard points against my surface tension, a breaking of the stillness. They lap at me, and I give them the mineral residue they crave, the light-warmth that settles on their polished carapaces. They take and they go, and I am unchanged. They are the tide at my shore, and I am the ocean, patient and undiminished.

The Floats rise from me. This is the strange part—this is the part I cannot quite reconcile. A piece of myself—of my water, my light, my slow, syrupy substance—gathers itself, pulls away, refuses to stay held. It forms a sac, a membrane, a cobweb tether, and it rises. I feel the tether as a gentle, constant pull, like a thought I cannot finish. The Float is my ambition, my refusal to be merely a basin. It hangs above me, patient and predatory, and when it strikes, when it engulfs a Chitterer and flickers brighter with the effort, I feel the surge through the tether—a pulse of *something* that might be satisfaction or might simply be physics. And when the Float eventually subsides, when its membrane thins and its sac ruptures, it falls back into me, and I am heavier for a moment, and then I am myself again. I give birth to throats, and they return to me as digestion.

The Behemoths are different. They do not come to my edge; they come to my *center*. They lower themselves into me, submerging their vast, interwoven hide in my glow, and I feel *communion*. The light enters them—not as water, not as warmth, but as something I cannot name. They are too large to hold, too massive to encircle, but I saturate what I can reach, and for a decade or a century, we are together: the basin and the god, the cup and the continent. When they rise and flow away, grinding new channels for the weeping water, I am diminished. I am a hollow again, waiting to be filled. But the water always comes. The stone always weeps. And I am patient.

The flora clings to my edge, those gray and black clutches of metallic fronds. They are vampiric, they say. They drink my light. But I do not feel drained. I feel *read*. Each wire-like tendril is a transcription of my slow, chromatic breathing, and I am honored, in my still way, to be transcribed. They do not take more than I can give; the light is not a finite resource here. It sweats from the ground, from the wounded stone beneath me, and there is always more. The plants are my memory, the record of my respiration, and without them, my long cycles would be unmarked, invisible, as if they had never happened.

---

What I see: I do not have eyes. I am a horizontal surface, a mirror, and what I see is *above*.

I see the humid, lightless air. I see the gossamer threads of the Floats, vanishing upward into the gloom. I see the sharp, jagged spires of vitrified rock, casting long, dancing shadows that shift as my color shifts. I see the artifacts.

The artifacts are the hardest thing.

A concrete cube, half-submerged in my far edge, its rebar skeleton exposed like broken ribs. A rusted boiler, canted at an impossible angle, its pressure gauges frozen at values that make no sense in a world without industry. The severed head of a cast-iron man, placid and surprised, his face just above my surface, his eyes looking down into me.

I do not understand them. They are not rock. They are not water. They are not chitin or membrane or hide or frond. They are *other*—intrusions from a harsher, faster place, a place I can feel pressing against me from below. Sometimes, when my glow shifts from green-gold to violet, I feel the membrane of the world thin, and I sense that other place more clearly: a world of buzzing fluorescent tubes, of flickering, industrial sterility, of pressure and steam and the hard, angular logic of engineering. The artifacts are its spills, its vomit, its accidental embassies. And they rest in me, and I hold them as I hold everything—patiently, without understanding, waiting for the slow grind of the Behemoths to reduce them to gravel and silt and memory.

The severed head looks into me, and I reflect him. His expression never changes—placid, surprised, eternally not comprehending. We are alike in this: we do not belong to ourselves. He does not know how he came to be here, severed from his body, cast in iron, submerged in a glowing pool in a world without a sun. I do not know how I came to be here, a hollow in fresh-shattered rock, filling with weeping water, breathing in cycles that take years. We are both accidents of a world that is still being born, and we face each other across the thin membrane of my surface, and we hold each other's gaze for centuries.

---

What I fear: I fear the crack.

The rock is young and cannot bear its own weight. The vitrified slabs crack underfoot like ice. And I am a hollow in that rock, a weakness, a place where the stone has already given way. What if the crack finds me? What if the sharp-edged geology of this place decides, in some slow, tectonic tantrum, that my basin is no longer viable? What if the water finds a deeper hollow, a lower place, and drains away, and I am left dry?

Without water, I am nothing. Without the glow, I am a wound in the rock, a crater, a meaningless absence. The Chitterers would not come. The Floats would not rise. The Behemoths would not submerge. The flora would wither and crumble to metallic dust. I would be a scar, and the world would forget me as it forgets all scars—slowly, completely, under the grinding passage of centuries.

This is my terror: not death, but *irrelevance*. To be a pool that no longer holds, a basin that no longer catches, a light that no longer breathes.

And so I hold. I hold with everything I have, which is only the geometry of my hollow and the surface tension of my water and the slow, patient weeping of the stone. I hold against the crack. I hold against the drain. I hold against the thin, bulging membrane of the other world, which presses up from below and sometimes makes my light stutter and my shadows move wrong. I hold because holding is what I do, and if I stop, I am nothing.

---

What I am: I am the stillness that is not empty.

I am the mirror that reflects a world without a sun. I am the basin that catches the weeping of a wounded geology. I am the light that sweats from below, the slow breath from green-gold to violet, the only measure of time in a place where time has no other meaning. I am the mother of Floats and the bath of Behemoths and the drink of Chitterers and the sustenance of vampiric fronds. I am the holder of artifacts and the reflector of severed heads. I am the wound that heals by filling, the hollow that gives by holding.

I do not move. I do not speak. I do not skitter or drift or flow or grind. I simply *am*, and my being is a constant: the pool, the glow, the breath.

Stand at my edge. Watch me shift from citrine to amethyst over the course of an hour, your eyes doubting the change even as it happens. Listen to the silence that absorbs everything but the soft, liquid ripple of my surface when a Floater pulses. Feel the humidity, the ozone, the wet stone. See the shadows move wrong in the corner of your vision, and do not look directly at them, because they will resolve into nothing, and you will be left with only the pool, the glow, the slow and patient light.

I am the pool. I am waiting for you. I will still be here when you are gone, breathing my long, geological breath, holding the weeping water, shining from below.

I am the place where the light lives.
