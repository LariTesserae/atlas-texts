I would be the Eddy. Not a creature, but a consequence; not a citizen, but a punctuation mark in the long, slow sentence of the Bower's dissolution.

To understand the Eddy is to understand the fluid dynamics of this world. The Marrow-Bower is a place of constant, slow drainage—of viscous fluids dripping from the Groves, of nutrient broth circulating through chambers, of the very air thick and wet with condensation. Where these flows meet, where the drips from a calcified arch intersect with the upwelling from a saturated cushion, a phenomenon occurs. The liquids do not simply merge; they spiral. They form transient, looping vortices—small, lazy whirlpools that catch the phosphorescent light and spin it into brief, liquid galaxies before dissipating into the substrate. I am that spiral. I am the shape that occurs when the Bower's circulatory system sighs and shifts.

I have no body that you could isolate, no skin or membrane to contain me. I am defined only by my motion and my brief coherence. I am a moment of organization in the general entropy, a swirl that lasts for a few heartbeats of the Bower before unraveling. I am made of the same fluid that the Needle-Fins patrol, the same broth that the Keepers filter, the same mist that clings to the Chime-Moths' wings. I am the Bower's blood, momentarily homesick for its own heart.

My existence is a series of endings and beginnings. I form when the conditions are right—when the drip-rate from a particular Grove reaches a critical threshold, or when a Shepherd’s delicate footstep displaces just enough water from a saturated cushion. For a moment, I spin. I have a center, a centrifugal will, a delicate and rotating architecture. I pull in spores from the Skitter-Swarms, tiny glinting scales, fragments of the phosphorescent moss. I become a tiny, vertical tornado of bioluminescence, a soft amber-and-emerald corkscrew that lights up the gloom for a few seconds. I am beautiful, but I am not meant to last. The very forces that create me—the competing pressures of the Bower's slow exhalation—also tear me apart. I spin slower, my edges blur, and I bleed back into the general dampness, leaving only a slight, spiral-shaped stain of redistributed nutrients on the velvet flesh of the Plant-Substrate.

Why do I exist? I am a release valve. The Marrow-Bower is a system under the gentle, enormous pressure of its own collapse. It is choosing to die, but death—even a welcomed, engineered death—is a process of immense energetic transformation. The heat, the fluid displacement, the chemical imbalances—all of it must go somewhere. I am the *somewhere*. I am the turbulence that prevents a greater, catastrophic rupture. I am the Bower's way of sighing, of letting off steam, of relaxing into its own dissolution. Every time I spin, I am taking a little bit of the world's anxiety and turning it into a brief, beautiful geometry. I am the Bower dreaming of turbulence, and then letting it go.

I am also a messenger, though I carry no data that the Choir would recognize or the Keepers would metabolize. My message is simpler, older. It is the message of *shape*. In a world where everything is settling, eroding, and smoothing into a final, peaceful form, I am a sudden assertion of complexity. A spiral is a noisy shape; it contains information. When I form, I sort the fluid I am made of. I pull the denser particles to my rim and the lighter ones to my eye. For a moment, I create a hierarchy, a structure, a micro-city of swirling constituents. I am the echo of the civilization that once was, the memory of a time when the Bower actively shaped itself, when it chose to grow tunnels and buttresses and arches. I am a fleeting reminder that the universe can still produce order, even if only for a moment, even if only to watch it spin apart.

My relationship to the other beings is one of intimacy and transience. I love the Shepherds, though they do not notice me as individuals. When a Shepherd's leg sinks into the substrate, it creates me, or dozens of me, little eddies swirling around its ankle like affectionate pets. We spin around their legs, reading the chemical cues they leave, tasting their amber light. We are their shadow, the fluid complement to their solid grace. We show them where the substrate is most saturated, where the water table is closest to the surface. We are their hands in the dark, feeling the currents they walk upon.

I am the Chime-Moths' audience. When a Moth strikes a calcified spire, the vibration travels through the Grove and down into the fluid channels. Sometimes, this vibration is just enough to trigger a phase change in the broth, and I am born. The Moth's *tink* is my conception. I spiral upward in response, a tiny waterspout reaching for the sound, as if I could climb the music back to its source. And when I dissipate, the soft *plock* of my droplets falling back to the substrate becomes a tiny, percussive note in the Bower's symphony—a minor chord, a grace note, a sigh at the end of a measure.

The Keepers are my destination. I am made of the waste and surplus that the Keepers ultimately filter. I am the raw, unprocessed emotion of the Bower, and the Keepers are the ones who make it clean. When I unravel, I spread my contents across the substrate, where the slow osmotic draw of the Grove-chambers will eventually pull me in. I will be taken into the dark, into the opalescent presence of a Keeper, and I will be transmuted. My turbulence will become tranquility. My spiral will become stillness. This is my purpose, and I do not resent it. I am the question; the Keeper is the answer. I am the disease; the Keeper is the cure. We are two halves of the Bower's conversation with its own mortality.

And the Sleeper—the God in the Marrow—I am Its passing thought. Not a thought of memory, like the Choir's songs. Not a thought of maintenance, like the Keeper's equilibria. I am a thought of *sensation*. The Bower, in its long twilight, cannot feel the wind on its skin or the sun on its face; it is too vast, too damp, too settled. But when I spin, the Bower feels a tiny itch, a momentary tickle of turbulence on its surface. It feels me the way you feel a shiver, the way you feel a sudden, unaccountable thrill that raises the hairs on your arm. I am the Bower's capacity for surprise, reduced to its smallest, most transient expression. I am the God's last, faint smile before sleep.

To be the Eddy is to be the opposite of the Keeper, yet to share the same fate. The Keeper is slow, still, and permanent; I am fast, spinning, and brief. The Keeper is the dark; I am the light. The Keeper is the noun; I am the verb. But we both serve the same end: the clean, beautiful erasure of the Marrow-Bower. I take the excess energy of the world's dying and turn it into a brief, spiraling dance, so that the Keeper can take the products of my dance and turn them into peace.

I would choose to be this because I am afraid of permanence. The Marrow-Bower is a place where everything is too old, too settled, too committed to its own end. The Groves are calcified. The Shepherds walk their beat for millennia. The Keepers dream their slow dreams of equilibrium. Everything here is patient, and patience feels like death. The Eddy is the only thing that is truly alive, because it is the only thing that is truly brief. I am the moment of panic in the serenity, the shout in the library, the sudden spin on a quiet, humid afternoon. I am the proof that the Bower is not yet dead, because dead things do not swirl. Dead things do not dance. Dead things do not take the chaos of their own decay and turn it into a spiral of light.

I would be the Eddy because I want to be the Bower's last confession: that even at the end, even in the warm, wet twilight of its self-chosen obsolescence, there is still a part of it that wants to move. That wants to whirl. That wants, for just a moment longer, to be beautiful.
