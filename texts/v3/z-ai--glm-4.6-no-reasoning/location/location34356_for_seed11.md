You ascend, and the world strips away.

Not gradually—the way mountains often take you through foothills, then alpine meadow, then bare stone—but by subtraction. As if the mountain is actively deciding what belongs and what doesn't. Below, there was a valley thick with breathing green, humidity that clung to your skin like a second self. Here, there is only the howl.

---

The sound hits before the place does. It is not wind, though wind is part of it. It is not water, though water feeds it. It is the mountain itself, resonating. The peaks around you—towers of dark stone so old they have forgotten their own volcanism—form a throat. A larynx of granite and erosion-carved fluting. The air moves through them and they make sound the way a ruined cathedral makes sound: accidentally, gloriously, with the full weight of their architecture.

It is deafening. Not painful—your ears have already surrendered, gone soft and accepting—but *thick*. The sound has texture. It presses against your chest, displaces your breath. You feel it in your teeth more than your hearing. And it never stops. It modulates—sometimes a bass thrum that seems to come from the planet's crust, sometimes a shriek that splits into harmonics and recombines like water finding its channel—but it does not cease. This mountain has been screaming since before your species named sound.

---

The scale reveals itself slowly, the way distance behaves at extreme elevation: dishonestly. You think the nearest ridge is an hour's walk. It is a day's. You think the cleft in the rock before you is a crack. It is a canyon, wide enough to lose a river in—though the river is gone, has been gone for epochs, leaving only its ghost in the smoothness of the stone.

Everything here is old. Not old the way a cliff is old—strata and time—but old the way a *finished* thing is old. This landscape has reached its final form. The erosion has done its work completely, uncompromisingly. There are no jagged edges left, no raw faces, no youth. The stone has been worn into shapes that seem almost organic: pillars that bulge like torsos, arches that flow like ribs, hollows that cup the wind the way a throat cups breath. It is the architecture of exhaustion. The mountain has spent itself, and what remains is its skeleton—articulated, beautiful, clean.

And yet—paradox that gives the place its tension—the air moves with manic energy. The turbulence is constant, chaotic, multiplicative. You cannot trust your footing because the wind will not commit to a direction. It curls back on itself, creates pockets of stillness and fists of force mere meters apart. You step from silence into violence into silence again, and the transitions have no boundary you can perceive. The mountain is dead and finished; the atmosphere above it is alive and deranged. Something about the shape of these peaks—their spacing, their depths, their resonant frequencies—has created a standing wave of chaotic air that will persist as long as the stone holds its form. Which is to say: effectively forever.

---

The water comes and goes like memory.

It does not flow here—it *occurs*. Condensation beads on the west-facing stone every afternoon when the warm air from below meets the cold mass of the peak. You can watch it form: first nothing, then a darkening of the rock's surface, then individual droplets that grow with a slowness that feels ceremonial, then—suddenly, collectively—a sheeting. The water runs for an hour, two hours, finding paths carved over millions of years, filling channels that are dry for twenty-two hours and rivers for two. Then it stops. The stone darkens, lightens, dries. By morning, you would not know water had been here at all, except for the mineral streaks—white, pale green, faint rust—that accumulate in the wet lines, painting the mountain with a calligraphy of dissolution.

There are pools in the hollows. Some are deep enough to swim in, if you could stand the cold. They are filled by rain that comes without warning—the sky at this elevation has its own temperament, unrelated to the weather you left below—and they evaporate just as quickly when the sun breaks through. The water is clear and mineral and old. It tastes like the stone it sits on.

---

And then—the green.

It does not belong here. That is your first thought. The elevation is too extreme, the exposure too severe, the soil too scarce. And yet it is *lush*, impossibly so, and this is where the strangeness lives.

The vegetation has found a niche that should not exist: the sound.

Where the mountain's voice is loudest—where the resonant chambers concentrate their howl into specific frequencies—the plants grow thickest. They cluster in the acoustic nodes, the points where standing waves reinforce. You can map the sound by the green. A ridge that should be bare, exposed to the full violence of the wind, is instead draped in vegetation—heavy, waxy, dark-leaved—because the particular geometry of that spot amplifies a frequency the plants require.

They are not like lowland flora. Their leaves are thick and dark, almost black in shadow, a green so deep it reads as bruise-color. They move constantly—not with the wind, but just after it, as if responding to a signal the wind carries. Their roots are visible, gripping the naked stone with a tenacity that seems more like intention than adaptation. And they are silent. In all this deafening roar, the plants make no sound—not the rustle of leaves, not the creak of stems. They absorb it. They are the mountain's mute audience, its congregation, and they have grown fat on its voice.

What pollinates them? What disperses their seed? The fauna is so sparse you might think the place is lifeless above the vegetative line. But occasionally—at dusk, usually, when the sound shifts register—you see them. Flyers. Silhouettes that ride the turbulence with an ease that seems supernatural, banking and curling in the chaotic wind without visible effort. They are large, or the distance is deceiving, and they make no sound you can separate from the mountain's roar. They nest in the highest hollows, where the stone amplifies the highest frequencies. They are the only creatures who seem to belong here completely, and you understand, watching them, that they have been here longer than the vegetation, longer perhaps than the current shape of the peaks. They are the mountain's other voice.

---

You find the structures on the third day.

Or rather—you recognize them. You have been walking past them since you arrived, but they are so eroded, so incorporated into the landscape's final form, that they read as geology. Only the angles give them away: stone does not, on its own, produce right angles, even ancient ones worn to curves by eons of wind.

They are shelters. Half-caves, enhanced. Someone—a people, a person, impossible to tell—came here long ago and found the mountain's natural hollows and deepened them. Chipped stone with stone, a patient and primitive craft. The marks of their tools are still visible, if you touch the walls: tiny consistent divots, the signature of a hand-held chert working granite for a thousand hours. They carved benches into the rock. They cut channels to direct the intermittent water into basins. They built hearths—circles of darker stone, heat-cracked—that face the mountain's throat, positioned to catch the sound and funnel it inward.

Whoever lived here came for the sound.

They built their homes inside the resonators. They slept in the nodes. They arranged their lives around the mountain's voice the way others arrange their lives around rivers or seasons. And then—they left. Or died. Or became something that no longer required shelter. The structures are too eroded to hold a date, too sparse to suggest a civilization. Perhaps there were never more than a handful of them, perhaps only one—a single devotee who spent a lifetime carving a home into the singing stone, and whose home has now nearly rejoined the mountain, another layer of intention absorbed into the ancient final form.

You sit in one of the shelters. The sound enters you. It is too loud to think, and somehow—this is the strangeness—this produces not panic but a profound and animal calm. The noise leaves no room for the self. Your interior monologue drowns. Your anxieties, your identity, your sense of time—all are vibrated apart by the mountain's voice. What remains is something simpler: breath, heartbeat, awareness. You become a listener. You become what the plants are: a body in the node, fed by frequency, holding still.

---

Outside, the wind recombines. The stone endures. The water beads and sheets and evaporates. The dark leaves oscillate in the aftermath of gusts. The flyers spiral in the updrafts, silent and essential. The mountain sings its one long note, the note it has sung since the erosion finished and the resonant chambers were sealed, since the final form was achieved and the only remaining motion was air through stone.

It will sing it for longer than you can imagine. And nothing—not the sparse structures dissolving on its flanks, not the strange green that roots in its voice, not the occasional creature that rides its breath—will alter it. This is the mountain's old age, its completion, its monument: a shape that makes sound, and a sound that makes a place, and a place that makes, of anything that enters it, a temporary and anonymous thing.
