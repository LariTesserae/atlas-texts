The first sensation is not sight, but sound. It is a pressure, a physical weight that presses on the eardrums and vibrates through the soles of your boots. It is the sound of a billion tiny hammers, an incessant staccato taps-taps-tapping that overlays a deeper, resonant hum, like a colossal cello string bowed in a distant, cavernous hall. This is the voice of the world.

The light is dim, a perpetual twilight born of a heavy, pearl-grey sky that weeps a constant, fine-drawn mist. It is not a gloom, but a flat, shadowless illumination that leaches colour from everything, leaving the world in shades of charcoal, slate, and umbral brown. The air is cool and smells of wet stone, ozone, and something vaguely metallic and sharp, like blood drawn from a stone.

You stand on the Floor, a vast, low-elevation plain that stretches to the horizon. It is not flat in the way of a desert or a prairie, but in the way of a thing that has been ground down over eons. The ancient erosion of this place has been not of wind and water in any common sense, but of uncountable cycles of substance and dissolution. The ground underfoot is a composite of shattered, dark minerals—glassy obsidian chunks pitted and softened at the edges, flakes of schist that crumble like chalk, and nodules of some heavy, leaden stone that rings like a bell when struck. There are no plants, not even a lichen or a moss. The fecundity of the world has been turned inward; life找到了 a different path to expression, and it is etched into the very geology of the landscape.

There is no soil, only a damp, gritty aggregate that holds the eternal dampness like a sponge. Water is everywhere, yet it has no rivers or lakes. The mist from above condenses on the surfaces of the rocks and trickles down in minute, thread-like rivulets, vanishing into the porous ground. Elsewhere, water weeps from fissures and cracks in the stone, a slow, steady exudation that never pools but is always absorbed, perpetuating the damp. The atmosphere itself feels thick enough to drink.

And the fauna are abundant. They are the source of the deafening sound, the dynamic life of this mineral world. They are The Tappers. They are creatures of chitin and calcite that look less like animals and more like ambulatory geological instruments. Some are like oversized woodlice carved from basalt, their segmented shells clicking as they move over the debris. Others are long, low-bodied centipedes of glossy shale, their dozens of legs tapping out individual rhythms on the stone. They move ceaselessly, their feeding mechanism a constant, percussive drilling or rasping against the mineral substrate. They are consuming the world, but the world is also producing itself, or so it seems in this stable, end-state ecology. Scattered amongst them are larger, docile grazers that look like animate boulders, their surfaces covered in smaller, symbiotic Tappers that maintain their carapaces, creating a layered, polyrhythmic tapping wherever they rest.

The weirdness of the place lies in the symbiosis between the living and the lithic. Studding the Floor are scattered, primitive structures. They are not built, but grown. Coerced perhaps. They appear like termite mounds forged from slag and fused crystal, their surfaces dark and slick with moisture. They are the work of the Architects, a different kind of creature entirely. They are solitary, rarely seen, beings of articulated silica that move with a slow, deliberate grace. They do not tap. They are silent. Using their spindly, exact arms and simple but effective tools—hammers of dense stone, chisels of impact-hardened glass—they gather specific minerals and induce them to fuse, building their posts, their buttresses, their hollows and hearths. The tech is primitive, but the effect is alien. They seem to be trying to introduce a new frequency into the world's percussive symphony, a vertical line against the immense horizontal. Their structures are sparse and scattered, lonely sentinels rising only a few meters from the Floor, their geometric simplicity a stark contrast to the chaotic rubble of the plain.

The world is active, dynamic in its own ancient way. The Tappers are in constant motion, a living carpet of sound. The ceaseless condensation, the slow weeping of water, the patient building of the Architects—the ecosystem is in perpetual motion, yet it is all motion without progress, a system perfectly balanced at the end of time. The erosion is a fact of history, not an ongoing process; this is the landscape that remains when the mountains have been ground to dust and the seas have been drunk by the stone.

To be here is to feel the immense, indifferent pressure of deep time. The sound is not an assault, but a presence, the sound of the world’s metabolism. The dim light is not a lack, but the world’s chosen complexion. In the vast, sparse openness, the fractured ground underfoot is the only history you need to read. You are a transient vibration in a world that has found its final, deafening, and unending note.
