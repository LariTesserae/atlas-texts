The first sense to return is hearing, not sight. It is a sound that is not a noise, a deep, resonant hum that vibrates through the soles of your feet and up into your teeth. It is the sound of a billion tiny heartbeats, of a billion legs rubbing against carapace, the susurrus of a trillion wings shivering in the perpetual dim light. It is the sound of life so dense it has become a geophysical force.

You are standing on the Floor of the Great Chitin.

The air is cool and smells of wet earth, fungus, and something vaguely like cinnamon and ozone. It’s a damp cool, a cold sink where the humidity is a constant presence. Water is not a river or a rain, but a feeling. It collects on every surface in slick, oily beads that run down vertical faces and pool in depressions on the ground. It condenses on the immense pillars that hold up the sky, tracing glistening, slow-moving paths through the dust. The light is like a twilight caught in amber, a diffuse glow that seems to emanate from the air itself, granting no source and casting few shadows. It is just bright enough to see by, a world held in a state of permanent, luminous dusk.

And what a world it is. You are below surface, in a place that could be the inside of a planet, a cavern of continental scale. The "ground" is not soil, but a flattened, pressed layer of cast-off exoskeletons, fragments of shells, dried wings, and the compacted dust of a million generations, all fused into a brittle, crunchy pavement. In patches, where the condensation is thicker, a leathery, dark moss grows, the only vegetation in this vastness, clinging to the remains of the dead. It is sparse, a dark green scatter like forgotten shreds of cloth across the tan and caramel floor.

The defining features are the Pillars. They are not stone or wood, but bone-white, organic towers that stretch up until their forms are lost in the gloom high above. They are young, newly emerged from the floor, their sides still sharp and fluted, none yet smoothed by the slow grinding of ages. They are the work of immense, blind things that pushed them up from the deeps, their growth leaving fresh, damp fissures in the floor from which the strange, earthy smell rises. Some are thin as ancient trees, others as broad as castles. They stand in a sparse, deliberate scattering, giving the space its immense, open feeling. You could walk for days between them, a constant, silent presence in the corners of your eye.

But the pillars are not the occupants. They are the architecture.

The life is overwhelming. It is the hum given form. The floor shifts with a constant, crawling carpet of things. There are beetle-like creatures the size of your thumb, their iridescent shells catching the dim light like drops of oil. There are legged worms that leave shimmering trails of slime, moving in slow, peristaltic rhythms. There are flies with wings of stretched membrane that swarm in thick, wavening columns, their buzzing a sharp counterpoint to the deep hum.

And there are the Shepherds. Ten-legged things the size of dogs, with bodies of overlapping plates like armored trilobites and long, delicate antennae that sweep the air. They move through the lesser fauna not as predators, but as gardeners. They nudge and herd the swarms, their legs making soft, clicking sounds on the chitin floor. They clear paths with their mandibles, not eating the debris but moving it, creating the broad, clean avenues that crisscross the cavern.

This is a machine of life, but a primitive, non-technological one. There are no tools of metal, no constructed shelters. The Shepherds are the built environment and the technicians, their behavior an inherited instinct that shapes this world. They guide the swarms to the pillars where the condensation is sweetest. The sound never ceases. It is a symphony with no conductor and no end. The clicking of Shepherd legs, the soft rustle of a million bodies passing over a million more, the drone of the swarms, the faint, almost sub-audible groan of the pillars as they settle into their newness. It is loud enough that you feel it as pressure, a blanket of vibration that muffles any attempt at speech. To communicate is to touch, to tap a rhythm on a companion's shell.

Life here is at a perfect, terrifying equilibrium. There is no day or night to mark the passage of time. The hum is constant, the dim light unchanging. There is no season, no flood, no famine. The Great Chitin simply is, a continent of untouched, constructed life humming forever in the cool, damp dark. It is a world that has never known change, a system so complete that it no longer has a concept of a beginning or an end. It is a frozen moment of life, not in death, but in the absolute fullness of its being. You are a foreign cell in a body that has been perfect and self-sufficient since before you were born, and your only purpose here is to listen.
