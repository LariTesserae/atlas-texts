The first sound is a grind, wet and unceasing.

It is the sound of teeth on bone, if teeth were stone and bone was also stone, and both were slick with a persistent, oily dampness. This sound is the foundational note of the world, a bass drone against which all other noises are brief, sharp shrieks. It comes from everywhere and nowhere at once, vibrating up through the soles of your feet if you were foolish enough to stand still.

There is no ground, not as one understands it. There is only scaffolding. An endless, convoluted, dense lattice of some dark, pitted material—part iron, part slate, part petrified wood—stretches in all directions. It is a total construction, a world made entirely of the mundane. The tech is primitive, evident in the crude joinery: straps of leather-tough gut tying immense beams together, massive pegs of bone-like material hammered through crooked holes, the occasional smear of a binding agent like cooled tar. Nothing is machined. Everything is shaped, cut, and placed by hand, or claw, or something altogether other. The structure is embryonic, forever on the verge of becoming a true architecture, a city, a floor, but it is trapped in a state of perpetual, chaotic emergence.

You are in the marrow of a building that has become a geology.

The scale is deceptive. A glance suggests a vast, single cavern, open and regional. But as you adjust to the perpetual twilight, you see the depth. The lattice-work of beams, platforms, and buttresses recedes into layers of absolute darkness. It is less a space and more a fractal density, a place where the concept of “air” has been replaced by a series of narrow corridors and grand, vaulted shafts, all tangled together. It is a forest of dead, man-made trees, growing down as much as up.

Coolness is the rule here. The air is chilled, like a deep cellar, and it smells of wet rust, rotting linen, and the faint, acrid bite of ozone. The dampness is a constant, a sheen on every horizontal surface. It isn’t rain, but a perpetual condensation that sweats from the structure itself, gathering in shallow pools of brackish, greasy water. In places, these pools have accumulated for millennia, forming silent, black lakes that reflect nothing. To touch the water is to feel a heavy, syrupy cold that seems to leech heat directly from the bone.

There are no plants. Vegetation is a forgotten dream. In their place is a slow, patient life. The beams and platforms are coated in thick, yielding carpets of what might be a lichen or a coral, but mineral. It feels like dense, cold foam, and its color is a flat, light-absorbing grey. Underfoot, it is soft and yielding, but it muffles sound poorly, often amplifying the grind into a wet squelch. In the drier passages, this fuzz gives way to crystalline growths: hollow, needle-like spurs of what looks like quartz, but they grow in spirals and concentric rings, humming with a low electrical tension. If you break one, it glows with a brief, sad blue light before crumbling to powder.

Light is a precious and violent commodity. The dominant source is bioluminescent, but it is rare. The fauna of this place are few, but they are the lamps. They drift through the dense architecture, beings of impossible geometry. One might be a tetrahedron of chitin, constantly revolving, humming a discordant chord that sets your teeth on edge, each face glowing with a soft, yellow light. Another is a long, ribbon-like creature, like a puppet’s ribbon cut from sheet-lead, that undulates silently through the air, pulsing with a cold, lavender luminescence. They are the only mobile things, the rare life that navigates the deeps.

Their presence makes the noisecape a constantly shifting tapestry. The foundational grind of the shifting structure is punctuated by the sharp, percussive cracks of a beam settling under a new weight. This is followed by the thin, crystalline tinkle of a thousand quartz-spurs shattering in sympathy. Then, the hum of a passing light-source rises and fades, and in its wake, a new sound emerges from the darkness: a skittering, the scrabble of many tiny, hard-pointed legs on the metal-grit fungus. This is the sound of the cleaners. They are not light, but they are life: swarms of pale, crab-like creatures, no larger than a thumb, that scavenge the detritus of the world. They are almost always there, just out of sight, a constant rustling that speaks of a hidden, teeming population in the places you cannot see.

The weirdness is in the rules of this place. Time is not a straight line. The archaeology is inverted. You don’t find older layers the deeper you go; you find them happening concurrently. A beam ahead of you, slick with damp and overgrown with the grey foam, will suddenly groan and splinter as a massive, gut-bound rope snaps taut from above, hoisting a fresh, newly-carved timber—identical to every other timber—into a new position nearby. The world is being built and retroactively installed at the same moment. This is the dynamic nature of the place: an eternal, chaotic construction site with no builders in evidence. The structures do not erode in the traditional sense; they are either discarded or re-incorporated. A collapsed platform doesn't fall to a floor below; it is slowly consumed by the cleaner-crabs, its bound leather and bone-peg joints unraveled, its material vanishing back into the inscrutable mechanism of the world as if it were a mistake being un-made.

To witness an event is to understand its horror. You stand on a wide, stable-looking deck. In the distance, you hear the approaching thing before you see it: a low-frequency thrum that shakes the foam at your feet and makes the water in the pools ripple. A vast, slow shape detaches itself from the upper darkness. It is not a creature, but a machine of such a scale it becomes a geology. A massive, entirely wheel-less assembly of bone and compressed wood, rolling not on ground, but on a path of newly laid beams that appear beneath it from unseen mechanisms in the darkness above and below. It rumbles past, its slow passage grinding whole sections of the lattice into powder. As it moves, you hear the characteristic sharp crack and fall of a platform a hundred meters away. It has not yet fallen. The sound of its collapse arrives first, a ghost of an event traveling ahead of its cause. You look, and the platform is still there, perfectly intact. Then, minutes later, with a sound that is almost anticlimactic, it simply gives up, its supports turning to dust, and it tumbles into the blackness.

This is a world built on a paradox of precedence. The sound is the prophecy, and the event is the memory. You live in the echo, a permanent resident in the aftermath of a present that has not yet occurred. And through it all, the wet, patient grinding continues, the sound of a world that is forever, and has never yet, begun.
