The altitude here does not belong to the world of the breathing. You stand on a plateau of such extreme elevation that the sky is not a dome but a black membrane, pressing close, thick with the static of a cosmos unfiltered by atmosphere. It is cool—not the cool of autumn air, but the sterile, radiational chill of a place that has long since bled away its thermal memory. The cold does not bite; it simply is, seeping through the soles of your feet like a quiet fact.

There is no water. Not a trace. Not a vapor, not a frost, not a molecule hidden in a micro-fissure. The land is absolutely desiccated—a mineral wasteland that has been baked by eons of vacuum and cosmic cold into something harder than stone. The ground beneath your feet is a vast, continuous plain of laminated silica and compressed oxidates, swept flat by an erosion so ancient it finished its work before the concept of "geology" was young. The mountains that once stood here were ground down to nubs a billion years ago, and those nubs were worn to this infinite, faintly undulating hardpan. This is the end-state of terrain: the final, immutable geometry of a world that has settled into its eternal sleep. Nothing moves. Nothing grows. The wind does not blow. Time here does not pass; it merely sits.

And yet, the density is unbearable.

The silence is total, a physical pressure against the eardrums, but the *presence* is deafening. The landscape is packed. Every cubic meter of this dead, flat expanse is saturated with life—or something that has replaced life. They are the fauna: a teeming, overwhelming biomass of creatures that do not respect the rules of a dry, frozen world. 

They are huddled everywhere. The plain is a living carpet. Millions—billions—of them, pressed flank to flank, carapace to carapace, stacked in shallow depressions, draped over the low ridges. They are eyeless, legless things resembling vast, turgid grubs or pale, fibrous pods, their skins a translucent grey through which slow, dark fluids can be seen. They do not eat, for there is nothing to eat. They do not drink, for there is no water. They simply *are*, packed so tightly that the ground beneath them is hidden, a seething, static ocean of mute flesh. They respire in a frequency too low to hear, a sub-audible hum of mass existence that you feel in your teeth rather than your ears.

The weirdness seeps in at the edges of your vision. Reality here has grown thin and exhausted, stretched to breaking by the sheer paradox of this ecosystem. You look down at a cluster of the pale pods, and for a moment, you see through them—not to the ground, but to a different arrangement of the sky. The horizon flickers, stuttering between a sharp line and a smeared blur of charcoal and bruised violet. Gravity feels like a suggestion you are slowly refusing; your boots barely touch the ground, held down by a force that seems more like habit than physics. The creatures, too, are half-unraveled. Some of them lack a back end, their bodies trailing off into a fuzz of static, their forms repeating like a broken record—a segment of grey flesh looping infinitely into itself. They do not bleed where they are cut by the fracturing of space; they simply cease, the cross-section as smooth and dry as the ancient hardpan.

Amidst this overwhelming, breathing carpet of paradox, the sparse vegetation stands like monuments to an alien physics. They are not plants. They are crystalline growths—spindly, jagged things of black glass and pale carbon lace, thrusting up from the packed flesh of the fauna at erratic intervals. They do not root in the soil; they root in the creatures, drawing whatever unimaginable sustenance flows through those slow, dark veins. They are leafless, branching into the dead air like frozen lightning, their surfaces etched with fractal grooves that seem to rotate if you stare at them too long. They are sparse, perhaps one every hundred meters, but they command the eye, standing as the only vertical interruptions in an otherwise flattened, horizontal infinity.

And then, the structures. They are scattered across the plain—few, faint, and utterly pathetic against the primordial vastness. They are cairns. Rough, hand-stacked piles of the native laminated silica, no taller than a man’s knee. They are marked with the crudest of tools: scratches from sharpened rocks, grooves worn by desperate, manual labor. The tech that made them was pre-metal, pre-logic, pre-hope. Just the basic, brute-force assertion of a maker: *I was here*. 

But the cairns are failing. They are being subsumed. The pale, legless creatures have crowded over and around them, their slow, immense pressure pressing the stones apart. Some cairns have collapsed entirely, their rocks now resting in the shallow valleys of grubs, slowly being crushed into dust by the sheer, patient weight of the biomass. Others still stand, but only half-existing, their right halves faded into the same spatial static that eats the edges of the fauna.

This is a world in frozen equilibrium, a terminal state where the erosion is complete, the water is gone, and the physics are failing. Yet the flesh endures. The silence screams with the closeness of a million silent bodies. You take a step, and your foot finds no purchase on the dry ground, only the turgid, unyielding squeeze of the pale things, shifting by millimeters, an infinite, dry tide that has not moved for a million years, and will not move for a million more.
