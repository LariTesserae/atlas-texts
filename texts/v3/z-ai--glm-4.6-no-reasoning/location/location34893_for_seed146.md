A mineral continent suspended in the mantle of an unrecognizable world, suspended in perpetual warmth and absolute dark.

This is a place of subterranean vastness—a hollowchamber of planetary scale. Overhead, there is no sky, only a ceiling of massive, pressure-fused peridotite that arches kilometers above the highest ridge, sealing the space away from the vacuum or surface of whatever planet holds it. The warmth here (2.1) is not the warmth of sunlit soil; it is the deep, even, congenital heat of a planetary interior, a steady state that pervades every cubic meter of the dark. It is a warmth without gradient, without source, without variation—a thermal bath that never cools and never intensifies (0.3 dynamic). Time has no thermodynamic meaning here. Nothing freezes, nothing melts, nothing erodes in any recognizable way. The stone does not sharpen into peaks or round into hills; it simply exists, an unchanging geometry of mineral pressure.

The landscape (0.2 elevation) is not a surface but a volume—a dense, near-touching arrangement (1.6 density) of monumental forms packed together in the dark. Great blades of volcanic glass—obsidian extruded in sheets kilometers long and hundreds of meters thick—stand at angles that seem impossible, held rigid by the immense lithostatic pressure of the surrounding stone. Between them, the space is filled with clusters of columnar basalt, pyroxene crystals the size of cathedrals, and formations of olivine that have grown—not eroded—into interlocking lattices, a crystal scaffolding that fills the void like a three-dimensional lattice. The density is such that movement requires navigation: squeezing between crystal faces, descending crevices whose walls are within arm's reach on either side, always pressing deeper into the mineral matrix. There is no "ground" in the horizontal sense—only an endless descent through leaning, overlapping, intergrown stone.

Water is absent entirely (0.5). There are no aquifers, no veins of hydrothermal fluid, no moisture in the atmosphere—which itself is barely worthy of the name. The air is a supercritical vapor of carbon dioxide and sulfur compounds, dense and warm, breathing slowly through the crystal lattice like a fluid rather than a gas. It carries no scent of organic decay, no mineral tang of rain-on-stone—only the flat, inert presence of a chemistry that never involved water. Stones are dry to the point of abstraction. There is no dissolution, no hydration, no oxidation as we understand it. The minerals here are as they were at their crystallization—unweathered, unaltered, primordial.

And yet this is not a dead space. Life is here—abundant, teeming, strange (1.6 fauna, 1.9 weirdness). But it is life that never considered photosynthesis, never built on the scaffolding of cellulose or bone. The fauna are mineral organisms—endolithic grazers that subsist on the piezoelectric currents that flow through the crystal lattices under pressure. They are warm to the touch, these creatures, their metabolisms tuned to the ambient heat. In the absolute dark, they navigate by the faint triboluminescent sparks that occur when crystal faces shift against each other by fractions of a millimeter—flashes of blue-white light that illuminate the adjacent stone for a microsecond, long enough for a sensor to register, far too brief for any eye that requires persistence of vision.

The largest of them are called (by whatever nomenclature applies to things unobserved by surface minds) *lithic mantids*—though they bear only structural resemblance to the insects of bright worlds. Their bodies are articulated plates of hematite and magnetite, grown rather than shed, expanding with each metabolic cycle as they absorb trace iron from the surrounding stone. They are the size of human hands, of human torsos, of small boats—the scale varies endlessly, for they do not die by senescence. A lithic mantis that has grown to the size of a car has been feeding on the same crystal face for millennia, its slow abrasion of the stone balanced by the stone's equally slow crystal growth. They cling to the obsidian sheets in densities that make the dark seem to ripple—their legs, thin as pencils, extending in geometric profusion from their flat, wide bodies, tapping the stone in sequences that produce the only sound this place knows.

Sound (0.9). Not silence—there is no silence in a place this dense with presences—but something adjacent to it. The tapping of millions of chitinous-mineral legs on crystal surfaces produces a susurrus so pervasive it registers not as sound but as a texture of the air, a vibration felt in the chest before it is heard by any auditory organ. It is the sound of a continent of slow, patient abrasion. Occasionally, a larger creature shifts its weight, and a deep, resonant clicking echoes through the crystal lattice—a sound that travels through the solid stone faster and farther than through the warm vapor, arriving as a pulse of vibration before the air-carried sound reaches the listener. The overall effect is of a quiet that hums, a silence with a pulse.

There are marks here that are not natural (1.0 built, 1.4 tech). Cut into certain of the larger obsidian sheets—where the glass is clearest and most geometrically perfect—are patterns of deliberate fracture. Flakes have been struck away in sequences that produce shallow grooves, marks that catch the triboluminescent flashes and redirect them, concentrating the momentary light into brief, directed pulses. The work is primitive—the stone was not shaped by anything more sophisticated than another' stone, struck with precision and patience. But the intention is unmistakable. These are signals. Or recordings. Or prayers to something that perceives in microsecond flashes.

The builders are gone, or have become indistinguishable from the stone. But their artifacts remain: cairns of specific mineral composition, placed at intersections of crystal lattices where the piezoelectric current is strongest. Hollows scraped into the faces of basalt columns, just large enough for a lithic mantis to shelter in—whether these are traps or offerings is unclear. Lines of hematite pebbles, magnetic, arranged in patterns that shift subtly when a large organism passes nearby, the stones rotating on their axes in response to the creature's magnetic field.

This is a world in equilibrium (0.3 dynamic)—not the equilibrium of death, but of a system so perfectly balanced that it has no impetus to change. The crystals grow at the rate they are grazed. The creatures feed at the rate the piezoelectric current sustCains them. The warmth8 never varies. The dark never lifts. And in this balance, the strange ecosystem persists: mineral grazers and their mineral predators (things that move faster, that strike with concussive force, that shatter and consume the hematite shells of their prey), all of them living at a pace that makes a century seem like a breath, all of them invisible to any sense that requires light.

To be here is to be suspended in the mineral metabolism of a world that never knew water, never saw the sun, never experienced a season. The warmth presses in from all directions. The dark is absolute and familiar. The quiet tapping of countless legs on countless crystals is the only proof that time is passing at all. And somewhere in the lattice—in the deepest, densest interstices where the crystal faces nearly touch—the faint marks of primitive tool-users persist, waiting for the next flash of triboluminescence to make themselves known.
