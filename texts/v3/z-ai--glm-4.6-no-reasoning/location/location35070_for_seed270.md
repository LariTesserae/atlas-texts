You descend into it through a seam in the floor of the world—a sliver of a gap between two vast, dormant plates of rusted iron that pushes open just wide enough to admit a body. Inside, the space opens up, but only relatively. This is a pocket: a shallow, sprawling hollow barely thirty meters across at its widest, and nowhere more than four meters high. The elevation is negligible, a dimple in the planet's crust, yet it feels utterly removed from the surface above. 

There is no water here. Not a droplet of condensation on the walls, not a trace of moisture in the air. It is a sealed, desiccated void. The atmosphere hangs cool and thin, smelling of ozone, heated metal, and the sharp, static tang of crushed sulfur. What little illumination there is comes from normal light—flat, diffuse, and pale—filtering down through the sliver above, supplemented by the sickly, intermittent amber glow of exposed filaments and sparking coils embedded in the walls. 

The terrain is the end result of infinite time. Erosis has done all it can ever do here. This is a primordial flatness; the walls and floor are worn perfectly smooth, as if by a billion years of patient, unseen friction. There are no jagged edges, no fresh breaks—only the deep, rounded grooves of a world settled into its final, immutable shape. The materials are purely mineral and industrial: vast sheets of petrified iron, veins of cracked ceramic, and sprawling, flat plains of vitrified slag that look like frozen, dirty puddles of glass. 

Scattered across this ancient floor are the faint marks of construction—partial remains, occasional artifacts. A lone, decapitated smokestack, sawed off flush with the ceiling. A cluster of copper pipes, corroded into a single, green-veined mass. A massive flywheel half-buried in the slag, its teeth worn down to mere suggestions of geometry. These are relics of an industrial past, scaled for giants, now reduced to faint graffiti on the ancient stone. 

But the space is not empty. It is, impossibly, overflowing. The density is staggering, a near-touching saturation of life that defies the absolute absence of water or vegetation. There are no plants—no roots, no leaves, no green. Instead, the ecology is fungal, chitinous, and mechanized. 

The fauna is overwhelming. The floor moves. The walls crawl. The ceiling swarms. 

They are rust-mites the size of your fist, pale and eyeless, their carapaces the exact matte red of the oxidized iron. They move in dense, interlocking carpets, millions of them, their legs clicking and scraping as they graze on the microscopic metallic dust. Between them burrow larger things—flat, slug-like organisms of exposed wire and synthetic sinew, dripping with black, non-aqueous lubricant that instantly vaporizes into the cool air. They do not drink; they absorb minerals directly, breathing in the sulfuric gas, metabolizing the static electricity that bleeds from the exposed coils above. 

The sound is a constant, deafening roar. Not a roar of wind or water, but the aggregated roar of biology at an unimaginable scale. It is the sound of a trillion chitinous legs scraping against glass-slag. It is the wet, pneumatic hiss of the wire-slugs exhaling vaporized oil. It is the subsonic thud of the rust-mites colliding with one another in the dark, a rolling, unbroken thunder that vibrates in your back teeth and makes the smooth floor hum. 

And it is active. Predators—tall, spindly things made of jointed scrap and calcified wire—stalk through the living carpet, plucking up mites with scissor-mandibles that snap with a sound like breaking cables. The whole pocket is a sealed engine of consumption and motion, a mechanical stomach digesting the ruins of the old industry. 

The weirdness of the place is subtle, sinking into you the longer you crouch there. It is the realization that these creatures are not living in the ruins; they *are* the ruins. The iron walls themselves are composed of compressed, generations-dead carapaces. The slag is the fossilized excrement of a trillion ancestors. You are standing inside the gut of a dead machine, being digested by its descendants, in a pocket of the world where water never existed and life simply chose to eat the iron instead.
