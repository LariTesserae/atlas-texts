The ground itself is the first thing you notice. It is not stone, not soil, but a kind of dense, dark ceramic, fired to a slick, glassy blackness in some forgotten, planetary kiln. It’s named Obsidian-Grit, and it makes up everything: the foundations of the structures, the paved ways between them, the very bedrock of this small, packed place. The scale is intimate, not vast enough to be a city, perhaps the size of a fortified hamlet, but the density is overwhelming. There are no empty fields, no sprawling gardens, no gaps between the crammed-together buildings. It is a puzzle fitted with no space for air, and yet, the air is what defines it.

The place is perpetually, unnervingly damp. A cool, heavy mist hangs low, hugging the sharp corners of the rooftops and clinging to the sheer faces of the walls. Water is not a visitor here; it is the resident. This is not the gentle moisture of a temperate forest, but the ceaseless weeping of a world that cannot hold its shape. The water beads on the ceramic ground, collecting in the hairline cracks that have begun to web across the otherwise pristine surfaces—a sign of the landscape’s youth, its recent emergence. The formations are still sharp, the edges still clean, as if the whole settlement were cast only a generation ago and has been slowly, stubbornly softening in the humid atmosphere ever since.

Vegetation is a footnote, a desperate afterthought. There are no trees, no grass. In the narrowest alleys, where the shadows are deepest and the moisture constant, a few tenacious things cling to life. They are not green, but pale, ghostly white. Thin, fleshy stalks, no thicker than a needle, push up through the cracks in the ceramic. They bear no leaves, only clusters of tiny, translucent nodules that look like pearls of condensed fog. They are called Mist-Marrow, and they are the only plant life known here. They do not photosynthesize; they seem to drink the very air, their flesh cold and damp to the touch.

The buildings are squat and windowless, crafted from the same Obsidian-Grit as the ground. They are primitive in technique but ambitious in design. No metal tools were used in their making; their surfaces bear the subtle grooves and planes of being shaped by hand, rubbed smooth with river-stones and abrasives. Roofs are steep, almost conical, designed to shed the constant condensation. The architecture is layered, a hallmark of the urban parameter. New structures are not built beside old ones, but *atop* and *against* them, creating a chaotic, terraced skyline where a veranda from one building might form the floor for another. The result is a multi-level maze, interconnected by narrow, slick-paved stairways and low-slung archways that are forever dripping. The sound inside this labyrinth is mostly quiet, broken by the gentle, rhythmic *plink... plink... plink...* of water finding its way from the eaves to the ground, a sound so constant it becomes a form of silence.

People, or their remnant presence, is felt but not heard. The buildings show the wear of hands—parts of the ceramic walls worn to a dull smoothness where countless shoulders have brushed past. There are simple, carved symbols inset into the doorframes: whorls and spirals that seem to mimic the patterns of water draining in a basin. There are no doors, only heavy, woven fabric curtains dark with damp that hang limp in the still, cool air. The smell is of wet wool, wet stone, and something else—a faint, electrical tang, like the air after a lightning strike, that emanates from the fabric of the place itself.

That tang is the core of the weirdness. The Obsidian-Grit is not inert. When the condensation is heavy, especially after a rare but furious downpour that seems to boil from the sky rather than fall, the ground and the buildings hum. It’s a low-frequency thrum you feel in your teeth as much as you hear. This is the dynamic process, the ecosystem in motion. The ceramic is dielectric, a vast, slow-moving natural battery. The weeping stones, the constant dripping water, are not water, not entirely. They are a weak, ionic solution, a form of naturally occurring electrolyte. The entire hamlet is a primitive, geological circuit.

Fauna here is rare because it must be adapted to this strange electromagnetic ecology. The only known creature is the Skitter-Lamp. It is a small, amorphous being, six-legged and translucent, about the size of a human thumb. It moves with a speed that makes it a blur, and it feeds not on the Mist-Marrow, but on the latent charge in the weeping stone. As it consumes this energy, its body begins to glow with a soft, internal light, a pale green-blue luminescence. They are the only source of light after the sky grows dark, bobbing and zipping through the dripping alleys like will-o'-the-wisps. They are utterly silent and harmless, avoidant of touch, disappearing into cracks in the ceramic when approached.

The rules of this place are unwritten but absolute. You do not wear shoes with leather soles; they conduct too much of the ground’s energy. You do not linger in the low places during a condensation storm; the hum can become disorienting, even painful. You harvest Mist-Marrow only at dawn, when its pearls are fullest of the night’s moisture and have the most flavor—a sharp, clean taste like cool mountain air. You do not harm the Skitter-Lamps; they are the omen that the great circuit is active, that the settlement is breathing. To find one glowing is a sign of balance; to find them all dark is a sign of a coming stillness, a warning that the high-pitched hum that precedes the storms is about to fade into a dangerous silence. This is a small, dense, young world, a wet and dark ceramic chamber built on a living, humming foundation, forever caught in the act of weeping light.
