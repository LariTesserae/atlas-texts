There is no water here. Not even the memory of it. The geology long ago surrendered its oceans and its ice, baked dry by an ancient, ambient warmth that still permeates the bedrock like a low, perpetual fever. 

The terrain is nearly flattened, ground down by eons of a process that was less like erosion and more like entropy yielding to a final, stable state. What remains is a sprawling, regional expanse of desaturated mineral: plains of fused silica and pulverized red-grey feldspar, featureless for leagues, interrupted only by the urban geometry that rises from the dust.

It is a city—or the fossil of one. The construction is organized, layered, repeating across the mid-range scale with the rigid logic of infrastructure, but it is entirely devoid of life. This is a built environment of industrial origin: vast, multi-tiered processing stacks, angular distillation towers, and sprawling conduit networks that march across the horizon in straight lines, indifferent to the minor undulations of the dead land. The architecture is scaled for production, not habitation. The doorways, if they exist, are sealed hatches; the windows are dark glass. There are no habitable volumes, only pressure vessels and reaction chambers. 

Everything is constructed from a dry-laid ceramic and a strange, dark alloy that has never known rust. Without water, there is no oxidation. The metal retains the matte, stamped finish it had the day it was extruded. The warm temperature—unusual for a place so high and so dark—emanates from within the structures themselves. The city is a thermal engine suspended at the exact point of equilibrium. It generates exactly as much heat as it loses, running a closed-loop alchemy of non-aqueous solvents and mineral slurries that never boil and never cool. The pipes are warm to the touch, thrumming with a subsonic, periodic resonance that takes several minutes to complete a single cycle. 

The darkness is profound, but it is not empty. There are no stars above; the sky is a solid, lightless ceiling of high-altitude particulate, an inversion layer that traps the city's waste heat and seals it beneath a dome of permanent, opaque dusk. Down at the scale of human navigation, the only illumination comes from industrial phosphorescence—faint, amber-green indicator lights spaced hundreds of meters apart on the gangways and valve stations. They blink in slow, synchronized unison, casting no shadows, barely sufficient to reveal the texture of the grit underfoot. In the deeper basins between the refinery towers, the dark is absolute, a physical medium that seems to absorb the sound of your breath.

And it is so quiet. The silence is not an absence of noise, but a heavy, localized dampening. The acoustic properties of the dry, compacted silica and the sound-deadening alloys swallow all reverberation. There is no wind to whistle through the superstructure. The only sound is the mechanical pulse of the city itself—a low, respiratory huff of pressurized gas moving through subterranean mains, so deep and low it is felt in the teeth before it reaches the ear.

The ecology, if it can be called that, is minimal and entirely parasitic. There is no vegetation, no sprout or spore, nothing that photosynthesizes or decays. But the city is not entirely sterile. Fauna exists here, though it borders on the mechanical. Rare, sightless scavengers traverse the wide, empty avenues between the processing plants. They are arthropodal things of lamination and chitin—the size of large dogs, moving on a multitude of silent, articulated legs. They feed not on flesh, but on the waste heat and stray voltage of the city. They navigate the dark by sensing micro-fluctuations in the ambient magnetic fields, plugging their needle-like proboscises into the warm exhaust vents to siphon the trace chemical slurry that bleeds from the pressure seals. They are born in the pipe-chases, live in the dark, and die crumbling into the same fine, dry powder that coats the ground. 

The weirdness of this place is found in its absolute stasis. This is a frozen equilibrium. Nothing is growing. Nothing is declining. The machines do not manufacture anything that leaves the city; they merely maintain themselves, a self-licking lollipop of industrial purpose, running because they were built to run, refining the refined, venting the vented. The dynamic is at zero. The warm temperature never fluctuates. The faint green lights never change their timing. The sky never lightens. 

Standing in the sparse, open gaps between the monolithic still-works, you are struck by the terrifying steadiness of it all. This is the end of a timeline—not a catastrophic end, but an asymptotic one. The world has settled into its final form: a warm, dark, dry engine, ticking over in the void, waiting for a heat death that its own perfect efficiency indefinitely postpones.
