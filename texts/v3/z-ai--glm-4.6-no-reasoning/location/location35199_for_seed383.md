The first sensation is not sight, but the slickness underfoot. The floor is a hard, dark stone, polished by some long-forgotten hand to a dull, obsidian sheen. It is cool, perpetually so, and beaded with a film of moisture that is neither sweat nor dew. It simply *is*, a constant presence that makes every step a silent, careful negotiation. This is the floor of the Sunken City, though "sunken" implies a sea that never existed. It is more accurate to say the world rose around it, leaving it in a basin, a geological scar.

The light comes from above, though not like sunlight. It is a sourceless, effervescent brightness that fills the vast, open air of the chasm, making the shadows dense and sharply defined. The glare on the wet stone is constant and blinding if you look at it directly. Up, far above, is the chasm's maw, a jagged circle of stark white rock, bleached by the unceasing radiance. It is so high it seems more like a concept than a physical entrance.

This is a mineral world. There are no trees, no grass, no moss to soften the ruinous geometry. The only form of life that clings to the stone is a crust of what you might first mistake for lichen. It grows in branching, crystalline patterns, fractal veins of dusty rose and deep indigo that creep across the building facades. To touch it is to feel a faint, resonant hum. It is not a plant; it is a slow-burning, mineral fire. It consumes the rock with glacial patience, and in the process, it weeps the strange, oily dampness that coats everything.

The city itself stretches out on a scale that beggars the mind. It is a valley of architecture, built from the same dark, glassy stone as the floor. Towers the size of mountains soar up towards the light, their surfaces featureless save for the creeping crystalline growth. Bridges, impossibly slender and delicate, arc between them, spanning distances that would make an earthbound engineer weep. The construction is clearly organized, a planned metropolis of straight avenues and grand plazas, yet it is all built to a primitive, brutal logic. There are no intricate carvings, no windows, no ornamentation. It is the work of a people who understood compression and mass, who could decisively cleave and erect, but had not yet learned to embellish. The join between two colossal blocks is a perfect, hair-thin line, but it is a line of pure function. This is primitive tech applied with godlike scale.

And this immense, silent stage is empty, save for the engines.

They are the only moving things, and they are abundant. The Silence of the city is a relative term; it is merely the hush between the grinding, colossal movements of the fauna. They are not animals in any recognized sense. They are the city's maintenance crew, its immune system. They are machine-biology, living mechanisms.

Some are vast. You feel one before you see it: a low-frequency thrumming that shakes the moisture from the crystalline crust. It is a Maw, a creature the size of a house that moves on hundreds of articulated, chitonous legs. Its body is a smooth, ovoid shell of the same black stone, and it progresses solemnly down a wide avenue, its only feature a circular, tooth-rimmed mouth that opens directly onto the ground. It lowers its head and the street surface, the very stone of the city, begins to flow like thick tar, up and into its gullet. It eats dereliction. It consumes the slow, patient erosion of time, erases the infinitesimal settling of a wall, and leaves behind a surface that is new, fresh, perfectly flat. It is a turbulent force of preservation, a chaotic, violent act of renewal. The sound is a deafening, guttural suck, a roar of rock and pressure.

Others are smaller, more numerous. Skitterers, like metallic centipedes made of interlocking plates of polished bone, swarm up the sides of towers, their countless legs tapping a frantic, ceaseless rhythm on the stone. They are cleaners, polishing the surfaces, scraping away the beginnings of the crystalline blight, their das of work leaving behind dark, clean scars that quickly heal over with the creeping, mineral life. They build nothing. They only maintain the stasis, a final, perfect form, fighting a war against the inevitable encroachment of age and life.

The air, despite the dynamism, is stale. It smells of ozone, wet stone, and a thin, acrid tang, like a battery that has been charging for a thousand years. This place is a closed system, an ancient engine settling into its final, most stable state of turmoil.

The rule here is one of violent equilibrium. The city was built to last forever, and to do so, it could not be allowed to change. The landscape is ancient, not static, but actively so. The primitive builders, in their final act of creation, engineered a perpetual loop of destruction and preservation. The crystalline life is the slow entropy, the rust of this world. The mechanical fauna are the anti-entropy, a hyperactive, turbulent force that grinds and polishes and consumes to keep everything exactly as it was. It is a city that has achieved the end of life by refusing to die, instead choosing an endless, gnawing, and noisy immortality.

You stand on the polished, wet stone, a lone point of stillness in the vastness. From a plaza half a mile away, a new Maw emerges from a sprawling, multi-leveled structure of ramps and platforms: its roost. It begins its slow, grinding journey, its rhythmic footsteps like the heartbeat of the world. A faint spray of atomized rock mist hangs in its wake. The light from above is unforgiving, absolute. There is no shelter. There is only the sheer, open scale of the place, the distance between yourself and the next moving, breathing engine, and the knowledge that you are a foreign variable in an equation that has been solving itself, with brutal efficiency, since the world was young.
