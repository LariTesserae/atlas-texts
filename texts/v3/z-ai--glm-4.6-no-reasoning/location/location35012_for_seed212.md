The first sense to return is sound. It is not a sound so much as the absence of silence, a constant, roiling presence that presses in on the mind like a physical weight. It has no single source. It is the shuddering groan of impossibly ancient wood fibers under stress, the wet tearing of a billion leaves ripping themselves free from their stems, the percussive crack of cellulose splintering, and beneath it all, a low-frequency hum that seems to vibrate up from the very ground through the soles of the feet. It is a deafening symphony of growth and destruction, played every second of every day.

To see this place is to understand the sound. There is no ground, not in the sense of earth or stone. The substrate is a slurry of living matter: a thick, wet, fibrous mash of decomposing and recomposing plant life. It is a warm, spongy, ochre-colored porridge that yields underfoot with a sigh and releases a cloud of sweet, fermenting vapors smelling of ripe melon, chlorophyll, and decay. The intermittent rain—more a constant, hot drizzle that never quite becomes a downpour—keeps this mire perpetually slick, its surface beaded with moisture that condenses and drips from the canopy above in a ceaseless, miniature deluge.

And the canopy is everything. It is a total plant ecosystem, a living architecture that has consumed the sky. There is no open air, only a dense, multi-layered immensity of vegetation that rises from the ground-slush to a ceiling of light-fuzzed leaves kilometers above. This is no simple jungle of trees and vines. Here, the concept of individual species has dissolved into one all-pervasive, all-consuming mass. Great, buttressed trunks—some as wide as hills, others slender as needles—are fused together at impossible angles, forming arches, bridges, and vaulted caverns whose walls are living, breathing tissue. Their surfaces are not bark but a mosaic of mosses, spongy air-plants, and broad, glossy leaves the size of shields. These surfaces, in turn, sprout their own offspring: epiphytic growths that dangle in thick, ropey curtains, pulsating sacs of translucent membrane, and fungal shelves that glow with a soft, internal light in the perpetual twilight below.

The light is normal, yet it feels miraculous. It filters down through the chaotic ceiling, fractured and re-fracted a thousand times, painting the interior of the place in shifting, dappled patterns. Sunshafts, thick and tangible with floating spores, stab down into the gloom like pillars of gold, illuminating swarms of glittering, motile-seeds that drift on the turbulent air currents. Yet the light is unstable. It "sours" in pockets, a patch of brightness suddenly winking into a sickly, violet-tinged darkness for a moment before righting itself. It is a symptom of the world's fundamental wrongness.

This is a place young in its form but ancient in its substance, a landscape born of a cataclysmic re-weaving of reality. The terrain is sharp and fresh, not from geologic uplift, but from the sheer velocity of its own creation. Hills are actually burgeoning domes of new growth, rising visibly over the course of a day, pushing through the older, dying layers with a sound like tearing canvas. Below them, ravines are not carved by water but are the scars of instantaneous collapse, where entire groves have forfeited their structural integrity and folded into the slurry, their memory still echoing in the groaning wood.

There is a semblance of order to the chaos, an "urban" quality to the growth. The living mass has constructed vast, settlement-scale structures. There are "plazas"—wide, open spaces in the canopy where the growth thins, dominated by a single, gargantuan, flowering organism whose corona pulses with light. There are "highways"—immense, flattened branches, kilometers long, that arch between the "towers" of the great trunks. And there are "buildings"—hollow, bulbous growths that cling to the sides of these towers, their walls a lattice of living cellulose, their interiors a warren of winding, fungal-walled tunnels.

But this is a city without builders, a metropolis without citizens. There is no animal life. The sheer noise and dynamic instability make it impossible for creatures with metabolisms and nervous systems to survive here. The only inhabitants are the plants themselves, in all their myriad, fused forms.

The rules of this world are laws of botany pushed to the point of madness. From the damp mire, things emerge with no precedent. A spiral of green light might unwind itself from the slush, climb a hundred meters, and then burst into a cloud of humming, needle-like filaments that hang in the air before dissolving. A perfectly smooth, white sphere might bubble into existence from a tree-trunk, quiver for an hour, and then deflate like a punctured lung, its substance reabsorbed by the parent. Gravity is a suggestion, not a law. Ropes of vine-like growth will grow horizontally for days, defying expectation, before suddenly kinking upward at a ninety-degree angle toward a distant light source that only they can perceive. The geometry is non-Euclidean; a "street" may turn a corner and bring you back to where you started, but ten meters higher than you were a moment before.

Memories do not belong to minds here, but to material. Touch a patch of bark and you might be overwhelmed not with touch, but with the phantom sensation of a day of intense sunlight a century ago, stored in the wood's cellular memory. Stand in a "ruined" plaza—the collapsed remains of a once-spacious dome—and the sound of the groaning wood around you will sharpen into a chorus of faint, overlapping voices, the residual vibrations of the quantum wavefunction collapse that created this space. This is the reality breakdown: the past, present, and future of living matter are concurring simultaneously.

There is no technology, for what need is there of it when the world itself grows you a bridge, a home, a source of light? There is no fauna, for what creature could endure the perpetual, turbulent creation? There is only sound, vibration, warmth, dampness, and the overwhelming, suffocating, terrifying beauty of a world that is a single, unending, and fractal act of becoming. It is a place trying to be everything at once, and in its effort, it is coming undone.
