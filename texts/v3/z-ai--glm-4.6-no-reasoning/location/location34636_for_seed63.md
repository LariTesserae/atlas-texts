Here, there is no sky, only a ceiling. It is not a roof, not in any way made, but a tight, latticed canopy of mineral growth that shuts out the stars and chokes away any notion of open air. The light is a memory, a faint crimson seepage that bleeds from the crystal veins threading the stone overhead, as if the world itself were a dying heart and this the slow, final pulse of its blood. It is just enough to see, to make you wish you couldn't.

You stand on the Floor of Chimes, though that is a name given to it by no one who has ever left. The scale is deceptive; it feels vast, like the floor of a valley, yet the crystal canopy is so low in places you can touch it, and you always feel the close, claustrophropic press of its boundaries. It is a single, enormous cavern, a geological room, and its floor is not stone, but life.

The waterlessness is absolute. There is no liquid here, no dampness on the tongue, no slickness underfoot. The air is thin, hot, and dry as a kiln. It carries the scent of hot metal and burnt sugar, of dust that has never known moisture. To breathe is to inhale the powdered ghosts of a billion desiccated forms.

There is no vegetation, no soft green, no Photosynthesis. The substance that carpets the floor, flows up the walls, and drapes the soaring crystal spires in thick, multi-hued tapestries is fungal, but a fungal existence so profound it has become geology. It is the Mycora. It is not a life that *grows*, so much as one that *is*. It has had ages—unfathomable, silent ages—to saturate this sealed world until every cubic millimeter is occupied. This is the packed, overwhelming reality.

The ground is a carpet of interwoven filaments, a felted mass of hyphae so dense it has the consistency of compressed cork or dense pumice. It is soft underfoot, but with no give, like walking on a planet-sized bar of soapstone. Its color is a uniform, bruised purple in the deep gloom, but where the crimson light from above touches it, it erupts. Ossified fruiting bodies, the size of boulders, jut from the floor at odd angles. They are not soft mushrooms; they are mineral sculptures, armored in plates of shimmering, fused silica that glitter like a dragon’s hoard. Within their crystalline shells, strange gasses swirl in slow, lazy currents, their inner lights a pale, sick green or an electric blue.

The overwhelming fauna are here because of the Mycora. They are not animals of flesh and blood, for that chemistry requires a solvent that does not exist. They are creatures of resonance and structure, of vibrating crystals and clacking carapaces. The silence is a lie. The place is mostly quiet, but in the way a held breath is quiet before the scream. When one of the large, six-legged Geophags—creatures of chitinous plates and grinding silica teeth—moves to feed on a section of the fungal felt, the sound is not a crunch but a low, resonant **BONG**, a clear, perfect bell tone that hangs in the hot, dead air for minutes, echoing from the crystal canopy and the mineral walls.

This is the life of this place: a slow, cavernous soundscape of perpetual chiming. A small, skittering thing—a Scuttler, like a daddy-long-legs made of obsidian rods—will trip over a triggering filament, and a cluster of smaller fruiting bodies will shiver into a cascade of high, lilting notes, like wind chimes stirred by a ghost. The Geophags are the bass tones, their slow, ponderous steps tolling like a funeral bell for a god.

The ecosystem is brutally simple and endlessly dynamic. The Mycora is the soil and the food. Its network of hyphae runs through *everything*, a single, planet-mind of neural complexity. It perhaps thinks—if thinking is a word for it—in vibrations. The Geophags are the herbivores, their powerful jaws cracking the armored fruiting bodies to get at the nutrient-dense spore-mass within. They leave behind perfect, geometric gouges in the ground and a scattering of crystal shards that glitter in the dimness. The Scuttlers are the opportunists, the scavengers, cleaning up the nutrient-rich dust left behind.

Then there are the predators. You do not see them. You hear them as a change in the music. A sudden, sharp, high-pitched tone, like a struck wine glass, will cause the slow, tolling walk of a Geophag to falter. A moment of silence. Then, a frantic, percussive symphony of clacking, skittering legs and a crescendo of panicked, chiming dissonance as the hunter attacks. The predator is called a Tuner, and it is a creature of pure sonic force, a woven lattice of razor-edged crystal that locates its prey by the harmonics of its body and strikes with a focused, razor-thin wave of sonic energy, shattering the Geophag’s armor from the inside out. The aftermath is the only sign of its passing: a pile of vibrating, iridescent dust and the slow, mournful decay of the Geophag’s deep tone into nothing.

The scattered built elements are heartbreaking in their desolation. Half-buried in the Mycora floor are the ruins of a single, cobbled-together structure. It is not of this world. A ship, perhaps, that crashed here an eon ago. Its metal is not corroded, but fused, subsumed. One wall of the bridge is sheared away, revealing consoles that are no longer electronics but crystalline growths, their screens now blank panes of slowly forming quartz. A single, skeletal command chair stands empty, its synthetic weave long since dissolved and replaced by a delicate, lace-like pattern of phosphorescent fungus. This is the sole evidence of technology, a tomb of a forgotten visit, now just another part of the geology. Another set of parameters for this world to digest.

The place is strange because it is fundamentally alive in a way we do not understand. It is not a place you visit; it is a place you are subsumed into. The heat is a constant pressure. The lack of water makes you feel your own body’s moisture being wicked away, a slow desiccation. The air is thick with spores that do not sicken but resonate in your lungs, making you feel the faint, deep hum of the Mycora in your own marrow. It is an active place, a living place, always in motion, always consuming, always chiming its slow, strange song into the oppressive, lightless heat. It is a world that is its own beginning and its own end, a sealed, hot, dark bottle in which a single, complex, and utterly alien form of life has learned to sing.
