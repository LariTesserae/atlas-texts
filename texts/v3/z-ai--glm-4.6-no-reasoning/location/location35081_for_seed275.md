To descend into the Root-Ward is to enter a city that has never known rain, never felt the wash of a river, and never seen the dryness of bare stone. It exists in a vast, subterranean gulf—a regional expanse of hollowed lithosphere where the ceiling is lost to a creeping darkness above, and the floor is nothing but an infinity of living wood. This is a place of total vegetation, a pure-plant ecosystem so absolute that wood, leaf, and root have entirely eclipsed the need for an aqueous world. Life here does not drink; it mineralizes. 

The architecture of the Root-Ward is not built from plant matter—it *is* the plant matter. Towering load-bearing trunks, hundreds of meters thick, rise from the primordial taproot floor and fan out into structural canopy-arches high overhead. These are the vertical pillars and the horizontal avenues. Between them, engineered burls grow into tenement blocks, their interiors hollowed out by regulated fungal rot, forming cool, dry chambers where the air smells faintly of cellulose and quiet combustion. Walkways are laid upon thick, interlocking root-mats; bridges are living vines, trained over centuries to span the vast, sparse distances between the great trunks. There is much emptiness here—the city breathes through it. Vast, open shafts of space drop away into the dark below the walkways, and upward into the misty gloom, giving the settlement a feeling of hanging suspended in a wooden void.

It is cool in this depth, the chill of a root cellar, of undisturbed earth and slow metabolism. The only warmth comes from the industrial district, which beats like a quiet, mechanical heart within the thickest burls. Here, the city’s engines turn. They are not steam engines, for there is no water to boil. Instead, they are hydraulic press-works and rotary mills driven by the immense, slow tectonic pressure of the roots themselves—roots that push ever outward against the stone walls of the gulf, translating the planet's geological stress into kinetic energy. The factories are electrical, humming with the dry ozone crackle of piezoelectric generation. Great conductive xylem wires, sheathed in bark, carry the current to the fabrication bays, where automated lathes shape lignin-pulp into precision gears and extrude hardened resin into panes of transparent, amber-like glazing. The production is scaled, relentless, and organized, yet it is almost entirely silent. 

The quiet is profound. Sound dies quickly in the porous wood, swallowed by the cork and pulp. There is no splash of water, no howl of wind. The loudest thing is the low, rhythmic thud of the sap-pumps—vast industrial pistons that pressurize the phloem, moving nutrient-rich cytoplasm through the city's arterial viaducts. It is a circulatory system that replaces water mains; a thick, amber syrup that sweats from glandular nodes and is collected in dry-runnel gutters by the few creatures that call this place home.

Fauna is scattered but abundant in its niche. The city is patrolled by blind, six-legged isopods the size of dogs, their carapaces the exact texture of cracked bark. They are the sanitation engineers, feeding on the shed cellulose dust and the occasional fungal bloom. Above them, in the sparse voids between the towers, drift the Gas-Weavers—translucent, lighter-than-air arachnids that spin vast, invisible webs across the chasms to catch the rising spore-drift. Their presence is only given away by the faint, sticky resistance felt on the skin when walking across an open plaza. Predation is a slow, quiet affair: a patient, venomous centipede waiting coiled inside a socket-pit for an isopode to wander past, a sudden contraction, and then stillness again.

The strangeness of the Root-Ward lies in its embryonic flux. The erosion parameter is nearly zero; this place is not being worn away, but is instead actively generating, just coming into being. The raw geology of the enclosing void is being slowly consumed. The city is a living frontier. At its edges, the great roots are still pressing into the virgin stone, excreting weak organic acids that dissolve the rock at a rate of millimeters a decade, converting mineral to biomass, pulling the very cavern walls into the body of the metropolis. It is a landscape of construction, not decay. Walk too far toward the perimeter, and the organized avenues give way to raw, knotted thickets—half-formed architecture, embryonic streets that are still just folded cambium, waiting for the hormonal triggers of the city’s central node to tell them what rooms to become.

Light comes from the flora itself. Bioluminescent lichen, engineered to respond to electrical stimulation, traces every thoroughfare in steady, cool white. It pulses gently with the rhythm of the sap-pumps, a slow heartbeat of light that casts long, parallel shadows down the wooden corridors. 

To stand in the Root-Ward is to stand inside a vast, dry lung, a mechanical organism of wood and sap and quiet voltage. It is a city that has solved thirst by becoming its own earth, an underground forest of engines and bark, endlessly carving itself out of the dark, one silent, microscopic bite at a time.
