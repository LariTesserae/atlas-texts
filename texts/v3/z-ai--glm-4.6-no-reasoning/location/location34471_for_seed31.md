The world is quiet. The breath of the wind is a soft, constant *shush* against high, impossible stone, and the only other sound is the water. Never a rush, never a gush. Water here is a weeping thing, a patient, endless exhalation of clear, mineral-laden tears.

This is the Verge, a new-cracked highland suspended like a raft in the upper air. The elevation is profound; the air is thin and tastes of clean cold and wet stone. Down below, or so it is said, the world plunges away into a sea of dense, impenetrable cloud that never clears. Here, on the Verge, you are above the weather, seeing only the flat white ceiling of the sky above and the featureless void of the cloud-sea below. The light is a steady, ambient glow, sourceless and shadowless, as if the very air radiates a soft, pearlescent luminescence. It is never brighter, never dimmer. Time is measured not by the sun but by the slow, geometric accretion of stone and the patient lapping of the water.

The terrain is aggressively young. Sharp fins of obsidian-dark rock, still raw and unweathered, jut from the plateau at chaotic angles. They are not the worn, rounded bones of old mountains, but the fresh, chitinous shards of a world whose shell has just broken. Erosion is a concept that has not yet taken hold in any meaningful way; the edges are knife-like, the fractures pristine. Between these jagged monuments lie wide, shallow basins, their floors paved with fine, black gravel and sand, the detritus of the Verge's violent birth.

Water is the sculptor of this nascent world, but it works with a gentler hand than elsewhere. The ground is saturated, a sponge of rock and earth that stores the perpetual condensation of the high-altitude air. Water doesn’t flow in rivers here; it seeps. It oozes from the faces of the stone fins in slow, trembling curtains, tracing dark, glistening lines that catch the ceaseless light. It beads on the edges of the rock like perspiration, welling up in perfectly circular pools that sit like dark coins on the gravelled plains. The sound of this weeping is the most intimate thing in the landscape: a soft, continuous *plink… plink… plink…* as a single, patient drop falls from a stone's chin to the pool below, a sound that seems to echo for an eternity in the vast, quiet emptiness.

Vegetation is sparse but not struggling. Where the water gathers, life has found a toehold. It is not a riot of green but a studied, deliberate presence. Clumps of a silvery-green moss, thick and plush as velvet, cling to the water-streaked rock. They drink the weeping stone directly. From the moss grow pale, six-petaled blossoms the size of a thumb, their flesh waxy and cool to the touch. They have no scent. In the shallow pools, a plant like a pale green string unfolds in perfect, slow spirals, its growth visible over weeks, its form a living illustration of a mathematical curve. There is a lushness to it, a density of life in its immediate vicinity—a cushion of moss, a cluster of flowers, the subtle coming-and-going of pale, stringy algae—but it is an oasis effect, islands of intense green in a sea of black stone and grey water. There is no grass, no trees, no sprawling undergrowth. The scale is simply too open.

Scattered across this high plateau, at what seems like random intervals, are the Constructions. They are the work of people whose bones are now as much a part of the Verge as the stone. This is their only legacy, for there is no animal life to have carried them away, no birds to have nested in their eaves, no insects to have hummed their ruins to sleep. No one has ever seen another living soul here, yet the Constructions stand, mute evidence that they were not the first.

They are urban in their scale and complexity, but primitive in their method. Entire structures, some the size of a large hall, others no bigger than a single room, have been hewn from the living rock of the Verge's fins. They are not built *onto* the landscape, but *from* it, their walls the raw, black stone, their surfaces still bearing the tool marks of crude stone chisels and hand-held saws. Inside, floors are sloped, the slow weep of the walls channeled into intricate patterns of hand-carved runnels that gather the water into central basins.

The town, for that is what it is, is not a place of streets and squares. It is a constellation of these carved monoliths, spaced across kilometers of open plateau. There is no visible infrastructure connecting them, no roads, no aqueducts—other than the water runnels carved within each building. They stand alone, isolated strongholds of a civilization that needed no community. Their purpose is unclear. Some seem like dwellings, with hollowed-out sleeping platforms. Others are empty basins, their only feature the complex drainage system that funnels their precious water. A few are towers, hollow from the inside, with a single window facing the cloud-void below, as if their inhabitants were watchmen, though what they watched for is lost. The silence in these high halls is a physical thing, a dense blanket that absorbs all sound. It is a stillness that has held since the last chisel fell.

This is a world of profound and immutable stillness. The water level in the pools never rises or falls. Erosion is so infinitesimally slow as to be non-existent. The light does not dim or brighten. The temperature is a constant, gentle warmth that is somehow independent of the wetness in the air. There is no weather, no season, no day or night. The Verge exists in a state of perfect equilibrium, a system that has found its balance and will not be disturbed. The slow spiral of the pool-plants and the patient weeping of the stone are the only markers of a time that is a flat, unbroken line.

To stand here is to feel like a ghost. It is a landscape of absolute presence—of sharp stone, of sure water, of silent construction—but an absence of life. A town with no people. A rain with no storm. A Genesis without the breath of spirit. The silence is not empty; it is full. It is the resonant chord of a world that is finished, stable, and holding its breath, forever.
