The first thing to understand is that it is not stone. It rises like stone, great continental plates of it, buckled and faulted into peaks that scrape the upper atmosphere. It has the sharp, young edges of stone, the sheer faces and the serrated ridges. But it is not stone. To touch it is to feel a thrumming, a deep, resonant hum that travels up the arm and settles in the teeth. It is a silicate alloy, cooled with impossible speed from a molten state, a continental crust of industrial scab. This is the Forge-Grave, the Cooling World.

The scale is planetary. There are no horizons, only vanishing points over immense, light-soaked distances. You stand on one spar of this megalithic structure and see others, leagues away, rising from a sea of something that is not cloud. The light is the source of the paradox. It is bright—painfully, eternally bright—yet the temperature is killing cold. The sky is a searing, sterile white, a ceiling of pure photons emanating from no sun, no discernible source. It is an ambient field of radiation, a leftover from the world's birth-blast, and it lends the black metal underfoot a chrome-like ability to reflect it, turning every surface into a blinding mirror.

And there is moisture. The light itself seems to condense on the frigid, hyper-smooth surfaces of the world. Under the glare, you can see a constant, silvery film of liquid bleeding from the seams where the great plates fused. It is not water, not exactly. It is a thin, viscous fluid, almost like a light oil, that weeps from the world’s pores and trickles down the vast flanks. In the lower interstices, where continental plates butt against each other, this fluid collects in vast, slow-moving rivers of silver. It is damp, but a sterile, humming dampness, the moisture of a machine left on in a deep-freeze.

The vegetation grows in this seeping damp. It is lush, a riot of growth that saturates every available surface, a density that leaves no bare metal exposed beyond the slick, weeping sheen. The ground is a packed carpet of these glowing, crystalline shapes, their soft light refracted through the eternal film of metallic dew, creating the illusion of walking through a nebula frozen to the ground.

The fauna is overwhelming. There is no moment of silence, no place devoid of motion. The sound is a constant, loud, high-frequency chorus—the hum of the world itself, amplified a billionfold by its occupants. The creatures are machine-biology, kinetic sculptures of silica and light. They are the maintenance crew of this slow, dying engine.

There are the Lumens, swarms of fist-sized, faceted creatures that glide a few inches above the crystal-fern floor. They move like hummingbirds, their wings a blur of polished gears and ruby light, harvesting the ambient radiation. Their call is a sharp, crystalline *tink-tink-tink* that adds a percussive layer to the world’s hum. They are so dense that walking through them is like wading through a hail of sparks, their bodies caroming off you with soft, chime-like impacts.

Then there are the Grazers, immense, slow-moving behemoths that clamber along the vast cliff faces. They are the size of mountains, with bodies like basalt and legs of articulated pistons. Their heads are /vast parabolic dishes/ that scrape against the cliffs, their great grinding mouths consuming the very crystal-ferns and cable-vines, ingesting the symbiotic ecosystem of metal, light, and life. Their movements are glacial, their footfalls taking minutes, each one accompanied by a groan of stressed metal and the cascade of a million shimmering Lumens, disturbed from their perches. Their digestive process is slow, inefficient, and the source of the world’s other dominant lifeform: the Scavengers.

The Scavengers are swarming, insectile things, all sharp angles and skittering metal legs. They flow in rivers around the colossal feet of the Grazers, feasting on the fine glitter of waste and shards of un-digested crystal that fall from their maws. The sound they create is a ceaseless, roiling rustle, a metallic susurrus that blends with the deep hum of the world and the high chime of the Lumens to form a single, overwhelming sonic tapestry.

This is the world's rule: it is a single, closed-loop system of slow decay, a planetary-sized machine running down. The Grazers eat the light-and-metal-harvesting flora. The Scavengers eat the Grazers' waste. The flora eats the light and weeps moisture from the metal. The metal is the world. The light is its power source, fading over geological time. The change is infinitesimally slow. The peaks are still sharp because the eons of wind have not yet begun, and the Grazers have only just, in cosmic terms, begun to nibble at them. The technology is industrial not because of factories, but because the world *is* the factory, the product, and the machinery all at once. There is nothing built by hands because nothing hands could make would be as intricate or as vast as the self-organizing machine that exists here.

To stand here is to be a witness to a process so grand you are rendered insignificant. The cold is a complete cold, a cold that sinks into your bones. The bright, featureless sky is a blinding that whites out thought. The sound is a physical pressure, a vibration that seems to want to un-knit your very molecules.
