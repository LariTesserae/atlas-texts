The plateau sits high above the cloudline, a vast, flat expanse of rust-colored laterite and exposed karst, elevated a full mile beyond the reach of the lowland forests. From this height, the horizon curves visibly, a faint bruise of atmospheric haze separating the burnt earth from a pale, washed-out sky. Yet the air here is not dry. The warmth—constant, pressing, humid—clings to the skin like a damp cloth. This is a place where water does not fall so much as it emerges. The geological strata beneath the city are saturated; aquifers trapped under impermeable shale for millennia press upward, seeking relief. Water weeps from the cliff faces at the plateau's edge. It oozes from the pore-spaces of exposed stone. It stands in shallow, warm sheets across the flatlands, never deeper than an inch, never fully evaporating under the diffuse, ordinary sunlight. 

The city—**Karsos**—occupies the center of this plateau, sprawling across several square miles of open, puddled ground. It is an urban organism, but a sparse one. The avenues are inhumanly wide, laid out in a grand, rigid grid that extends past the immediate sightline, giving the settlement the feel of a massive, emptied-out machine floor. The blocks are dense with layered construction—brick, poured concrete, and riveted iron—but the spaces between the blocks are vast, punctuated by empty lots of standing water and bare, laterite dirt. There is no suburban transition, no fringe of greenery. The city simply stops, yielding to the weeping rocks and the wide, damp flats. 

Vegetation is an afterthought here. A few specialized organisms survive: pale, rootless horsetails that lodge in the gutters, and a type of encrusting lichen—sulfur-yellow and rubbery—that thrives on the perpetual dampness of the north-facing walls. Fauna is similarly rare, driven away by the noise. The only visible life forms are the long, filamentous nematodes that swim in the warm, stagnant runoff, and the occasional blind isopod that scuttles across the weeping cliff edges, feeding on mineral residue. It is a warm, wet world nearly devoid of blood and chlorophyll.

Karsos is an industrial city, though its factories do not burn coal or choke the sky with smoke. They run on the plateau’s geothermal gradients and the immense hydraulic pressure of the deep aquifers. The architecture is strictly utilitarian: blocky manufactories with saw-tooth roofs, towering condenser coils, and massive, iron-ridged pipe networks that snake between the buildings like the arteries of a buried leviathan. Everything is oversized, built to withstand the constant, slow vibration that thrums through the ground. 

The defining feature of this landscape is the sound. It is deafening. The acoustic environment is an inescapable, physical weight, a low-frequency roar that settles into the chest cavity and blurs the vision. This thunder is generated by the **Regulator Pipes**—a city-spanning network of immense boreholes that tap directly into the pressurized aquifers below. To prevent the plateau from fracturing and collapsing under the hydrostatic pressure, the city must constantly bleed the water out. It erupts from the Regulator Pipes as superheated steam and boiling water, expanding into the cooler upper air with a continuous, earth-shaking bellow. The sound is unvarying. It does not fluctuate with the days or the seasons. It is a mechanical tinnitus that drowns out speech, footfalls, the wind, and the rain. Communication among the city's inhabitants is done entirely through sign language, vibrating batons pressed against the collarbone, or written slips. 

Because of the noise, the world feels strangely isolated. Sight becomes the primary sense, and the visual landscape is one of slow, patient decay. The erosion parameter here is fully established; the city is not crumbling, but it is weathered into a permanent state of exhaustion. The iron latticework is rusted into deep, stable oranges and blacks. The concrete is stained by centuries of mineral-heavy condensation, mapped with long, vertical rivulets of white lime and green copper sulfate. The weeping rocks at the plateau's edge are carved into smooth, undulating grooves by the endless flow of groundwater, perfectly balanced in their worn-down forms. 

There is a deep weirdness to this place, born of its contradictions. It is a high-altitude plateau that feels like a sweltering, shallow swamp. It is a sprawling urban center that is as quiet as a tomb, save for the mechanical screaming of the earth itself. The dynamics of Karsos are agonizingly slow. The steam vents never falter; the water never stops weeping; the rust never stops forming, inching forward over decades. Change is measured in the gradual occlusion of a window by mineral scale, or the century-long subsidence of a block's foundation as the water table shifts by inches. 

To stand in the middle of one of Karsos's wide, puddled avenues is to stand in a vast, humid void. The warm water soaks through the soles of your boots. The diffuse light casts no shadows, turning the iron monoliths into flat, rust-colored cutouts against the haze. Above, the condenser towers bleed white steam into the sky. Below, the pressurized earth bellows. And all around is the empty, open space of the plateau, a high, wet, deafening desert of iron and stone, enduring its slow, mechanical eternity.
