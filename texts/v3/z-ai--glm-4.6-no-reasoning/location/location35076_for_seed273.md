The world here is entirely a made thing. There is no bedrock beneath, no original soil, no geological substrate waiting beneath the skin of the place. Everything is constructed, extending down and out in an engineered infinity. This is a vast, mountainous architecture—a built landscape of extreme elevation that rises through the humid atmosphere in tiered cantilevers and colossal structural ribs. It is a city-mountain, a forge-peak, rising so high that its upper reaches are lost in a permanent haze of warm condensation, while its roots sit in a wide, regional basin of interconnected machinery.

Despite its totality of construction, the place is deeply, overwhelmingly lush. Vegetation has claimed every available surface, and because every surface is manufactured, the green things have adapted to grip iron flanges, root in the accumulated detritus of clogged gutters, and wrap their tendrils around steam pipes. Broad-leafed vines with leaves the size of shields drape over railings and catwalks, their surfaces running with condensation. Mosses and lithophytic ferns choke the joints of massive girders, softening the industrial geometry with thick, spongy carpets of chlorophyll. The warmth is pervasive, radiating up from the depths of the structure where hidden engines turn, heating the metal bones of the city-mountain like a slow, giant bloodline. The air feels like the inside of a vast, green greenhouse—damp and heavy, smelling of wet iron, humus, and the sharp, ozone tang of old electricity.

Water is not a flowing river here, but an intermittent, pervasive presence. It manifests as condensation. Warm air rises from the industrial guts below, hits the cooler structural plates of the upper tiers, and weeps. Water beads on the undersides of walkways, gathering into fat, heavy droplets that fall with a metronomic patience into the moss fifty feet below. In the hollows of collapsed roof-tanks and the basins of disused cooling vents, pocket-pools of rust-brown water gather, thick with duckweed and the eggs of amphibians. When it rains—which is often, as the heat and moisture generate their own weather systems—the water cascades down the stepped architecture in a thousand makeshift waterfalls, finding pathways carved by centuries of established erosion.

And erosion is everywhere, though it is an erosion of manufacture. This is not a landscape of sudden change, but of incredibly slow, patient degradation. Rust blooms across steel plates like a slow-motion fungus, taking decades to eat through a single bolt. Concrete crumbles into a powder that, mixed with the organic fall of leaves and dead vines, forms a thick, rich loam in the crevices of the infrastructure. Limestone deposits from the mineral-rich condensing water accrete around pipes, slowly engulfing them in travertine shells. The balance between the constructed and the grown is an ancient, settled treaty. The structure does not collapse; it settles. It weathers. It accepts its green overcoat.

The scale is open and regional. Standing on a mid-tier platform, you cannot see the edges of the built environment. It extends past the immediate sightlines, a sprawling valley-sized complex of interconnected factory halls, ventilation towers, and residential blocks that have long since been overtaken by the jungle. It is dense to the point of claustrophobia, despite the altitude. There is no sky visible from the lower paths—only the layered undersides of the tiers above, draped in hanging roots and illuminated by the harsh, bright glow of industrial lighting.

The light is intense and unyielding. Massive, ancient arc-lamps encased in rusted cages and vine-tangles cast a brilliant, flat glare that cuts through the humid fog. It throws long, sharp shadows from the railings and pipes, illuminating the dust motes and spores that churn in the air. The light makes the greens vibrate—neon moss, the glossy dark of saturated ferns, the pale chartreuse of new vine-growths seeking the nearest bulb.

Fauna is abundant, filling every niche this strange, warm, wet iron-forest provides. Geckos with specialized, setae-covered toe pads scuttle up sheer, weeping glass and rusted steel. Colonies of hard-shelled beetles click and rustle through the leaf litter accumulating in the cable trays. Birds—bright-plumed and loud—build nests in the protective cages of the floodlamps, their calls competing with the ever-present mechanical thrum. In the deep, shadowy runoff channels, pale amphibians stalk the rust-crabs. The food web is entirely parasitic upon the infrastructure; the creatures eat the insects that eat the lichen that eats the metal, or they eat each other, living out their lives in a biosphere built on engineering.

The soundscape is a loud, sustained drone, a harmony of patient industry. It is the deep, subsonic rhythm of engines far below, the high-pressure hiss of steam venting from cracked pipes, the rhythmic chunk-chunk-chunk of aging pumps. Over this mechanical baseline, the biological world layers its own noise: the constant drip of condensation, the sudden squawk of a bird, the chirring of insects, the hollow, metallic ring of a weeping droplet striking a pan. It is loud, but it is a static loudness, a white noise of heat and work and growth that has sounded exactly the same for centuries.

Nothing here changes quickly. The dynamic of the place is profoundly slow. The vines take a generation to pull down a gantry; the rust takes a century to fell a wall. The machinery runs on automated loops, grinding out parts for purposes long forgotten, powering lights that illuminate no human workspaces, venting steam into a sky no one watches. It is a world in its own state, a stabilized entropy. The gradients of change are so weak that to a casual observer, it seems frozen in time—a single, eternal, humid afternoon of green and rust and noise. But given enough years, the gears will falter, the lights will dim, and the roots will pull the last of it down into the soil they have made from its bones.
