It begins with a mouth.

Not a metaphor. An actual mouth—stone lips parted around a throat that descends at a sharp angle into the earth. You found it because the grass changed. Above, the world is ordinary: cool autumn air, grey sky, a field of dry stalks ticking against each other in a wind that smells like distant rain. But here the grass stops in a perfect oval, as if something swallowed the soil and seed both, leaving only a raw throat of pale, unplastered clay.

You descend. Seven feet down, the passage tightens, then opens—unexpectedly, impossibly—into brightness.

The light is the first wrong thing. There is no sun down here, no skylight, no phosphorescent fungus. The illumination comes from the walls themselves, or rather from the process the walls are undergoing. The stone—or clay, or whatever this embryonic substance is—has not yet decided what it will be. It glows with the blank radiance of a thing still forming, still writing its own atomic signature. Streaks of white mineral run through the body of the passage like veins in a fetus, soft and branching and not quite hard. When you touch the wall your finger leaves a groove, and the groove seals itself slowly behind you, the material remembering its wholeness, healing the way flesh heals, the way water fills a vessel.

You are in a pocket. The chamber is no larger than a bedroom—intimate, contained, a space that feels as though it was made for one body, or perhaps for one event. The ceiling arches low enough that you could press both palms against it. The floor slopes toward a center that never quite arrives; the geometry wavers, the angles suggest a sphere but refuse to commit. There are no corners. There are no straight lines. The walls breathe—not metaphorically, not poetically, but literally expand and contract by millimeters, a slow respiratory rhythm that corresponds to nothing you can measure, some clock that belongs to the geology itself.

And it is loud with life.

This is the second wrong thing, or the third, or the hundredth—by now you have stopped counting. The chamber should be empty. Instead it is full. Not of vegetation—the sparse pale threads that cling to the glowing walls are barely plants at all, leafless and rootless, more like expressions of the stone's desire to become plant than like actual photosynthetic organisms. No, the fullness is movement. The fullness is bodies.

They are everywhere. They are the reason the light seems to shift and pulse, though the walls themselves shine steady. The creatures—arthropodal, perhaps, though the terminology of the surface feels absurd here—move across every surface in such numbers that the walls appear to shimmer, to breathe in a second rhythm layered over the stone's first. They are the color of the stone, which is the color of milk, which is the color of bone just before it sets. Their legs are long and filament-fine, and they move in patterns that are not random, not territorial, not any behavior you have a name for. They flow. They redistribute. They gather in dense clusters along the veins of white mineral, then disperse in a wave that propagates from some center you cannot locate, some pulse-point in the mathematics of their collective body.

There are thousands. Tens of thousands. In a room the size of a bedroom, in a pocket hidden seven feet below a field of dead grass, in a chamber that did not exist until the stone began to dream itself into form—they teem, and the sound of their movement is a whisper, a hiss, a dry and papery rushing that is almost silence, that sits exactly on the border between sound and the absence of sound, so that you hear it more with your skin than with your ears, a vibration in the air, a friction, the noise of process itself.

One of them stops. It raises the front third of its body—segmented, luminous, blind—and turns toward you. It has no face. It has no eyes. It has a mouth, though, or something that functions as a mouth: a vertical slit that opens and closes in the same rhythm as the walls, in the same rhythm as the healing grooves, in the same rhythm as the pulse that drives the white mineral through the stone like blood through a vein. The slit opens. You see inside it a darkness that is not the absence of light but the absence of formation—raw unmade space, the tissue of reality before it has committed to being anything at all.

The creature is not looking at you. It has no mechanism to look. But it is attending to you. It is registering your presence the way the stone registers your touch: as a disruption that must be incorporated, a deviation that must be smoothed back into the pattern. You are a groove in the wall. You are a wound that the chamber will heal.

You feel it beginning.

The air changes. Not temperature—temperature was always cool here, always that underground neutral chill—but texture. The air thickens. Or thins. Or becomes something else that air can become when the rules that govern air are still being written. You breathe in and the breath does not come back out the way it went in; something is subtracted, something is rearranged, the ratio of gases shifts toward a composition your lungs have never encountered. Your heartbeat sounds wrong. Not faster, not slower, but metrically wrong, as though the chamber has begun to suggest a different tempo, a different measure of time, and your body is trying—unsuccessfully—to syncopate.

The creatures continue to flow. The walls continue to breathe. The white mineral continues to branch. And in the center of the chamber—the center that the floor slopes toward, the center that never quite arrives—a shape is beginning. Not a shape that is there, but a shape that is becoming there. A thickening of the air. A densening of the light. A place where reality is starting to curdle, to clot, to congeal into something that will have edges and surfaces and a name, but does not yet, has not yet, is still only the intention of a shape, the memory of a geometry that has not been built, the ghost of a form haunting the space it will eventually occupy.

You are inside something that is being born. Not the chamber. Not the creatures. Not the shape in the center. All of these, together, and something else that contains them—the way a body contains its organs, the way a thought contains its words, the way a world contains its physics. You are inside a world that is being born, and you are not native to it, and you are being incorporated, the way a splinter is incorporated into flesh, the way a foreign word is incorporated into a language—slowly, with difficulty, with the body changing shape around the intrusion until the intrusion is no longer intrusion but feature, no longer wound but scar, no longer foreign but native.

The creature with the vertical mouth opens that mouth wider. The darkness inside is deeper now. Or you can see further into it. Or it is expanding. The distinction does not matter. What matters is that you can feel the pull—not gravitational, not magnetic, but logical, as though the unmade space inside the creature's mouth is the same unmade space from which the chamber is forming, and you could step into it, you could pass through that mouth and into the pre-formation, the raw geology, the place where reality is still a decision that has not been made.

Behind you, the passage is closing. The clay is healing. The groove you left in the wall has sealed, and the wall has continued sealing, narrowing, the throat of the passage contracting like a sphincter, like a healing wound, like a mouth closing around a word it has decided not to say.

You are going to stay. You understand this with a certainty that is not your own, that has been placed in you like a seed, like a mineral vein, like the memory of a shape. You are going to stay because the chamber needs you to stay, because the world being born requires an anomaly around which to crystallize, because the creatures have been waiting—without knowing they were waiting, without having the capacity to know—for something to arrive that is not of the stone, not of the mineral, not of the process, something foreign and disruptive and alive in a way that nothing here is alive, something that breathes out as well as in, something that leaves grooves that do not heal on their own.

You sit. The floor accepts you. The cool clay yields slightly,> then firms, then begins—very slowly—to grow warm.

The creatures flow over your feet. They do not avoid you. They do not swarm you. They treat you as they treat the stone:& as surface, as topology, as something the process must work around and through and into. Their legs—filament-fine, bone-white, numbering six or eight or a number that shifts depending?—prickle against your skin. They are dry. They are warm. They sound like whispers, like pages turning, likeB the subtraction of sound from sound.

The shape in the center is clearer now. It will be a column. Or a tree. Or a column that is also a treeF tree that is also a column, a form that belongs to the grammar of this place and no other, a form that could not exist on the surface where the physics are finished and locked, where reality has hardened into rules that do not breathe. Here, the rules are still soft. Here, the rules are still deciding what they will allow.

The white mineral branches through the stone and branches through the column-tree-shape and branches through you—not painfully, not physically, but informationally, the way a signal branches through a network, the way a pattern branches through a population. You can feel it: the chamber reading you, the process incorporating you, the birth proceeding with you as one of its organs, one of its features, one of its words.

The vertical mouth closes. The creature lowers itself. The flow resumes.

And in the quiet—in the whisper-sound, the friction-sound, the sound of ten thousand legs on breathing stone—you begin to hear, very faintly, the sound of something forming. Not music. Not voice. The sound of reality arranging itself. The sound of a world becoming itself.

It sounds like rain that has not yet fallen. It sounds like a question that has not yet been asked.

It sounds, most of all, like the beginning of something that will not need you once it is finished, but needs you now, needs you desperately, needs you the way a wound needs a splint, the way a sentence needs a verb, the way a birth needs a channel—temporary, sacred, and already beginning to heal.

The walls breathe. The light holds. The shape in the center clarifies.

You watch it come into being.
