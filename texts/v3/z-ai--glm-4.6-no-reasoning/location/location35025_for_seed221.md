You descend through a hatch that shouldn't exist in a wall that shouldn't be there, and the world contracts to a single manufactured gullet.

This is a pocket—a cavity sealed within the superstructure of something vast and industrial. There is no sky. The ceiling is a vault of riveted iron, so low you could press your palms to it, sweat-streaked and weeping. The walls are close enough to brush both shoulders simultaneously. Every surface is built: extruded steel, stamped brass, vulcanized rubber sheeting, miles of insulated cabling bundled like subcutaneous fat. There is no natural substrate anywhere. No rock. No soil. Even the floor is a grating—latticed steel over a shallow sump, and below that, more steel, more pipe, the manufactured body of the structure extending infinitely down. This place was engineered, bored into the guts of a greater machine like a cyst or a forgotten maintenance void.

And it is wet. Not flowing—not a stream or a flood—but pervasive, atmospheric, inescapable. Moisture beads on every surface, swells, fatens, rolls. The walls sweat. The ceiling weeps in a thousand points, slow tears that find you regardless of where you stand. The air is near saturation; you feel it enter your lungs like drinking. Water emerges from seams in the metal, from over-pressurized joints, from condensation on pipes running chilled fluids through the walls. It collects in the grating underfoot, pools in the sump, re-circulates through some hidden system. The sound of it—drip, patter, hiss of steam bleeding from a pinhole leak—layer over layer over layer.

But the water is only the beginning of the sound.

The place is deafening. Not chaotically, but densely—a hundred mechanical processes occurring simultaneously in this compressed volume, each contributing its voice to an impenetrable roar. A pump thrums somewhere behind the wall, vibro-tactile in your teeth. A valve chatters. Steam screams through a fracture. Somewhere, metal flexes under thermal stress, groaning like a throat. The grating underfoot vibrates with subsonic rhythm. Sound reflects off the close walls, compounds, builds interference patterns that press against your eardrums like physical weight. You cannot isolate sources. You cannot think in words. The sound is a single entity—a drone-choir that has existed so long it has become the place's identity.

And it has existed so long. That is the terrible equilibrium of this pocket. Nothing changes. The leaks leak at the same rate. The pressure holds. The temperature is a constant, vicious cold—not winter cold, not wind cold, but the indifferent cold of engineered space, of refrigeration coils and chilled water systems doing their work without reference to biology. Your fingers go numb. Your breath fogs and is absorbed into the wet almost instantly. The pipes are cold to the touch, burning cold, and the water that runs across them steals heat from your skin on contact. You are inside a machine's climate, and the machine does not care whether you live in it.

The vegetation is almost absent—almost. In the seam where the wall meets the floor, where the rubber gasket has pulled away and constant moisture pools, something grows. A single species of black filament, no thicker than hair, colonies of what might be fungus or might be something engineered and escaped. It mats across the metal in patches, drinking from the weeping walls, and it is the only growing thing. It does not spread. It has reached the limits of its niche and holds there, stable, another component in the frozen system.

But the space is not empty. It is saturated—dense with presences that fill every cubic centimeter of available air.

They are the fauna. They are overwhelming.

At first you think it's steam—wisps of vapor drifting in the dim light. But the wisps move with purpose. They are organisms. Hundreds of them. Thousands. They fill the space between your body and the walls, the space above your head, the space in the sump below the grating. They are small—some no larger than a rice grain, some the size of a thumbnail—and they are shaped like nothing terrestrial. Flattened discs that rotate slowly as they drift. Segmented toroids that pulse along invisible axes. Things with too many fine appendages, trailing like cilia, drinking moisture from the air. They are the color of the place: gunmetal, brass-oxidized green, rubber black, pipe-rust orange. They mimic the manufactured surfaces. They might have been built here too.

They do not avoid you. They drift across your face, settle momentarily on your shoulders, probe the warmth of your skin with appendages like fine wire before lifting away. They are not aggressive. They are not curious. They simply *are*, in the same way the moisture is, the same way the sound is—pervasive, environmental, inescapable. There are so many that the air itself seems textured with their bodies, that breathing means inhaling some of them, that you cannot lift your hand without displacing a cloud. They cluster thickest near the warmest surfaces—the steam leaks, the pump housings—and in the cold zones they drift in sluggish suspension, their metabolisms synced to the thermal gradients.

And they make sound too. Each of them. A tiny sound—click, whir, creak of microscopic carapace—but there are thousands, and the sounds layer into the greater roar, become indistinguishable from it, become part of the machine-choir that fills the pocket to bursting.

This is a place of total artifice, but it has escaped its designers. Something was made here—a component, a process, a product—and when it was complete, the pocket was sealed and forgotten, and the things inside it continued. The leaks found their paths. The organisms found their niches. The cold settled to its equilibrium. And now it holds, static, in perfect frozen dysfunction—a room-sized machine-ecology running forever on pressures and temperatures it set long ago, deafening, dense, wet, cold, and so alive with organisms that the life has become another form of machinery.

You could die here and the room would not notice. Your heat would bleed into the pipes. Your moisture would join the weeping walls. The things that fill the air would settle on you, investigate briefly, find you irrelevant, and drift on. The sound would not falter. The cold would not deepen. The equilibrium is total.

This is the pocket. This is what it is to be inside a forgotten organ of an industrial body, still functioning, still full, still deafeningly, overwhelmingly alive in the only way a made place can be alive—by accident, by persistence, by the refusal of engineered systems to simply stop.
