The first thing you notice is the heat. Not the shimmering, passive heat of a desert, but a deep, radiating presence that seems to emanate from the bones of the earth itself. It clings to you, a thick, second skin, and makes the air feel heavy, as if you’re breathing water. This is the Spine, a range of mountains that don’t so much rise from the plains as sheer through them, a blade of obsidian thrust toward a pale, washed-out sky.

The elevation is punishing. You are high enough that the air thins into something brittle and sharp, yet the temperature remains infernal, a paradox that defines the place. The mountains are not the sprawling, verdant ranges of old lore. They are compact, immense, and crowded. They thrust against one another, their shoulders and spires jammed together in a geological traffic jam, their ridges so dense with jagged peaks and deep, shadowed chasms that there’s no sense of a horizon, only more mountain, always more mountain. The scale is open, yes, but an open of verticals, a vastness that yawns downwards into canyons and upwards into blade-thin arêtes. It’s a landscape of packed immensity.

The rock is the dominant feature. It’s a dark, almost black volcanic stone, but eons of erosion have established a a strange, soft-looking patina. It’s not crumbly, but smooth, as if the entire range has been slowly tumbled in a cosmic polisher. Yet, clinging to this smoothness, is a thin, sweating film of condensation. The water here isn’t from rain, though occasional, startlingly violent cloudbursts do happen, hammering the stone and vanishing in minutes. The primary source of moisture is the air itself, drawn up from unseen lowlands to cool and condense on the rock faces. It weeps from the stone, tracing innumerable, fine, silver veins that connect to form trickling streams, then larger rivulets, which in turn plummet into the abyssal gorges in curtains of mist that are constantly torn apart by the wind. This is the ecology of water in the Spine: a vast, gossamer net woven by the vapor in the air.

Vegetation is sparse but mighty. There are no forests, no meadows. Life clings wherever the silver veins of water run thickest. You see tenacious, leathery lichens in blotches of rust and orange that stain the black rock. There are clumps of a sharp, metallic grass that grows like clusters of needles. Most impressive are the Sentinels: solitary, colossal, almost skeletal trees that seem forged more from iron than wood. Their bark is the color of rust and hammered steel, their thorns the size of daggers. They dot the high saddles and precipitous ledges, their roots like gnarled cables anchoring them to the stone. They are the only thing breaking the relentless line of the horizon.

And they are not alone for long.

The fauna is abundant in a way that is unnerving. This is not the gentle abundance of a thriving ecosystem, but a frantic, crowded, almost desperate proliferation. The primary residents are the Locusts, but not insects of flesh. They are mechanical, ranging in size from a fist to a draft horse. Chitinous bodies of burnished brass and copper, with multifaceted crystal lenses for eyes. They move in ceaseless, skittering swarms, their thousands of metal legs creating a perpetual, dry whisper that is the dominant sound of the Spine. It is not loud, but constant, the sound of sand pouring over sand, of a thousand clockworks running down at once.

They are the living heart of the industrial paradox of this place. For this is an industrial world, yet one without factories as we would know them. The mountains themselves are the machines.

Down in the deepest chasms, where the water veins converge into roaring, mist-filled rivers, are the Forges. These are not buildings, but geothermal vents where the earth’s heat meets the water, and the Locusts congregate. They don’t mine the rock; they repurpose it. They ingest silica and metal ores, secreting molten alloys and forging new components in the superheated steam vents. The air in the ravges is thick with the scent of ozone and hot metal, and pulses with a faint, internal, orange-red glow.

The Locusts are the architects and maintenance crew of a landscape they did not create but now utterly control. They coil the metallic grass into spools that they process into wiring. They harvest the crystalline lenses from their dead to install in new generations. They have built structures, but not dwellings. You see them—slender, impossibly tall transmission towers woven from scavenged metals, vast bridge-like gantries straddling canyons, all humming with a low, electric thrum. They are scattered, faint marks on a grand scale, but they are the evidence of a vast, unseen purpose: this entire mountain range, in its packed, extreme state, is a power plant. A slow, geological, self-contained engine.

The weirdness is in the silence and the life. For all its industrial activity, the place is mostly quiet. The water’s sound is muffled by the vastness; the metallic whisper of the swarms is like tinnitus. There are no birds. The wind, when it does blow, is hot and carries no scent of pine or earth, only the sterile mineral tang of the rock and the faint, electric smell of the Locusts. The only true movement is the thrum of the ecosystem itself: Locusts building and dying, water condensing and flowing, heat rising and cooling. It is an active, dynamic place, but its processes are so large and so slow they feel like the shifting of tectonic plates. And standing there, on a high, smooth saddle of black stone, feeling the sweat of the mountain on your skin and watching a river of brass insects flow into a chasm that glows with a faint forge-light, you understand that you are not in a wilderness. You are inside a machine that doesn't care you exist. You are a momentarily interesting speck of grit in its gears.
