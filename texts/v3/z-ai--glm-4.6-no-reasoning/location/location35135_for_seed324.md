The first thing you understand is the sound. It is not a sound you hear with your ears so much as one you comprehend with your skeleton. It is a constant, seismic thrumming that vibrates up through the soles of your boots, a low-frequency drone so immense that the air itself seems to hum. It is the sound of a sleeping god’s snore, the resonant frequency of a world-sized bell being rung by a ghost. Within this drone, higher pitches weave and clash: the speculative *tink* of stressed alloy, the drawn-out mournful groan of bearings the size of wine barrels, the high-pitched, mosquito-whine of something spinning too fast. It is a deafening cacophony, a universal noise floor that erases silence and makes quiet an alien concept.

You are in a room, but to call it that is like calling a cathedral a building. The scale is hidden, embedded. Up is not sky, but a curve of gunmetal-dark steel, punctuated by rivets the size of your head and seams that leak thin, steady streams of vapor. The light is dim, provided not by a sun but by a weak, ubiquitous blue-grey luminescence that seems to emanate from the metallic walls themselves, caught and diffused in the heavy air. The effect is like being trapped in perpetual twilight inside a storm cloud.

The terrain is the floor of this chamber, a landscape of sharp-edged, geometric planes and angles. It is young erosion, a world only newly emerged from its forging process. The decking is made of interlocking plates of a dark, oily-looking alloy, their edges still crisp, their surfaces unworn by time or footfall. It is all built. There is no geology here, only engineering. No trace of natural rock or soil; every surface is machined, planned, and purposeful. The air is cool and damp, clinging to your skin and the metallic surfaces. A heavy, metallic scent, like ozone and old iron, hangs in the atmosphere, so thick you can almost taste it. Water is everywhere, an industrial tear weeping from the engineered world. It condenses on the high, unseen ceiling in fat droplets, gathers in the channels and seams, and runs in a million trickles down the walls and across the floor. The surface sheens with it, a thin film of slickness over the precision-tooled metal. In places, it pools in shallow, purpose-built channels, black and undisturbed. The sound of the dripping water is there, a quiet *plink… plink…*, but it is utterly lost, crushed beneath the overwhelming mechanical drone.

Vegetation is a sparse, stubborn miracle. It clings to existence in the only places it can. Down in the water-channels, a few dark, stringy algae churn in the sluggish current. On the driest, highest ledges of the angled floor, nestled against the warm hum of what must be massive machinery behind the walls, grow pockets of pale, lichen-like organisms. They are not soft and green, but brittle and grey-white, the colour of chalk, fronding out in delicate, metallic-looking structures. They do not photosynthesize in this gloom; they seem to drawn their scant energy directly from the vibrations, the heat, and the chemical seepage in the water. They are the only life here, and they are lonely.

Presences are sparse. Across the vast, empty expanse of the floor, other structures scatter like abandoned thoughts. A gantry crane hangs motionless from an unseen ceiling, its hook dangling a hundred feet above the deck. A squat, box-like console with a single, dark-glassed eye stares out into nothing. A cluster of thick, ribbed pipes emerges from the floor, runs for twenty feet, and then vanishes back into it again. The sheer scale of the space between these features creates a profound sense of emptiness, a balanced, almost reverent sparseness. You could shout, and the sound of your voice would be subsumed, a single drop in an ocean of noise.

This place is active, dynamic in its relentless, inorganic way. The drone of the engines is not a steady-state hum. It shifts and modulates. You can feel the cadence of it in your chest—a slow build in pressure, a moment of peak intensity, a release, and then a slow, quiet gathering of force again. A cycle, one that might take minutes or hours to complete. As the pressure builds, the water that weeps from the walls turns to a misty spray, boiling out of the seams. The blue-grey light flickers and brightens for a moment, revealing complex filigree patterns in the walls that vanish back into shadow. When the pressure relents, the mist subsides, the water slows to a trickle, and the light dims again. The air itself seems to thin and thicken with the pulse.

There are no animals. No skittering things in the corners, no birds nesting on the gantries, no fish in the channels. The rules of this world preclude it. The only life is the mineral, the cautious, silent lithophytes that have learned to digest vibration. The only movement is the slow, inexorable, cycling respiration of the machine. You are a visitor, an anomaly of flesh and breath in a place of steel and steam and endless, deafening function. You stand on the knife-edge of a newly formed cliff of metal, in the cool, damp twilight of a planet-sized engine, and you feel not like an explorer, but a misplaced gear.
