# The Weeping Terraces

It is always arriving and never settled.

---

You climb to it — that is the only way. The path switchbacks up a mountainside that wants to be a cliff, and by the time you reach the terraces, your breath comes in white rags and the cold has found every gap in your clothing. The elevation sits in your chest like a stone. But the light — the light is impossible. It falls from a sky so pale it is nearly white, and it reflects off every wet surface until you have to squint, until the world is overexposed and rim-lit, every edge burning.

The terraces are small. A hamlet, perhaps, or less — a single complex built into the mountainside, a honeycomb of stacked chambers and retaining walls that covers perhaps two hundred paces end to end. It has boundaries. The mountain drops away below; the rock closes above. What exists between is dense beyond any logic — walls touching walls, passages that become tunnels, rooms stacked on rooms in a pile that seems to have grown rather than been planned. There is no space here that does not want to be smaller.

And it is wet. That is the first thing you understand, and the last thing you stop understanding. The water does not fall from above in any ordinary way. It emerges. It comes from the rock faces in a thousand slow threads, weeping from fissures too fine to see. It condenses on the undersides of every overhang and falls in irregular drops that never find a rhythm. It sheets down the retaining walls in films so thin they are more like wetness itself than like water. The stone — a dark, vesicular basalt, porous and ancient — drinks what it can and exhales the rest. The air is saturated. Your skin tastes it. Your lungs fill with it. You are inside a cloud that has decided to be a place.

The cold sharpens the wetness into something almost mineral. Water on stone near freezing has a particular quality: it is patient, it is transparent, it seems to hold the light rather than merely reflecting it. Every surface glows. The terraces gleam like the inside of a tide pool, if tide pools could exist at altitudes that kill most life.

---

There is vegetation, but it has learned to be minimal. Where the water flows fastest, there is nothing — only black rock and white water and the overexposed glare. Where the water slows, where it pools in the shallow grooves carved into the terrace floors, there is a single kind of moss: pale, almost white, growing in tight rosettes like small mouths opening toward the light. It is the only green. In the deeper chambers, where the water drips and the light barely reaches, there are threads of something that might be lichen or might be something else — they are orange, the orange of rust or old blood, and they hang in vertical lines from the ceilings, always vertical, always parallel, as though they were spun rather than grown. They do not respond to air movement. They do not sway.

You touch one once. It retracts into the ceiling with a speed that makes you pull your hand back, and then, after a long stillness, it extends again, exactly as before.

---

The terraces are built, but the word *built* feels wrong — it implies intention, plan, a maker holding a picture in mind. This is more like accretion. The walls are fitted stone, dry-laid, no mortar, each block shaped by hand and fitted against its neighbors with a precision that seems impossible for the technology on display. There are no metal tools here. The marks on the stone are the marks of other stone — chert, perhaps, or obsidian, hammerstones and grinders. The work is slow-work, generational, the kind of construction that happens across lifetimes where each builder adds only what they can carry and shape in a day and the wall continues after them.

But the layout has no logic you can find. Chambers open into chambers. Some have no entrance except from above, through a hole in the ceiling that lets water in and light down. Passages narrow until you must turn sideways, then widen into rooms that could hold twenty. There are shelves carved into the walls at heights that do not correspond to human use. There are basins that drain into channels that drain into other chambers, the water moving always, circulating, as though the entire structure were a single slow machine for moving water through itself. Which, perhaps, it is. Perhaps that is all it ever was.

The erosion tells you it is old. The stone has softened at its edges. The channels are worn deeper than carving alone would account for — the water has been flowing here for long enough to have its own geology. The moss is established in its places; the orange threads have colonized every ceiling surface that drips. The structure has reached some equilibrium with its own decay, each wet season taking a little and each dry season (there are dry seasons, supposedly, though you cannot believe it) leaving it be. It is in balance the way a spinning top is in balance: not still, not stable, but continuing.

---

The fauna is where the strangeness concentrates. The terraces are full of life, but the life is wrong in ways that are difficult to pin down until you have watched for a long time.

There are creatures in the water channels. They are small — the length of a finger, the width of a worm — and they are translucent, their internal structures visible as darker lines within their pale bodies. They move against the current, always uphill, always climbing. When they reach the source of a channel, where the water emerges from the rock, they press themselves against the fissure and stay. You have watched them do this. They do not feed. They do not rest. They simply press against the place where the water enters the system, and after a time — hours, perhaps, or days — they dissolve. Their bodies come apart in the current and flow outward with the water, and where they dissolve, the water runs darker for a moment, and the moss grows thicker in that place downstream.

There are birds, perhaps, or things like birds. They are white and silent and they move through the chambers in patterns that seem choreographed — groups of five or seven entering a room, circling once, exiting through a different passage, always in motion, never settling. They do not have visible eyes. Their wings make no sound. They are the only thing here that is dry.

And in the deeper rooms, the ones where the orange threads hang thickest and the water pools rather than flows, there are the crustaceans. They are crab-like but not crabs — their shells are the same dark basalt as the walls, rough and porous, and they are motionless for so long that you take them for stones. Then one moves. It unfolds from a crevice with a slowness that makes each second feel like a minute, extends legs like thin black branches, and walks to the pool's edge. It lowers itself into the water. It stays. And as you watch, its shell begins to lighten, the dark rock-color leaching away, and you understand that it is dissolving too — more slowly than the channel-creatures, but in the same way, feeding something back into the water, returning something to the system.

Nothing here dies in the way you understand dying. Everything dissolves. Everything enters the water. The water carries it onward, and where it deposits, something else grows.

---

The sound, or the absence of it, is what finally unsettles you. The place should be loud — that much water, that much motion, that many creatures. But the stone absorbs sound. The wet moss absorbs sound. The air, so saturated, seems to absorb sound. What reaches you is a composite hush: the drip, the flow, the movement of bodies through water, all of it compressed into a single soft complexity that is more texture than noise. You can distinguish nothing. It is like listening to rain from inside a room with no windows — you know the rain is there, you can sense its totality, but you cannot hear a single drop.

When the bird-things pass near you, there is a brief displacement of air and then silence again. When the crustaceans move, there is a faint wet sound like a tongue against stone, and then silence. Even your own footsteps are swallowed. You become quieter here. You begin to move carefully, not because the ground is treacherous (it is, slightly, but you could manage), but because silence seems to be the correct behavior. As though noise were a violation of some compact the place has made with itself.

---

The turbulence is not visible, exactly, but you feel it. The water does not flow in consistent directions — it shifts. A channel that runs north in the morning runs east by afternoon. The orange threads retract and extend on schedules you cannot predict. The moss appears in new places overnight, pale rosettes blooming in spots that were bare stone the day before. The crustaceans move in patterns that seem to correspond to nothing — not temperature, not light, not water volume. The bird-things change their choreography: groups of five become groups of three, their circling reverses direction, they enter rooms they previously avoided.

The terraces are in constant reconfiguration, but the reconfiguration is slow and subtle enough that you doubt it unless you are watching carefully. Which, perhaps, is the point. Which, perhaps, is what the place requires: long attention, stillness, the willingness to notice that a wall has grown wetter or a channel has shifted its flow by two degrees. The system is turbulent the way weather is turbulent — not chaotic, but sensitive, responsive to variables too numerous to track, finding new equilibriums as quickly as it abandons old ones.

You begin to understand that the terraces are not a ruin. They are not a remnant of something that was. They are ongoing. The structure, the water, the organisms, the dissolution, the deposition — all of it is one process, one slow turbulent event, and you are inside it. It does not care about you. It does not ignore you. It incorporates you. Your breath adds moisture to the air. Your body heat shifts the temperature in a chamber by some fractional degree. The channel-creatures in the wall near where you sleep climb toward you through the night, pressing against the stone from the inside, and you do not know what they want.

---

On the last morning, you notice something new. In a chamber you have passed through a dozen times, where the water runs fastest and the stone is barest, there is a mark on the wall. It is low, near the floor, and it is fresh — the rock is lighter where something has worn it, a groove about the length of a hand and the width of a finger. It could be a tool mark. It could be the beginning of a new channel.

It could be a word.

You kneel. You touch it. The stone is cold and wet under your fingers, and the groove is smooth, polished, as though whatever made it passed over the spot many times.

Above you, the white sky pours light into the terraces. The water moves. The creatures dissolve. The orange threads hang motionless in their parallel rows. And the terraces continue their slow, wet, turbulent becoming — building and eroding and building again, a city that has never been finished because it has never been meant to be finished, a machine with no product, a mouth that speaks only in the language of water and stone and patient, silent change.
