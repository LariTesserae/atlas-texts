You ascend, and the world thins.

At 2.9 elevation, the sky is not above you; it is a surrounding medium, a cold indigo ocean that presses against the mountain's shoulders. The air here is rarefied, sharp, tasting of ozone and pulverized granite. This is a realm of extreme verticality—a sprawling, jagged spine of black basalt and pressure-fractured quartz that extends for hundreds of miles, a regional-scale wall separating the lower clouds from the void. The peaks are not singular spires but a vast, interlocking ruin of razor-backed ridges and plunging cirques, open and sprawling, yet desperately empty.

The emptiness is the first thing that registers. Despite the regional scale of the landscape, there is a near-void of spatial density. The distances between the monumental peaks are measured in hours of breathless traversal. You can see for eighty miles in any direction, and in that vast expanse, there is almost nothing. No shelters. No roads. No cairns. The built environment is absolute zero; humanity has left no artifacts here, not even a rusted piton or a faded strip of surveyor's tape. It is untouched by hand.

And yet, it is not untouched by machine.

The weirdness of the place manifests in the industrial infrastructure that grows from the rock like a cold, metallic fungus. There are no buildings, no operators, no settlements—only the machines. Massive, riveted iron ducts, painted a peeling, matte black, snake up the leeward sides of the mountains, bypassing avalanche chutes and clamping onto the stone with rusted pitons the size of trees. These are the breathers. At the summit of the highest ridge, an automated extraction array stands sentinel: a lattice of steam-cracking towers and intake fans, their blades rotating slowly in the high-altitude gales. They are electrified; thick, insulated cables droop between the peaks like the webbing of some colossal, iron spider, humming with a latent, lethal voltage. The machines do not mine the rock. They mine the atmosphere itself, condensing the rare isotopes hidden in the stratosphere, operating on scales of production that demand this extreme, inaccessible elevation. 

It is cold. At 0.4 temperature, the cold is not a weather condition; it is the baseline fabric of reality. It is a cold that does not bite, but rather infiltrates, turning the moisture in your lungs to frost before you can exhale. The darkness (0.3) is profound. The sun is a weak, distant rumor, blotted out by a permanent deck of dense, slate-grey cirrus. The world is cast in a twilight of deep indigo and shadow, where the only illumination comes from the stroboscopic, slow-blinking amber lights on the extraction arrays, and the faint, sickly bioluminescence of the local ecology.

Against all logic, in this freezing, dark, empty altitude, the vegetation is lush. It is a parasitic, impossible lushness. The flora has tapped into the industrial infrastructure. Thick, fleshy vines of a deep, bruised purple—the *cryovines*—coil around the hot exhaust pipes of the breathers, stealing the residual warmth. They form dense, hanging curtains over the iron grating, their leaves waxy and translucent, weeping a viscous, anti-freezing sap. Where the electrical cables droop, luminous mosses colonize the insulated rubber, feeding off the electromagnetic fields, casting a faint, greenish halo around the wires. In the crevices where the machines bolt into the stone, massive, sponge-like lichens grow, their pale, branching structures mimicking the ductwork they cling to. It is a fully realized symbiotic ecosystem: the machines bleed heat and radiation; the flora drinks it and colonizes the metal.

The dampness (1.1) is a stealthy thing. It does not rain here; instead, the machines condense the high-altitude mist. Inside the massive iron ducts, the humidity is trapped, pooling and dripping from the rivets. A constant, cold rain falls directly beneath the exhaust pipes, feeding localized pockets of dense, mossy growth—a micro-oasis in the freezing void. Elsewhere, the moisture simply hangs as an irradiated rime, coating every surface in a slick, freezing glaze.

The fauna is abundant, though unseen at first glance. They are drawn by the warmth of the pipes and the chemical runoff from the extractors. Wingless, blind alpine springtails swarm the heated patches of rock, their carapaces clicking softly against the iron. Packs of *vent-crawlers*—segmented, multi-legged crustaceans the size of dogs, pale as cave worms—scurry along the ductwork, picking at the freeze-thaw cycles of the metal. Above, in the dark updrafts, vast, membranous gliders ride the thermal exhausts from the factories. They have no eyes, only complex, feathery antennae that navigate the electric hum and the chemical traces. They are predators, and the ecosystem pulses with their unseen, high-altitude chases.

Despite this dynamic biological and mechanical engine, the world is nearly silent (0.2). The thin air refuses to carry sound. The massive extraction fans spin, but they rotate so slowly—geared down for torque—that they produce only a subsonic thrum, a vibration felt in the molars rather than heard. The fauna is deaf and mute; the springtails communicate through seismic taps that are lost in the vastness of the stone. The loudest sound is the occasional, rhythmic *drip-drip* of condensation falling from a heated pipe into a freezing puddle, echoing once before being swallowed by the open, hollow space.

Erosion (1.5) is in a state of ancient balance. The mountains have stood in this exact, jagged form for eons. The freeze-thaw cycles have found their equilibrium; the rock is weathered, settled, and long-established in its own state. The only disruption is the industrial, and even that is being slowly swallowed by the ice and the vines. The rust is patient. The cold is patient. 

You stand on a ledge of fractured quartz, looking down a cirque of black rock draped in purple vines and rusted iron. The wind does not howl; it merely pushes, a solid force of cold pressure moving you toward the edge. Below, the amber light of a processing unit blinks, slow as a sleeping heart. The gliders turn in the dark above it, tracing invisible rings around the heat. The world is a paradox: a frozen, empty altitude where life and industry have mated in the dark, producing something vast, quiet, and deeply strange.
