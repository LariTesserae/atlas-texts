# The Green Rise

You ascend for days before you reach it—not climbing, exactly, but *rising*, as if the ground itself swells upward in a long, slow breath that has never finished exhaling. The elevation is extreme, but you cannot see a peak. There is no summit, only a vast convexity: a plateau that curves like the back of some buried titan, rising so gradually that your legs ache before your eyes register the slope. Clouds scrape past at eye level. The air thins, yet stays warm—pressed and heated by the sheer mass of living matter below you, a biological furnace the size of a mountain range.

## What Grows Here

Everything is plant. Not *covered* in plants—*composed* of them. The ground beneath your feet is root. The horizon you see is canopy. The air you breathe is transpiration given form. There is no mineral substrate exposed anywhere, no bare rock, no soil that isn't more mycelium than silt. You stand on a mat of vegetation so old and so deep that its lower layers have compressed into something like peat, something like coal, something like a memory of swamp that never quite became stone. The surface yields slightly with each step, springy and uncertain, a footbridge of living matter over an abyss of its own dead ancestors.

The vegetation is not tall in the way of forests you have known. It is *deep*. The canopy—if you can call it that—rises only thirty or forty meters overhead, a roof of interlocked broad-leaved stolons that have grown horizontal for millennia, seeking light in a place where light falls at an angle the sun itself seems confused by. But below you, the biomass continues downward for hundreds of meters, layer on layer, each stratum a different epoch of growth. You walk on the skin of an organism that has been adding to itself since before your species learned to chip stone. The erosion parameter says *ancient*, and you feel it: this is a landscape that has arrived at its final form. It will not change again. It has worn itself into perpetuity.
