The plateau sits so high that the clouds bruise against it, trailing grey rags across the stone. The air is thin and bitingly cold, carrying that particular high-altitude hollowness that makes breath visible and short. Yet against all expectation, this elevation is not sharp or craggy. The peaks were ground down eons ago. What remains is a vast, flat escarpment—ancient beyond reckoning, eroded to its final, immutable form. The mountains that once stood here are ghosts; only their roots remain, planed smooth by ice and wind into a pale, almost featureless plain of fractured granite and exposed quartz veins.

At the plateau's edge, where the land sheers away into kilometers of nothing, sits a city. Or rather, the memory of one.

It is small—cathedral-sized, a dense cluster of structures huddled within a natural bowl of the stone—and impossibly old. No tools built this place. No chisels shaped these blocks. The walls are grown from the rock itself, massive interlocking slabs of granite and feldspar that bubbled up from the depths in some primordial architectural fever, forming rooms, corridors, archways, and stairs that lead to nowhere. The construction is organized, even urban in its density—streets align, doorways frame views, galleries echo with proportion—but it is entirely biological. The stone grew this way, the way coral grows, cell by mineral cell, secreting its own habitation. And then it stopped. The city died, or matured, or simply finished whatever it was doing, and settled into the silence of the deeply aged.

Now it stands empty. Not merely uninhabited—*empty*, in the theological sense. The hollow spaces between the grown walls are cavernous. A single wind moves through the corridors like a resident, and it never ceases. The sound is enormous. The plateau's flatness offers no resistance, and the air funnels through the stone streets and shattered arches with a roar that never fully silences. It rises and falls in pitches that seem almost intentional—drones, howls, whistling keens that resonate in the fluid-filled chambers of the inner ear until the boundary between sound and vibration dissolves. You feel the wind as much as hear it. It is the loudest silence imaginable.

And yet—life persists, stubborn and strange. The cold is pervasive, but moisture clings to the stone in ways that defy the wind's assault. Condensation weeps from the ceilings of the inner galleries, pooling in shallow depressions worn into the floors over millions of years. In the most sheltered crevices, where the roar of the wind drops to a whisper, a strange ecology has taken hold. Mosses and lichens grow in profusion—not the sparse, crusty lichens of alpine desolation, but lush, improbable mats of deep green and violet, thick as carpet, damp to the touch. They thrive on the condensation alone, drinking from the air, building tiny humid microclimes in the lee of grown-stone buttresses. Ferns uncurl from the joints between walls, their fronds heavy with caught droplets. In some interior chambers, where the wind cannot reach at all, the air is still and almost warm with vegetal respiration, and the dampness beads on every surface like a slow, perpetual rain that falls upward from the stone.

The sounds within these green chambers are different. Water dripping—*tink, tink, tink*—into ancient pools. The soft compression of moss underfoot. Occasionally, a deep groaning from the city itself, as thermal contraction shifts a megalithic block by a fraction of a millimeter, and the whole structure sighs like a sleeping organism.

Fauna is almost absent. There are no birds. No rodents scurry in the walls. But in the deepest interior, where the lushness is thickest and the condensation forms actual rivulets down the grown-stone walls, pale things move. Eyeless, legless, the color of quartz—some relict species of amphibian or something older, that emerged in the deep cold and never left. They are rare beyond rare. You might live a year in this place and see only one, slipping soundlessly into a crack, leaving only a smear of wet on the lichen. They are the city's only citizens.

The weather cycles without reference to any observer. Fog rolls in from the void below and saturates the stone, then burns off as the pale sun clears the horizon. The condensation swells, then freezes as the temperature drops, coating the inner walls with frost flowers that sublime slowly over the cold mornings. The mosses expand and contract with the moisture. The wind shifts direction twice daily as the thermal gradients below the plateau invert and re-invert. The city breathes with it—pressure rising in the corridors, then falling, the sound rising from a moan to a shout and back again.

This is a place at the end of its story. Not dying—dying implies movement toward death. This place has already arrived. It is the final form of something that began before the first frost, before the first wind, before the concept of tool or fire. It simply *is*. The grown-stone city on the eroded plateau, roaring with wind, green with impossible life, cold and damp and almost empty, going through its ancient motions with the patience of geology.

There is a strange comfort in it. Nothing here will ever change again. Nothing here can be threatened, because it has already survived everything. The eyeless amphibians know this. The moss knows this. The wind, which has blown through these corridors since before the corridors were fully formed, certainly knows this.

It is the loudest, oldest, most alive dead place you will ever stand in.
