It begins dimly, a shade of sepia and rust seen through closed eyelids. Then the thought opens its eyes.

There is no sky, just the distant, vaulted ceiling of a place so vast it has its own weather. The air is not thin, precisely, but it is empty. For every cubic meter of space, there might be only a few motes of matter, and yet it feels heavy, laden with immense heat and the low, grinding thrum that is the place's only constant sound. This is the hum of the Great Engine, the sound of life itself, which is not life as any water-worlder would recognize it.

The heat is a physical presence, a pressure against the skin that is more like a weight than warmth. It is a dry, mineral heat, the heat of a stone that has lain under a fictional sun for a thousand years. It is the temperature of incubation.

You stand on a platform, or what was once a platform. The ground underfoot is a latticework of a dull, brassy metal, the material of this world. It is etched with the fine, web-like patterns of age, not the sharp chaos of rust but the slow, organized bloom of crystallization. The metal is expanding and contracting with a geological patience, and the sound of its straining is part of the великий шум—the Great Noise. Looking down, the grid recedes into a gloom that is not black, but a deep, colorless absence, an internal void. There is no bottom. There is only more of the Great Engine.

The vegetation does not grow from soil. It erupts. From the seams and joints in the metal flooring, from the walls of colossal, distant pillars, a lushness improbably unfolds. These are not plants. They are mineral growths, lithoflora. The most common are the Rust-Fans, great fronds of a flaky, deep-copper material that slowly uncurl from a nodular base. They are silent, brittle, and under the dim illumination, they glow faintly with an internal heat, like embers that never cool. Other forms protrude like crystalline cacti, their facets slowly turning to catch a light that isn't there, their surfaces shimmering with a rainbow of greys. There are no colors, only shades of heat: the bruised purple of a super-heated joint, the dull orange of a new growth, the blood-red near-incandescence at the heart of a particularly vigorous colony. The air smells of hot metal, ozone, and a sweet, cloying scent like overheating clay.

The whole world is urban, a city without citizens. It is a snarl of causeways, buttresses, and plazas, all wrought from the same brassy, weathered metal. The scale is impossible to judge. A wall rising into the dimness could be the side of a single building or the hull of the machine that contains this entire reality. Everything is constructed with a purpose that is now unfathomable, its logic eroded by millennia of unceasing function. This is a technology so primitive it has become god-like, a simple machine whose gears are the size of mountain ranges and whose purpose is simply… to be.

For this place is active. The Great Noise is not static. It is a symphony of processes. Walk in any direction and the dominant tone shifts. Here, a low, percussive rhythm, like an iron heart the size of a hemisphere, beats slowly. Over there, a high-pitched, keening whistle as super-heated gas is vented from a distant, unseen pipe. On a catwalk that groans with thermal stress, you can feel a rhythmic *shudder* pass through the floor, a ripple of force that makes the very air vibrate. And always, there is the sound of the growth. It is a sound like a thousand tiny needles tapping on a sheet of glass, the constant, microscopic cracking and popping as the lithoflora push new crystals through the metal matrix. It is a loud world, a world that never stops shouting its own existence.

Its weirdness lies not in aliens or magic, but in its absolute, resolute defiance of all known biology. The ecology is one of metallurgy and thermodynamics. The "soil" is the structure itself. The "water" is thermal energy, flowing like a river of heat from the core of the machine to its outer edges, where it dissipates into the gloom. The lithoflora are the primary producers, converting heat and raw material into complex mineral structures. There are no animals to eat them, no herbivores or carnivores. There is only the slow, inexorable process of construction and erosion. A bridge, over eons, will sag and deform, mineral growths colonizing its stress points, until it becomes a tunnel, a cliff-face, a ravine. An empty plaza will slowly crust over with new formations, becoming a forest of silent, glowing stone. The city is continuously rebuilding itself, but not for anyone. It is simply the manifestation of the Engine's function.

The light is dim, a constant ambient luminescence that seems to emanate from the heated air and structures themselves. Shadows are long and soft, bleeding into one another. There are no sharp lines. In the deepest gloom, where the structure folds in on itself, the growing crystals may cast a faint, colored glow, the only points of brightness in the sepia-toned world. This is a pocket world, a self-contained environment built inside of some greater thing—a starship's forgotten life-support? A god's discarded furnace? A tesseract-garden left to its own devices? It doesn't matter. It is a room that has become its own universe, empty of everything except for its own hot, loud, and unending life. To be here is to be an intruder on a process so much older and grander than oneself, a tiny warmth-witness to the slow, grinding apotheosis of a machine that dreams in rust and crystal.
