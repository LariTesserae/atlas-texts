# The Drowned Cloister

The water does not move. It hangs, thick and warm, saturating everything so completely that the concept of wetness loses meaning — there is no dry to contrast it against, no surface where air meets liquid. This is not flooding. This is the permanent condition. The world is bath.

---

The place is small. Walk the perimeter and you would return in four hundred paces. It has boundaries — not theoretical ones, but walls. Real walls. Everything here was made. The floor underfoot, the columns that rise in close ranks, the low arching ceiling barely three body-lengths above — all of it constructed. Hewn from something like stone but denser, darker, shot through with veins of mineral that have no name because no one remains to name them. The tool marks are still visible. Chisel edges. Grooves where a blade ran a thousand times along the same line, patient, manual, grinding away material a fraction of a hair's width at a pass. Whoever built this worked with hand-tools and persistence. No machine assisted. The evidence of that labor is everywhere — in the slight irregularity of planes that should be flat, in the deep grooves where a worker grew tired and pressed too hard, in the corners that almost meet at right angles but don't quite.

The craftsmanship is primitive. The ambition was not.

---

It sits high. This is the strange thing, the thing that takes time to understand. The water presses from all sides, warm and heavy, yet the space has the quality of altitude — not the thin-air feeling of mountains, but something internal. A spatial elevation. As though the cloister occupies a register above the surrounding sea, perched on some submerged plateau or raised on a foundation of the same dark stone that walls it in. The pressure should be immense at this depth, but it isn't. The water is merely present, volumetric, a warm medium that fills space the way air fills a room in the world above. You do not feel crushed. You feel *elevated*. Suspended in a high place that happens to be liquid.

Perhaps the builders understood something about pressure that allowed them to create this pocket of calm in the deep. Perhaps the stone itself does something to the water around it — rejects it, barely, holds it at a density that permits warmth and stillness where cold and crushing force should prevail. The mineral veins in the walls pulse faintly with retained heat. The stone is warm to the touch, if you touch it, and you can touch it easily — the walls are close, the columns thick and numerous, spaced so near each other that you must turn sideways to pass between some of them. Density. Everything here is packed tight. Surfaces crowd. The eye finds no horizon, no distance, only the next column, the next wall, the next surface receding into darkness.

---

And it is dark. Not the absolute dark of the deep ocean — something else. A darkness with texture, with layers. The water itself carries a faint luminescence, biological and mineral both, but it is weak, barely enough to define shapes. The columns emerge from gloom like trees in a wood at the bottom of twilight. The ceiling is more felt than seen — a presence overhead, a weight of constructed stone holding back the infinite water. What light exists seems to come from the things that live here.

---

They are everywhere. Fauna, abundant, filling the spaces too small for larger bodies to enter. Not fish, though some are fish-shaped in the way that necessity makes shapes — streamlined, finned, built for moving through warm liquid in tight quarters. But they are wrong in the details. Their surfaces are not scaled or skinned but *ceramic*, bonded somehow to the stone of the walls, growing from it the way coral grows but with an organization that is not coral's blind accretion. They have segments. Joints. The too-precise articulation of things that were designed, or that have designed themselves into forms too regular to be accidental.

There are the small ones — finger-length, flat, disc-shaped — that cling to the walls in layers, their edges touching, overlapping like shingles. They breathe — you can see the pulse of water through their bodies, in and out, a slow rhythm synchronized across the whole colony so that the wall itself seems to inhale and exhale. When something larger passes, they flatten and darken, becoming nearly invisible against the stone.

There are the walkers — hard-shelled, many-legged, climbing the columns in spirals from floor to ceiling and back again. They move with mechanical slowness, each leg placing itself with deliberate precision, and they make a sound — the only sound, sometimes — a faint click of chitin on stone, barely audible, like a fingernail tapping teeth.

There are the drifters — translucent sacs the size of a fist, trailing filaments that extend outward, searching, interlinking. When two drifters meet, their filaments merge for a time, exchanging something — fluid, information, heat — then separate. They are the closest thing here to communication. They make no sound at all.

And there are the eels — though "eel" is a convenience, not a truth. They are long, flexible, built from segments that flex on visible hinges, their surfaces smooth and dark and reflective in the faint light. They move through the water between the columns with an ease that seems impossible in such tight space, bending through gaps that should not admit them. They are the largest things here. Some are as long as an arm, some as long as a person, and the longest ones never stop moving. They circle. They have paths they follow, circuits through the cloister that they have traced so many times they have worn grooves into the stone — shallow channels in the floor and walls where generations of segmented bodies have passed, polishing the rock to a different texture. The grooves are ancient. The stone around them has eroded to match them, settling into this configuration as its final shape. The cloister has been here so long that its architecture has been revised by the things living in it, their patient circuits becoming structural elements.

---

No plants grow here. No algae films the walls, no moss softens the corners, no kelp sways in whatever currents exist. This is not an ecosystem based on photosynthesis or its chemical equivalents. Something else feeds the drifters, the clingers, the walkers, the eels. Perhaps they eat each other — but their populations seem stable, balanced, each species present in numbers that do not fluctuate visibly. Perhaps the stone itself provides. The mineral veins carry heat, and heat can be the foundation of a food chain in the absence of light. The ceramic-shelled things may draw energy directly from the warm rock, converting temperature differential into something like nutrition. The drifters may absorb what the shelled things excrete. The eels may eat the drifters. It is a closed loop, small, dense, sufficient.

Or perhaps the system runs on something else entirely — something in the water itself, some dissolved mineral or ancient additive that the builders introduced when they made this place and filled it with liquid. The water has a taste, if you could taste it — slightly metallic, slightly warm, faintly electric, like blood without the iron's immediacy. It is not ocean water. It is not fresh water. It is a constructed medium, and it may be that everything living here depends on its specific chemistry, would die instantly if exposed to the sea outside the walls.

---

The silence is the defining quality. Sound dies here — absorbed by the water, swallowed by the dense stone, dampened by the layers of living things that cushion every surface. The click of a walker's leg. The faint rush of water through the disc-things as they pulse. The nothing-sound of an eel's segments rotating on their hinges, a sound more felt than heard, a vibration in the water that registers as pressure change rather than noise. That is all. No current. No wave. No grinding of stone or groaning of pressure. The cloister sits in acoustic suspension, a pocket of near-total quiet in the deep warm dark.

---

Time moves strangely here, or rather it doesn't move at all — it accumulates. The place is ancient beyond reckoning. The erosion tells the story: every sharp edge softened, every corner rounded, every tool-mark fading toward smoothness under the patient attention of warm water and biological chemistry. The stone has reached its final shape, or near enough that the remaining changes will be invisible on any human timescale. This is the end of a process, not the middle. The cloister has been here long enough to become what it will always be.
