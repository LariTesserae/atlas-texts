The plateau goes on for a horizon that the eye cannot reach. Up here, the curvature of the world is almost perceptible—a slight, breath-catching bow to the edges of things, where the sky sits heavy and gray upon a tableland so vast it generates weather of its own. The elevation is considerable, the air thin and cool, carrying that high-altitude sting to the lungs, yet there is no alpine cragginess here. No peaks. No dramatic thrust of stone. Instead, the terrain is flat, or flat enough—a continental shelf of ancient, compressed limestone and shale, worn down by epochs into a surface of shallow, undulating swales and ridges.

And the water. It is everywhere, though it hides. It does not rush or carve; it seeps. The rock here is porous, and the water table sits so close to the surface that the ground is in a perpetual state of weeping. You see it in the way the light catches a film of water across the stone pavements—a glaze that never fully evaporates because the atmosphere above is a lid of cool, condensing mist. After the frequent, sweeping rains—which arrive not with violence but with a slow, continental exhaustion—the water rises. It pools in the shallow depressions, creating a mosaic of mirrors stretching for miles, reflecting the low, normal light of the diffuse sun. The rock itself seems to sweat; you can press a palm to the gray stone and feel the damp pushing back, an upward pressure from an aquifer too full to contain itself.

Into this saturated expanse, they built. Not with tools—there are no devices here, no engines, no metal—but with hands and patience, stacking the loose, flat shards of limestone that the plateau offered. The construction is dense, staggeringly so, yet it does not rise. It sprawls. It is a city of low walls, of interconnected courtyards, of roofless chambers and sunken walkways, all built from the same pale gray rock so that, from a height, the settlement would be nearly invisible—a mere geometric rash on the skin of the plateau. But at ground level, it is overwhelming. The walls are near-touching. You walk through corridors barely wider than shoulders, the stone blocks slick with condensation, threaded with slow trickles of water that follow the mortar lines like veins. 

The density is layered horizontally. Deeper into the maze, you find older sections, the walls settling, tilting into one another, finding a balance they have held for centuries. The erosion here is established; nothing is actively crumbling, but everything has been weathered into its final, patient form. The edges of the stone are rounded like old teeth. There are no sharp corners left. The buildings have surrendered to the damp, accepting the moss and the slow mineral stains that streak down the facades in blacks and iron-ochres.

Vegetation is sparse, but it makes itself known in the margins. Where a wall shelters a crack from the sweeping winds, a single species of silvery, succulent leaf takes hold—low, rubbery, drinking from the air itself. There are no trees. The wind would not allow them. But in the shallow pools that form between the structures, a reed-like sedge grows in tufts, its roots threading the standing water. It is enough to soften the stone, to give a gray-green fuzz to the horizons, but it does not compete with the architecture. The rock is the dominant life here.

Fauna is rare, and when it appears, it is as a whisper of motion. A pale, amphibious thing, blind and slow, might be seen suctioning its way up a wet wall in the deepest twilight, seeking insects that also hide in the mortar crevices. Or a single, large-beaked bird might be heard—a dry, hollow *klok* echoing between the walls—though it is more often felt as a shadow passing over the water-glazed courtyards. They do not stay. The place does not feed them well.

The sound is what defines this place more than anything. It is quiet—not silent, but hushed in a way that only a vast, damp, high-altitude space can be. The wind does not howl; it presses against the walls and slides over them, producing a low, continuous friction, like a hand dragging across fabric. Beneath that, there is the sound of water: the drip from a ledge into a pool, the slow seep of a crack, the almost inaudible *tss* of rain landing on wet stone. These sounds do not break the silence; they are the texture of the silence. They layer into a constant, gentle susurrus, a rhythm that feels like the breathing of the plateau itself.

It is an active place, despite the stillness. You can feel the processes at work. The clouds sweep in, heavy and wet, dropping their loads, and the water rises an inch in the streets before slowly subsiding into the porous rock. The mist thickens and thins, revealing and hiding the endless walls. The sedge nods in the wind. The slow, blind creatures move. The city sits at the nexus of these cycles, not abandoned—abandoned implies a departure—but simply unpeopled now, maintained by the water and the wind, doing what it has always done: holding back the damp, while letting it in. 

There is no strangeness here. No surreal logic. It is simply a place at the extreme end of the possible—a city built flat against a weeping sky, dense with walls that lead to more walls, standing on a rock that will never, ever dry.
