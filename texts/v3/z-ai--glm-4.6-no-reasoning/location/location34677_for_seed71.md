The plateau goes by many names in the dialects that fringe its edges, but most translate to some variation of *the Old Floor*. It is a continental shelf of exposed primordial stone, worn down by billions of years into a near-perfect, featureless flatness. The elevation is staggering—high enough that the atmosphere thins into a bruised, violet-tinged rarity, and the horizon curves visibly, a subtle bend to the world. Yet, there is no jagged peak, no triumphant summit. The erosion parameter here has reached its absolute terminus: the mountains that once stood here were ground to dust before the first cells divided in the seas below. What remains is an endless, flattened plain of gray-veined gneiss, stretching further than a human mind can comfortably hold.

It is deeply, profoundly cold. The temperature hovers always just above the point where the air would crystallize, a biting, humid chill that seeps into the marrow. Moisture is intermittent but persistent. It does not rain in the conventional sense; instead, the vast, cold air masses push against the plateau, squeezing out a constant, heavy condensation. Beads of ice-water form on every available surface, dripping in a slow, arrhythmic syncopation. Pockets of standing water gather in the slight depressions of the ancient stone—shallow, mirror-still tarns that reflect the bruised sky with perfect, unsettling clarity.

Against all ecological logic, the place is lush. But it is a lushness of the cold and the damp, a hyper-dense, creeping biology that ignores the absence of fauna entirely. There are no animals. No insects hum, no birds call, no predators stalk. The silence is a heavy, physical presence, broken only by the drip of condensation, the low whistle of thin wind, and the wet, expanding sound of growing things. The vegetation is a mono-culture of deep, cold-weather mosses and sprawling, rootless lichens. They drape over the flat terrain in towering, undulating carpets of black-green, indigo, and bruised magenta. Massive fungal-like brackets, tough as leather and covered in fine, freezing dew, erupt from the stone in sprawling geometries. It is a thriving, respiring ecosystem locked in a state of perpetual, silent inflation.

Scattered across this boundless flatness, separated by leagues of moss-draped stone, are the settlements. They are urban, organized, and layered—testament to a society that once engineered with purpose—but they are sparse, built on a scale that makes them islands in the vegetative sea. The construction is primitive, crafted from the only available materials: the shattered megaliths of the plateau and the dense, petrified peat of the mosses. There are no metals, no mortars, no wheels. The architecture relies on colossal interlocking stones, balanced with prehistoric precision, and massive bundles of woven lignin-like stalks lashed with gut-sinew. The buildings are sprawling, multi-tiered ziggurats of bare rock and bio-matter, designed to trap the ambient condensation into complex, gravity-fed cisterns. They are towns that breathe, their porous walls wicking up the constant damp and blooming with the same dark, quiet moss that surrounds them. 

But the settlements are empty. Not ruined by war, nor fallen to disease, but evacuated. And the reason why is etched into the fabric of the place itself.

The weirdness here is not a visitor; it is the underlying physics of the plateau, a localized breakdown of reality pushed to the absolute threshold. The world is deeply aged, and at the end of its life, the rules loosen. Space grows thin. The vast scale of the plateau means these localized fractures propagate across hundreds of miles, creating a landscape where geometry and causality are suggestions, not laws.

Walking across the plateau, one might notice a distant ziggurat reflected not in a tarn, but in the empty air itself, hovering upside down a mile above the stone. The constant, heavy condensation sometimes falls upward, pulling a mist of freezing droplets toward a tear in the sky that shimmers like a closed, wet eye. The dense, lush moss occasionally flickers, revealing for a fraction of a second a completely different substrate—perhaps burning sand, perhaps a void of hard light—before settling back into its cold, damp reality. 

In the deeper chambers of the stone settlements, where the primitive architects built massive, load-bearing corridors to hold back the weight of the ancient plateau, the spatial breakdown is worst. A corridor might take four days to walk down, but only ten minutes to walk back. A room might contain a pocket of weather—a localized, perpetual snowstorm contained entirely within a twenty-foot cube, while the rest of the chamber remains damp and still. Sometimes, a person standing at a crossroads will see their own back, walking away from them down an intersecting hall, the distance between them stretching into impossible leagues.

It is an active landscape, alive with the slow, terrifying mechanics of decay. The weather cycles churn, the condensation flows, the moss respirates, inching outward, slowly digesting the stone towns. But the most active process is the quiet, constant unraveling of the coordinates. The world is settling into its final form, and its final form is a flattening not just of stone, but of meaning. Distances compress and elongate. Time stutters in the presence of large bodies of water. 

The people who built those primitive, interlocking towns came here to study the end of the world. They left their cisterns and their terraces behind because, eventually, the plateau stopped leading anywhere. The paths back down the cliffs dissolved into non-Euclidean loops. The towns remain, populated only by the creeping moss, the dripping condensation, and the slow, silent folding of space upon itself.
