There is a single room, or what a visitor might first mistake for a room. It is roughly the size of a small cathedral, its boundaries defined not by stone walls but by the.tight, interlaced substance of the world itself. This is the Grove, and it is a paradox of screaming emptiness.

The air is warm and thick, holding a perpetual, equatorial humidity that has no source in a visible sky but seems to emanate from everything. It’s a dampness that invites fungi, that slicks every surface with a fine, greasy condensation. But this is a dry-damp; there are no rivers, no pools. The occasional rain is a bizarre event: a single, prolonged drop of water, the size of a tear, will form out of the atmosphere and hang in the stillness for minutes before its own weight pulls it down. It strikes a surface with a soft, audible *plink*, the only quiet punctuation in the cacophony.

The substrate is not ground, not earth, not rock. It is plant. A single, continuous, living thing. Imagine a vast, slow tide of dark, woody material that has cooled and solidified in place, forming floors, buttresses, and high, vaulted ceilings. This is the Root-Mass. Its surface is a landscape of its own: knuckles of woode-grain forming hills, shallow valleys filled with a fine, phosphorescent moss that glows with a soft, internal green light, and deep, chasmal fissures that drop into an absolute, lightless black. The light is normal in the sense that there is no sun or moon, yet the entire space is evenly illuminated by this glowing moss, by the faint, pulsing luminescence of fungal veins that trace through the Root-Mass like a nervous system, and by the creatures themselves.

Vegetation is total. There is no bare, inert matter. The very air you breathe is thick with a pollen so fine it’s a shimmering, gold-tinged haze. From the ceiling hang great, leathery fronds that do not swing or stir, but slowly, almost imperceptibly, unroll and retract over the course of hours, their edges glowing with a softer, warmer light. In the crevices of the Root-Mass, flowers bloom that are not flowers but mouths, their petals lined with vibrating, iridescent cilia that catch the light and disperse it in shimmering, rainbow-ed sprays. They bloom and die within a single, mournful note of the Grove’s symphony. The space is full, yet utterly empty. This is the heart of the paradox: the scale is small, bounded by the Grove’s walls, but the density is near-void. You could walk for an hour in a straight line and encounter nothing but the featureless, rolling expanse of the Root-Mass floor. Vast, cathedral-spanning arches of the living structure hang overhead, with nothing between them but the damp, hazy air. The sheer volume of the space makes the relatively sparse presence of features feel like a profound hollowness.

It is the sound that defines the place. It is deafening. A three-point-zero on a scale that ends at three.

There is no moment of silence. The sound is a continuous, layered, ever-shifting roar. It begins with the hum. A low, gut-thrumming C-sharp that feels less like hearing and more like a standing wave in your own skeleton. This is the metabolic drone of the Root-Mass itself, the sound of unimaginably slow digestion and growth. It is the foundational bass note of the world. Woven through the hum is the chorus. It is the sound of the fauna, for they are abundant, and they are all singers.

The dominant form of life is the Chime-Moth. They range in size from your thumb to the span of your outstretched arms. Their bodies are faceted, crystalline structures,exoskeletons of thin, translucent amber. Their wings are not membrane but sound-boards, broad and kite-like, stretched over ribs of what looks like spun glass. They do not flap their wings to fly, but to sing. By contracting and vibrating their wing muscles, they generate notes: pure, flute-like tones that hang in the air like arrows. The multitudes of them create a cloud of dissonant yet strangely melodic music that fills the void. This is the chorus.

But the chorus is not alone. There are the Clicker-Beetles, armoured things that scuttle in legions across the Root-Mass, their carapaces striking the woody floor with a syncopated, percussive clicking that sounds like a million typewriters at once. They move in unseen waves, the noise rising and falling as they pass beneath you. There are the Moaners, long-bodied, serpentine creatures that hang from the ceiling fronds by their prehensile tails. They are albinos, corpse-white, skin-semitransparent, and they produce a single, mournful, sustained note that moans and wavers. When a Moaner dies, its note simply stops, and that cessation is sometimes more jarring than any sound.

The weirdness is the unreality of it all. The sound is more than sound; it *is* the ecosystem. The Clicker-Beetles do not eat plant matter; they consume the *vibrations* of the hum. Chime-Moths do not drink water; they absorb the dampness of the air, their tissues harmonizing with it. The Moaners feed on the dissonance created when a Chime-Moth’s frequency clashes unpleasantly with another’s. Predator-prey is not a matter of tooth and claw, but of frequency and resonance. A predator here isn’t a hunter, but a tuner. There are creatures called Silences, amorphous, semi-corporeal things that look like heat-haze. They move not against the wind, but against the sound. Where a Silence flows, the sound thins and warps. It creates a pocket of relative quiet, and this is its weapon. It wraps around a Chime-Moth, and the sudden lack of resonant feedback, the abrupt drop from a symphony to a near-solo, is what kills the moth, causing its crystalline body to shatter into silent, glittering dust. The Silence then consumes the echo of its final note.

There was a city once. Or something like one. "Urban" is the word that comes to mind, a ghost in the data. Here and there, clinging to the vast sides of the Root-Mass buttresses, are structures that are clearly built, not grown. But they are failing. They are grand, tiered platforms of a bleached, petrified something—bone? coral?—woven with living vines that have since grown to consume them. Ramps and walkways spiral up into the glowing mist, leading to terraces that now bloom with the mouth-flowers.

This was a city of sound. The "buildings" are resonator chambers, acoustic horns. The "infrastructure" is a network of hollow, tap-root-like tunnels running through the living substrate, designed to channel and amplify the Grove’s music. The builders of this place didn't fight the sound; they shaped it. They lived in the heart of the chord.

But the builders are gone. Or perhaps they never left. The weirdness is deep. Sometimes, in the overwhelming cacophony, a sound will emerge that is too clear, too structured to be natural—a three-part harmony rendered with impossible precision, the sound of a hammered gong with a perfect, mathematical decay. These sounds last for a moment, and are then swallowed by the roar. But their presence suggests the city is not entirely empty. Perhaps the residents are no longer biological. Perhaps they were subsumed by the ecosystem they mastered, their consciousness now just another ghostly frequency in the overwhelming song, a memory built into the hum of the world itself.
