Here, at the edge of what used to be the world, the dark is a physical substance. It does not merely fall; it condenses. You breathe it in, and it settles in the lungs like silt. This is a high place—a plateau of exposed, embryonic geology where the crust is still remembering how to be rigid—but the sky presses down with the closeness of a cave, making the altitude feel like a kind of burial.

It is cold, but it is the cold of deep space mediated by stone, a temperature that doesn't bite so much as it arrests. The landscape is just coming into being. The raw geology is a dark, glassy basalt shot through with veins of volcanic obsidian, still groaning with the strain of its own uplifting. It is a pre-formational world, unsoftened by time or root. Nothing grows here. No chlorophyll stains the dark. There is no rot, no branch, no leaf. This is a mineral kingdom, stripped of all biological softness, yet it is utterly saturated.

Water is everywhere, but it does not flow; it weeps. The atmosphere is so overburdened with moisture that it condenses constantly against the cold stone. Sweating cliffs. Weeping monoliths. The water emerges from the near-surface, beading out of the porous basalt, running in silent, gelid sheets down the facades of the city. It pools in the raw grooves of the rock, never freezing, but never warming—a perpetual, glacial slurry that soaks the air with a dampness that coats the throat.

And built into this weeping, raw geology is a density of construction that defies the age of the rock. The city clings to the plateau, a labyrinth of high, near-touching structures. The architecture is layered, vertical, suffocatingly dense—towers and struts and infrastructural webs that lean against each other like a bruised ribcage. There is no art to it, only organization, only the stark necessity of shelter stacked upon shelter. The built environment is carved from the same dark stone, making the city feel as though it were extruded from the fault lines rather than constructed upon them.

But the stone is not inert. The technology woven into the city’s infrastructure operates on principles that bend the current understanding of physics. The constructions are seeded with post-modern engineering—metamaterials that drink the ambient dark, structural fields that hold the high, dense stacks rigid against the plateau's seismic groaning. Deep within the weeping walls, nanoscale orchestrations are at work: they do not generate heat or light, but they subtly reorder the molecular lattice of the water, keeping it liquid in the freezing dark, pulling it through the stone in directed, capillary streams. The city is a machine for surviving the dark, and it breathes the water like blood.

Yet, for all its engineered endurance, the city is not alone. The fauna here is overwhelming. In the absolute dark, the absence of light is their domain. They are not animals of flesh and fur—they are something older, simpler, and far more numerous. Think of a fungal or sub-geologic biology: vast, colonial organisms made of chitinous micro-tubes and mineralized sinew, flowing through the weeping water like a living sludge. They are the fauna of the deep hydrosphere, macro-scale entities composed of billions of synchronized, simplistic components. They move through the saturated pores of the rock and the flooded subterranean streets of the city in dense, interlinked biomass. They press against the walls of the structures, a near-touching, undulating presence that radiates a strange, mucosal heat into the freezing air. There are millions of them, overlapping, crawling, pulsing—filling the city's infrastructure like a secondary circulatory system. They are not aggressive, but their sheer mass makes them an ecological force of suffocation.

And it is loud. The sound is not the chatter of life, but the sound of mass in patient motion. It is the low, subsonic thrum of the plateau’s geology shifting under its new burden. It is the ceaseless, percussive drip and rush of the weeping water echoing through the dense, vertical stacks. It is the chittering—billions of microscopic legs and hydraulic tube-feet gripping the stone in synchronization, a sound like grinding glass or a distant, relentless hailstorm. The noise reverberates off the near-touching walls, trapped in the urban density, amplifying into a deafening, low-frequency roar that you feel in your jaw and the soles of your feet.

Nothing happens quickly. The dynamics of this place are agonizingly slow. The plateau lifts a centimeter a century. The weeping water carves millimeters a millennium. The chitinous fauna takes days to traverse a single street, their simple neural networks firing in long, patient cascades. The city does not decay; it merely settles, infinitesimally, into the saturated rock. The dark does not shift; the cold does not break.

It is a landscape of terrible patience: a high, dense, urban machine forged in raw, weeping stone, standing deafeningly still in the freezing dark, while an overwhelming tide of unrecognizable, simple life flows through its bones like water through a sponge, waiting out the long, slow erosion of the world.
