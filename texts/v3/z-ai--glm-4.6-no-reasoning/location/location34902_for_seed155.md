The plateau goes by many names in the surviving municipal ledgers, but those who still work its upper terraces call it the Sapwood Reach. It sits high above the cloud line, an ancient escarpment so old that the mountain beneath it has forgotten it was ever shaped by tectonic rage. The bedrock—what little of it remains—is flattened into a primordial plain, worn down by epochs of wind and the slow, crushing embrace of roots. Elevation here does not mean crags or peaks; it means an endless, horizontal expanse of living architecture, brushing the grey belly of the sky.

There is no stone here. There is only plant.

The Reach is a total ecosystem, a pure-substrate biome where vegetation is not a feature of the landscape but the landscape itself. Towering *Corticusta* pines—whose trunks are fifty meters of dense, interlocking burl and vine—serve as the load-bearing pillars of the city. They did not grow around the infrastructure; the infrastructure was milled and hammered into them. Walkways of cured phloem stretch between the boughs, suspended hundreds of meters above the forest floor. The buildings are carved from the heartwood of still-living colossi, their rooms formed by the natural hollowing of ancient boles, their walls reinforced with riveted iron that the trees have since swallowed, the metal emerging from the bark like rusted bones piercing skin. 

The dampness here is a cold, persistent reality. It does not rain heavily, but the high altitude forces a constant condensation out of the fog. Every surface weeps. Silver beads of distilled moisture roll down the needle-clad arches, dripping in a rhythmic, polyphonic percussion into the catch-basins and aqueducts carved into the root-matter below. It is a cool, damp chill that settles into the lungs, smelling of chlorophyll, ozone, and the sour tang of cellulose rot.

Despite the immense organic mass, the space feels strangely empty. The city is sparse—a scattered archipelago of industrial districts and residential hollows separated by leagues of uninhabited, barely-tamed canopy. You can walk for an hour along the suspended tram-lines without seeing another soul. The buildings are massive, but they are few, separated by vast gulfs of dark, breathing foliage. Fauna is virtually non-existent. The insects died off centuries ago, unable to compete with the chemical defenses of the hyper-vegetation; the birds left for lower altitudes. There are no rustling undergrowths, no chittering squirrels, no predator-prey chases. The only animal is the city itself, and it is starving.

The industrial heartbeat of the Reach is deafening. Down in the lower boughs, where the mist turns to a thick drizzle, the extraction factories run non-stop. Massive bark-strippers and hydraulic pulp-crushers roar through the living wood, harvesting material to feed the boilers. The air is thick with the scream of band-saws cutting through green timber, the hiss of steam-venting pipes, and the clatter of overhead gantries hauling massive slabs of xylem. Electrical generators, bolted into the swaying trunks, hum and spark, their copper wiring tangled with the trees' own vascular systems. The sound is a relentless, mechanical assault—the grinding of gears and the shriek of stressed wood—bouncing off the fleshy walls of the arboreal canyon, trapped by the dense canopy above. 

But the noise serves another purpose. It drowns out the unreality.

The weirdness here is not a sudden intrusion; it is a structural decay, a consequence of the ancient erosion meeting the unnatural industrial extraction. The Reach has been settled so long, milled so deep, that the trees have begun to remember things that never happened to them. Reality breaks down at the edges of the industrial zones, where the sap runs too hot and the electricity leaks into the root-networks.

Walk the outer tram-lines at dusk, and you will see it. The mist takes on colors that have no names, pooling in geometric patterns that defy the wind. Shadows detach from the bark and walk independently for a few paces before melting into the condensation. The trees themselves whisper—not the creak of wood, but a modulated frequency, a garbled broadcast of old factory radio signals absorbed through the roots and regurgitated through the leaves. Sometimes, a walkway will simply lead to a place that wasn't there the day before—a district of hollowed trunks that smell of ozone and warm copper, populated by faint, flickering silhouettes that mimic the motions of long-dead mill workers. The wood grain on a doorframe might, over the course of a week, resolve into a screaming human face, only to be sanded away by the wind. 

The city is active, locked in a desperate, churning cycle of biological and mechanical motion. The trees grow; the machines cut them down. The condensation falls; the generators boil it into steam. The roots tear up the iron rails; the maintenance crews bolt them back down. It is an ecosystem of pure conflict, moving at a frantic, exhausting pace, yet going nowhere. The escarpment is too old, too flattened by time to ever shift again. 

And so the Sapwood Reach screams. It screams with the noise of its engines, it screams with the drip of cold water on iron, and it screams with the quiet, localized insanity of a wooden city that has been alive for too long, rotting and growing and dreaming of a mechanical heaven it can never reach.
