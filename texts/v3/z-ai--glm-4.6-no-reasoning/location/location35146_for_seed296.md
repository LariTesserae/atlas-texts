# The Vertigan Tiers

## Where the Mountain Learned to Build

The first thing you notice is the angle. The world tilts — has tilted for geological ages beyond counting — and everything here has spent its long existence adjusting to that tilt, accommodating it, growing into it like a body grows around a splinter it cannot remove.

You are on a mountainside. But that word fails for what this place is.

---

## The Bones of It

The elevation is extreme, but not in the way of young peaks — those dramatic, thrusting things that haven't yet learned their mortality. This mountain is ancient. Erosion has been at it so long that the sharp edges are memory. What remains is a vast, slumped shoulder of granite and gneiss, a ridge so old it has nearly flattened into a plateau in places, though it still holds enough rise to make your calves ache. The rock is deeply aged — you can see the stripes of time in its face: bands of metamorphic pressure, the ghost-rivers of mineral veins long dried, the slow exfoliation of mica shedding like dead skin from every exposed surface.

It is the end of something. The mountain is in its final form, settling into the earth from which it rose, giving back grain by grain what it once seized. And yet — and this is crucial — it is not passive. It is not dead. The processes continue. Frost wedges into cracks every cold night. Water — when it comes — finds the same paths it has found for millennia, wearing grooves deeper, carrying particles away. The mountain is actively dying, and that activity gives it a strange vitality, a persistence that is more impressive than any young peak's defiance.

You walk on surfaces that were carved before your species had language.

---

## The Water That Persists

Water is scarce here, but not absent. It manifests in the particular way of high places that have nearly worn flat: it collects, it condenses, it seeps.

Most mornings, a fog settles against the mountainside — not the dramatic fog of lower valleys, but a thin, clinging dampness that breathes out of the rock itself. The granite is porous in places, and overnight, moisture condenses in the microscopic spaces and bleeds to the surface. By dawn, every west-facing stone wears a film of wetness. You can drag your hand across a boulder and come away with a palm that glistens.

There are seeps — places where groundwater emerges from the rock face at a rate so slow you cannot see movement, only the dark stain of perpetual damp, the thin trail of biological green that follows the water down. Occasionally these seeps gather enough to form a thread of flowing water, a stream no wider than your finger, that runs for twenty meters before the mountain re-absorbs it, pulling the water back down into fractures and gravel.

Once, perhaps, there were rivers here. The topography suggests it — the wide, U-shaped valleys that now hold only intermittent creeks, the rounded cobbles scattered on slopes too high for any current flood to have deposited them. The water has retreated over geological time, finding lower paths, leaving these upper reaches to hold only what the sky gives and what the rock breathes out.

Rain comes irregularly — perhaps once every ten days, perhaps less. When it does, it is brief and cold, and the stone surfaces become treacherous. The water does not soak in; it sheets across the ancient rock and gathers in the channels that erosion has prepared, filling them for hours before they empty again. The sound of it — when it comes — is the only time this place approaches what you would call loud.

But mostly, the water is here as humidity, as condensation, as the slow drip from a seep into a stone basin that has been carved by that same drip over ten thousand years. The basin never overflows.
