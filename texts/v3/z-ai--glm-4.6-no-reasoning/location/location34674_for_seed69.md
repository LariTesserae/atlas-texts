Here, there is no sun. The concept of a single, distant point of light is a forgotten story. Instead, the world has a ceiling of light, a slow, sentient aurora that breathes across the firmament.

This is the High Mire, a plateau so vast it has its own weather, its own physics, its own tides. The elevation is immense, a continent perched in the chilled upper airs, but it is a land drowned. The ground is not so much earth as it is a sponge, a matrix of spongy, peat-like matter shot through with phosphorescent fungi and waterlogged, mineral-rich stone. It is perpetually, deeply wet. The water here doesn’t just fall from a sky; it sweats from the ground, condenses from the air, and bleeds from the rock. Pools of still, black water sit like obsidian mirrors, reflecting the ever-shifting, oil-on-water colors of the atmospheric aurora—emeralds, violets, bruised indigos, and the throbbing arterial reds of the planet’s own magnetic field.

The temperature is a strange, equable warmth, a cloying, humid bath that holds the scent of wet metal, ozone, and the sweet decay of a billion generations of succulents. The air is thick, so dense with vapor and spores that it feels like a liquid to breathe. Sound travels forever here, muted and layered. There is no true silence, only variegations of the constant, low-frequency hum—the sound of the world machine at work.

And it is a machine. The industrial nature of this place is not a recent addition; it is as ancient and established as the weathered stone it is built from and into. Scattered, not in a settlement pattern but as if sowed by a cataclysm, are the colossal shapes of the Works. They are like fossilized giants, half-swallowed by the Peat Mire. Great piston-houses the size of mountains lie on their sides, their rusted cylinder-walls overgrown with curtains of luminescent moss. The titanic, skeletal frames of conveyor systems stretch across valleys, their belts long since rotted away, leaving only the cyclopean supports that now serve as highways for the native fauna.

These machines are not dead. They are slumbering. From deep within their bowels comes the intermittent groan of stressed metal and the thrum of something that might be a pump, a heart, a geothermal tap. The tech is not electronic in a clean, silicon sense. It is industrial on a planetary scale. Gigantic bore-holes, kilometers wide, plunge into the plateau’s crust, lined with spiraling stairs and gantries, and from them, a steady column of warm, mineral-laden steam rises to meet the aurora. These steam columns are called Dancers. They waver and sway in the upper atmosphere, creating micro-climates, their own strange physics.

This is where the world’s light comes from in the absolute darkness. The planet’s magnetic field excites the gases and the metallic particulates in the steam from the Dancers, creating the aurora-ceiling. But there is more. The water itself is laced with bioluminescent plankton. Every step, every stir in a pool, sends clouds of glittering, golden specks swirling into life. The succulent vegetation—fat-leafed, dangling vines, bone-white fungi shaped like trumpets—pulses with a gentle, internal light, a soft cyan or ultramarine, a counterpoint to the frenetic strobing of the sky. This layered light creates a world without true shadows, only a depthless, shifting sense of obscurity.

Ecology has adapted to this 24-hour bioluminescence and industrial hum. Fauna is abundant and overwhelmingly strange. The primary grazers are the Geode-Backs, creatures like a cross between an armadillo and a bison, whose shells are interlocking plates of crystalline, silicon-based life. As they graze on the glowing vegetation, their shells slowly accumulate energy, glowing softly themselves. They move in ponderous herds across the great gantry-roads, their chitrous feet clanking a slow, percussive rhythm against the metal.

Predators have evolved to hunt by sound and sense, not sight. The most feared is the Resonant Maw, a creature of cartilage and adapted sonic organs. It clings to the underside of the massive conveyor-belts, hanging in upside-down worlds. It does not roar; it emits a series of focused sonic pulses that can stun a Geode-Back, shatter its crystalline shell, and then it descends on a gliding membrane to feed. The hunt is a cacophony of sharp, clean cracks and the low, mournful toll of the Maw’s distress call, which travels for kilometers.

The sound is loud, but not chaotic. It is a dense, layered symphony. There is the fundamental hum of the planetary machinery—the deep Bass Note that you feel in your teeth. Over this is the constant, percussive drip-drip-drip of water from everything, landing on a million different surfaces to create a soft, rolling sizzle. The hissing of the Dancers provides a treble line of white noise. The calls of the fauna—the metallic clicks of beetle-swarms, the chittering of things that scuttle in the Peat, the tolling of the Maws—are the melody. It is a world so loud it creates its own silences, pockets of interference zones where sound waves cancel each other out, leaving a vacuum of pure, ringing quiet that is more terrifying than any noise.

The built and the natural are inseparable. The scattered artifacts are not ruins to be explored; they are the new landscape. A crashed ore-carrier, a kilometer long, has become a dam, its ruptured hull forming a waterfall that cascades into a chasm where glowing river-weed grows thick. A habitation sphere, half-buried, its transparent hull shattered, is now a terrarium for a grove of crystalline trees that chime in the wind. People, if any remain here, would not be masters of this world, but parasites. They would live within the nooks and crannies of the Works, their dwellings lit by fungi, their power drawn from stray capacitance in the machine’s framework. They would navigate not by maps, but by the hum-dissonance of the different regions, learning the Song of the land to know where the ground is stable, where a steam column might collapse, where a Maw is hunting.

This is a world in motion, active on a geological and biological scale. The Dancers shift, their steam-columns migrating, causing warm, wet seasons and colder, dryer seasons in different regions of the Mire. The aurora-ceiling thickens and thins, plunging whole continents into deeper or brighter light. The Peat Mire itself is alive, a gelatinous entity that swallows the slow and the unworthy, its digestive acids mineralized from the deep-bore pumps. Life here is a cycle of being eaten, incorporated into the machine, and then processed back out as steam, light, and hum. It is a world that has forgotten the difference between the organic and the mechanical, a dark, warm, wet, and scintillating engine, running on its own magnificent and terrifying logic forever.
