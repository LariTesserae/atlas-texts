There is no sky here. There is only up.

The first thing to understand is the Wet. It is not a condition of weather but a quality of being, as fundamental as gravity. The air is a thick, warm broth, perpetually at the point of condensation. To inhale is to drink. Your own breath plumes before you, not with cold, but with a denser swirling of the moisture that is already thick on your skin, in your hair, beading on any exposed surface like a second, living sweat. The temperature is constant, the kind of enveloping warmth that denies the existence of night or frost, a heat that feels less like the sun’s and more like the planet’s own deep, patient pulse.

You are in the Grotto of First Things. The scale is deceptive; it feels cavernous, yet you can sense its boundaries close by, a sense of enclosure that has nothing to do with walls. It is a pocket realm, a single expression of a world still deciding what it wants to be. The elevation is a matter of geometry, not altitude. ‘Up’ is not a direction to a cold, starry void, but a denser pressure, a thickening of the dark. ‘Down’ is a direction toward the single, solid plateau that is the only floor you know.

The plateau is the only thing that is truly finished. It is a single, vast sheet of stone, dark as jet and so smoothly polished it feels like a frozen flow of oil. Underfoot, it is not abrasive but yielding, giving slightly with a soft, fleshy resistance. It is wet, of course, but the moisture does not pool; it is absorbed into the stone itself, which weeps the excess back into the air in a rhythm so slow it is geologic. This is the only truly flat thing in existence.

The rest of the landscape is raw, embryonic. Rising from the plateau are the Spires. They are not mountains in any earthly sense; they are incomplete thoughts in solid rock. They claw upward, jagged and crystalline, their surfaces a chaos of newly-split facets that have never known the touch of wind or rain. The erosion value is an absolute: these things are being born, not worn down. Some Spires are slender needles that vanish into the absolute blackness above, their tops unseen. Others are stunted, rounded buds of stone, wrapped in a skin of still-crumbling minerals. They do not stand in neat ranges; they erupt in dense, high-saturation clusters, their bases so close together you must thread your way between them, their sharp edges sometimes nearly brushing your shoulders. It is a dense, manufactured forest of stone emerging from its own mirror.

Vegetation is sparse, a testament to the youth of the place. It does not grow so much as adhere. The only form is the Lacing, a single species of something that is neither plant nor fungus. It is a/network of glistening, thread-like filaments, phosphorescently pale, that spreads thinly over the surfaces of the Spires. It does not draw from soil—there is none—but sucks its sustenance directly from the water-saturated air and the latent minerals of the raw rock. In the absolute darkness, this is the only light. It is a faint, venous glow, tracing the fractures and sharp edges of the Spires with a cold, internal luminescence. It gives the impression of a circulatory system for the stone itself, mapping the slow, invisible flow of life through the geology.

Sound is mostly quiet, the world held in a stable equilibrium. The only persistent noise is the Weep. It is not a drip or a trickle, but a soft, continuous singing sound. It comes from the Clefts. Where the Spire-clusters are densest, there are narrow, bottomless crevices that split the plateau. They are not canyons; they are fault-lines, spaces where the new reality has not yet fully congealed. From their unseen depths, the Weep rises, a sound like a thousand tiny waterfalls all playing the same mournful, endless note. It is the sound of the world’s water-table finding its level, the pressure of the Wet being released. The hum overlaps with itself, creating a low, resonant chord that vibrates in your bones, a sound you feel as much as hear. This is the only dynamic process, and it is as timeless as the stone itself.

Fauna is rare, but present. It is as unusual as the vegetation. The Glimmers are the only creatures. They are formless, silent. They appear as momentary shifts in the Lacing’s light, a brief brightening or a sudden dimming, as if a patch of the glow has decided to move. They slide over the Spire-surfaces, their passage marked only by this fluctuating aura. They are made of the same stuff as the air—warm, dense, wet—and they consume the Lacing, though so slowly you would never witness it. It is a perfect, stable ecology: the Lacing consumes the air and stone, the Glimmers consume the Lacing. Nothing is wasted.

The world is not empty, but it is untouched in the way a new-born is untouched. It is scattered with primitive, built things. They are not shelters. They are Marks. Carved by hand, with simple chisels of harder stone, into the obsidian-smooth surface of the plateau near the edges of the Clefts. The marks are abstract: spirals, nested curves, patterns of dots. They are not language, not maps, but pure notation. A record of observation. Someone was here before. Someone with eyes sensitive enough to see the Glimmers, with a mind patient enough to learn the chord of the Weep, with hands steady enough to leave a small and final record of their presence in a place that does not change.

There are no tools left. No bones. No remains. Only the Marks, testifying to a will to witness. It is the only history this place possesses, and it will be the only history it ever needs, for the Grotto of First Things is stable, in equilibrium, never changing beyond its slow, silent, self-contained emergence. The Weep will always weep. The Lacing will always glow. The Spire-clusters will always rise. The Glimmers will always drift. When you leave, the place will be exactly as it was. It is waiting for nothing, full to its own brim.
