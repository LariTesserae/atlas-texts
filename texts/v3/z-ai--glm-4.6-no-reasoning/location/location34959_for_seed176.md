The first mistake is thinking you are on a mountain. You are not. You are on the geode.

This is the world's core, or a piece of it, shorn and thrust upward through the crust of some forgotten planet. The ground is not soil, but a shattered plane of crystalline matrix. It fractures underfoot into a trillion glittering facets, most the color of amethyst and smoked quartz, but threaded with pulsing veins of raw copper and deep, arterial green. The crystals are young; there is no hint of the centuries of wind and water that could ever hope to soften their knife-edge geometry. To fall here would be to be flayed by the earth itself.

The scale is misleading. From a distance, the space looks impossibly vast—a cathedral of stone shafting into a sky the color of weak tea. But walk for half an hour and you will find its edge. The geode rises from a sea of emerald, wet fae, a mist-wreathed jungle that roars with hidden life. The crystalline plateau is not a mountain but a foundation, a single, bounded island in the sky. The air up here is warm and thick, drunk on water. It tastes of metal and ozone. It is so saturated you can almost feel its weight on your skin, a constant, gentle pressure.

The water is the engine, the source of everything. It does not fall as rain, but bleeds from the stone. The entire plateau is a weeping rock. Knuckle-sized rivulets of pure, clean water trace frantic paths across the crystal faces, chasing the veins of metal. They drip from the sharp undersides of overhangs, creating a percussive, ceaseless music. It is a loud place, but not a chaotic one. The sound is layered and dense, a constant drone of interaction. There is the high, thin *plink-plink-plink* of a thousand steady drips, the lower hum of water flowing over resonant crystal chambers beneath your feet, and beneath that, the grinding, tidal roar of the jungle at the base of the plateau.

The water does more than fall. It condenses in the heavy, warm air, coalescing on the crystal spires that grow like trees in chaotic groves across the plateau. These spires, some as thin as needles and others as thick as ancient oaks, are covered in a lush, velvet-like moss that glows with a soft, internal, emerald light. This is the densest vegetation you can imagine, not a forest of plants but a fur ofliving light, clinging to every available surface. It never thickens into true plants; it remains a slick, bioluminescent lichen, pulsing slowly, its glow a slow-fade response to the frequency of the dripping water. The ground is a treacherous carpet of this glowing moss over wet, sharp-edged crystal, a beautiful and deadly surface.

The ecology is scarce but symbiotic. The only fauna are the Skitterers, metallic insects the size of a human hand, with bodies of iridescent chitin mandible blades of the same crystal as the ground. They are almost silent, moving with unnerving speed across the crystalline terrain, their long legs finding purchase where a human would be instantly cut. They do not seem to eat the moss. They drink. They gather at the base of the water-drips, their flat faces held to the flow, mandibles clicking softly. They are the only moving things in the vast, stony stillness.

And then there is the technology. It is scattered, partial, and utterly integrated. At the center of the plateau stands the Artifact, the reason this place exists. It is a machine of impossible scale that appears small because of its setting, a single structure that could be the size of a house or the size of a mountain, its dimensions refusing to resolve. It is a masterwork of industrial alienness, a core of whirring gyroscopes nested within skeletal rings of a black, non-reflective metal. Water from a dozen large, unnatural weep-holes in the crystal floor is routed into its base through channels of seamless, blue-lit glass. Inside its heart, you can see the water flash-steam, spinning turbines that in turn rotate the gyroscopes with a low, powerful thrum that you feel more than hear. The machine serves no obvious purpose. It is not generating power for any city; there is no city. It is not mining; the process creates no waste. It simply *is*. It is a process made manifest, a slow, patient engine that seems to be holding the geode aloft, or perhaps keeping the jungle at bay. The slight tremor underfoot, the dynamic quality of the place, is the slow, faithful heartbeat of this machine.

The light is deceptively normal. The sky above is a uniform, pale grey, so diffuse that shadows are soft and indistinct, despite the sharp features of the landscape. During the long day, the light is flat and merciless, making all the glittering facets of the ground a blinding, fractured mirror. At night, the true light emerges. The moss glows brighter, the copper veins in the ground faintly pulse, and the Artifact’s heart becomes a lantern of cold, blue-white energy. The jungle below becomes a carpet of phosphorescence writhing in the mist, its roars softened by distance into a constant, surf-like hiss.

Living here would mean learning new rules. Time is measured by the slow rotation of the Artifact’s outer ring, a cycle that takes days. Silence is an impossibility; the only respite from the sound of water is inside the machine’s direct hum. Don’t touch the ground without thick-soled footwear. Don’t approach the Skitterers when they are drinking. Most importantly, never mistake the place’s beauty for safety. This is a construction, a single, zealous, and ongoing process happening in a sky that is too warm and too wet, a piece of the world’s sharp and glittering machinery slowly turning, forever, over a jungle that waits.
