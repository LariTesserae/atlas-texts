It begins not with a vision, but with a sensation: the cool, humid pressure of the air, heavy with the scent of damp stone and the sweet, loamy breath of moss. The light is ordinary, a soft, grey daylight as if filtered through a perpetual, high-altitude mist, but it seems to be eaten more than it illuminates. You are in a place of boundaries.

This is the Verdant Ward, a name etched onto a rusted plaque at its only entrance, though no one who lives here uses it. To them, it is simply "the Ward," or "the Deep," or "Home." It is a single, vast, enclosed chamber, its scale that of a gothic cathedral or a grand train depot. The ceiling is lost in a constant, dripping gloom, but its supporting architecture is visible—massive, groaning buttresses of riveted iron, thick as ancient redwoods, disappearing into the shadowed heights. Between these iron pillars, the walls are not stone, but plant.

The vegetation here is not a life that clings to a substrate; it is the substrate itself. The walls are a pulsing, layered mass of Phloxignum, a symbiotic colony of mosses, fungi, and a woody, vine-like organism that has grown to create a seamless, living skin. It exudes a constant, gentle warmth that fights back the ambient chill, and its surface is a tapestry of deep greens, silver-grey lichen, and the electric blue of capillaries that thread through the mass, carrying nutrients. The floor, too, is a springy, damp composite of compressed root-matter and fallen Phloxignum leaves that have decayed into a rich, black humus. Pockets of water gather in shallow, natural depressions, their surfaces perfectly still, reflecting the iron夤atures above like geometric islands in a black sea.

Water is a constant, quiet presence. It is not a deluge, but a patient, rhythmic seepage. Condensation gathers on the uppermost reaches of the ironworks, forming beads that swell and then fall, plinking into the pools below with a sound that is the Ward’s only true punctuation in the silence. Great, slow-moving water-logged leaves, the size of dinner plates, form natural gutters along the Phloxignum walls, channeling the moisture in a living, slothful plumbing system. The air is damp enough that you can taste it, metallic and vegetal all at once.

Into this ancient, living gloom, a city has been built. It is not constructed upon the plant, but *within* its hollows. The architecture of the Ward — the "Built" parameter made manifest — is a marvel of industrial necromancy. Structures are carved out of the densest parts of the Phloxignum or are suspended from the iron framework. Dormitories are hollowed-out geodes of living plant, their interiors smoothed and polished, warm to the touch. The wider corridors and thoroughfares are lined with planks of treated Phloxignum-wood, laid over the spongy floor. And everywhere, there is the industry.

The low, pervasive hum is the heart of the Ward. It is the sound of the Extractors. These are the city’s machines — great, brass-and-steel fittings bolted directly into the Phloxignum walls. They tap into the organism’s circulatory system, drawing off a slow, steady stream of nutrient-rich sap. This sap is the Ward’s sole resource. In the Refinery, a central hub of clanking pipes, vats, and distillation coils, the sap is processed. It is rendered into fuel for the few humming emergency generators, into a palatable nutritional paste, and into a potent, glowing liquid called Lumen. The Lumen is pumped through a network of pressurized glass tubes that run like veins through the entire city, providing illumination in the form of soft, bioluminescent lamps that cast a green-gold glow on the damp walkways. The air smells faintly of ozone and cooked sugar near the Refinery.

The scale is intimate, the density sparse. You are never in a crush of people. The population is small, perhaps a few hundred souls, scattered through the carved-out rooms and suspended walkways. Their lives are dictated by the rhythm of the Ward, by the slow, active dynamism of its single, all-encompassing organism. There are tenders who prune the aggressive fungal growths, engineers who maintain the Extractors, and algae-scrapers who clean the pools of water to prevent stagnation. Everyone has a role in the maintenance of the symbiosis. They do not rule the Ward; they serve it.

Fauna is rare, and what exists is part of the system. Small, blind, amphibious creatures — "slicks" — live in the pools, their skin a mosaic of phosphorescent patterns that pulse in the darkness, feeding on algal blooms. They are sometimes harvested for their oils. The only other known life are the "Grove-Moths," insects with wings like cathedral glass that are drawn to the Lumen-lamps, their brief, whisper-soft life a counterpoint to the Ward's glacial permanence.

The rule of this world is one of balance. It is an established, weathered equilibrium. To draw too much sap would weaken the Phloxignum, causing sections of the wall to wither and collapse, starving the city of its heat, structure, and lifeblood. To draw too little would allow the plant's own internal processes to grow unruly, leading to the chokes of aggressive mold or the strangulation of pathways by unchecked vines. The hum of the Refinery is the sound of a constant, precise calculation.

The "weirdness" is in the anachronism, the deep uncanny valley of a life lived inside the innards of a petrified god. Some whisper that the Phloxignum is not merely a plant, but the last remnant of a terraforming effort from a forgotten age, and the iron framework is what's left of the scaffolding. Others say the organism is aware, that it feels the prick of every Extractor needle and the warmth of every human body, and that it allows their presence for reasons of its own. When the Refinery is shut down for its rare deep-cleanings, in the utter dark and absolute silence, some residents claim they can feel it—not as a heart, but as a single, slow breath, drawn and released by the entire space.

Here, in the cool, damp, quiet hum of the Ward, life is an act of careful, perpetual negotiation. You are a tenant in a living cathedral, a citizen of a metropolis carved from the flesh of a sleeping giant, your small existence lit by borrowed blood, under a roof of patient, rusting iron.
