This is a land at the exhaustive end of its history. The erosion parameter—nearly maxed out—speaks of a primordial flatness, a terrain that has been worn down to its ultimate, immutable state. There are no jagged peaks, no dramatic cliffs, no youthful tectonic upheavals. Instead, the elevation barely lifts off the baseline, creating an immense, open expanse that stretches to the horizons without a single obstruction. It is a coastal plain of such profound age that the hills have melted into swells, and the valleys have filled with the pulverized dust of a billion years. The bedrock—where it still exists beneath the unending mats of organic duff—looks like the wrinkled skin of something impossibly old, folded and refolded until all the sharp edges have been ground away into gentle, undulating saddles.

Yet, for all its flatness, it is not dry. The warmth of the climate—pleasant, heated but not scorching—acts upon this low-lying expanse to create an atmosphere that feels like a slow exhalation. Water is everywhere, not in dramatic torrents, but in a pervasive, inescapable saturation. The ground is a vast, shallow bowl, and the water table lies so close to the surface that the earth cannot truly dry. After the frequent, gentle rains, the water pools in the subtle depressions, creating a mirror-shard landscape of scattered, shallow pans. The rocks—those ancient, embedded boulders that sit like sleeping beasts half-submerged in the mud—are "weeping." They are coated in a perpetual slick of condensation; warm air hits the cool, dense stone and simply weeps, sending constant, tiny rivulets trickling down their sides. The soil is a dark, anaerobic muck, rich and deep, holding water like a sponge. It squelches underfoot, releasing bubbles of ancient marsh gas that pop with a wet, organic *thuck*.

The vegetation is sparse, but what exists is profoundly adapted to this soggy, warm stillness. There are no dense forests here; the canopy does not close. Instead, the landscape is given to vast, open distances, where the primary sensation is the space between things. Scattered across the saturated plain are massive, solitary trees—something like banyans or swamp cypresses—acting as monuments in the emptiness. They are widely spaced, each commanding its own territory, their roots forming broad, flattened buttresses that spread over the waterlogged ground like the fingers of a drowning giant. Beneath them, the undergrowth is limited to hardy, blade-like reeds and floating mats of liverworts that sit directly on the water's surface. The green is muted, grayed by the constant humidity and the age of the soil. There is no frantic growth; the plants here grow slowly, adding rings in the long, patient warmth, their roots content to simply sit in the shallow bath of the world.

The abundance of fauna is what gives this place its loudness. Despite the sparse vegetation and the slow dynamics of the landscape, the warm, shallow water is a perfect incubator for life, and the sheer biomass of the wetland is staggering. The sound is a continuous, enveloping roar, though it is born of thousands of small voices. It is the deafening, rhythmic thrum of cicadas and amphibians—a wall of sound that vibrates in the warm, dense air. From the reed-beds comes the clacking of countless crabs and the splashing of wading birds stalking the shallows. The soundscape feels almost physical, a low-frequency buzz that settles into the teeth and the chest. It is not a chaotic sound, but a synchronized, ancient choir, driven by the warm blood of the swamp. The creatures here are numerous but small; the world is too flat and wet to support massive grazers, so the food chain is built on a foundation of insects, fish, and water-dwelling reptiles, all multiplying in the endless warm water.

The dynamics of the place are imperceptibly slow. The erosion is nearly finished; the mountains that once fed this plain were ground down eons ago, and now the rivers that traverse it are old, mature, and lazy, winding in vast, meandering loops that frequently breach their banks and alter course in a single slow, silty breath. The water moves, but sluggishly, stained the color of dark tea by the tannins of the ancient vegetation. The sky above is often overcast with high, flat clouds, diffusing the light into a soft, directionless glow that eliminates shadows and makes the wet landscape shimmer. On the rare clear days, the light reflects off the standing water with a blinding intensity, turning the plain into a duplicate sky.

There are no built structures, no technology, not even the remnants of a forgotten path. This place has never been settled because it offers nothing to stand on and nothing to build with. It is a pre-tool world, existing solely within the cycles of water, warmth, and rot. The rules here are simple and absolute: everything that falls stays; everything that dies is slowly dissolved in the warm, wet dark; and the water always finds its level. Standing in the midst of it, the openness is overwhelming—a vast, horizontal eternity of water, weed, and the ceaseless, patient hum of life at the edge of the world.
