There is no sky here. Not a surface that can be pointed to. There is a ceiling, and it is not stone or metal, but the inverse of a root system. A forest, turned inside-out and grown overhead. It is ausu- behavior at its most extreme, the total application of vegetation as substrate, taken to its logical and horrifying conclusion.

The place is a Chancel. A single, bounded chamber, perhaps the size of a grand cathedral nave, but one whose walls have not yet fully formed. The primary material is a pale, fibrous matter that looks like spun sugar and petrified fungus, a kind of proto-flesh that weeps a constant, thin condensation. This is the raw material of the world, laid down in swaths and sheets, like a potter's rough-thrown clay. It is the emergent geology, the uneroded base state of this reality. In some patches, it is smooth and blank; in others, fragile, membrane-thin walls have extruded themselves from the floor, only to collapse back into mushy pulp, the process of their own negation. The air itself feels thick and newly minted, tasting of damp yeast and mint.

The predominant features, both architectural and ecological, are the Pillars. They are not columns but colossal, parasitic fruits, suspended half-formed from the ceiling-forest. Each is the size of a tower, veined with a phosphorescent, cobalt-blue ichor that pulses in a slow, arrhythmic tide. This is not firelight; it is a cold, living luminescence that offers just enough definition to turn the pervading darkness into a landscape of silhouettes. The light is without heat. The temperature is a relentless, penetrative cool, a chill that settles in the marrow and seems to emanate from the very conceptual lack of a sun.

From the ceiling, these Pillars descend. From the floor, a different life answers. This is the urban part, the built aspect, but it is not made by hands. The city’s infrastructure is biological. Long, segmented cables, like the petioles of some monstrous plant, snake across the floor and climb the emergent walls. They are not dead vines; they are conduits. Through their translucent skin, one can see a traffic of shimmering motes, like insects in a tube, moving with frantic purpose. This is the industrial substrate, the mechanized ecology that powers the Chancel. In places, these cables bundle together and plunge into metallic-looking gourds—transformer stations or galvanic pods—that emit a low, sub-audible hum, the only constant sound in a mostly quiet place.

The density is near-void. This is not a crowded jungle, but a necropolis of giants. The space between the massive, suculent Pillars is vast, creating wide, echoing plazas carpeted in a strange, spongy moss that is actually a flattened, symbiotic algae. Your footsteps make no sound, merely compressing the living floor beneath you, which slowly springs back. The emptiness is what's most unsettling. It feels like a model of a world, built at large scale, with the lifeforms intended to inhabit it yet to arrive.

But they have. The fauna is abundant and strange. They are not animals as we know them. They are echo-constructs. Flicker-lizards, made of fractured light and held together by a clicking, chitinous skeleton, dart across the ceiling-roots, their passage sounding like a deck of cards being shuffled. In the airy gloom between the Pillars, vast, slow-moving things drift. They resemble jellyfish woven from shadow and dust, their trailing tentacles barely visible, brushing against you with the static surprise of touching a TV screen. They leave no wake and make no sound. They are simply present, their existence a brief, silent passage through the hollow space. There are predators here, too: things that look like stained-glass windows come to life, hummingbird-fast, that shatter when they strike their prey, which scintillates for a moment before ceasing to be.

This is the core of the weirdness, the breakdown of reality. The Chancel is a place of active, ongoing rule-bending. The laws of physics are suggestions, not mandates. The conduits sometimes leak, and a spore of pure concept will fall out—a memory of rain from a world that never existed, a patch of floor that briefly believes it is molten glass. The Pillars' healing light propagates not as photons but as a property, a state of luminescence that things can momentarily acquire and then lose. Time feels… thick. Walking from one side of a plaza to the other can feel instantaneous, or it can feel like it takes a month, with no sense of acceleration or deceleration. You simply arrive. The most common phenomenon is the Lacuna. A space will simply lose a property for a moment: a hole will appear in a Pillar, yet the Pillar will still be structurally sound; a section of the floor will forget texture and become perfectly smooth, silent glass; the air will momentarily forget to carry sound for a beat that stretches into an eternity of silence. These are not glitches. They are the processes of the world's construction, the seams between this reality and whatever is being used to build it.

You are in the engine room of a god that is still being assembled. The dampness is the condensation of divine sweat. The cool is the ambient temperature of pre-creation. The emptiness is the void being filled. The infinite, silent industry of the place is the sound of genesis. And you, a small, solid, and shockingly consistent thing, are an anomaly. An impurity in the living machine, a fossil in a world not yet old enough to have any. The system does not seem hostile, only vast, alien, and profoundly uninterested in your familiar logic. It is simply… active. And you are a spectator in the dark, cool interior of a world being born.
