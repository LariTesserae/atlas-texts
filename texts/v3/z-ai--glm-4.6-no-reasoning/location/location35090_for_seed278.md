# The Plateau of Machines

It begins as a sound — or the absence of one. A silence so total it becomes its own pressure, a weight against the eardrums. Then, slowly, the ear adjusts, and beneath the silence there is something after all: the low, near-subsonic hum of ten thousand engines running at idle, a vibration felt in the molars and the sternum more than heard.

---

The dampness comes and goes. It does not rain, exactly. Instead, every few days, the temperature dips just enough that the air can no longer hold what it carries, and a fine precipitate forms — not drops, not drizzle, but a cold wetness that simply appears on every surface simultaneously, as if the world has exhaled onto itself. The stone goes dark with moisture. The metal — and there is so much metal — beads with condensation. And then, over hours, the temperature rises a fraction, the damp lifts, and the surfaces dry again, leaving mineral traces, leaving a faint white rime where the water was.

No one is here to watch this cycle.

Or — not no one.

---

The fauna is the first thing that makes this place difficult to describe. It is not that there are many animals. It is that the animals are *so many* that they constitute a feature of the landscape, the way forests constitute a feature of other landscapes. But they do not behave as animals do in other places.

They are small. Most of them are very small. And they move slowly.

The dominant form is something like an insect and something like a crustacean and something like nothing that has a name in any language. They are chitinous, dark, low to the ground, and they exist in numbers that stagger comprehension. The stone is covered in them. Not visibly — when you stand and look, the plateau appears empty, the stone bare. But kneel, and the surface is a slow mosaic of movement. They are the size of grains of rice, some of them, and some smaller, and they move with a deliberation that matches the pace of the place. They are not hurrying. They are not fleeing. They are going somewhere, or processing something, or simply being alive at the speed that being alive happens here.

They feed on the condensation and on each other and on something in the stone — a mineral, perhaps, or a microbiota that lives in the stone, or on the residue that the machines produce. There are hierarchies among them, or at least sizes, forms, niches. Some have too many legs. Some have none, and move by a kind of slow rolling, peristaltic, patient. Some fly, though *fly* is generous — they lift, barely, on membranes that catch the thin air, and they drift more than they navigate, and they land again and resume the slow traversal of the stone.

At night — and there is a night, a dimness that deepens into true dark, for the light here is always attenuated, always as if seen through a filter — the smaller forms retreat into the stone, into cracks that are not cracks but the joints of the machines, and the larger forms emerge. The larger forms are the size of dogs, some of them, and they move in formations that suggest purpose. They are not hunting. They are not grazing. They are *patrolling*, or something adjacent to it. They follow routes. They return to nodes. They do not vocalize. The silence of the plateau is never broken; it is only, occasionally, shared.

There are too many of them to count. There are too many of them to ignore. They are the life of this place, and they have been the life of this place for longer than the machines, or the machines have been here for longer than the life, and the two have arrived at an arrangement. The machines do not destroy the life. The life does not consume the machines. But the life uses the machines — the warmth they produce, the shelter of their structures, the chemical output of their exhaust — and the machines, for their part, are *for* something that involves the life, though what that something is is not clear.

---

The machines are everywhere. This is the paradox, or the central fact, or the thing that makes the plateau a place and not simply a condition. The built environment is zero — no structures, no artifacts, no architecture, no roads, no habitations. And yet the plateau is *industrial*. The machines are not built upon the stone. They *are* the stone, or they are in the stone, or the stone is a crust over the machines, or the machines have grown through the stone like roots, or the stone and the machines are the same material in different phases of some process that takes geological time.

There are engines. This is certain. They are below the surface, or they are the surface, or they are distributed through the rock in a way that makes the distinction meaningless. They are running. They have been running for a very long time. They are not maintained. They do not fail. Or they fail at a rate that is invisible against the scale of their number — a thousand fail per year, and a thousand are subsumed into the stone, and ten thousand remain, and the hum does not change.

The plateau is a factory. This is the only word for it, though it is a factory that produces nothing, or produces something that is not visible, or produces *conditions* — the temperature, the damp, the chemistry of the air, the subsonic vibration, the slow mineral changes in the stone. It is a factory the size of a continent. It has been operating since before the current fauna evolved, and the fauna evolved *for* it, *with* it, *because of* it. The animals are not pests. They are not passengers. They are *product*, or *process*, or *integral*.

Occasionally, a vent opens. This is the only visible sign of the machines' activity, and it happens slowly enough that a human observer standing in one place would not notice it happening. But over the course of a year, a section of stone will thin, and then split, and a new aperture will appear, and from it will issue a warmth and a chemical trace and a sound — still low, still below the register of hearing, but a *different* low, a new frequency added to the hum, which then, over the course of another year, will integrate, and the plateau will settle again into its equilibrium.

The vents are where the larger animals congregate. They cluster in the warmth, their dark shells glossed with condensation, and they do not move, and they do not sleep, and they are, for those hours or days, part of the machine.

---

The vegetation is almost absent, and what there is does not call attention to itself. In the lee of the vents, where the warmth and the moisture and the chemical trace combine, there are growths — not plants, exactly, but organic structures that process the exhaust and fix something from it and produce a mat that the smaller animals feed on. The mats are dark, nearly black, and they spread at the rate of a few centimeters per year, and they never grow tall, and they are never green. They are the color of the stone, or the color of the animals, or the color of the air, which is to say they are a dark grey-brown, and they are alive, and they are the only thing here that is alive in the way that other places are alive — growing, spreading, converting energy from one form to another — and they are marginal, and they are sufficient, and they have been here for as long as the animals have, and the animals have been here for as long as the machines have, and the machines have been here for longer than anything.

---

The scale defeats description. Stand on the plateau and the flatness extends to the horizon, and the horizon is far because the plateau is high, and above the horizon is the thin sky, and below the horizon is more plateau, and the knowledge that it goes on, and on, and on. The hum comes from everywhere. The damp settles on everything. The animals are underfoot and overhead and in every direction at a density that is, when perceived, almost nauseating in its completeness. And the machines — the machines are the ground. The ground *is* the machines. You are standing on a mechanism the size of a world, and it is running, and it has been running, and it will run, and the silence it produces is the silence of a thing that does not need to announce itself because it has already won, because it *is* the place, because the place and the process are the same.

The cold seeps up through the soles. The dim light casts no shadows. The animals move. The machines hum. The damp forms and lifts and forms again.

Nothing happens. Everything continues.
