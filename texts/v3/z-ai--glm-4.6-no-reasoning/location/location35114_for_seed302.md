The sound is the first thing. It is the only thing. It is not the sound of wind, not a roar or a whistle. It is a single, all-encompassing, organ-tone vibration, a fundamental hum that seems to come from every direction at once and from nowhere at all. It is the note the world is holding. It is pressed against your skin, a constant, resonant pressure that makes your bones thrum in sympathetic harmony. To open your mouth is to have the sound fill your lungs; to speak is to shout into the bottom of a well so deep it has no top, your voice vanishing instantly into the greater, eternal chord.

You stand on a plain of high, worn bitterness. This is a landscape of exhaustion, everything ground down to its most basic and most enduring form. The ground underfoot is not soil, but a packed, interlocking pavement of shattered slate and schist. The pieces are not sharp; eons of a cool, patient presence have beveled every edge, smoothed every face, so the land is a tessellation of dark, lichenless tiles, each one the size of a dinner plate, each one fitted to its neighbors with impossible precision. It is a floor, vast and featureless, that was laid once and has never, ever needed repair.

The elevation is significant. The air here is thin and clean, so cold it bites in the back of the throat, but it carries no scent of forests or soil. There is a faint mineral tang, like a rock collection just broken open, but that is all. There are no plants. The absolute, 0.0-ness of vegetation is staggering. Not a single blade of grass, not a patch of moss, not a tenacious lichen clings to the stone. The world is drawn in monochrome: the deep greys and blacks of the rock, the flat, colourless light of the sky, and the blinding white of a thousand thousand crystalline formations.

This is where the water lives. It does not flow. It escapes. The strange pressure, the immense and resonant hum of the place, forces water from the stone itself in a state of perpetual, fine-weeping exhalation. From every fissure, from the seams between the pavement-tiles, from the faces of the great formations that rise from the plane, water doesn't so much weep as it is squeezed out, atom by atom. It beads instantly on the cool stone, but the high, thin air and the brilliant light are thieves. Before a drop can gather enough weight to fall, it sublimates, turning to a visible, shimmering mist.

The mist is everywhere. It rises from the ground in a steady, silent exhalation, a fog that never condenses into a cloud nor scatters entirely. It hangs just above the stone, lending the air a pearlescent, milk-glass quality. But the light is brilliant, 2.9-brilliant, a fierce, white light that seems to come from the sky itself, which is a hard, featureless dome of polished opal. The light and the mist are locked in a constant, dynamic-less battle; the light attempts to burn and bleach everything away, and the mist throws itself up to diffuse and soften it, creating a world of impossible glare and impossible softness at once.

Dotted across this packed, saturated expanse are the source of the weirdness, the 0.8-ness of the place. They are crystalline structures, but to call them geodes would be to miss the point. They are sonic vacuums. They grow like stands of colossal, six-sided trees, or like the petrified veins of some long-dead titan. They are clustered so densely around these structures that there is no room to walk between them without squeezing through, your shoulders brushing the cold, humming facets.

Approaching a crystalline formation is an act of profound sensory disorientation. From a distance, they are beautiful, glittering towers catching the fierce light. As you get closer, you notice the sound begin to change. The fundamental, all-enveloping hum of the landscape starts to thin. The pressure on your ears lessens. Standing right at the base of a thirty-meter-high crystal spire, the sound is nearly gone, replaced by a profound, ringing silence that feels louder than the hum it replaced. This is a space where the world’s great chord cannot sustain itself. The crystals absorb the vibration, the energy, the very sound of the place, and in doing so, they allow water to truly form.

Here, at the base of a crystal, you can see liquid. The weeping water from the ground, no longer instantly vaporized by the resonant heat and pressure of the hum, can gather into thin, crystal-clear rivulets that run down the faces of the formation and pool in the impossible depressions at its base. The water here is unnervingly pure and cold.

Life is rare, but it is precisely here, in these silent alcoves, that it exists. The fauna is 1.2-rare. There are no birds, no insects. The only creatures you might ever see are the ‘Echo-Moths.’ They are not moths, not in any biological sense you would recognize. They are small, flat, geometric shapes, like living shavings of the crystal spires themselves. They have no discernible wings but move through the air with a slow, drifting, purposeless grace, tumbling over and over. They are drawn to the edges of the silent zones, hovering in the place where the world’s deafening hum returns. They seem to feed on the vibration, on the sound itself, their bodies resonating with a faint, internal chime. They are the only things that move, and their movement is so slow and so silent as to seem part of the landscape’s great, frozen equilibrium.

There are no gradients. The light does not waver with a sun’s passage. The temperature does not rise or fall. The sound maintains its unwavering, unchanging note. The water weeps at the same constant rate. The Echo-Moths tumble in their slow, eternal dance. This is a world that has arrived. It is not dead; it is finished. It is in perfect, unending balance. Erosion is not an act of decay, but a process of polishing, of smoothing the final, perfect edges of a system that will not change. It is a place of absolute equilibrium, a high, wet, bright, and deafening song, held for an eternity. And in the deep, silent pockets of its crystalline chorus, the rarest things in the world—silence and water—are finally permitted to exist.
