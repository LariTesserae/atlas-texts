# The Plateau of Congregations

The land has given up its mountains. What remains is a vast, high tableland — elevation without drama, altitude without peaks. Somewhere in deep time, this was a range that rivaled anything the world has built since. Now it is a surface so ancient it has forgotten the word for ridge. The horizon runs flat in every direction, and the sky sits close, a pale warm lid pressed down upon a world that finished becoming what it would be long ago.

The rock underfoot is something between sandstone and dream — too soft to resist, too massive to have washed away entirely. It has been worn into a subtle topography of shallow swales and broad, barely-perceptible rises, a relief of inches across miles. Walking here feels like crossing the palm of a sleeping god. The warmth comes from below as much as above; the stone holds heat the way old wood holds smoke, and the soles of your feet register the patient thermals rising out of the earth's memory of daylight.

---

## The Water That Persists

Rain comes, but not as rain comes elsewhere. A storm will pass overhead and drop its water in a curtain that the plateau barely catches. The surface is too flat, too worn, to channel flow into streams. Instead, moisture beads and spreads in thin sheets across the stone, pooling in the countless slight depressions that dimple the tableland — each one a temporary mirror, each one gone by mid-afternoon. But the deepest of these hollows hold water longer. Days longer. A week, after a good rain.

These are not ponds. They are bruises in the rock where water sits and waits, rarely more than ankle-deep, warmed by the stone beneath until they are near body temperature. Some are no larger than a table. Some stretch for hundreds of meters — shallow, warm, perfectly still. The water tastes of mineral and age. It leaves rings on the rock as it evaporates, concentric tide-marks that record each retreat like the growth rings of something that is both dying and being born.

Condensation is the plateau's more faithful lover. Each dawn, a mist rises from the warm stone into the cool air above, and for an hour the world is wrapped in a dampness that beads on every surface and runs in tiny rivulets toward the nearest hollow. The place breathes water in and out, a slow pulmonary cycle that has repeated for more mornings than can be counted.

## The Few Who Root

Vegetation has learned the futility of ambition here. What grows is low and slow and close to the stone. There are crustose lichens in vast expanses — pale gray-green, black, a strange ochre — that have colonized every surface not regularly scoured by standing water. They are so thin they seem to be a property of the rock itself, a color it possesses rather than something living upon it. In places, a single lichen body covers hectares, an organism so old it has lost track of its own edges.

Where water pools longest, there are mats of something like moss but not quite — they are darker, almost black, and when you kneel close you see they are composed of tiny succulent leaves pressed flat against each other in dense rosettes, each one no bigger than a fingernail, each one holding a droplet of water at its center like a lens. Walking on them produces a quiet squelch and leaves footprints that fill with warm water almost immediately.

Nothing stands upright. There are no shrubs, no grasses, no stalks. A few scattered growths put up fleshy stems no taller than a hand's span, leafless and strange, topped by small closed structures that might be flowers that never quite open or fruits that never quite ripen. They emerge from cracks in the stone where some deeper moisture persists, and they are the same pale color as the rock, nearly invisible until you are on top of them. The plateau's green is a gray-green, a reluctant green, a green that has almost decided to give up.

## The Congregations

What makes this place impossible to prepare for is the life.

The plateau is dense with fauna in a way that contradicts its spare vegetation and minimal water. The mathematics seem wrong — there should not be enough here to support what is here. And yet.

They come in bodies. In masses. In carpets and towers and streams of living bodies that move across the stone in patterns too large to see from within and too regular to be accidental.

The most visible are the ones that walk. They are the size of large dogs but built nothing like dogs — low and wide, with too many legs, six or eight depending on the species and the individual, and they move with a fluidity that makes the count hard to verify. Their carapaces are the color of the wet stone, darker when they've recently passed through a pool, lighter when they've been walking on dry rock for hours. They travel in lines. Not single-file lines, but broad processions ten or twenty bodies wide, moving in the same direction with a coordination that has no visible source — no leader, no calls, no signal. They simply walk, hundreds of them, thousands, and the sound is a soft continuous whisper of articulated legs on stone, like wind through grass if grass were made of shell and tendon.

Where these processions pass, they leave the rock scoured clean of lichen in paths that take seasons to regrow. The plateau is traced with these highways, some ancient and worn an inch deep into the stone, others fresh and still pale. They cross and recross without logic apparent to a human mind, but they must have logic of their own, because no procession ever deviates from an existing path and no two paths merge unless they were already the same path.

And then there are the ones that cluster.

In the hollows where water lingers, around the margins of the warm pools, the smaller creatures gather in densities that redefine the word. They are the size of fists, or hands, or the lobes of a liver — dark, soft-bodied, glistening with the moisture of their environment. They do not move much. They sit pressed against each other in masses that can fill a pool-edge for meters, layered three and four deep, slowly pulsing with a shared rhythm that is visible as a wave of contraction passing across the congregation. What they are doing is not obvious. Breathing, perhaps. Eating something in the water, perhaps. Communicating through touch in a language older than light.

The smell of these clusters is organic and warm, not unpleasant but overwhelming in its intensity — a smell of life concentrated, of metabolisms running in parallel, of warmth and waste and the strange sweetness of bodies that have found a way to exist together that no single body could sustain alone. Approach one of these clusters and you feel the heat they generate collectively, a degree or two above the already-warm stone, like standing near a low fire.

They are not the only congregations.

Above, in the air that hangs still and warm over the plateau, things fly. Not birds — nothing with a spine. They are broad and flat and translucent, like the skin of a bubble given locomotion, and they drift in loose formations that shift like smoke, like the shapes of clouds seen in time-lapse. They are filtering something from the air, or perhaps they are the air given partial solidity, given appetite. When one of the walking processions passes below, the flyers above will reorient, tilting their collective flight to parallel the march, and for a while the two streams move together — one on stone, one in air — before the flyers gradually diffuse and the connection, if it was a connection, is lost.

In the deeper cracks, the fissures that open where the ancient stone has finally, after eons, begun to surrender to its own weight, other things live — things that never see the light of the shallow pools and never cross the open rock. You hear them, sometimes. A low vibration that is more felt than heard, a resonance in the stone itself, as if the plateau were a drumhead and something below were slowly, patiently striking it. The sound comes and goes on a cycle longer than a day, perhaps tied to the condensation, perhaps tied to something else. It is the only sound that carries any distance here, and it makes the silence that follows it feel like a quality of the world rather than an absence.

## The Marks of Those Who Came Before

Technology has never touched this place, but something has.

In the furthest swales, where the water pools deepest and stays longest, there are arrangements of stone that do not match the natural wear of the plateau. Small stones — none larger than two hands could carry — placed in circles around the pool margins. Single upright stones, barely a meter tall, standing at points where the congregation highways intersect. Low walls, no higher than a knee, that do not enclose anything but instead run in straight lines across the flatness for distances too long to have been built for any practical purpose.

They are so weathered they are nearly features of the land itself. The upright stones lean at angles dictated by ten thousand years of wind. The circles are missing half their members. The lines of wall are interrupted by gaps where stone has fallen or been removed, and in these gaps the walking processions now pass, having incorporated the ruins into their own patterns as if they were natural features — as if the distinction between built and grown had lost any meaning here.

There is no writing. No art, unless the circles are art. No tools, unless the stones themselves were tools, used and left in place. Who made these marks, and when, and why, is a question the plateau has no interest in answering. The congregations do not acknowledge the structures. The lichen grows over them exactly as it grows over everything else. The water pools against them and evaporates from them and leaves the same rings.

And yet there is a feeling — and it is only a feeling, nothing so certain as knowledge — that the structures were made *for* the congregations. That the circles mark places where the soft-bodied clusters gather thickest. That the standing stones stand at intersections not of the highways as they are now but as they were when the stones were placed, and the highways have moved since, and the stones remain, marking a coordination that no longer exists. That the long low walls were built to shape the flow of something — the walking processions, or the water, or the mist, or something else that moves across this plateau in patterns too large and too slow for any single observer to perceive.

If this is true, then whoever built here understood the congregations in a way that we do not. They read the movements of the many-legged walkers the way a sailor reads currents. They predicted the clustering of the pool-edge masses the way a farmer predicts rain. And they left their markers not as monuments or shelters but as annotations — a marginalia written in stone across the page of the plateau, recording the living patterns of a biosphere that has outlasted them by longer than they could have imagined.

## The Quality of Time

The plateau operates on durations that make a human life feel like a single heartbeat inside a longer body.

The pools fill and empty on a cycle of days. The lichen grows and is scoured and regrows on a cycle of seasons. The processions establish and abandon their highways on a cycle of decades, perhaps centuries — it is impossible to know without watching, and no one has watched long enough. The congregations of the pool-edge masses swell and contract on some rhythm tied to the water but also to something else, something interior, some shared pulse that may be the same rhythm that thrums up from the cracks in the stone.

And above all of it, the stone itself continues to wear. Millimeter by millimeter, century by century, the shallow swales deepen, the low rises flatten, the fissures widen. The plateau is moving toward a perfect flatness it will never reach, because before it reaches it the world beneath will shift and the cycle will begin again — uplift, erosion, flatness, sea. But that is a story so long that even the congregations, with their ancient coordinated patterns and their patient clustered breathing, are only a brief chapter within it.

Standing on the plateau at the hour when the condensation rises, with the mist forming around you and the first walkers of the morning procession appearing out of the gray like a thought emerging from sleep, you can feel the slowness. It is not stillness — things are happening, many things, dense and layered and alive. But they are happening at a speed that the rock can follow. At a speed that allows the stone to remember where the water was, where the lichen grew, where the processions walked. At a speed where even erosion is a form of memory, each inch of lost surface a record of the wind that removed it.

The warmth comes up through your feet. The mist dampens your skin. A congregation of the soft dark bodies pulses quietly in the nearest pool, and above them the translucent flyers tilt and drift in a formation that will be different tomorrow but only slightly different, only as different as a cloud is from the cloud it was an hour before. The low vibration sounds from somewhere in the stone and ceases, and the silence that follows is not empty but full, full of the soundless breathing of ten million bodies in patient proximity, full of the heat of their collective metabolism, full of the slow wearing of the rock toward a flatness that is the plateau's oldest dream.

This is a place that has reached the far end of its history and found that the far end is not an ending but a condition — a way of being old that includes more life, not less, that has solved the problem of how to persist by becoming something that does not need to change. The plateau has finished its becoming. The congregations have finished their arriving. And what remains is this: the warm flat stone, the intermittent water, the countless bodies arranged in patterns that no one placed them in, moving together across a surface that was once a mountain and is now a stage, and the play that is performed here is a play about patience, about the way that life, given enough time and enough flatness and enough warmth, will find ways to gather that have nothing to do with the individuals gathered and everything to do with some larger pattern that the stage itself was built to hold.
