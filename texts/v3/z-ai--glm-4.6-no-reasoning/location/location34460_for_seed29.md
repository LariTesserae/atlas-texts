A pocket universe, no larger than a cathedral's sacristy, buried in the mantle of an industrial world.

The space was never carved; it erupted—a nascent blister of heat and pressure that ballooned between strata of slag and basalt, and then paused, half-formed. Its walls are close, oppressive. The ceiling presses against the top of your head if you stand. The walls are close enough to touch simultaneously with both hands spread wide. It is a room without architecture, without doors, without seams—an embryonic cavity still wet with the violence of its creation.

And it is full. Horribly, claustrophobically full.

There is no emptiness here, no air, no gap. The space is packed with a dense, interlocking lattice of metallic spicules—nickel-iron growths, crystallized from superheated vapor, that thrust from every surface and interlock with their opposite numbers. They are the rebars of an unfinished furnace, the ribs of some mechanical throat, growing in fractal profusion: thick primary columns bristling with secondary tines, those with tertiary needles, on down to a fur of metallic whiskers that fills the interstitial space. The overall effect is of being inside the chest cavity of a vast, heat-soaked machine, buried in its works.

The temperature is extreme—2,000 degrees Fahrenheit or more. The metal lattice glows in the darkness. Not brightly; the light parameter is low. There is no illumination here save what the matter itself provides: a deep, sullen, suffocating cherry-red emanating from every surface. It is dark the way a kiln is dark when viewed through its spyhole. The glow is so uniform and omnipresent that there are no shadows, only a dim, baleful rubicund haze that makes depth perception falter. The spicules ahead and the spicules an inch from your face glow the same dull red. The mind starves for contrast.

There is no water. None. The chemistry here is dry as a calcined bone. The air—if it can be called air—is a superheated vapor of sulfur and vaporized trace metals. To breathe would be to inhale a soft rain of iron filings. The walls sweat not water but a thin, lustrous film of liquid metal—gallium, perhaps, or an alloy—that creeps downward in impossibly slow rivulets, joining the pool at the floor, if there is a distinct floor beneath the dense thicket of vertical growths. The slow, viscous movement of this metallic sweat is the only visible change. It is the blood of this place, and it moves with the torpor of honey in January.

Everything here is slow. The lattice grew in a spasm of accretion, and now it sits, cooling across geological time, contracting with infinitesimal slowness. Occasionally, a thermal gradient causes a spicule to contract a fraction of a millimeter against its neighbor. The sound this makes is immense.

The sound. The roar is constant, physical, a pressure in the chest. It is the sound of the earth groaning under its own weight, compressed into this tiny volume. It is the bass thunder of an industrial furnace heard from inside the firebrick. It is a brown note of mineral stress—sub-sonic, felt in the sinuses and the back teeth. Through it, as a stratum within it, comes a higher, ringing harmonic: the tinny, multiplied chime of a million metal needles vibrating in sympathy, a vast, resonant, deafening tintinnabulation. It is the sound of a factory the size of a continent, heard from the place where the strain is greatest. There is no silence. There is no quiet. Sound is a substance here; it fills the space alongside the metal, a dense medium of vibration.

And in this Hadean closet, life.

Not life as recognized by water-chemistry, not life that photosynthesizes or respirates in any terrestrial fashion. These are thermopiezotrophs—fauna of the extreme gradient, endemic to the deep industrial mantle. They are abundant, and they are the only thing here that moves with any rapidity, though that is a relative term.

The primary form is a kind of armored worm, three inches long, segmented in black tungsten carbide. They are warm to the glow—they are hotter than the surrounding metal, running at a higher internal temperature, which they maintain by pressing their flat bellies against thermal gradients in the lattice, absorbing heat on one side, radiating it on the other, a living heat-engine. They crawl with deliberate, hydraulic slowness along the spicules, their many hooked legs gripping the metal whiskers. When two meet on a narrow tine, they do not contest or mate; they simply pause, pressing their flanks together, and there is a minute transfer of some substance—a dot of brighter orange-gold glowing briefly at the junction—before they separate and continue. They are feeding. On what, is unclear. Perhaps on the electromagnetic potential between differently-alloyed growths. Perhaps on the sound itself.

Smaller forms exist, too small to see clearly in the dim red murk, but detected by the trails they leave: microscopic scratches in the metallic sweat, spiraling paths etched into the gallium film by mouthparts adapted to drink liquid metal. And larger forms—something moves in the deeper tangles of the lattice, something that displaces the spicules as it passes, causing a wave of tiny ringing collisions in its wake. It is never seen, only heard as a localized intensification of the chorus, a moving knot of resonance. It is the size of a dog, perhaps, or a large cat, navigating the dense metal forest with the patient certainty of a thing born in a furnace.

There is no vegetation. No carbon cycle. No decay. This ecology runs on heat, pressure, and the slow chemical potential of cooling alloys. It is an ecology of the in-between, the transient—the biosphere of a moment, of a cooling pocket that will, across a thousand years, contract and solidify and go still, killing everything within it. It is a world with an expiration date.

The weirdness of the place is subtle. It lies in the combination: the intimate scale with the industrial intensity, the organic with the mineral, the deafening roar contained in a space the size of a closet. It is not alien; it is recognizable. It is the inside of every machine, the hidden pocket of heat and pressure and slow, catastrophic strain that exists in the heart of every engine, every furnace, every steel casting as it cools. It is the landscape of stress. It is where the metal sings.

Stand here—and you cannot stand here—and you would feel the heat entering your bones, the sound rearranging your heartbeat, the walls pressing in with the patience of stone. You would watch the armored worms trace their slow calligraphy on the glowing lattice. You would wait for something to change. And nothing would change. Not quickly. This is a place of minimal flux, of patient processes. The metal sweats. The worms feed. The dark red glow persists. The pocket cools by one degree per century.

It is a room in the process of becoming. It has not yet decided what it is.
