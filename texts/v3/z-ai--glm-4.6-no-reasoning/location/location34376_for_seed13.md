The city stands at the throat of the sky, carved from the ribs of a young mountain range that has not yet learned to erode. The peaks here are sharp, unhumbled by time—their granite faces sheer and fractured, their ridges biting into the clouds like dark, uneven teeth. This is a landscape still raw from its making, where the stone has not yet been smoothed by wind or worn by ages, where the talus slopes beneath the cliffs are coarse and angular, the rubble of a world still settling into its skin.

And into this sharpness, someone built. The city clings to the high valley walls and spills across the broad saddle between two summits, a dense cluster of stone and timber that seems to have grown directly from the mountain itself. The construction is layered—lower tiers of massive grey blocks, fitted without mortar, their edges still precise, bearing the chisel-marks of hands that worked with stone tools and patience; above these, newer structures of dark wood and packed clay, roofed in heavy slate that clicks and shifts under the occasional weight of rain. The streets are narrow, stepped, turning on themselves as they climb. There is no grand plan to the layout—only the logic of accumulation, each generation building against and atop the last, so that the city has become a kind of coral reef, calcifying upward in its sheltered hollow.

It is cool here. The air carries the chill of elevation, the faint mineral bite of stone dust and meltwater. Breath visible in the early mornings, frost clinging to the slate eaves even when the sky is clear. The cold is not bitter—it is the cold of altitude rather than season, steady and impartial, the kind of cold that makes you move deliberately, that preserves what it touches.

Moisture comes inconsistently. The city exists in a pocket between the dry wind-sweep of the higher peaks and the mist that pools in the valleys below. Some days the air is merely damp, stone surfaces dark with condensation, the cobblestones slick underfoot even when no rain has fallen. Other days, rain arrives—a thin, persistent drizzle that runs down the channel-cut streets in shallow rills, collecting in cisterns carved into the rock beneath the lower squares. Fog presses in often, softening the hard edges of the architecture, muffling sound, filling the alleys so that the city seems to float in grey suspension, its uppermost towers and temples poking through like islands. There are seasons of relative dryness too, when the stone lightens and the cisterns drop low, but the place never becomes truly arid. The damp is always there, waiting in the stone, breathed out by the mountain at night.

Against this grey and damp and cool, the vegetation strikes a note almost loud. It is lush—not the sparse alpine scrub you might expect at this height, but a true verdancy, a profusion that suggests something in the soil or the water feeds growth beyond what the climate should allow. Moss and lichen coat every surface that holds still long enough—the lower walls, the street stones, the roofs of abandoned upper-tier buildings, the raw cliff faces themselves. They form deep pads and cushions, silver-green and dark emerald and occasionally a strange pale gold, thick enough to sink a finger into. Ferns erupt from every crack and joint, their fronds uncurling in the constant wet, some waist-high in the sheltered courtyards. And in the spaces between buildings—where a street widens into a small plaza, where a staircase reverses on itself and leaves a pocket of earth, where a wall has crumbled and been left to settle—small trees have taken root: mountain birch with dark bark and small bright leaves, a shrub-juniper that berries blue-black in the colder months, occasionally something taller and unnamed, with bark like ironwood and leaves that stay green year-round and rustle in the thin wind. The growth is opportunistic, dense where it finds purchase, but it does not overrun the city. The built and the green exist in a kind of agreement, the vegetation filling what is unused, the stone containing what would otherwise spill.

This equilibrium holds. That is the strangest thing. The city does not change—or rather, it changes so slowly, so imperceptibly, that from any human vantage it appears fixed. No new construction rises. No old structure falls. The moss grows, but at a pace that makes watching seem to slow it. The rain runs the same channels it has run for centuries. The people—if there are people; you see evidence of them but never in numbers—maintain the same routines in the same paths, wearing the same grooves deeper into the same steps. There is no decay, not in the familiar sense. Things do not deteriorate. They simply *are*, persisting in the state they've held since some moment of completion that no one can name. The chisel-marks on the oldest stones are as sharp as the day they were made. The wooden beams do not rot, though the damp should take them. The slate roofs do not crack. It is as if the city exists in a paused breath, an instant stretched to permanence.

The quiet reinforces this stillness. Sound dies quickly here—the stone absorbs it, the fog diffuses it, the moss dampens it. Footsteps are soft and close; voices do not carry. The loudest sounds are the intermittent rain on slate, the click of a shutter in a wind that comes and goes without pattern, the distant and occasional call of birds. Mostly, though, it is quiet—a thick, textured quiet, not empty but full of the small sounds that only become audible when larger ones retreat: the drip of condensation from a gutter, the settling of a wooden joint, the whisper of fern fronds touching as they shift. You can hear your own pulse, after a time. The silence is not hostile; it is simply the absence of any need to fill space.

The fauna moves through this quiet in abundance, visible because there is so little to startle them. Small birds are everywhere—swift and swallow in the upper air, finch and sparrow in the streets and courtyards, picking insects from the moss. They are unafraid; they hop aside at your approach and return the moment you pass. Lizards sun themselves on the stone walls when the fog clears, small and dark and quick, their bellies warm against the cold rock. In the cisterns and the runoff channels, pale amphibians breed—newts and salamanders with translucence to their skin, their movements slow and considered in the cold water. Something larger moves in the green pockets and the upper ruins—you catch glimpses of it, a flank or a shadow, never the whole shape. Goats, perhaps, or something goat-adjacent: a stocky, climbing creature that browses the ferns and grasses on the terraces, sure-footed on the steep walls. It watches you with dark eyes and does not flee, and you are never quite certain, after, that you saw what you think you saw. There are insects too, when you look for them: beetles in the moss, thread-thin flies above the cisterns, a small moth that seems to live only in the deeper rooms where the light barely reaches, its wings pale and papery, its flight soundless.

But there is something else. Something that does not fit. The more time you spend in the city—walking its stepped streets, pressing your hand to its stone, breathing its cold damp air—the more you become aware of a quality to the place that resists naming. It is not overtly strange. No impossible geometry warps the architecture; no unclean thing lurks in the wells. And yet. The equilibrium is *too* perfect. The vegetation *too* well-contained. The stone *too* enduring. You begin to feel that the city is not merely preserved but *intended*—that some will holds it in its current state, preventing the decay that should come, restraining the growth that should overrun, balancing the moisture and the cold and the green in a formula that does not waver. The chisel-marks are sharp because something keeps them sharp. The wood does not rot because something forbids it. The birds are unafraid because nothing here has ever given them reason to fear.

You cannot find the source of this intention. It is not in the temples that occupy the highest points of the city—small buildings, windowless, their doors sealed with stones that have not been moved in lifetimes. It is not in the cisterns or the channels or the foundations. It is not in the mountain itself, which is merely rock and the ordinary agonism of geology. It is *everywhere* and *nowhere*, a property of the place rather than a presence within it, like the way a held breath is not a thing but a suspension of a thing.

And you realize, eventually, that the city may not have inhabitants in the way you understand the word. Those signs of occupation—the worn steps, the maintained cisterns, the doors that hang true on their frames—are not evidence of people currently present but of people who were present once and whose actions have been preserved, along with everything else, in the unchanging equilibrium. The city is not maintained. It is *frozen*. Not in ice, but in something deeper—a pause that holds all processes at their current state, a stillness that is not death because nothing has died, merely ceased to progress.

The rain falls and the rain falls and the rain falls, and the cisterns fill to the same level they have always filled to, and the overflow runs the same channels it has always run, and the ferns drink and the moss drinks and the pale salamanders breed in the still water, and the swifts turn in the thin cold air above the sharp peaks, and the city stands exactly as it stands, unmoving, unchanging, a fragment of time torn free and fixed in place, waiting for nothing, holding everything, in the high damp green silence at the roof of the world.
