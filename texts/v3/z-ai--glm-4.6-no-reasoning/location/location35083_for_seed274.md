The air hangs, thick and wet as a soaked sponge. It is never truly dry here. The warmth is a gentle, pervasive thing, a humid blanket that smothers sharp edges and dulls sounds. To breathe is to drink. The ground, at least in the few spaces it can be seen, is a flat, packed clay of a uniform ochre, soft enough to take the imprint of a finger but firm enough that it does not yield underfoot. It is perpetually damp, gleaming with a thin film of condensation that seeps not from above or below, but from the air itself. This is the floor of the Sump.

The scale is intimate. You could not get lost here, not in the conventional sense. The walls that define the space are never more than a few dozen meters away in any direction. These are not walls of deliberate construction, though they are unnervingly uniform. They are formed from the same tough, mineral-laden substance as the ground, but they have been shaped. They rise in ways that stone should not, forming curved surfaces, shallow domes, and seamless ribs that meet the floor without joint or mortar. They are a solidified version of the atmosphere, a captured moment of flow.

The landscape is defined by water, but there are no streams, no rivers, no pools. Instead, the world weeps. From innumerable, microscopic pores in the walls and ceilings, a slow, constant exudation occurs. Droplets form, hesitant and plump, weighing down on themselves until the tension breaks and they fall. The sound is a percolation, a million tiny taps and drips that blend into a single, omnipresent hiss, like static or the sound of a far-off crowd of people whispering. The water itself is strange, heavy with dissolved minerals, and when it lands it does not erode so much as polish. The surfaces it touches for millennia have developed a luster like dark soapstone, smooth and cool.

There is no plant life. The concept of green is alien here. The ontology is entirely mineral. The scrubbing is done by a low, almost invisible ecology. On the walls, where the trickles are most active, grow crusts of vibrant, stromatolite-like accretions. These are not plants to be eaten, but colonies of lithophagic chemovores. They are the only source of color: swirls of rust-red, ochre, and a deep, bruised indigo where they have ingested specific mineral traces from the weeping rock. They do not grow. They simply are, in a state of perfect, unchanging equilibrium, consuming the faintest ions from the water and the air at exactly the rate they are deposited.

To call the structures "scattered" is to misinterpret their relationship. They are not separate objects *in* a space; they are disruptions *of* the space. The signature of the builders—whoever they were—is sparse. At the base of one of the great, curved ribs stands a single, low table carved from the same stuff as the walls. It is rough-hewn, the tool marks still visible: a primitive, chipped-stone technology that left a coarse, pitted surface. There is no chair, no vessel, nothing else with it. Its purpose is unknowable. In another, wider section of the Sump, a series of shallow channels has been gouged into the floor, radiating out from a central depression. They are too wide and even for natural erosion, yet they don't seem to lead anywhere. An altar? A workbench? A place for processing something that required a draining surface? The marks, like everything else, are weathered by the perpetual damp, the edges softened, their original intention faded into the general static of the place.

Fauna is equally rare, its presence felt more than seen. The only known inhabitants are the Skitterers. They are pale, translucent things, like slivers of clay given a faint, nervous life. They are the size of a human hand, flat-bodied with too many legs, moving with a silent, flowing motion that lets them glide over the wet surfaces. They do not seem to eat or reproduce in any observable way. They simply exist, a population that is always the same. Occasionally one will be found, perfectly intact, having simply ceased its motion, its body slowly dissolving back into the mineral moisture over weeks, its components rejoining the great, patient cycle. Their rarity is a function of the scale; to see more than one at a time is an event.

The light is a perpetual, source-less twilight. It is not bright enough to cast sharp shadows, but sufficient to see the muted colors of the mineral crusts and the wet gleam of the stone. It comes from the air itself, a faint, ambient luminescence that seems to be a byproduct of the humidity or the unseen energies that power this place. There is no day and no night. The light, the temperature, the dripping, the silence—it is all constant. The dynamic of the world is not of change, but of maintenance. It is not a river carving a canyon; it is a still pond whose surface tension remains unbroken.

The silence is the dominant sensory experience. The sound of the dripping is so constant and so soft that the mind quickly edits it out, leaving a profound and heavy quiet behind. To speak is to feel the sound absorbed utterly by the wet air, the words dying a few feet from your mouth. This pressure of silence amplifies your own internal noises: the beat of your heart, the rush of breath in your lungs, the soft creak of your joints. The Sump feels less like a place and more like a long, slow thought held by a vast, unknowing mind. It is a world defined not by what happens, but by what, forever and perfectly, does not. It is an equilibrium so absolute that it has become its own kind of motion, a stillness that moves.
