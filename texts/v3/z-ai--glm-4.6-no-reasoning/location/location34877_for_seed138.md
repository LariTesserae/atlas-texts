A low dome of ribbed metal holds back the saturated dark. This is the Steaming Ward—a compact, cathedral-sized processing chamber sunk into the barely-elevated coastal flats. There is no horizon here, only the curved walls of the settlement pressing inward, defining the limits of the world. The space is small enough to feel intimate, yet vast enough to lose a person in its repeating geometries.

The heat is immense, a physical weight pressing down from the low ceiling. At 2.8 on the scale, it is an aggressive, damp heat that opens the pores instantly and steals the breath. It radiates from the heavy iron pipes that snake along the floor and climb the walls, carrying superheated runoff from the settlement's engines. The temperature never fluctuates. This place is locked in a thermal stasis, a furnace-bright equilibrium where nothing cools and nothing heats further. 

Despite the scorching air, water is everywhere. It does not flow; it weeps. The immense pressure and heat force moisture out of the very masonry. Iron walls sweat continuously, shedding heavy, teardrop-sized droplets that fall with a metronomic consistency into grated channel-stones. The air is thick with condensing vapor, a dim, yellow-tinged fog that catches the low, sickly glow of suspended industrial filament bulbs. The light barely reaches the walls; it haloed and suffocated by the wet atmosphere. Puddles stand perpetually on the grid-iron floor, their surfaces broken only by the constant impact of falling condensation.

Vegetation is nearly absent. A few pale, rubbery funnels of thermophilic fungus cling to the highest, coolest points of the ceiling arches, feeding on the vapor, but they are stunted and sparse. Life here belongs instead to the fauna. The weeping walls and standing water breed an abundant, teeming ecology of heat-loving creatures. Blind, pale amphipods—some the size of a thumbnail, some as large as a man's thumb—swarm in the warm puddles, writhing in dense, churning mats. Above them, fat-bodied, wingless moths skitter up the wet iron pilings, drinking the mineral-rich condensation. There are thousands of them, a living, clicking carpet that shifts like a single organism when a drop of hot water falls among them.

The sound is oppressive, a ceaseless mechanical roar that never rises and never falls. The Ward exists in a frozen dynamic; there are no gradients, no shifts in rhythm, no starting or stopping of machines. The bass thrum of subterranean turbines vibrates through the iron floorplates and into the marrow. The high, hissing shriek of pressurized steam venting through safety valves is a constant, unvarying tone. Over it all is the resonant, syncopated drumming of water droplets striking hollow pipes and metal grating. It is a loud, unyielding soundscape, so perfectly steady that it ceases to be heard as separate events and instead becomes a single, deafening chord of industry.

This is a young place, freshly carved and erected. The erosion rating is low; the edges of the iron plates are sharp, the bolt-holes unworn, the concrete still bearing the precise grid-marks of its casting molds. But the water attacks it differently. The atmosphere is so wet, the condensation so constant, that the iron weeps rust as readily as the walls weep water. Bright orange streaks carve down the pilasters, not through the slow patience of centuries, but through the aggressive, accelerated rot of a hothouse. The features are sharp and fresh, yet they feel ancient, painted by the mineral-heavy condensation in deep streaks of copper-green and iron-red.

There is a deep emptiness to the Ward. The density is low; the space is sparse, balanced heavily with vacancy. The massive industrial infrastructure—the boilers, the condensation towers, the thick bundles of insulated pipe—are spaced far apart, leaving wide tracts of empty, wet floor between them. A person walking here is an isolated figure, dwarfed by the vaulted ceiling and separated from the next piece of machinery by a long, puddle-strewn expanse of grating. 

There is nothing weird here, nothing fantastical or supernatural. It is simply the profound, mundane reality of an industrial pocket at the edge of the sea—a room-sized ecosystem defined entirely by the exhaust of human machinery. It is a world of wet iron and hot vapor, of blind creatures and screaming pipes, locked forever in a loud, dim, unchanging purgatory of heat and water.
