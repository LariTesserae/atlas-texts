The cold here is a patient sculptor. It is not the violent, teeth-chattering cold of a blizzard, but a deep, mineral cold that seeps into stone and bone, a stillness that feels like an absence of motion rather than a presence of chill. You are high, so high the sky is a faded, thin bruise of ultramarine above a crown of jagged, young mountains. These are not ancient, weathered ranges. They are raw and new, their granite faces still bearing the sharp-edged scars of their violent birth. The erosion is slow, a glacial polish that will take millennia to soften them, leaving them defiantly sharp.

The scale is the first thing that unmoors you. It is a vertical world. It stretches not so much horizontally as it does plum-bob straight down into an abyss of mist. From a knife-edge arête, you can see other such ridges, arrays of stone teeth jutting from a sea of cloud that fills the canyons between them. The world here is made of stone and air, and nothing else for countless miles.

But the parameters told you it would be dense, and built.

And so it is.

Clinging to every conceivable surface, wherever physics will grant the briefest foothold, is the city of pulp and peg. It is a primitive urbanism, a layered and dense logic of survival in an impossible place. The structures are not built so much as they are *grown* and *woven*. The sparse vegetation is key: claw-like, iron-hard trees, a species of conifer that grows almost sideways out of rock faces, their roots acting like organic bolts. Their wood is dark and dense, almost stone-like itself. From these trees, ropes are woven, and from these ropes, platforms are hung. The entire city of Sere is a tapestry of swinging, swaying walkways, ladders that are more like knotted vines, and small, box-like houses constructed from the ironwood laths and sealed with a paste of pulverized rock and moss.

The density is overwhelming. The structures do not stand apart; they are layered, one over the other, creating deep, shadowed crevices in what is already a world of crevices. Walkways of woven hemp and wood slats are nailed directly to the cliff faces, passing perilously close to windows shuttered against the endless wind. Light, when it penetrates, does so in sharp, slanted beams, illuminating motes of dust and the flicker of oil lamps within. The sound is a testament to this density, or lack thereof. It is mostly quiet. The vastness of the open space between the peaks swallows sound. What you hear are not the clamors of a normal city, but the close, intimate noises of life lived in a stack: the creak of a rope under a person's weight, the low murmur of voices from a platform above, the skittering of claws on wood, the constant, weary groan of the city settling.

The dampness is a constant presence of dread. It does not rain in the conventional sense. The city exists within its own microclimate. The clouds filling the canyons are not passive; they roil and churn, and great, cold waves of condensation wash over the cliff faces. They leave the stone slick and all surfaces—rope, wood, skin—coated in a thin, persistent film of moisture. Water is precious. Every roof, every walkway is designed with a slight incline, channeling this moisture down a complex network of gutters and bamboo pipes into cisterns carved out of the rock itself. The sound you hear most often, after the wind and the creaking, is the gentle, steady *plink... plink... plink... of water dripping into these deep, dark reservoirs.

The fauna is abundant and is the city's reason for being. This vertical world is a migration path. The Silent Drakes, vast, leathery-winged creatures, move through the high peaks. They are not predators of people, but something else. The people of Sere are herders. They do not walk on grassy plains but on vertiginous walkways, guiding their flocks of ridgewings, smaller, pterodactyl-like creatures used for transport and meat. The greatest resource, however, is the silk of the Spinnerwurms, colossal, pale caterpillars that spin vast sheets of diaphanous, incredibly strong silk between the peaks during their brief, slow metamorphosis. The people of Sere harvest this silk, using it to repair their walkways, to make their gliders, to weave the very fabric of their society.

The city has rules of its own physics. You do not run; you move with a deliberate, swaying rhythm, like a tree in the wind. Children are tethered to platforms. Nothing is left unsecured. The dominant technology is primitive but exquisitely suited: the hand-wrought iron spike, the block and tackle, the woven net, the water-carved cistern, the hand-launched glider for traversing the smaller gaps between peaks. It is a society built on knots and balance.

And beneath it all, in the deepest and most permanent shadows of the city’s underbelly, is another life. Where the light never reaches, where the condensation is constant and cold, different things grow. Fungi that phosphoresce with a cold, green light carpet the shaded sides of buildings, providing the only illumination in the lower levels. The sounds here are different—the rustle of pale, eyeless things, the soft scuttling of creatures that have forgotten the sky. This is the city's underbelly, a place of constant, slow decay and rebirth, a world unto itself.

This is Sere. A place of vast, cold emptiness and crushing, primitive density. A city that does not sprawl across a landscape but is a parasite upon it, a testament to the stubborn, tangled, and ingenious persistence of life against a canvas of stone and sky. The change is slow, measured in the breaking of a rope, the carving of a new cistern, the gradual groaning of the granite as the patient cold continues its work. The city is old, but the world is young, and in the tension between the two, this strange, vertical place has learned to exist.
