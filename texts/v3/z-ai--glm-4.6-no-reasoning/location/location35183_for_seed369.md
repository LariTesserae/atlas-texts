The world does not so much have a ground as it has a skin. This is the Skin of the Elder Kelp, a vast, slow, breathing organism that has formed the only land for uncountable years. It is a high place, a plateau that floats in a warm, wet air, its edges curling down into an eternal, sunless mist below. You are standing on its back.

The terrain is a shallow, rolling landscape, the gentle swells of a sleeping titan. The "ground" underfoot is not soil or stone, but a dense, fibrous mat, spongy and resilient. It gives slightly with each step, a surface woven from countless millennia of compressed and fossilized kelp fronds. The color is the deep, bruised purple of a storm cloud at twilight, cross-hatched with the fossilized imprints of veins like the filigree on ancient armor. In the hollows between the swells, water gathers. It is not stagnant, but perpetually renewed, welling up from within the Kelp itself—sweat and nutrient, clear and cold and tasting of salt and minerals. These are the Pools of Weeping, shallow mirrors that perfectly reflect the sky.

The vegetation here is not *on* the Skin; it *is* the Skin, in its living state. From the fibrous ground, stalks of living kelp rise, not as seaweed, but as trees. The fronds that branch from the upper reaches are broad and waxy, overlapping to form a dense canopy that filters the normal, bright light into a soft, cathedral-like gloam below. In the constant warmth, these fronds grow with a palpable, patient slowness, unfurling like the hands of a waking giant over the course of weeks. Where the living kelp is scarred or has died back, patches of phosphorescent fungi bloom, pulsing with a soft, internal, chartreuse light that contrasts with the shimmering violet pools.

The atmosphere is the first thing you hear. It is loud, a constant, enveloping presence that is never silent. The primary sound is a low, resonant tone, a hum that you feel more than you hear, the vibration of a metabolism on a geological scale. This is the breath of the Elder Kelp. Layered over this is the gentle, steady *drip-drip-drip* of condensation running down the broad kelp fronds to patter onto the spongy ground. When the wind, which is rare and gentle, stirs the canopy, the fronds rustle not like leaves, but like the heavy silk of immense banners. Occasionally, a deeper resonance emanates from the Weeping Pools, a visual shudder in the water that produces a sound like a distant, submerged bell, the Kelp flexing a deeper muscle.

Fauna is rare, but what lives here is profoundly strange. The most common inhabitants are the Mollusk-Whales. They are not whales in any oceanic sense, but large, mobile gastropods, their shells grown from the same crystalline salts as the Kelp-sweat. They move with extreme slowness, grazing on the younger, softer kelp stalks. Their passage is a sound in itself—the dry, scraping rasp of a calcified radula against fibrous plant, and the soft, squelching impact of their immense, gelatinous foot on the damp ground. They are solitary lords of their small territories, often found submerged up to their shells in the cool pools, their eyestalks—dark, intelligent, and unwavering—watching the world above the water's surface. Other, smaller things flicker at the edge of perception: translucent insectoids with delicate, latticework wings that skim the surface of the pools, their bodies filled with a luminous fluid that makes them look like fleeting sparks of captured lightning.

The "rules" of this world are the rules of the Elder Kelp. Time is measured in the length of a shadow across a pool, which takes days; in the unfurling of a new frond, which takes seasons. To act here is to act with immense patience. The Kelp is the source of all things: the water you drink is its sweat, the floor you walk is its dead, the light you see is filtered through its flesh. To harm it is to unmake the world. There is no stone not shaped by it, no water not belonging to it. The technology of any potential inhabitants would belong to a primitive, pre-metal age, because there are no metals, only the Kelp and its byproducts. Tools would be fashioned from its hardened fronds, its crystalline salts, the Mollusk-Whales' beautiful, durable shells. Fabric would be woven from its softer fibers.

This is a place of established, settled existence. The erosion is not of rock, but of life upon life. The scars in the Kelp-skin are ancient, overgrown with glowing fungi or new shoots, a record of past trauma and renewal. The scale is grand, yet confined. You can walk from one "edge" of the plateau to a place where the air grows thin and the ground slopes away into the bottomless mist in a matter of hours, but to truly experience a single square meter of this place—to watch the light change, to listen to the slow conversation of hum and drip, to witness the minute movement of a grazing Mollusk-Whale—could take a lifetime. It is a vast room, a lonely grove, a hamlet-sized world, humming with the slow, strange, and loud song of its own creation.
