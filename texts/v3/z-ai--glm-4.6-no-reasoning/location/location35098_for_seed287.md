The air is wet and heavy, tasting of metal and bitter sap. It presses down, a physical presence, coating everything in a perpetual sheen of condensation that never quite dries. The light is dim, a sullen bronze glow that has no apparent source; it seems to emanate from the air itself, from the mist, from the glossy surfaces of the vegetation. It is the color of a forgotten penny, lighting a world rusted into being.

You are below the surface, though not underground in the way of caves or cellars. This is a place, a *hollow*, carved at some ancient, forgotten point into the body of a colossal, sleeping thing. It is a single vast chamber, no bigger than a grand cathedral, its boundaries defined less by walls and more by the transition from the curated interior to the Dense, Impenetrable Thicket that forms the outer world. The "ground" is not soil but a lagging crust of oxidized metal, gridded with long-cooled channels and puckered with the bolt-holes of titanic machines. Water, warm as blood, gathers in these low places, forming shallow, oily pools that reflect the bronze light without any sparkle.

The vegetation is the overwhelming presence. It is lush in a way that feels both manufactured and monstrous. Vast, metallic leaves, dark green alloyed with copper, unfurl from thick, cable-like stems. They are not soft; they have the rigid, articulated feel of industrial girders. Light catching on their serrated edges glints like a whet blade. The dominant structures are towers of what looks like brass vent-stack, but they are alive. They grow in spiraling corkscrews, their surfaces flaking with greenish patina, their joints weeping the same warm, mineral-laced water that pools on the floor. From these towers droop chains of willow-like fronds made of bundled, semi-translucent wire, which chime softly when disturbed, a sound like a thousand tiny bells falling on thick carpet.

The sound is loud, but it is a constant, foundational roar, the echo of a process too slow to perceive directly. It is the sound of an engine the size of a mountain turning over once in a century. It is the deep, thrumming *hum* that vibrates through the metal floor, up into the soles of your feet, a resonant bass note felt more in the bones than heard with the ears. Layered over this are the grinding clicks and shudders of the vegetation itself, as thick leaf-stems pivot to track the dim light, or as the stalks contract and expand in a patient, geological tide. The air hisses faintly, a slow exhalation from vents hidden in the ceiling of the hollow, releasing the hot, damp atmosphere.

This place is a ruin, but it is a living ruin. An old industrial logic is tangled beyond recognition with a biology that has come to inherit it. Strange fruit hangs from the metallic branches: glassy, hive-like orbs that glow with a faint internal pulse, casting rippling shadows. Nestled among the roots that pry apart the floor-plates are shattered porcelain insulators, lengths of fracturing vacuum tube, and spools of thick cable whose copper wires have sprouted into fine, hair-like roots. A single, massive reactor housing, its surface pitted and stained, stands silent in the center of the hollow. A great crack runs down its side, and from this crack erupts the most magnificent growth of all—a profusion of crystalline flora, sharp and faceted, growing like coral in a slow, glittering cascade. The reactor is dead, but its heat and energy have become the soil for a new kind of creation.

There are no animals. The noise and heat tolerate no soft, fleshy thing. The only movement are the patient turnings of the mechanical leaves, the slow growth of the crystal flowers, the perpetual drip of water. The built structures and the growing things have merged into a single entity, dense and layered. The faithless pipes that once carried cooling fluid now serve as vines, their connections lost. The gantries that once allowed for maintenance are now arboreal walkways, slick with moisture and overgrown with lichen-like deposits of rust and corrosion.

The world's rules are simple: heat and water react with the latent core of metal here, and life is the result. It is a strange, alchemical ecology. The deep, slow thrum is the world's heartbeat, the dim light is its photosynthesis, and the slow erosion is not a decay but a transformation—the continuing process of machine becoming earth, factory becoming jungle. To stand here is to feel an immense, geological patience. The change is happening, but it unfolds on the scale of eons. The hollow is in its own state, a balanced ecosystem of rust and electricity, heat and flora, humming its singular, lonely song into the dense, wet dark.
