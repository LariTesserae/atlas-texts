The water does not sit below the land; the water *is* the land. 

This is a high-elevation ocean, a vast and shallow inland sea perched on a plateau so immense that its far shores are lost to the curvature of the planet. The water here is warm—not merely temperate, but bathwater-warm, heated from below by the slow, patient breath of a recently awakened mantle. The seabed is newly risen, thrust up from the deeps in a tectonic event so recent that the mud has not yet settled into ancient contours. The topography is sharp and fresh. Submerged ridges run like tensed tendons beneath the surface, and newly carved trenches drop away into the gloom, their walls still raw and unworn by the patience of centuries. There are no gradual slopes; the young terrain moves in sudden escarpments and broken staircases of naked, pale clay.

Everywhere, there is water, and everywhere, there is growth. The vegetation is not constrained by the shoreline because there is no shoreline. This is a fully submerged biome, yet it breathes with a lushness usually reserved for tropical rainforests. Vast meadows of armored kelp rise from the fresh crevices, their stalks thick as timber and ridged with cartilaginous spurs that keep them rigid in the gentle, omnipresent current. They grow so densely that their canopies interlace a few feet below the surface, forming a layered, near-touching ceiling of verdant shadow. Between the kelp, thickets of spherical algae bloom in heavy, oxygen-saturated clusters, their surfaces sticky with a warm, amber mucilage that traps the ambient light. It is a saturated world; life presses against life, layer upon layer, filling the water column until the very medium feels viscous.

Above, the air is thick with atmospheric water—a permanent, heavy vapor that erases the sun into a diffuse, pearl-white glare. There is no horizon, only a seamless gradient where the white sky merges with the white-capped water. Rain does not fall in drops; it exists as a continuous wall of fine, warm mist, drifting downward with an almost imperceptible patience. When the rain strikes the water, it does not splash; the surface is already so dense with biological film and atmospheric moisture that new rain simply merges into it, leaving expanding, silent rings that overlap and dissolve.

Light reaches this place in diffuse, greenish shafts. It penetrates the mist, filters through the tangled canopy of the giant kelp, and illuminates the mid-water in dappled bands of jade and tourmaline. It is enough light to see, to photosynthesize, to thrive, but it carries a strange quality. Because the terrain is so young and the water so full of suspended organic matter, the light refracts unpredictably. A straight ridge of new rock might suddenly appear to curve; a trench might seem to breathe as the light shifts through the thermal gradients. The landscape has a subtle, unsettling geometry, as if the rules of perspective are still being written here.

Despite the overwhelming density of plant life, this place is nearly devoid of fauna. There are no schools of fish darting through the kelp, no cetacean calls echoing in the blue. The absence of animal motion is profound, lending the entire region a solemn, frozen feeling. The only fauna are extremophile grazers—slow, armoured things like chitons the size of human heads, clinging to the fresh basalt pillars. They move so gradually that their travel is measured in centimeters per tide. They do not hunt; they do not flee. They simply persist, radulae scraping the sweet, bacterial slime from the young rocks, their flat bodies casting hard, dark shadows in the green light.

The silence is immense. Sound requires chaos, collision, and breath, and here, everything is slow and cohesive. The rain merges without percussion; the warm water rises and sinks in patient, languid convection cells; the kelp sways in unison, a synchronized ballet driven by the weak, unvarying currents. The only sound is a deep, omnipresent hum—the resonance of the plateau itself, vibrating at a frequency felt in the marrow rather than heard by the ear. It is the sound of land still stretching after its long sleep below.

This is a world in first bloom, untouched by tool or fire. It is a pristine, high-altitude flood, a slow-motion collision of water and fresh stone. There are no ruins, no artifacts, no markers of human passage. The world operates on a gradient so flat that change is a seasonal drift, a multi-decadal settling of silt, a century-long creep of the kelp forest up the side of a new ridge. It is a landscape of profound patience, where the boundary between liquid and solid, between plant and stone, has not yet been drawn.
