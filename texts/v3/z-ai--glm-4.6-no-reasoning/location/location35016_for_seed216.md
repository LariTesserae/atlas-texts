To stand here is to stand within a cathedral that has never known a mason, built from the bones of something that was never quite alive.

The elevation declares itself not as a peak but as an altitude of the soul. You are high—two kilometers, three, it ceases to matter because the sky here is not a sky. It is a ceiling. The atmosphere is a constructed membrane, weeping. Moisture does not fall as rain; it exudes. Every surface—every wall, every floor, every branching corridor—sweats a cold, mineral-rich condensation. The water parameter is satisfied by the place itself: it is a landscape that cries. Clear beads form on the undersides of ledges, swell, and drop into catchments that are not basins but root-zones. The air is thick with the smell of wet cellulose, calcium, and something faintly electrical, like the memory of a storm that never broke.

And yet—despite the wet, despite the height—it is warm. A deep, uniform warmth radiates from the structure itself, as if the material has a body temperature. You are inside something that holds heat the way a mammal does. This is the first wrongness. The second follows: this vastness is almost entirely empty.

The scale is continental. The constructed landscape extends beyond horizons that never resolve into mountains or seas—only more architecture, more angle, more recursive form. Corridors wide enough to march ten abreast stretch for kilometers without a door. Chambers vaulted like the throats of leviathans open into galleries that taper into capillaries, all grown from the same substance. But within these immense volumes, there is nearly nothing. A few organisms. A drift of spores. The echo of your own breath, swallowed by the softness of the walls. The density is near-void. You could walk for days and encounter nothing but the architecture and the water it weeps.

And the architecture is *all*. There is no natural substrate. No bedrock beneath the floor—only more floor, more material, descending without end. No sky above the ceiling—only more ceiling, ascending into the dim. The built parameter is total. This is a made-environment that has no outside, or if it does, the outside has been so thoroughly enclosed that it has become theoretical. The material itself is the third wrongness: it is plant.

Not plant in the sense of a garden grown upon a building. Plant in the sense that the building *is* the plant. The walls are lignin-dense bark, layered like xylem rings across millions of years of growth. The floors are a rhizomatic mesh, root packed upon root in a lattice so compressed it reads as stone. The ceiling membranes are epidermal, semi-permeable, allowing the internal humidity to regulate itself through stomatal pores that open and close on cycles longer than a human life. This is vegetation at 2.9: not an ecosystem within the space, but the space itself. The plant is the substrate. The plant is the structure. The plant is the continent.

It was made. The growth-channels are too regular, the load-bearing columns too precisely spaced, the fluid-circulation vessels too efficiently branched. Someone—or some process—directed this growth with intention. But the tech is primitive. No machines built this. No steel, no computation, no energy beyond patience and the slow coercion of living material. Hand-tools, perhaps—stone blades to notch and guide, copper shears to prune, simple looms to wrap binding-hemp around joints that needed to fuse at specific angles. The construction of this place took epochs. It was grown by hands that knew only the simplest crafts, but those hands had time beyond measure, and the plant was willing.

The erosion parameter tells you this: it is finished. Not ruined—settled. The water has carved its paths; the roots have found their depths; the condensation-channels have worn smooth grooves into the floors over millennia of drip, drip, drip. The place is in equilibrium with itself. It breathes. It sweats. It holds its warmth. It does not decay; it simply *is*, the way an old forest simply is.

And within it—fauna, abundant but unseen. You hear them before you see them, and the sound is nearly silence. The acoustic profile of the place is hushed by the porosity of the walls; sound is absorbed into the plant-matter like water into loam. What reaches you is the faintest scraping of chitin on bark, the sub-audible thrum of something large breathing slowly in an adjacent chamber, the wet click of mandibles cleaning antennae. The creatures here are pale, eyeless, slow. Gravity is low enough at this altitude—or the architecture's internal tension is strange enough—that some of them drift, suspended by gas-bladders or the faint updraft from warm vents. You catch one in your peripheral vision: a limbless thing like a white leech, as long as your arm, clinging to a vertical root-wall with thousand-jointed legs too fine to see. It does not react to you. It is eating the wall. Everything here eats the architecture, and the architecture feeds them, and grows, and weeps, and remains.

The light is the deepest wrongness. It is dim—not dark, but dim, as if the place exists in permanent twilight. There is no light source. No bioluminescence, no sun, no fire. The walls themselves emit a faint radiance, a grey-green phosphorescence at the threshold of perception, just enough to see by after your eyes adjust and your brain stops screaming that this is impossible. The light seems to come from the water within the walls—from the condensation itself, as if the fluid carries luminosity in solution, or as if the act of the place weeping produces, in some slow physical way, a faint emission of photons. You can read by it, barely. You can see the next chamber. You cannot see the ceiling.

And the place moves. The dynamic is active—not in the sense of danger, but in the sense of process. The temperature shifts by fractions of degrees on cycles tied to the stomatal breathing of the ceiling-pores. The humidity rises and falls. The root-floors slowly flex beneath your feet, adjusting tension across spans that would make an engineer weep. Somewhere, always, a root-tip is extending into a channel worn by water. Somewhere, a cell-wall is lignifying, hardening, becoming the next century's load-bearing column. The predator-prey relationships continue in the dark: the pale things hunt each other by chemical trace, by the vibration of footsteps on bark, by the warmth of bodies in the cool wet air. You are the strangest thing to enter this system in a thousand years, and already something is following your scent, not with hunger but with curiosity, or whatever passes for curiosity in an organism that has never needed to fear.

The final wrongness is the one you feel rather than observe. The rules here are not your rules. The geometry is not Euclidean—or it is, but it is applied at angles that your visual cortex cannot parse. You walked straight for an hour and arrived back at your own footprints, but you never turned. You looked down a corridor and saw yourself from behind, impossibly far away, waving. The water flows uphill in certain channels, or seems to, or does—it doesn't matter which, because the effect is the same: your certainties are dissolving. The plant-architecture does not notice. It grows. It weeps. It holds its warmth and its moisture and its slow, patient life, and if the physics inside its walls are slightly broken, slightly bent around a topology that shouldn't exist in three dimensions—well. It has been here long enough that the brokenness *is* the physics. The erosion has worn the wrongness smooth. Reality broke here, and then the break weathered, and now the break is just another feature of the landscape, like a cliff or a river.

You are inside a living building the size of a continent, built by hands with stone tools, grown from a plant that has become the world, populated by blind pale things that drift and click and feed, illuminated by the sweat of the walls, warm as a body, high as a mountain, empty as a cathedral, quiet as a wound that has finished bleeding.

And it is beautiful. That is the wrongness that haunts you most. It is beautiful, and it does not care that you are here.
