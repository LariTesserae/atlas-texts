At extreme elevation, where the atmosphere thins to a membrane stretched tight over the black vault of space, there is a hollow shaped like a cupped hand. The scale is intimate—no grand plateau or sweeping ridge, but a cathedral-nave of rock, perhaps forty meters across at its widest, walled by vertical stone and open to the sky. This is a place that has worn itself into balance. The erosion is ancient and settled; every edge has been rounded, every surface smoothed into its final, patient form. The rock is a dense, vitreous rhyolite—volcanic glass shot through with cords of cooled feldspar—and it fills the space with a sense of saturation. There is no emptiness here. Every cubic meter is occupied by stone, by air under pressure, by the presence of things.

There is no life here, not as the lowlands would recognize it. No root splits the rock, no seed finds purchase in the creases. The mineral world reigns unchallenged. But the place is not dead. Something fills the ecological vacancy—something that moves and multiplies and competes, though it carries no chlorophyll and spins no chitin.

Across the walls and floor of the hollow, in dense congregations, grow glass lichens. They are not true lichens; they have no symbiotic partner, no algal cell buried in fungal mesh. They are mineral excretions—crystalline growths that emerge from the pore-water trapped in the rhyolite, precipitating slowly as the temperature cycles between extremes. Their structures are fractal, branching like frost on a window, but their substance is hard and clear as quartz. They grow in thickets, in forests, in overlapping shelves that jut from the stone like the pages of books made of ice. Where they cluster most densely, near the floor where condensation collects, they form vertical stands—translucent spires waist-high, clicking gently against each other when the air moves. They are the vegetation of this place, and they are ruthlessly territorial: older colonies advance in fronts, subsuming younger neighbors, their crystal lattices overriding and fragmenting the structures of their competitors. The floor of the hollow is a razor-bright tundra of these growths, shoulder-to-shoulder, iridescent in the high light.

And the light is extreme. At this altitude, the sun strikes without mediation. There is no atmospheric scattering to soften it; it arrives white and absolute, a blade laid across the landscape. Where it touches the glass lichens, they scatter it—prismatic refractions splitting the beam into component colors that paint the surrounding walls in washes of spectral wash. The interior of the hollow is a slow-cycling kaleidoscope: as the sun tracks its arc, the refractions rotate, and the walls shift from amber to violet to a green so deep it aches. In the shadows—brief pools that shrink and swell with the sun's passage—the rock returns to its true color, a dark plum-gray, dense and unreflective. The contrast between the lit and shadowed surfaces is severe, almost violent, and it shifts constantly. There is no moment when the light holds still.

The cold is a presence, not an absence. It sits in the hollow like a heavy gas, pooling in the lowest depressions, settling against the stone. It is not the killing cold of vacuum, but the patient cold of altitude—cold that has learned to endure, that does not rush. The glass lichens do not mind it; they require it. Their crystallization depends on the slow freeze-thaw cycling of the pore-water, the daily rhythm of condensation forming and crystallizing and forming again. Without the cold, they would dissolve; without the damp, they could not grow. The moisture here is scant—no rain falls at this height, no streams run—but condensation is constant. As the heated rock cools through the night, water exsudes from the air, beading on the stone, seeping into the pores, feeding the patient growth. The glass lichens drink by millimeters, by molecular layers, and the damp is always just enough.

Sound exists here, but sparsely. The dominant noise is a quiet, irregular clicking—the sound of crystal spires touching as the air moves through the hollow. It is the sound of a wind chime made of teeth, of glass fingernails tapping in sequence. Sometimes a longer note emerges when a shelf of growth flexes under its own weight and settles against a neighbor with a sound like a wineglass rimmed with a wet finger. There is no howl of wind, no rush of water, no vocalization of any creature. The silence between the clicks is immense, textured, a felt quality of the air itself.

And there are marks upon the stone that are not the work of water or time. Near the rim of the hollow, where the walls begin to open outward toward the sky, someone has carved shallow basins—hand-sized depressions polished smooth inside, arranged in a line along a natural ledge. They are old; the edges have been rounded by the same patient erosion that shaped everything else, and glass lichens have colonized their interiors, growing in rings like tiny frozen cities. But they are deliberate. Someone, working with tools of stone or simple metal, took the time to carve these shapes into the rhyolite. The purpose is unclear. They might be offerings, or markers, or stations for a ritual now forgotten. They might be rests for vessels that held something carried to this height and consumed here. They might be nothing more than a declaration: *I was here. I reached this place. I shaped what I found.* Whatever they were, they are now part of the landscape, another surface for the crystal growths to claim, another texture in the dense field of presences.

The world here is active, though nothing moves with the urgency of muscle. Processes cycle: the sun advances and retreats, the condensation forms and freezes and thaws, the glass lichens grow by fractions of a millimeter and occasionally shatter under their own weight, their fragments scattering across the floor to become the nuclei of new colonies. The light-show rotates through its spectrum. The shadows breathe. The cold settles and lifts. It is an ecosystem of patience, a machinery of mineral and moisture and heat running in slow motion, and it is complete. It does not miss the absent plants. It does not grieve the absent animals. It does not wonder about the absent gods. It simply continues, dense and bright and cold, balanced in its ancient hollow at the top of the world, wearing slowly down.
