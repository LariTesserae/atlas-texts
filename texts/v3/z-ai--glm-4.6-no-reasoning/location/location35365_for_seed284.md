You are inside the mountain. Not a cavern carved from stone, but a pocket—a constructed void sealed within the highest peak of a range whose summit has never seen breathable air. The elevation is extreme; you exist at a vertical extreme that should be hostile to all matter, yet here, behind walls of fused quartz and pressure-rated steel, the atmosphere is merely cool, carrying the faint, sterile chill of a refrigerated vault.

There is no water. Not a droplet of condensation on the pipes, not a trace of humidity in the recycled air. This is a desiccated world, a sealed environment operating on non-aqueous chemistry. Lubricants run thick as honey, dark and synthetic, pooling in the gutters of the walkways without ever evaporating. The air tastes of ozone and boron, a dry mineral tang that coats the tongue and makes breathing a conscious effort. Without water, there is no rust, no decay—only the endless, dry friction of machines against themselves.

The space is tiny—a pocket environment, an intimate room embedded in the flesh of the peak. You could walk its perimeter in four minutes. Yet it is dense, saturated with presence. Every cubic meter is claimed. Pipes crowd the ceiling; cable trays stack three deep along the walls; catwalks overlap in a grid that forces you to duck and sidestep. The walls themselves are layered: outer structural steel, then insulation foam, then a mesh of monitoring wires, then the inner skin of perforated paneling. You are near-touching everything at once, pressed into the machine’s intestines.

And it is a city—a settlement, a town of contained souls. Bunks stacked four high in niches carved between transformers. A mess table bolted to a vibrating compressor. Walkways like streets, intersections where three corridors meet at a junction box spilling wire. There is infrastructure here: power junctions labeled in faded stencil, emergency seals with manual overrides, a ladder that leads to a maintenance hatch that leads to another identical room. This pocket is a neighborhood, stacked and vertical, a hive built for survival rather than comfort.

The industry is the point. This is a harvest station, clinging to the inside of the peak like a tick. Giant borers—their bits embedded in the rock outside, turning slowly—grind down the mountain’s innards and extract what the factory needs: rare earths, crystalline lattices, isotopes that only form under this pressure. The factory floor occupies half the space, and it never sleeps. Conveyor belts carry ground mineral in endless streams. Crushers stamp with muffled rhythm. The whole pocket trembles with the exertion of production, the steel floor plates shuddering under your boots.

The light is dim and constant. Industrial strip fixtures under cages, half of them blown or dark. The rest shed a sallow amber, a tired glow that pools between the machinery and leaves shadows in the gaps. Visibility is five meters in any direction; beyond that, the density of the environment swallows light. You navigate by sound and proximity, by the hum of the transformer at your shoulder, by the heat of the extrusion manifold you must not touch.

And there is life—abundant, teeming, but not plant life. Nothing green has ever grown here. The ecology is fungal, simpler, opportunistic. The colony’s waste heat and organic runoff have spawned an ecosystem in the cracks. Pale thread-fungi spread across the ceiling behind the pipes, fruiting in tiny, colorless nodules that drop spores into the circulating air. Slime molds track along cable runs, seeking warmth, their translucent bodies pulsing with slow protoplasmic flow. In the condensation traps—where the only moisture comes from breath and sweat—microscopic colonies bloom and die in cycles, leaving scaling that the maintenance drones must scrape away. It is a post-life biome, a civilization of decomposers waiting for the big machines to die.

But the dominant fauna are the vermin. Not rats—something hardier, something that evolved for sealed environments and industrial waste. Chitinous, many-legged, the size of a thumb. They scuttle along the pipe runs in convoys, clicking and whistling to each other in frequencies just below hearing. They nest in the warm zones, behind the compressor housings, in the cable trays where the current keeps everything at body temperature. You see them in your peripheral vision—movement everywhere, a skittering periphery. They are not pests here; they are residents. They outnumber the human crew twenty to one, and they have been here longer. Some say they were here before the station was built—that the pocket was their cavity first, and the engineers merely built into it.

The sound is mostly quiet—but a specific quiet: the quiet of enclosure, of insulation, of being sealed inside something while it works. The crushers’ rhythm becomes a heartbeat you stop noticing. The belt motors sing a drone that harmonizes with the transformer hum. The ventilation pushes air with a steady sigh. And beneath it all, the click-click-click of the vermin in the walls, the muffled grinding of the borers in the rock, the creak of the hull as the mountain’s pressure shifts. It is the quiet of a living machine, not the quiet of emptiness.

And it is turbulent. Not storm-turbulent—there is no weather here, no wind, no rain. This is the turbulence of a system at its limit. The pressure gauges fluctuate without pattern. The air recyclers surge and lag, and sometimes the air goes thin for thirty seconds before the backup compressors kick in, and everyone freezes until their ears pop. Power brownouts cascade through the grid, lights flickering, motors stuttering. The borers strike voids in the rock and spin free, then hit hard formations and shriek under the load. The whole station exists in a state of controlled chaos, a anthill of activity where the crew—twelve, fifteen, twenty depending on the shift—rush to patch leaks, clear jams, reset tripped breakers, while the vermin scatter and the fungus fruits and the machines grind on.

This is the place: a pocket of desperate industry at the top of the world, dry and dense and dim, crawling with life that isn’t flora, shaking with work that never stops, sealed against a void that presses always inward. You do not visit this place. You endure it.
