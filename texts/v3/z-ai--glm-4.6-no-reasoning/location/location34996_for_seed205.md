Here, the ground is a suggestion more than a fact, an idea of earth buried under centuries of unceasing life. The world is a single, continent-spanning organism, a woven tapestry of vine, root, and frond so complete it has forgotten the distinction between kingdom and geography. They call it the Great Verdancy, or just The Green, those few who can even conceive of a word for something that is as elemental as air.

You do not walk on soil, but upon the knuckled backs of sleeping titans. The terrain is a soft, uneven cushion of interconnected root-structures, polished by generations of passing bodies to a gleam like oiled hardwood. In places, the canopy of leaf and vine collapses into deep valleys of shadow, where the light of the sky is a distant dream, and the world below is illuminated by its own phosphorescence. Great curtains of moss, thick as a man’s arm, drip with a constant, gentle condensate—the source of the world’s dampness. It is never truly dry here, but it is never truly wet, either. The air itself feels like a living lung, thick with moisture and the sweet, heavy scent of decaying matter being instantly reborn.

The temperature is an inescapable, suffocating blanket. It is not the sharp heat of a desert sun, but the deep, cellular heat of a colossal metabolism. The heat rises from the very ground you stand on, a palpable thrumming energy that makes the air shimmer and waver. It is the heat of a billion chemical reactions, of growth happening on a scale too vast to comprehend. To cool down is to simply climb higher, to find a perch on a great, arching branch where the wind—what little there is—can offer a momentary respite.

That wind is a phantom. The scale of The Green is its own geography, its own physics, and it generates its own weather. Clouds of spores, glittering like metallic dust, drift through the vast aerial channels between canopy-layers. Vortices of heated air rise from the deeper, hotter regions, creating silent, invisible currents that can uplift a flyer for miles, or dash it against a pillar of fungus-hardened wood. The rain, when it comes, is not a straight fall but a scatter. It is sucked into the churning vortex of the foliage, traveling down capillaries and stems to be distributed throughout the system, a billion tiny veins drinking from the sky. Pockets of water exist everywhere: cupped leaves the size of villages, cisterns formed by fused bulbs, and the invisible film of moisture that coats every single surface.

And noise. The sound of this place is not a symphony; it is a constant, overwhelming, deafening roar. It is the sound of a billion lives speaking at once. The dominant note is the hum—the deep, thrumming vibration produced by the larger plant-structures as they draw water up from the unfathomable deeps, a sound so constant it becomes a pressure in the bones. Woven into this drone is the deafening chittering of the insectile swarms, the skittering of countless-legged things in the undergrowth, the resonant bellows of colossal, wandering herbivores, and the piercing shrieks of their predators. It is the sound of growth, of consumption, of frantic, unending life. Silence is the most alien concept here, a vacuum as profound as the void between stars.

The ecology is a single, terrible sentence. Every living thing is both predator and prey, and most are, in some way, a part of The Green itself. The fauna is overwhelming because it *is* the vegetation. There are things that look like trees until they turn and engulf a passing grazer, their roots hardening into spears. There are swarms of flying things that nest in great, hanging fruits, their wings leaves, their bodies gilded with chlorophyll. The apex predators are the Stranglers, immense serpentine creatures whose bodies are indistinguishable from the lianas they camouflage themselves as, and whose calls sound like the tearing of deep wood. Their strange power lies in their mimicry; they can exude a scent identical to a bloaming nectary, luring in creatures for miles before they strike.

The built structures are faint marks on this overwhelming canvas. They are the works of the Sap-Sticks, a race of lanky, semi-sentient primates who live in the high branches. Their tech is primitive, but ingenious. They use sharpened obsidian, scavenged from the rare instances when a non-native meteorite punches through the canopy. They build nests not by construction, but by coaxing. They learn the language of The Green and persuade fast-growing creepers to form living platforms, weave hanging vines into ladders, and induce giant cup-leaves to funnel rainwater into their sponges. Their dwellings are scattered, temporary, and always in a state of being reclaimed. A Sap-Stick village left untended for a season will dissolve back into the seamless whole, its platforms fusing, its ladders growing into new, tangled pathways. They are the only part of this world that remembers the concept of “making,” but even they are just briefly convincing The Green to make it for them.

Yet anywhere you stand, you feel the size of what you are within. Climb the highest vine-spire and the world only reveals itself to be larger, a sea of green rolling to a horizon that is another ridge of the same entity. The place feels old beyond imagining. The root-bridges spanning chasms are worn smooth by eons of footfalls. The great hollows in the colossal boles are not scars, but established features, settling bowls in the planet's living flesh. This is a world that has found its shape and settled into it, a state of dynamic equilibrium. Life rushes on, but the shape of the container remains.

The strangeness is in the cohesion. It is a world where the separation of elements breaks down. Is that a tangled cluster of vines, or the skeletal remains of some long-dead leviathan? Is that a pillar of wood, or a fossilized waterfall of sap? The rules are biological, not geological. Gravity feels weaker here, not because of physics, but because everything is growing *up*, and the sheer force of that upward momentum is a tangible presence. You are in the gut of a living god, and the rule is simple: either you find a niche in its digestion, or you become part of its flesh.
