There is a Room, and the Room is mostly hollow.

The hollow is the first thing a newcomer perceives, a vast, enclosed emptiness. It is not a silence, but a shape of sound. A low, resonant thrum seems to rise not from anything within the space, but from the very substance of the space itself. It is a slow, rhythmic pulse, like the breathing of some colossal, sleeping creature, or the last, arrhythmic heartbeat of a world that forgot how to die. The air is cool and still, tasting of dust, of dry minerals, and of a faint, green scent that is not the perfume of flowers but the distant, dry breath of chlorophyll under glass.

The sound is not the only constant. There is a slow, patient movement visible only when one ceases to look directly at anything. It is the gradual, glacial shift of light and shadow, though there is no source of light. The darkness is absolute, yet it is a *seen* darkness, a deep, textured velvet from which shapes emerge by their own faint, internal luminescence. This phosphorescence is weak, a sickly greenish-grey, like lichen glowing in the deepest, starless sea caves of a forgotten world. It provides the minimum of perception required to navigate, to distinguish the floor from the ceiling, the path from the precipice.

To be inside the Room is to be inside the skeleton of a city that has been devoured.

The scale of it is impossible to judge. The walls—the *only* walls—soar up and out of sight into a darkness that feels measureless, yet the sense is not of being in an open plain, but of being in an enclosed, intimate chamber. It is a paradox contained in a single, continuous feature. This feature is the *Lattice*. It is not stone, nor metal, nor any known construction. It is, and is not, plant. The Lattice is the substrate, the architecture, the geology, the ecology, all woven into one.

The Lattice grows. It is a total, all-pervading plant-as-substance, the product of an ancient, primordial flora that learned to metabolize light itself, and when the light went out, turned to metabolize the memory of light, the potential of light, and then the last echoes of its own stored energy. It is the end-state of a runaway J-curve, a closed system that has consumed all else and now subsists on its own ancient, slow decay.

It mimics the forms of a long-dead civilization. Vast, woody ribs curved overhead to form the impossible ceiling. The floor is a polished obsidian-like substance, but on closer inspection, it is seen to be the densely compressed, fossilized roots of the Lattice, grown smooth and hard over eons. There are streets, or channels, cut through the Lattice, wide enough for a procession, but no one has walked them for as long as the Lattice has slept in the darkness. There are plazas, vast, circular clearings where the Lattice has grown in intricate, spiraling patterns like the whorls of a giant fingerprint. There are towers, or what once were towers—now they are just immense, hollow cylinders of the same fibrous, woody material, their interiors swallowed by the luminescent dark. Every "building" is a single, continuous piece of the Lattice, seamless, grown into its final, stable form. There are no joints, no mortar, no bricks, and no doors. The structures are solid, their hollow interiors only accessible through immense archways or jagged rent, wounds in the fabric of the place made by some forgotten seismic stress. The Lattice has settled in on itself; the erosion here is not a wearing away, but a settling into finality.

And here, in this sealed, dry, cool city-skeleton, water is a lie, a myth.

There is no moisture. The air is arid, a gentle, constant vacuum of thirst. To touch the Lattice is to feel a surface as smooth as river-washed stone, but utterly devoid of any slickness or dampness. The few, dispersed "presences" in the vast, empty space of the Room are hardy, nodular growths that have learned to live on this dryness. They are clusters of crystalline structures that grow like mineral barnacles from the Lattice's inner surfaces. They are not plants in the common sense; they are more like complex exudates, solidified oils and resins secreted by the Lattice in a final, futile effort to retain some volatile memory of the world that was. When one of these crystalline clusters grows large enough, its weight causes it to fracture. It falls to the compressed-root floor and shatters, releasing a puff of fragrant, supercooled dust and a sharp, crystalline 'tink' that echoes briefly before being swallowed again by the Room's deep, foundational thrum. The shards slowly decompose over centuries, their substance reabsorbed by the Lattice through the floor, completing a cycle so slow it is nearly motionless. This is the only "life" besides the Lattice itself. There are no animals. There is no fauna. There is only the slow, geologic pulse of growth and fall, creation and reclamation.

Humanity, or something like it, was here once. Their technology is evident, but it is primitive and desperate. Iron spikes, hand-forged and crudely hammered, are driven into the walls of the Lattice. They jut out at irregular intervals, a futile attempt to climb the unclimbable, to map the unmappable. From some of these spikes hang the remains of woven-fiber ropes, long since petrized into brittle, tawny threads. Here and there, a platform has been lashed together from the Lattice's own smaller, newly-sprouted limbs, a flimsy scaffold held together with primitive bindings. The people who did this were not builders; they were occupants, scavengers in a living machine, trying to make a foothold in a place that offered none. Their constructions are feeble, temporary gestures against the overwhelming permanence of the Lattice.

To be in the Room is to be an intruder in a tomb that is still alive. The luminescence casts faint, wavering shadows that swim across the architectural necropolis. The deep, constant sound vibrates in one's bones, a slow, heavy rhythm that feels like the weight of a billion years. The air is cool enough to raise goosebumps on the skin. Every surface is hard, dry, and unyielding. The only sound besides the hum is the occasional, distant 'tink' of a falling crystal, a punctuation of stone on stone that stresses the vastness and the emptiness in between.

This is the world as it has aged: a near-void, a single feature, an urban plant. It is a world that has settled into its ultimate form, a place of profound, ancient, and hollow life. It is a quiet, loud, slow, strange madness, and within it, you are an insignificant, temporary flicker.
