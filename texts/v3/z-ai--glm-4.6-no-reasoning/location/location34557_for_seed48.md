The air doesn’t move so much as it is breathed. It is a thick, humid mantle that clings to the skin, heavy with the smell of hot metal, wet stone, and something meticulously alive. There is no wind here in the low basin, only the slow, almost imperceptible circulation of heat and moisture that rises from the flooded streets and condenses on the moss-scabbed undersides of the iron superstructure far above.

This is a city built in a swamp, or rather, a city that has become a swamp. Once, the grid of streets and plazas might have been made of poured aggregate and tile, but now they are canals. Shallow, sluggish water moons between the buildings, its surface a perfect, oily mirror for the perpetual dimness. The light, such as it is, is a diffuse, mineral gray that filters down from the high ceiling of the sky, a ceiling composed not of clouds but of the latticework of the city’s upper levels. Great gantries and pipeworks crisscross above, blocking direct sun, casting the lower levels into a permanent, murky twilight. The light is enough to navigate by, to see the shapes of things, but never enough to truly bleach the world of its shadows. It is the light of a deep, overgrown cavern.

The vegetation parameter is a misnomer. There are no plants to speak of. No grass, no weeds, no trees. The ground is not soil but a carpet of rust, fine and red as dust, or cracked, weathered concrete. The only things that grow are the claw-like blooms of corrosion that creep up the stanchions and support pillars, and the shimmering, iridescent films of petrochemical sheen that float on the water’s surface. The city’s life is elsewhere.

Because the fauna is overwhelming.

They are not animals in any recognized biological sense, but they live. They are the mechanical descendants of a purpose long forgotten, the wild offspring of a manufacturing age that never ended. They are the ecosystem. Skittering things the size of a hand, their chassis of stamped steel and brass, spider across the walls, their multifaceted glass lenses whirring as they scan for detritus. They are the scavengers, the insects of this world. In the canals, torpedo-shaped creatures with articulated fins of interlocking plates cruise silently, their movements powered by a low, guttural whine of internal turbines. They are the fish, hunting the smaller clusters of flickering lights that swarm near the algae-like growths on submerged cable housings.

The world is humming. It’s a mostly quiet place, but the silence is an illusion. It is a silence woven from a thousand layers of sound so consistent they become a baseline of nothing. There is the deep, resonant thrum of some massive, unseen machine, the planet-scale heartbeat of the city’s infrastructure. There is the metallic click-and-clatter of a thousand tiny legs on rusted iron. The buildings themselves are the mountains of this range. They are not skyscrapers but industrial ziggurats. Brooding structures of soot-stained brick and riveted iron that rise not to a point, but to a dense, tangled ceiling walkways, pipes, and conduits. Windows are rare; most openings are vast, cavernous bays that once allowed for the loading of raw materials and the shipping of finished goods. Now, these dark mouths are nests for larger entities—perching platforms for winged things that look like flying engine blocks, their wiring sparking with static charge as they preen. Water drips constantly from the pipes and superstructure, a rhythm of *plink… plink…* that has been eroding the masonry for centuries. The weeping rock is not natural, but every bit as weathered, the stains of mineral-laden water tracing dark patterns down the ancient steel like tears on a forgotten face.

This is the established state. Erosion isn't a force of opposition here, but a partner in creation. Rust doesn't decay; it builds. The layers of flaking paint, the patina of oxidized copper, the calcine deposits left by evaporating water—they are the city’s sediment, the evidence of its long, active life. The place is not collapsing; it is settling into a new and permanent form, a balanced system of consumption and creation, industry and nature fused into one.

To walk here is to walk through a vast, empty machine that is thrumming with life. The scale is open. A plaza might be the size of a town square, but its emptiness is defined by the sheer volume of space between the few moving things. The rules of this world are simple and brutal. Energy is food. Conductive materials are like rich soil. A loose power cable arcing in the water can create a blooming frenzy of electrical-siphoning scavengers, a feeding frenzy of light and sound that disappears as quickly as it came, leaving only a scorched cable and a slick of petroleum and cooled metal in the water. The large Automatons are the regulators, clearing blockages, disassembling structures that have become too unstable, and culling the populations of smaller machines when they become too numerous. They are the weather, the natural disaster, and the constant, looming presence that defines the ecosystem.

The dynamic is a slow, ponderous cycle. "Day" and "night" are meaningless, marked only by the subtle dimming or brightening of the ambient light from above as atmospheric conditions shift. The true cycle is one of function. A factory will groan back to life, its great furnaces casting a hellish orange glow over a district for a few days, consuming raw materials brought in by unknown means and producing… something. Scuttling conveyors will ferry the new products—the next generation of smaller machines—away along glowing tracks before the furnace banks its fires, and the complex falls silent again, returning to its slow, patient slumber. The city is not dead. It is not abandoned. It is simply obeying a different logic, a logic of metal and electricity, of corrosion and construction. It is an urban wilderness, a machine-jungle, and it is entirely, terrifyingly, normal.
