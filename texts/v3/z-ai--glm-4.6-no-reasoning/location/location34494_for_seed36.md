It is not a sky that illuminates this place, but a glow that has no source. The light is everywhere and nowhere at once, a patient, unwavering brightness that fills every space without casting shadows. It is the color of fresh cream, warmed through, and it hangs in the air, thick and drinkable.

You are in the Fleshy Garden. There is no other name for it. You are below the level of the world, in a hollow whose size is unknown but feels vast, a valley of soft tissue. The ground is not ground. It is a single, continuous organism, a great, breathing plain of vegetative mass. To step on it is to feel a gentle, resilient give, like pressing your hand into a perfectly ripe melon. The surface is a mottled tapestry of deep crimson, bruised purple, and earthy umber, threaded through with veins of a phosphorescent green that pulse with a slow, silent rhythm. The air is hot, nearly the temperature of blood, and it hangs heavy and humid, thick with a sweet, loamy scent, like soil after a rainstorm mixed with the nectar of thousand-year-old blossoms.

Water is not a river or a lake here, but a weeping. A constant, fine condensation beads on every surface. The broad, leaf-like fronds that sprout in dense, overlapping clusters from the false floor are perpetually slick with it. These droplets gather, coalesce, and slide slowly down the waxy surfaces to collect in the shallow, bowl-like depressions formed by the natural topography of the flesh. The water is warm and tastes faintly of minerals and sugar. This is the only moisture; it doesn't fall from above, it simply exudes from the world itself.

The vegetation is the world. It fills the volume of space with a density that defies emptiness. There are no discrete trees, no individual bushes. Instead, the environment is composed of a single, interwoven architecture of plant-matter. Thick, cable-like stems, as wide as a man’s waist and armored in a smooth, leathery bark, rise from the floor and weave together high overhead, forming a vaulted ceiling. From these primary structures, a hierarchy of lesser growths proliferates.prehensile vines, thick with succulent leaves, hang in dense curtains. Clusters of fungal growths, in shades of opalescent blue and dusty rose, sprout from the crevices where the great stems meet. Towering stalks, topped with heavy, unfurling fronds of a deep, velvety green, stand like lonely columns in a ruined temple. They don’t grow from the soil; they *are* the soil, specialized outgrowths of the greater whole. The light finds every surface, diffusing through the canopy and softening the edges of everything, creating an effect of immense, layered depth.

This is a place without edges and without history. The principle of erosion is an alien concept here, for the place is not being worn down; it is being constantly built up, maintained, renewed. The surface you walk on is embryonic. If you were to cut into it—not that anything here can cut it—you would not find layers of sediment, but a uniform, cellular mass, raw and unformed, pulsing with the same slow energy that animates the glowing veins. There are no cliffs, no canyons, no worn stones. There are only rises and hollows in the living tissue, and they are as permanent as they are gentle, for the world heals and reshapes itself continually, but so slowly as to be imperceptible. This is the state of never-changing things. A wound, if one could be made, would likely seal over in a matter of days, leaving no scar.

Silence is the dominant sound, but it is a living silence. The air is mostly quiet, but for the faintest of sub-audial hums, the resonant frequency of a single, colossal organism metabolizing light and water. Occasionally, a sound will emerge: the soft, leathery rustle as a great frond unfurls itself, a process that takes hours and sounds like a slow sigh. Or the gentle, percussive plink-plink-plink of condensation dripping from a leaf-cluster into a puddle below. There is no wind. The air moves by diffusion and by the slow, tidal respiration of the Garden itself, a minuscule rise and fall of pressure against the skin.

Life here is rare and strange. There are no herds of beasts, no flocks of birds. The primary fauna is the Emptier. It is a creature that looks, from a distance, like a pool of shadow that has gained the will to move. It is a soft, amorphous being, comprised of a fluid, dark substance, and it glides silently across the yielding surface of the floor. It is a scavenger of sorts, but it does not consume flesh or plant-matter. It consumes stillness. It pools in the places where water has settled and, over long periods, it seems to absorb the latent potential, the raw energy, from the dampness. As it feeds, the water beneath it becomes clearer, purer. To watch it is to witness a process, not an action. It is a part of the world's slow self-regulation, a mobile stomach for potential.

Scattered through this endless, living expanse are the built structures. They are not constructed, but grown. Here and there, rising fifty or a hundred feet from the floor, are solitary, architectural forms. They appear to be spires or towers, but they are made of the same plant-matter as the rest of the world, simply encouraged to grow in a different form. Their surfaces are smoother, more geometric, arranged in intricate, crystalline patterns. They are purposeful, but their purpose is utterly, magnificently mysterious. Are they waymarkers? Temples? Resonance chambers for the world’s subsonic hum? They stand as mute statements of intent in an otherwise formless paradise, the only signs that something, at some point, had a plan. There are no tool marks, no joinery, no signs of technology beyond the biological. They are bonsai skyscrapers, cultivated by an unknown hand or perhaps by the Garden's own strange will.

To be here is to be inside a thing that is alive, stable, and utterly complete. It is a world that has already achieved its final form and has now settled into a state of perpetual, pre-dawn becoming. It is a greenhouse for an embryonic god, a womb of geology, a place of dense, hot, patient life that is waiting for nothing, because it already is everything.
