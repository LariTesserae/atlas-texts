To stand in the High Plateau of Kael is to stand on the roof of a manufactured world. The elevation here is not merely high; it is extreme, defining the very physics of the place. The air is thin but carries a persistent, dry warmth—an ambient heat that feels baked into the architecture itself. There is no water. Not a drop falls from the pale, washed-out sky, nor pools in the infinite geometric depressions that pockmark the ground. This is a domain of absolute desiccation, where hydration is a forgotten concept and the chemistry of survival runs on non-aqueous soups of liquid siloxanes and heated gases. The wind that does move across the plateau is a thermal ghost, carrying no moisture, only the faint, abrasive kiss of micronized glass.

The landscape is staggeringly vast, a continent-sized shelf of engineered stone and metal. It is sparse in its presence—there is terrifying distance between any two points of interest, and the emptiness is a physical weight. What does exist here is built. This is an urban environment, but not one designed for human comfort or neighborhood cohesion. It is a city of infrastructure, a settlement scaled to the demands of an industrial epoch: a lattice of extraction towers, atmospheric processors, and freight conveyors stretching to the vanishing point. The constructions are immense, brutalist things of basalt and forged ceramic, layered over geological strata that have been dead for eons. There is no vegetation. No green thing softens the hard angles. The world is post-life, or perhaps pre-life—a mineral engine running its slow, hot course long before biology would ever think to emerge.

Yet, the place is not static. It has arrived at a state of deep, established erosion. The balance here was struck millennia ago, and the world has sat in its own weathered equilibrium ever since. The sharp edges of the industrial spires have been worn down by the patient, relentless rubbing of silicate dust storms. The massive casements of the factories are streaked with iridescent patinas—chlorides and sulfur oxides that have oxidized and re-oxidized into permanent, chemical tapestries. It is a landscape of settled forms, where the decay has become the architecture.

Despite the mechanical vastness, it is mostly quiet. Sound dies in the sparse, open air. There is no resonant hum of a bustling grid, no roar of furnaces—only the low, subsonic thrum of deep-earth compressors cycling over hours, and the occasional, distant clatter of a gantry shifting a fraction of a degree in the heat. It is a quiet that feels heavy, industrial, and deliberate, as if the city is holding its breath through a long, slow metabolic cycle. The dynamic of the plateau is glacial. Change happens across centuries. A furnace might take a decade to power down, its cooling fins creaking in the stillness; a conveyor belt might shift its timing by seconds each year. It is a world of minimal flux, where patience is not a virtue but a physical law.

And in this quiet, mechanical desert, there is life—though life of the strangest, most aberrant kind. The fauna here is abundant, scuttling and drifting through the warm, dry air, but it is entirely fungal-simpler, operating on biological logic that defies terrestrial norms. They are the ferrovores: colonial assemblages of lichenous metallic threads and heat-synthesizing pods. They cling to the exhaust stacks like glistening, lead-colored moss, drinking the warmth and the toxic vapors. Some take the form of slow, tumorous blisters the size of vehicles, clinging to the undersides of processors, pulsing with internal pressures of heated gas. Others are loose, drifting things—fragile, hollow chitin-spheres that catch the thermal updrafts, sailing the vast distances between towers to deposit acidic spores onto the weathered streets. They are mindless, tireless, and numerous, a crawling, swelling ecology that feeds on the machine world and recycles its waste into the crystalline dust.

The weirdness of Kael seeps into you the longer you look. The scale of the construction begins to feel less like an achievement and more like a geological inevitability, a strange loop where the machines mine the dust to build the machines that produce the dust. The light is normal, but its effect is deeply uncanny. The pale sun shines down through the haze of suspended particulates, casting sharp, multiple shadows from the stacked infrastructure. Every angle feels calculated, yet no mind can be found; every process feels purposeful, yet the ultimate product is only heat, dust, and the slow creep of the mineral blight.

It is a place that does not care for you. It is warm, dry, and ancient, an urban desert of infinite shelves and silent engines, populated by creeping metal-flesh and ruled by the long, slow tick of a clock that measures time in the erosion of stone.
