There is no soil. Not in any familiar sense. The ground underfoot is a dark, glassy obsidian, fractured into a tessellation of hexagons and pentagons, each plate the size of a city block. It is not the result of volcanic flow; it is too clean, too perfect. It feels new. Embryonic. The fractures between the plates are deep, geometric wounds in the world's skin, and from them rises the only sound: a constant, low hiss, like a thousand punctured tires or the planet's slow, patient breath.

This is the great panoptical expanse, the Cradle of Glass, a high plateau vast enough that the curvature of the world is a visible, subtle bend in the horizon. The air is thin, searingly cold, and tastes of ozone and minerals. It scrapes the lungs. The light is from a steady, distant sun, white and unforgiving, casting shadows that are sharp as razors. And yet, the world is wet.

The condensation is the first principle of life here. The thermal differential between the supercooled, inert rock of the plain and the slightly warmer, stable air column above it is a permanent engine of weather. Water vapor drawn from lower altitudes or from some unknown atmospheric source clings to the glassy surface. It doesn't bead; it sheets, forming immaculate, silvery films on the obsidian. In the deeper cracks, the condensation runs, pooling into crystalline rivers that follow the geometric seams with a mathematics too perfect for nature.

Those who built the city that is not a city understood this. The 'urban' presence is not a collection of buildings but a system of infrastructure from which buildings have long since been excised. Everything here is a support, a conduit, a regulator. Gigantic, four-limbed scaffolds of the same dark material—girders as thick as sequoias—stride across the plateau. They do not hold anything up; they *are* the structure. They are skeletal arches connecting nothing to nowhere, their only purpose to serve as hosts for the life that teems upon them.

The fauna is abundant, yet the landscape remains sparse. The creatures here are not of flesh and blood in the terrestrial sense. They are crystalline, metallic, gelatinous, and gaseous things, life forms engineered for a raw world still being born. They are the true citizens of this urban skeleton.

Most common are the Slicks, great, amoeboid masses of iridescent, conductive gel that flow through the cracks, their movement a slow, deliberate procession. They consume the mineral-laden condensation, their bodies tailing shimmering, electrostatic veils that crackle faintly in the cold air. They are the waste-management and nutrient-cycling system of this world, leaving behind a film of inert silica that polishes the obsidian even smoother.

Scaling the vast girders are the Architects. They resemble colonies of metallic insects, each the size of a wolf, with six legs tipped in diamond-hard claws. Their carapaces are a complex jigsaw of shifting plates, and they move with a tireless, clicking industry. They are the earthworms and corals of this place, their droppings a composite ceramic that slowly, agonizingly, fills in the fractures, patching the plates. This is the process of erosion in reverse, of active, directed formation. The world is actively healing its own embryonic cracks.

Higher up, clinging to the underside of the great arching struts, are the Ghost Webs. These are not creatures but colonies of symbiotic, semi-intelligent filaments, like spiderwebs woven from fiber optics. They pulse with a soft, internal luminescence—a shifting aurora of pale blues and greens. They are the information network. It is from the Ghost Webs that the occasional true sound originates: a synthesized series of tones, a signal too complex to be a mere byproduct, too infrequent to be a constant chatter. It is the architecture speaking to itself, adjusting the flow of power or the temperature of a specific girder, creating micro-climates to encourage certain growths on one side of the plateau while quelling others.

Looming over all are the Aerials. They are vast, leviathan-like beings, composed almost entirely of a lighter-than-air, shimmering gas contained within a membranous, kite-like structure. They drift kilometers above the surface, their bodies catching the high-altitude winds, their long, tentacular tendrils dangling thousands of feet below to the Ghost Webs, siphoning data or perhaps nourishment. They are the weather; their slow, majestic migrations across the sky cause subtle shifts in pressure, drawing more condensation from the air in a kind of gentle, self-engineered rain.

To stand on this plain is to feel utterly minuscule. The scale is oppressive. The silence is only broken by the hiss of the earth's breath, the faint, distant chime of the Architects' claws, or the ethereal synthesized harmonies of the Ghost Webs. It is a world of immense, patient, and silent construction. It is a planet being completed, its monstrous, post-modern technology not conquering nature but playing the role of geology, biology, and time itself. The sparse, scattered presence of life, each a vital cog in a continental machine, creates an ecology of pure function. There is no chaos here, only the slow, vast, and active transition from a raw idea of a world to a finished one. The weirdness is only in its alien perfection; the rules are clear: maintain the network, consume the condensation, and build the world, one hexagonal plate at a time.
