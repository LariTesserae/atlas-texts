What lives in the Drowned Ward has learned to breathe water as much as air. Not by changing their bodies, but by changing their definition of self to include the damp, the cold, the patient encroachment of a world that is methodically unmaking the one that came before.

They are called the Marsh-stitched, a name they earned themselves, and it speaks to their primary precept: nothing that is useful is ever discarded, and nothing that connects is ever severed. Theirs is a world of fracture and seam.

They are thin, but not with the fragility of the starved. With the economy of the streamlined. Their movements are economical, their limbs spare muscle stretched over bone honed by climbing and bridging. They do not walk so much as flow across the network of planks and lines, their weight distributed, their center of gravity low. Their clothes are patched, but the patches are not signs of poverty, they are maps. Each piece of salvaged oilcloth, each strip of waxed canvas, each rectangle of waterproofed leather sewn onto a tunic or a pair of trousers marks a repair, a victory, the day a weak spot was reinforced and another year of dryness was guaranteed.

Their skin is perpetually cool to the touch. They do not shiver unless the ice-sickness takes them, a deep, shaking chill that can only be fought off in a communal sleeping heap, a tumble of bodies in the driest corner of an attic, sharing warmth like a resource shared from a common well. They accept the cold as a baseline, a constant hum against the skin. The feeling of true, blood-warming heat is a luxury, a memory, an event.

Their senses are strange to outsiders. Hearing is about resonance, not clarity. They can discern the difference in the drip from a moss-tuft on the north side of a building versus the south side—a subtle shift in timbre that speaks to the moisture content of the stone. They can feel the faint, almost subsonic vibration in the wood of a walkway that tells them a support post is shifting in the saturated ground. They see not by light, but by contrast: the green-black of a mossy wall against the black of a flooded cellar, the shimmering silver of a fish-flash in the depths, the soft, diffuse yellow of a neighbor’s lamp pressing through the fog on a winter night.

But it is their hands that are most remarkable. They are their primary interface with the world. Long-fingered and perpetually pale, they are calloused in a way that speaks to a specific kind of labor: the rasp of rope, the grain of waterlogged wood, the tack of fresh-patched tar, the soft grip of a reed stalk, the delicate tension required to tie a knot that must hold weight in the damp, when fraying is a constant threat. They learn a language of touch before they learn a language of words. They can read the history of a wooden beam by running their fingers along it, feeling the grain, checking for the tell-tale softness that signals the rot that hides beneath the surface. They can read the water’s depth by prodding it with a pole, feeling the change in resistance and the change in the echo that comes back up from the bottom.

They live in the borrowed lofts and attics and upper-story rooms. Their homes are nests woven from what was left behind. Insulation is a precious commodity, gathered from the dry wool of old clothing found in drowned wardrobes, packed into the cracks between floorboards and walls. Light is measured. A precious oil lamp is a family heirloom, its flame a carefully guarded fire, the smoke venting through a patched flue or a broken windowpane. Their windows are not for looking out of, but for filtering. They are covered in thin, scraped animal gut or oiled paper, stretched taut on frames. This diffuses the weak gray light into a soft, even glow while keeping the mist and the wind from seeping in. At night, these squares of light glow like islands in a dark archipelago, seen from across the canals of drowned streets.

Their society is not based on blood but on the upkeep of connections. A child’s first responsibility is to learn the names of every rope bridge between their home and the home of their nearest neighbor. Their first craft is to learn to splice rope. Their first act of community is to help re-caulk the seams of a neighbor’s walkway. Status is not derived from age or lineage, but from utility. The most respected person in the district is the one who can best predict a building’s shift, the one who can tie a knot that will not fail for ten years, the one who can tell the depth of the sink by the color of the water, the one whose hands never tremble in the cold.

They have a gentle, simmering animism. They do not worship gods, but they speak to the Ward as a living entity. The slow groan of a building settling is the Ward "stretching in its sleep." A sudden rush of water down a collapsed wall is the Ward "clearing its throat." They do not pray for deliverance. They listen for portents. They leave small offerings of smoothed stone or a single dried fungus on the wide, moss-capped sills of the windows that look out onto the deepest water, a gesture of respect to *the swimmer* or whatever other deep thing moves in the darkness below. They do not fear it; they recognize it as a part of the Ward's body, just as they are a part of it.

They do not have a story of a great flood. Their story is one of a long settling. Their myths are about the cunning of the first moss that learned to drink from condensation, about the bravery of the first reed that grew through a cobblestone, about the memory of the last fire that was made on a ground-floor hearth. Their heroes are not warriors but menders, not conquerors but adapters. They tell stories of The Great Bridge Builder, who single-handedly linked two entire city blocks before the vines reclaimed his work; of The Silent Listener, who could predict a roof collapse by the tone of the dripping water; of The Last Drysoil Farmer, who coaxed potatoes from a single patch of earth on a rooftop for twenty years before the moss finally took it.

Their relationship with the water is complex. It is the enemy that is always at the door, but it is also the larder. They are anglers, not of sport, but of sustenance. They fish with lines lowered from windows, the hooks baited with grubs they find in the rotting wood. The small, dark fish are fatty and sustain them. They harvest the reeds, weaving them into baskets and mats. They harvest the frogs, whose calls are the district’s clock. They know which pools are stagnant and which are fed by the colder currents from below.

The sickness that they fear most is not plague, but seep. It is a spiritual malady as much as a physical one. It is the slow descent into the same state as the world around them. A person with the seep grows lethargic, their skin taking on a grayish pallor. They begin to speak in monosyllables. Their movements slow. They lose the desire to maintain the connections, to patch the wood, to splice the rope. It is said that they begin to hear the music of the deep water, a low, resonant hum that calls them down. When a resident succumbs to the seep, they do not force them to stay. They close the door to their room, and it is understood that when the door is finally breached by the water, the resident will have gone to join it. It is a release, not a tragedy.

What lives in the Drowned Ward is a continuity that is not about permanence, but about persistence. They are a thread woven through a fabric that is slowly unraveling. They are not holding the Ward together; they are simply the last knots in the net, holding on to each other. They know their world is sinking. They count their lives not in years, but in floors. When their floor is claimed, they will haul their world—one more time—up to the next. And they will go on, tending their small warm lights, listening to the frog-song, and feeling the steady, patient, endless breathing of the place that is, very slowly, swallowing them whole.
