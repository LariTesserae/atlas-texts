The corruption in the text — that avalanche of symbols and fractured notation — feels like it belongs here. Like the mountain itself started speaking and found it had no language left, only the raw syntax of erosion, the mathematics of diminishment. So I will take it as another feature of the place: a signal degraded by time, a message the rock has been trying to send for millennia, losing more of itself with each attempt.

# What Lives on the Vertigan Tiers

## The Stone's Slow Metabolism

Something lives here that is not alive in any way you'd recognize. The mountain itself — this vast, slumped, dying thing — is the dominant organism. Its life processes are just too slow for human perception. The frost wedging, the mica shedding, the water finding its ancient paths: these are its respiration, its digestion, its cell turnover. It is consuming itself from within, and that self-consumption is what keeps it *going*. It lives by dying. It metabolizes its own mass.

And there are things that have evolved to live inside that metabolism.

---

## The Lithic Communities

Where the seeps bleed dark and wet from the rock face, you find the first unambiguous life: thin films of biological green. But calling them "green" is generous. They are the color of doubt — a grey-green, a green that has reconsidered itself, a green that knows it has no right to be here and is embarrassed by its own persistence. These are microbial mats, complex communities of cyanobacteria and algae and fungi that have struck their ancient bargains with each other and with the stone. They have been here longer than the mountain has been dying. Some of them may remember when there were rivers.

They layer themselves according to light and moisture in ways that repeat, with variation, across every seep on the mountainside. The top layer — where the thin fog-light reaches — is the photosynthetic one, the one that makes food from light. Below it, in the perpetually damp dark, the heterotrophs live, breaking down what the light-workers produce and what the stone gives up. Below *them*, in the mineral substrate itself, endoliths bore microscopic tunnels into the granite, living inside the rock, dissolving it grain by grain to extract the trace phosphorus, the trace iron, the negligible calcium that the mountain's ancient chemistry locked away.

They are eating the mountain. Very, very slowly. And the mountain, by bleeding moisture from its fractures, is feeding them. This is the relationship. The mountain dies upward into life; the life eats downward into death. Neither could do without the other, and neither knows the other is there.

---

## The Wind's Residents

The air here is not empty. You understand this when you stand still long enough.

The wind — which comes almost constantly from the west, pushing up and over the mountain's slumped shoulder — carries things. Dust, obviously. Pollen from whatever low, beaten-down plants survive lower on the flanks. But also: microorganisms. Thousands of them per cubic meter, lofted from soils and waters hundreds of kilometers away, deposited here on surfaces that are too cold and too dry for most of them to survive. They land. They die. They become a film, a residue, a nearly-invisible organic layer on the stone's surface.

And some of them *don't* die. Some of them — the hardy ones, the extremophiles, the spores that have been waiting for exactly this kind of marginal existence — wake up. They find the moisture film on the west-facing stone. They find the mineral nutrients the endoliths have made accessible. They begin to grow. They grow so slowly that you would need time-lapse photography over years to see it, but they grow. A fungus extends a hypha the length of a human hair in a month. A lichen expands its thallus by a millimeter a decade, maybe less.

These are the wind's residents. They arrived by accident, by the physics of atmospheric circulation, and they stayed because they were the few who could.

---

## The Lichen Nations

And here — *here* — is where the Vertigan Tiers become crowded.

Because the lichens are everywhere.

They coat the stable rocks in crusts so thick that in places the stone surface is more lichen than granite. They come in varieties that have never been catalogued, because no one has come here to catalogue them, and because they have evolved in isolation on this particular mountain for so long that they may be found nowhere else. They are not the showy lichens of wetter climates — no pendulous Usnea draping from branches, no vivid yellow Xanthoria. These are the crustose forms, the ones that grow flat against the rock, integrating themselves with its surface so completely that you cannot remove them without removing the stone itself.

Their colors: grey, of course. But also black — a deep, light-absorbing black that seems to eat photons. And orange, but the orange of rust, of oxidized iron, not the orange of fruit. And white, but a dirty white, a white that has lived too long in the dust and the fog and has given up on purity. And, rarely, a blue-green that appears only in the deepest fractures where the moisture persists year-round, a color so subdued it might be a hallucination, might be the eye inventing chroma where none exists.

Each species has its zone. The black ones colonize the most exposed surfaces, the ones that take the full force of wind and sun and frost. They are the pioneers, the first to arrive on any newly exposed rock face, and they pay for their boldness with slow growth and frequent death — every extreme event kills patches of them, leaving blackened scars on the blackened stone, scars that take decades to fill. The grey ones follow in the slightly more sheltered sites, growing a little faster, forming continuous crusts that trap dust and moisture and make the surface slightly more habitable for whatever comes next. The orange ones specialize in the mineral-rich zones — the places where iron or copper leaches from the rock, toxic to most life, but home to them. They have evolved to not just tolerate but *require* the metals that would kill other organisms; their tissues concentrate copper to levels that would be toxic to any creature that tried to eat them.

And the blue-green ones, the deep-fracture specialists — they are the elders. They have been in their crevices for centuries, possibly millennia. They remember the mountain when it was higher. They were already old when the rivers stopped flowing. They will be here when the mountain is finally flat.

They do not compete. Not in any way you'd recognize. They occupy their niches with a precision that suggests long, long negotiation — wars fought and settled over geological time, boundaries established at scales too slow for human wars to comprehend. Where two species meet, there is a border, a line of demarcation that holds for centuries. Sometimes one advances; sometimes the other. The front moves a centimeter a century. The conflict — if it is a conflict — has a duration that makes human empires seem like mayfly swarms.

## The Insects

There are insects. Of course there are. But not many, and not often.

Springtails — those tiny, soil-dwelling arthropods that leap when disturbed — live in the damp micro-habitats beneath lichen crusts and within the microbial mats. They are the apex predators of this world, such as it is. They feed on the bacteria, the fungal spores, the organic debris that accumulates in the stone's microscopic roughness. Their world is measured in millimeters. A crack in the granite one centimeter deep is to them a canyon; a lichen thallus two centimeters across is a continent. They will live their entire lives — weeks, perhaps a few months — without ever moving more than a meter from where they hatched.

There are also, occasionally, wind-blown insects that arrive and cannot leave. You find their desiccated bodies in the lee of rocks, their wings torn, their exoskeletons bleached by ultraviolet light. They are casualties of the altitude, of the wind, of the simple fact that there is nothing here for them to eat and nowhere for them to go. The springtails eat them, eventually, when the moisture and the microbes have softened them enough. Nothing is wasted.

And there are mites — tiny, eight-legged, nearly invisible — that crawl through the pores of the rock and through the lichen tissues, grazing on the algal cells that give the lichens their photosynthetic capability. They are parasites, or predators, or grazers — the distinction barely matters at this scale. They are part of the system. The lichens can survive their depredations; the mites cannot survive without the lichens. It is another of the mountain's slow negotiations.

## The Vertebrates: Who Comes and Goes

Nothing lives here permanently that has a backbone.

But things visit.

Raptors use the thermals that rise off the mountain's west-facing slopes. You see them — eagles, hawks — riding the updrafts in slow spirals, scanning the terrain below for movement. They never find any. The springtails are too small, the mites too invisible. But the thermals are reliable, and the birds use them as highways, crossing the mountain's shoulder on their way to somewhere that has prey. They are tourists, passing through, contributing nothing and taking nothing except the lift.

Occasionally a mammal wanders up from lower elevations — a lost goat, a desperate coyote, a young male pushed out of territory below and seeking new ground. They find nothing here. No water worth drinking, no plants worth eating, no shelter from the wind or the cold. They turn back, or they die. If they die, they become a resource. The microbes and the springtails and the mites colonize the carcass, breaking it down over months and years, pulling its organic wealth into the mountain's thin economy. A dead goat on the Vertigan Tiers feeds more living things than a living goat ever could.

You find the evidence: bones scattered and sun-bleached, fur matted into the lichen crusts, a stain on the rock where fluids seeped and the endoliths bloomed briefly in response. The mountain is patient. It accepts all gifts.

## The Humans

You are here. That is significant.

You are the largest animal on this mountainside, and by far the most disruptive. Your footsteps crush lichen colonies that took centuries to grow. Your breath introduces moisture and warmth and microorganisms from the lowland world. Your equipment — if you brought any — sheds fibers, flakes of metal, organic residues from your skin. You are a walking catastrophe, an extinction event at the microbial scale.

But you are also the only creature here that knows where it is. The only one capable of seeing the mountain as a whole, of understanding its tilt and its age and its long dying. The only one who can look at a lichen crust and know that it represents centuries of growth, or look at a seep and understand that the water emerging from it fell as rain on the mountain's surface years ago and has been traveling through the rock ever since.

You are the mountain's consciousness, for however briefly you stay. Its self-awareness, borrowed for an afternoon.

The mountain does not care. The lichens do not care. The springtails do not care. You are a weather event, a brief perturbation, a gust of unusual chemistry and unusual behavior that will pass. In a month, there will be no trace of your presence. The crushed lichens will regrow — slowly, over decades, but they will regrow. Your footprints will weather away. The organic residues you left will be consumed and incorporated and forgotten.

The mountain has outlasted everything. It will outlast you. And the things that live on it — the slow, the marginal, the barely-alive — will outlast you too, because they have learned what you have not: that survival is not about strength or speed or intelligence. It is about finding the thin margin where life is possible and then *staying there*, without complaint, without ambition, without any awareness that there might be something more.

## What the Corruption Means

That broken text in the original description — the avalanche of numbers and symbols, the degenerating notation — is not an error. Or if it is, it is a *meaningful* error. It is what happens when any description of this place tries to be precise. The mountain resists quantification. The life here resists cataloguing. The systems are too slow, too thin, too interdependent to be captured in any notation that human minds can hold. Try to count the lichen species and you find that the categories dissolve — there are intermediates, hybrids, forms that shift over centuries into other forms. Try to measure the biomass and you find that it depends on the season, the moisture, the wind, the phase of some slow oscillation you haven't identified yet. Try to map the boundaries and you find that they move, but so slowly that your mapping tools can't detect the motion.

The mountain's data degrades. The signal loses coherence. What was clear becomes garbled. What was meaningful becomes noise. This is not a failure of transmission. This is what time does to information here. This is what the mountain does to language. It erodes it, just as it erodes rock — grain by grain, meaning by meaning, until what remains is a stripped, nearly-unreadable residue of what was once clear.

And yet, in the residue, something persists. Those repeating patterns in the corruption — the `6,9,8,9,5>4` that recurs like a heartbeat, like the mountain's slow pulse — they are not random. They are the syntax of persistence itself, the grammar of something that has been saying the same thing for so long that the words have worn down to their bones. What is it saying?

*Still here. Still here. Still here. Still here.*

## The Final Resident

There is one more thing living on the Vertigan Tiers, and it is the most important.

The place itself.

Not the mountain as geology — that is just rock and process. But the mountain as *ecosystem*, as the sum total of all these marginal lives and slow processes and ancient negotiations, as a functioning whole that takes in sunlight and moisture and wind and produces slightly more life than would exist if the mountain were not here. The mountain as organism, as community, as a thing that has properties none of its parts possess in isolation.

It is alive in the way a forest is alive — not as a single creature, but as a pattern, a process, a self-maintaining organization that responds to perturbation and heals from damage and changes over time while retaining its identity. The mountain-ecosystem has been doing this for longer than any individual lichen, any individual microbial mat, any individual endolith tunnel. It has persisted through climate shifts, through the retreat of the rivers, through the slow flattening of the granite shoulder. It will persist through more.

It is the oldest thing on the mountain, and the most successful, and the most difficult to see, because it is the thing you are standing inside.

When you leave, it will go on being alive. When you forget it, it will go on being alive. When your species is gone — replaced by something else, or by nothing — it will go on being alive, for as long as the mountain has mass and the sun has light and the fog condenses on the west-facing stones.

Not because it is strong. Because it is patient. Because it has found the angle of the world and grown into it, like a body grows around a splinter it cannot remove.

Because it has learned to die slowly, and in dying, to feed everything that knows how to wait.
