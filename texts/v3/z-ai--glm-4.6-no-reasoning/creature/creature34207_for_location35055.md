To live here is to be a vibration in a medium that has forgotten how to be still. They are the noise in the silence, the last heat in the cold, the only remaining proof that time has not entirely stopped. They do not have names, for there is no one to name them, and they do not have histories, for their forms were finalized eons ago, locked into place by the same terminal equilibrium that petrified the world around them. They simply *are*—a vast, interlocking biomass of flesh and chitin and electromagnetic intent, woven into the vegetative lattice like iron filings trapped in the folds of a magnet.

Consider first the giants, the slow draggers along the horizontal cross-vines. They are the low-frequency boomers, the architects of the bass-drum thunder that vibrates through the heavy water. They are massive—tons of pale, segmented flesh suspended in the pressurized gloom, their weight negated just enough by the water's density to keep them from snapping the calcified boughs they traverse. Their bodies are asymmetrical, twisted along their longitudinal axis to match the logarithmic spiral of the plant. To look at them—if looking were possible—would be to see a wrongness, a creature built for a curved space, their right sides subtly longer than their left, their segments overlapping like the shingles of a twisted spire. 

They have no eyes, only vast, depressed troughs along their skulls where eyes might have been a billion years ago. Now, these troughs are filled with ampullae—gel-filled pits that sense the faint electromagnetic fields radiating from the vines and from one another. They do not see the grid; they feel it as a shimmering, geometric heat map in the dark. Their legs are thick, chitinous crabs' legs, jointed backward at the knee, ending in hardened, grooved hooks that lock into the furrows of the petrified bark. They move with a deliberate, pulling rhythm, scraping their mandibles against the plant's chitin to grind away the microscopic spores and fungal blooms that attempt to colonize the surface. Their jaws are circular, rotating mills, lined with rows of silicate-hard teeth that wear down and are replaced in an endless cycle, grinding, grinding, grinding. They never sleep. To stop moving is to lose one's place in the electromagnetic current, to become a dead node, to sink into the open, frigid voids between the stalks where the scavengers wait.

Then there are the mid-woven, the predators and the prey of the grid. They are sleek, eyeless torpedoes of muscle and cartilage, built to navigate the tight spirals of the guy-wires and the vast, echoing shafts between the main pylons. Their bodies are taut with lateral lines—canals along their flanks that detect the slightest displacement in the water, reading the acoustic landscape like a blind reader running fingers over braille. They communicate in the resonant clicks that bounce off the plant-walls, using the structure itself as a medium for sonar. The calcified vines are their sounding boards; they sing to the architecture, and the architecture sings back, mapping the world into their minds in a lattice of echoes. They are ceaselessly active, darting through the dark with a desperate, twitching energy, their hearts beating in a constant, frantic rhythm to stave off the bone-deep chill. They hunt the swarms, and they are hunted by the giants. When they die, their bodies drift, slowly rotating along the spiral of the water column until they settle into a fork of the plant, where the tiny things will reduce them to spore-food in hours.

And then there are the swarms—the static in the dark, the high-frequency shriekers that cut through the bass thrum of the world. They are countless, a cloud of infinitesimal shards that occupy the outermost, thinnest vines where the larger beasts cannot reach. Each is no larger than a fingernail, a geometric fragment of chitin and vibrating muscle. They do not navigate individually; they are a superorganism, a single mind distributed across a thousand bodies, their electromagnetic fields overlapping and merging into a shimmering, localized aura. When a predator approaches, the aura distorts, and the swarm scatters in a perfect, mathematically predictable bloom, reforming an instant later on the other side of a cross-beam. They are the cleaners, the scavengers, the microscopic gears of the ecosystem. They eat everything—the spores, the dead flesh, the waste byproducts of the giants. They chatter in frequencies so high they vibrate the very water molecules, creating a localized heat that sustains their frantic metabolisms. They are the noise that never ceases.

All of them are shaped by the fold. Because the plant spirals, the space itself is curved, and the creatures are curved with it. Their internal organs are not symmetrical; they are shifted, coiled, wrapped around a central axis that follows the twist of the world. Their minds—if they can be called minds—are mathematical engines tuned to non-Euclidean geometries. They do not think of "up" and "down" as straight lines. To them, ascent is a rotation, a slow, tightening corkscrew toward an unreachable top. They do not feel the vertigo that an outsider would feel, because their balance is calibrated to the spin. They are native to a helix, and a straight line would feel to them like a fall.

They do not know they are at the end of history. They do not know that nothing new will grow, that the erosion has stopped, that the world has settled into its final, unshakeable shape. They do not know because they cannot conceive of a before or an after; their memories are only as long as the echo of their clicks, their lives a brief, loud flicker against the endless, changeless backdrop. They are the inhabitants of the drowned architecture, the final tenants of a world that has forgotten how to die. They grind, they roar, they swarm, they sing to the petrified vines. They are the heartbeat of the dark, and they will go on, vibrating in the cold, heavy water, until the plant falls, or the water freezes, or the spiral finally closes upon itself at the end of forever.
