To live here is to be a tenant in a body not your own.

There are no independent lives in this jade-throated senescence, only arrangements—gradations of integration within the flesh of the sleeping continent. The distinction between resident and environment has dissolved like the light, bled into the same chlorophyll-sick medium. To exist here at all is to be partially digested by the geometry of the place, incorporated into its slow, patient metabolism.

Consider the spores. They are the words of this world, its breath, its loose change. They drift in the wet weight of the air, a million dandelion-wishes each trailing a winking mote of phosphorescence. They are not merely floating; they are *sent*. Each is a single, desperate idea—a genetic fragment, a chemical whisper—launched from some fruiting body deep in the lattice, seeking a moist crevice to take root. To inhale them is to be seeded. The interiors of any creature with a cavity are lined with a fine, harmless down of nascent mold, a symbiotic dust that lives off the warmth of your breath. You are a vector, nothing more. The spores do not care about you; they use you.

Then there are the grazers, the gelatinous slug-things that leave shimmering, mucous-slick trails across the vast, village-sized leaf-scapes. They are stomachs on bellies, driven by a single, ancient imperative: to find the Quiefing pools and consume the algal film that skins the still water. They are creatures of the surface, their bodies translucent bags of pale fluid, slow and impervious. They have no predators, only passengers. The shimmer in their slime is not an accident of chemistry; it is a garden. Microscopic fungal colonies ride along in the mucus, seeding the broad leaves where the slug-things pass, fertilizing the substrate with a trail of nutrients. They are tractors, tilling the living ground with their passage, their lives a closed loop of eating and excreting, entirely beholden to the plant-body they walk upon. They do not think; they simply route themselves toward the scent of standing water, biological automatons in a factory of flesh.

Above them glide the manta-things, their membranous bodies pushing through the heavy, liquid air with slow, undulating patience. They are little more than parasols—thin sheets of dark, vein-laced tissue stretched over a simple cartilage frame, trailing long, gill-like tendrils that drag through the humidity. They are the atmosphere's scavengers. They cannot stop; to rest is to sink, to be absorbed by the patient ground below. They ride the imperceptible thermal currents rising from the warm, living substrate, their tendrils combing the air for moisture, for spores, for the chemical exhalations of the superorganism. They drink the air. They are the air, given a fleeting, structured shape. When they die, they dissolve almost instantly, their thin bodies consumed by the very medium they once navigated, leaving nothing but a brief, oily slick on the surface of a Quiefing pool.

But the true inhabitants, the ones who *understand* this place, are the Weavers.

They are not its rulers; they are its immune system, its gardeners, its white blood cells. They are arthropoidal in the way that a cathedral is architectural—a description of form, not of spirit. Their bodies are long, spindly, composed of segments that move with a fluidity that should be impossible for things so rigid. Their carapaces are not grown, but *built*, woven from the same dark, lignin-rich material as the plant-pillars, seamless and matte-finished, absorbing the exhausted green light until they are nearly invisible. They do not scuttle or clatter; they flow. Their myriad limbs move with the slow, deliberate precision of calligraphers, each step premeditated, each gesture an act of curation.

To watch a Weaver work is to watch a mind commune with a body far larger than itself. They do not see with eyes; they sense with the tips of their limbs, reading the tension in the stems, the humidity in the air, the chemical dialect of distress or overgrowth. When a water channel becomes blocked by a collapsed frond, a Weaver will arrive—not hurriedly, but with the inevitability of a tide—and with infinite, multi-limbed patience, it will clear the blockage. It prunes. It splices. It grafts. It tends to the fungal gardens below, brushing away debris with the tender care of a mother cleaning a child's hair, coaxing the cold, phosphorescent mycelium to spread its ghostly blue light where the superorganism needs it most.

They shed their carapace plates as they grow, the hollow *thock* of the falling shell the only punctuation in the sub-audible hum of the world. The shed plates do not go to waste; they are incorporated. Fungi colonize them, slugs graze upon the algae that grows on their inner surfaces, and eventually, they sink into the spongy ground and become part of the living substrate. The Weavers are the loop made visible—the superorganism's self-maintenance given legs.

They do not notice you. You are not a threat; you are not even a nuisance. You are a minor perturbation, a slight indentation in the springy ground that will be relinquished the moment you move on. Their purpose is too ancient, too fundamental, to be interrupted by something so ephemeral as a traveler. They were here before the Memory-Plains were scars, and they will be here long after the last circular pool has been overgrown and the geometry of the forgotten civilization has been fully reabsorbed. They are the continuation of the superorganism's will, expressed in chitin and patience.

And then, there is whatever lives in the Memory-Plains.

Nothing lives there, not anymore. The Weavers will not cross them. The slug-things skirt their edges, sensing the sterility in the firmer, unyielding ground. Even the spores seem to drift away from the thin, geometric acreage, repelled by some ancient chemical signature. But there is a presence in those scars. It is the shadow of a different kind of life—a life that did not build *on* the world, but *within* it, a civilization so deeply integrated into the superorganism that its removal left a wound. They were not builders of stone or metal; they were weavers of a different sort, shaping the living substrate into rooms and channels, their architecture indistinguishable from the landscape. And when they left—or were consumed, or became something else—their shape remained, a fossil not of bone, but of *purpose*. The Memory-Plains are their ghosts: the perfectly circular pools, the flattened terrain, the geometric orientation of the leaves. They are the echoes of minds that thought they could live inside a god, only to find that they were simply another layer of its skin, destined to be sloughed off and healed over in the slow, geological patience of its dreaming.

To be here is to be inside a body. The fauna are its contents, its passengers, its parasites and symbiotes, moving through its capillaries and breathing its breath. They are not separate from the warm, wet weight of the air, or the spongy, yielding ground, or the exhausted, green light. They are expressions of it—temporary condensations of the same ancient life, playing out their small roles in the maintenance of a continent-spanning sleep, before dissolving back into the jade and moss, the bruised blues and the quiet hum.
