Below them—always below them, in the thick drizzle where the mist becomes a wall—are the Mill-Cult.

The Arborists maintain the architecture; the Mill-Cult feeds it. They are a people who have been deafened by purpose. The bark-strippers and pulp-crushers define the limits of their perception. But the Arborists and the Mill-Cult are merely the meat. The true residents of the Sapwood Reach do not walk, do not breathe, and do not die in any way that the ledgers could record.

There is the Unrot.

It is not a species. It is a condition of the wood. In the upper terraces, where the condensation is silver and the silence is vast, the trees are dying, hollowed by the ceaseless extraction. But the wood, saturated with copper, threaded with the rusted bones of swallowed iron, does not rot. It petrifies into a dense, conductive mineral-wood, a substance that rings like a bell when struck. The Unrot is a geological memory of a biological form. It grows nowhere and everywhere, spreading through the root-networks as a slow, crystalline frost. If you press your ear to a wall of Unrot, you do not hear the wet, spongy pulse of a living tree. You hear the grinding of tectonic plates. You hear the memory of the mountain, the ancient, forgotten rage of the stone that the roots once crushed. The Unrot is the city becoming a machine, a mineral heaven, and it is the loneliest thing on the Reach. It stands in the fog, grey and metallic, reaching for a skyline that will never have crags, only the endless, flat expanse of a canopy that is no longer breathing.

And then, there are the Phosphenes.

The weirdness. The unreality. The localized insanity. It is not a haunting; it is a broadcast. The Phosphenes are the silhouettes in the ozone-smelling hollows, the faces in the wood grain, the detached shadows walking the outer tram-lines. They are the ghosts of the wood's own false memories, given heat and light by the electricity bleeding from the generators into the xylem. A Phosphene is not the spirit of a dead mill-worker, though it may wear the flickering outline of a woman hefting a bandsaw, her gesture perfect, her face a smear of static. They are the trees dreaming of the machine. The vegetation, hyper-evolved and chemically aggressive, has absorbed so much copper, so much electrical hum, so much mechanical rhythm that its root-brains—if such a word can be used for a distributed network of chemical gradients and electrical potentials—have begun to hallucinate a mechanical ancestry.

The Sapwood Reach is not a place where people live. It is a churning. It is a closed system of desperate, churning motion, and what lives here are the expressions of that churn.
