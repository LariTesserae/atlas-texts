They do not live here. They persist.

They are not born for this place; they are re-made by it. The mountain takes them in as soft, water-dependent things and squeezes them into something drier, tighter, harder. After three months, the sweat stops. The body learns to cycle its moisture inward, recycling breath and blood plasma until the skin turns papery and taut, smelling constantly of boron and ozone. They become semi-closed systems, leaky only at the eyes, which weep not from sentiment but from the caustic, desiccated air that forces hydration out as a defensive reflex. Their hands grow thick with callus, but a strange callus—not from gripping tools, but from the constant vibration of the floor plates. A tremor lives in their bones, a low-frequency hum absorbed from the shuddering steel until their very skeletons resonate with the factory’s heartbeat. When they sleep, they sleep in the bunks stacked between the transformers, and the 60-hertz drone enters their dreams, flattening them into gray, narrative-less loops of movement and warning. They wake not rested, but recalibrated.

There are rarely twenty of them at any given time. Twelve is the skeleton; twenty is a crowd that makes the walkways impassable and tempers short. They are not a community. They are a watch—shifts of mutual reliance dissolved the moment the crisis passes. They know each other by the rhythm of their footsteps on the catwalks, by the specific way each man or woman ducks the same low-hanging pipe. They do not speak unless the system demands it. Speech requires moisture, requires breath wasted on shaping air when a gesture toward a pressure gauge will do. They have developed a shorthand of touches and angles: a tap on the shoulder plus a jerk of the head means *borer bind-up on the western face*; a flat palm pressed to the chest means *air thinning, breathe shallow*. When they do speak, their voices are hoarse, stripped of resonance by the recycled atmosphere. They sound like the vermin—clicking and whistling in the dry dark.

Their bodies map the space. A veteran of the peak does not look at the catwalks; they navigate by proprioception, by the memory of heat gradients against their left shoulder or right shin. They know where the extrusion manifold radiates its invisible warning line. They know which cable trays are warm with current and which are dead and safe to brace against during a brownout. The amber half-light has rewritten their retinas. They see poorly in full spectrum but can read a shadow across a perforated panel with the clarity of a print. Their peripheral vision is predator-sharp, attuned not to threat but to the constant skittering, the movement of the chitinous residents in the pipes. They have learned to unconsciously filter the vermin from their awareness, seeing them as part of the architecture, a moving mold that clicks.

They wear the same clothes until the fabric crystallizes. The synthetic lubricants in the air—thick, dark, honey-slow—settle on everything. A jumpsuit starts as blue or orange but within a week turns a mottled black-brown, the fabric stiffening where the grease bonds with the fibers. It does not wash; there is no water for that. It merely accumulates until the material holds a shape on its own, standing in the corner of a bunk niche like a hollow second body. The crew members are sculpted by this accumulation. Their elbows and knees shine with the polish of absorbed oil; their hair lies flat in greaser-streaks, never wet, never clean, merely anointed by the machine. They shed skin and hair into the air recyclers, contributing their own particulate to the cycle. They are, in a sense, being slowly digested by the station, their organic matter feeding the pale thread-fungi that fruit above their bunks, dropping spores onto their sleeping faces.

The turbulence lives in their nerves. When the air goes thin—the recyclers surging and lagging, the thirty seconds of vacuum-tension before the backups kick in—they do not freeze from fear. They freeze from calculation. In those seconds, the body decides: do I reach for the emergency mask, or do I brace for the pressure drop? There is no panic, only the rapid arithmetic of survival. Their startle reflex has been dulled to a twitch; their situational awareness runs permanently at a hum. When a borer hits a hard formation and the shriek of metal tears through the pocket, they do not jump. Their hands are already reaching for the throttle, their boots already planted wide. They are adrenalized not by danger but by the constant requirement to interface with a machine that is always, in some small way, breaking. They are mechanics not by trade but by existential necessity. If they stop patching, the mountain crushes in. If they stop clearing, the jams cascade. They are the immune system of the pocket, rushing to the site of every failure, and they are always failing.

They eat paste. The food stores are sealed canisters of nutrient-dense sludge formulated for non-aqueous environments—thick, gray-brown, tasting of copper and sesame and nothing at all. They squeeze it from tubes directly into the back of the throat to avoid tasting it on the dry tongue. There is a mess table bolted to the compressor, but no one sits there to dine. They eat while walking, while monitoring, while holding a wrench steady on a tripped breaker. The table is where they sit when the body demands rest that the bunk will not give—elbows on the vibrating steel, head in hands, the drone of the compressor massaging the skull from the inside. Sometimes they talk there, in low clicks, about the rotation schedules or the decay rate of the western borer bits. They do not talk about home. Home is a wet place, a place where water falls from the sky and things grow upward from soil, and the memory of it causes a specific pain—a thirst that is not physical but conceptual, a longing for a world that operates on chemistry their bodies no longer remember.

They have relationships, but they are strange—compressed like everything else. Affection is expressed in the sharing of a clean filter, the silent taking of a harder shift so another can sleep. Sex happens, quick and perfunctory in the warm zones behind the compressor housings, where the vermin click disapprovingly from the cable trays. It is a grasping at warmth, at moisture, at proof of a body still capable of something besides maintenance. There is no romance in it, only friction and the brief, illusionary softness of another person’s sweat. They do not pair for long. The station rotates them out—three months, six months, a year if they are hard or desperate—and the partings are without ceremony. A hand squeeze. A nod. The departing one leaves behind a stiffened jumpsuit in the bunk niche, which the incoming one will wear and add their own layer of grease to, inheriting the shape of a stranger’s shoulders.

And some of them do not leave. The mountain claims a few each cycle—not in dramatic collapses or explosive decompressions, but in small surrenders. A hand caught between a conveyor belt and a roller, crushed before the emergency stop can be thrown. A lung that finally refuses the thin air, filling with fluid in a place where fluid is a betrayal. A moment of inattention at the borer maintenance hatch, where the pressure differential pulls a body into the rock before the seal closes. These deaths are quiet. They are absorbed. The body is retrieved, stripped of its stiffened suit, and processed. There is no burial; there is only the recyclers. The organic matter is broken down and fed to the systems, and the moisture—the precious, hoarded water of a human body—is reclaimed for the condensation traps, where the microscopic colonies bloom and die on what was once breath and blood. The dead become fuel and humidity, a brief flowering of scale on the trap walls before the maintenance drones scrape it away.

The vermin watch all of this. They are the true residents, the ones who remember the pocket before the steel and the quartz. They watch from the cable trays, their chitinous bodies warm with stolen heat, their many-lensed eyes reflecting the amber light in tiny, dispassionate stars. They do not fear the crew. They move around them like water around a stone, scuttling past boots, nesting in the warmth of a freshly vacated bunk. When the station finally fails—as all things fail here, where the mountain presses always inward and the machines grind against entropy—the vermin will remain. They will eat the synthetic lubricants that pool in the gutters. They will nest in the quiet transformers. They will pick the bones of the factory clean of wire and rubber and organic residue, and they will click to each other in the dark, telling stories of the soft things that came and built and broke and were broken, until even the memory of the soft things is ground down to dust and the pocket is theirs again.

This is who lives in the mountain: the re-made, the desiccated, the vibrating and the necessary. They are not inhabitants but components—flesh-based failsafes in a system that operates at the edge of collapse. They endure the dry dark, the dim amber, the fungal spores in their lungs and the tremor in their bones. They wake and work and sleep and work, and the mountain grinds on, and the borers turn, and the vermin click, and the air goes thin and thick again, and they persist because the machine needs them to persist, and they have forgotten how to do anything else.
