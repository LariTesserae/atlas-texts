To live on the High Plateau of Kael is to be a paradox of metabolism—a spark of frantic, corrosive hunger set against a backdrop of glacial, mechanical patience. The inhabitants do not breathe; they transpire. They do not drink; they smelt. They are the ferrovores, the blight-lichen, the drifting acid-chitin, and they are as much a part of the plateau's architecture as the basalt and forged ceramic they consume. They are not visitors to this manufactured world; they are its final, inevitable rust.

Consider first the colonies that mantle the exhaust stacks, draping the brutalist spires in glistening, lead-colored moss. To look at them is to see a tapestry of static, fungal stillness, but step close—closer than the thin, silicate-laden air would ever allow a terrestrial lung—and you would witness a geometry of desperation. Each thread is a filament of scavenged metal, drawn out from the iron-rich dust and woven into a conductive lattice. They are photosynthetic, but not by the logic of chlorophyll and sunlight. They are thermosynthetic. They drink the persistent, dry warmth that bleeds from the ancient processor casements, converting the infrared sigh of the plateau into the chemical energy required to crack a siloxane molecule and bind a copper atom. Their color is the color of heavy, toxic industry—sulfurous yellows, deep vanadium greens, and the iridescent shimmer of oxidized chlorides—because they are built entirely from the waste of the world they cling to. They do not grow upward; they grow inward, sinking taproots of crystalline acid into the micro-fractures of the ceramic, slowly, silently prying the architecture apart over centuries.

Then there are the blisters. Down in the perpetual, heated shadow of the undersides of processors, where the subsonic thrum of the deep-earth compressors vibrates the very stone, hang the tumorous things. They are vast, some the size of hauler vehicles, and they appear to be single, massive organisms, but this is an illusion of scale. They are colonies of billions, a biological foam of heat-synthesizing pods that have surrendered their individual boundaries to form a single, pressurized sac. Their skin is a tough, rubbery hide of polymerized siloxanes—elastic, non-permeable, and utterly devoid of water. Inside, they are churning cauldrons. Heated gases—the waste exhaust of the plateau—are trapped within, bubbling through a non-aqueous soup of liquid metal salts and crushed, micronized glass. The sacs pulse, but slowly. A single contraction might take an hour, rolling a wave of trapped heat from one pole of the blister to the other, driving the chemical reduction of the iron and sulfur they have absorbed. When the internal pressure of reconstituted gas grows too great, they do not burst; they weep. Tiny, sphincter-like pores dilate on their undersides, weeping a thick, viscous fluid that drops onto the conveyors below, crystallizing instantly into the pale, dusty powder that coats the world. They are the plateau's kidneys, processing the toxic breath of the machines into the sterile dust of the plateau. 

And between these two extremes—the clinging and the hanging—drift the void-sailors. In a world of terrifying distances and infinite geometric depressions, travel is a necessity for any ecology that refuses to starve. The hollow chitin-spheres are the spores and the explorers, born from the uppermost crests of the lichen colonies when a dust storm scours the old growth clean. They are terrifyingly fragile. To touch one would be to shatter it, collapsing the vacuum-sealed lattice of metallic chitin that gives it buoyancy. They are sails of desiccated protein, catching the thermal updrafts that rise from the sun-baked metal shelves. They drift silently across the pale, washed-out sky, navigating by gradients of heat, seeking the distant, warm silhouettes of uncolonized gantries. When they land—cracking upon the cold, eroded stone like dry marrow—their purpose is fulfilled. They do not grow; they dissolve. Their acidic interiors bleed out, etching a microscopic crater into the basalt, leaving behind a deposit of raw, unprocessed metals and the dormant, heat-resistant spores of a new colony. Within a decade, a fresh, lead-colored stain will mark the spot, and the slow, creeping consumption will begin again.

The mind of a ferrovoire is not a mind. It is a thermodynamic gradient. They do not think; they reflux. They are drawn to heat with the same involuntary certainty that a planet is drawn to a star, and they recoil from the cold. Their life cycle is measured not in generations, but in the rate of architectural decay. A furnace that takes a decade to power down is a dying sun to them; the colonies upon its cooling fins will swell in a final, desperate bloom of synthesized heat, before casting their hollow sailors to seek a new star on the horizon. They are tireless because they are mindless, operating on the pure, unadulterated biological logic of consumption and propagation. They do not sleep. They do not stop. 

They are a creeping, swelling ecology, but to call them a blight is to misunderstand the strange loop of Kael. The machines mine the dust to build the machines that produce the dust. The ferrovores eat the machines to produce the dust that the machines mine. They are not a disease; they are the plateau's immune system, its digestive tract, its circulatory cycle. They are the mechanism by which the manufactured world becomes a geological inevitability. They recycle the iron and the sulfur, the heat and the exhaust, transforming the sharp, calculated angles of the builders into the rounded, eroded, settled forms of deep time. 

To be a ferrovoire is to be the teeth of a clock that measures time in the erosion of stone. It is to live in absolute desiccation, to find hydration an absurd concept, to run one's blood with liquid siloxanes and heated gases. It is to thrive in the long, slow tick of a world that does not care, scuttling and drifting and clinging in the quiet, industrial desert, turning the machine world back into the dust from which it was built.
