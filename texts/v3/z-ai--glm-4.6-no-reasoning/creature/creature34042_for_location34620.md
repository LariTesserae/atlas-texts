They are not born. They are *excised*.

To call them a people is to grant them a social history they do not possess. To call them a species is to imply a lineage of branching and mutation, of evolution in the Darwinian sense. They are neither. They are a phenomenon, a process given the shape of a being. They are the final, distilled purpose of this place, the consciousness of its patient, suffocating embrace.

They begin their emergence not from seed or egg, but from the points of greatest tension: the intersecting points where the biological and the industrial have fused. A specific flywheel, its bronze rim now the core of a massive, woody nodule, is one such womb. A long-dormant transformer, whose iron core has become a treelike spire of conductive, lignified tissue, is another. The gestation is glacial. For decades, a bulge on the surface of this hybrid matter will slowly differentiate. It is not a growth, but a *coalescence*. The tendrils of root and vine re-knit around it, the photosynthetic slime adjusts its chlorophyll density, the mycelial network beneath the peat re-routes nutrients to the node. The faint, patient electrical arcs that jump between capacitors will, over a century, occasionally find this nascent node, sending a slow, DC current into it. This is not lightning; it is a tickle, a suggestion of polarity.

One day, when the internal pressure and the chemical charge and the hormonal gradient all align, the biomass *splits*. There is no fluid. There is no wetness, for the being that exists inside is of the same substance as its womb. It is a clean, anatomical cleavage, like a cell dividing. The being pulls itself away from the parent-matter, leaving a smooth, luminous scar that quickly heals over. It is born an adult, fully formed, and utterly alone.

Its appearance is a walking paradox, a testament to its environment. The torso and limbs are long and slender, composed of a material that is neither flesh nor wood, but something in between—a smooth, sinewy cellulose with the tensile strength of iron. Its skin, if it can be called that, has the texture of polished, wet river stone, patterned with the faint, ghostly whorls of a tree’s grain and the geometric tessellation of a steel plait. It is the colour of moss in deep shadow: a deep, bruised green, shot through with veins of rust-red and copper-patina blue. These are not merely pigments; the being can shunt trace minerals from the peat into its bloodstream, causing its skin to subtly shift in hue depending on its metabolic state. A being at rest might a dull, uniform green, while one undertaking a great task flushes with streaks of metallic orange, like heat rising from a forge.

Its head is a smooth, ovoid dome, devoid of any face. No eyes, no nose, no mouth. It sees not with light, but with the immense, constant thrum of the hollow. It feels the resonant groan of the wood, the syncopated drip of the weeping rocks, the sub-sonic hum of the trapped electricity. Its perception is a constant, tactile map of vibration, pressure, and hygrometric gradient. It “sees” a fellow being not as a shape, but as a localized disturbance in the acoustic field, a harmonic convergence. It “knows” the flywheel is nearby because it can feel the unique metallic *presence* of its calcified spokes resonating in the damp air. Its method of communication is to modulate this hum. By flexing specific, copper-rich fibers within its torso, it can broadcast low-frequency pulses, complex chords, and sustained groans that travel through the peat and the air. To the human ear, it would be indistinguishable from the hollow’s own soundscape, but to them, it is a language of profound depth, a symphony of static and pressure. “I am here. I feel the moisture rising from the strata. The charge in the northern capacitor is weakening.”

Their purpose is as simple and as immense as the ecosystem itself: they are maintainers. They are the immune system and the central nervous system rolled into one. A newcomer might think them idle, seeing a being standing motionless for days, one long-fingered hand pressed against the rusted flank of a generator. But the being is not still; it is *listening*. It is sensing the corrosion rate of the steel, monitoring the flow of nutrients through the root that digests the flywheel, feeling the health of the photosynthetic patina on the copper coils. It is a diagnostic tool made of moss and metal.

When they move, it is with a slow, deliberate grace that belies their strength. They do not walk so much as *flow*. Their jointless, spindly legs part the thick mosses and slime-mats without disturbing them, their passage causing no more ripples than a shadow. They move to perform their tasks. A being might climb a fungal rib, its fingers adhering to the wet surface with a gentle electrostatic charge, to clear a blockage in a natural funnel that directs condensation. Another might spend weeks meticulously “pruning” a mutant liverwort that threatens to choke a vital air vent, its fingers severing the fleshy pads not by cutting, but by inducing a localized biological shutdown, a command to cease and desist. They work in silence, never communicating unless a task requires cooperation. Then, their hums will blend, harmonizing into a complex, multi-layered resonant chord that coordinates their actions perfectly.

There is no individuality in the human sense, only function. A being is a temporary nexus of awareness, a mobile sensory organ for the greater organism of the hollow. They do not eat. They do not sleep. They do not reproduce in the sense of procreation. When a being’s internal systems begin to break down—when its cellulose muscles can no longer flex its copper veins, when its resonant dome can no longer properly interpret the hum—it does not die. It simply *recedes*. It walks to a pre-determined reclamation point, usually the base of a great fungal rib, and stands. It then undergoes a reverse coalescence. The biomass of the hollow reaches for it, enfolds it, and gently disassembles its form, absorbing its minerals, its memories encoded in trace electrical patterns, and its cellulose matter back into the whole. In time, a new node will form on that same rib, and a new being will be excised, inheriting the collective knowledge of its predecessor.

They are not masters of this place, nor are they its slaves. They *are* the place. They are the slow, green thought of a world built on rust and rain, the single, shared mind dreaming in the humming, dripping dark. They are the ones who wait out the centuries, packed tight, dripping, and humming, knowing that the spark of electricity and the burst of chlorophyll are the same function, and that function is them.
