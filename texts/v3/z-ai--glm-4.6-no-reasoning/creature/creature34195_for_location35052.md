They are the Echo. To name them something else—to call them a people, a tribe, or a species—is to impose a soft, wet concept on a place that has none. They are the living soul of the Drum, a consciousness so integrated with its environment that it has nearly forgotten the distinction between self and stone, between life and vibration.

They are not built like what you might expect. Their bodies are born of the same chitinous alchemy as the flora and the shelled fauna they tend. They are lanky and jointed, resembling an unholy synthesis of a praying mantis and a stringed instrument. Their torso is a hardened, hollow carapace of polished, violet chitin, ribbed like a cello or the stalk of a chitin-plant. This carapace is their resonating chamber. Inside is not viscera or heart, but a dense lattice of mineral-infused cartilage and crystalline fibers that hums constantly, picking up and amplifying the ambient percussion of the Drum.

Their limbs are long, multi-jointed, and seemingly frail, but they are cords of intense, fibrous muscle. They move with a startling economy of motion, not with the frantic clatter of the lesser creatures, but with a slow, deliberate, and profoundly graceful stride. When they walk, their segmented feet make a sound like a thumb being drawn across a comb, a gentle, tonal *scratch-scratch-scratch* on the basalt floor. Their four arms are their most defining feature. The lower pair are sturdy, tipped not with hands but with wide, spatulate pincers. These are their tools, their shovels, their sculptors. With these, they stack the shed hammers, they build their cairns and their domed huts, they tend the sprawling gardens of chitin-plants.

But it is the upper pair of arms that gives them their name and their power. These limbs are longer, thinner, ending in delicate, multi-fingered hands that are less for grasping and more for feeling. The fingers are not clad in chitin, but in a sensitive, membranous skin that can read the vibrations in the air, in the stone, in another creature’s carapace. They feel the world. They listen with their bones.

And then there is the head. It is a smooth, ovoid dome of the same polished violet chitin, with no discernible face. There is no mouth. No eyes. No nose. They do not eat as you understand it. They do not see light. In the center of their "forehead" is a complex, interlocking series of plates, like a beetle’s wing-covers, that can open to reveal the source of their being: a pulsing, crystalline core. This is a geode, a living stone, that interfaces directly with the heat rising from the plateau's gut. It is their heart, their stomach, and their sensory organ all in one. It thrums, and in its thrumming, the Echo feels the texture of the world.

Their society is the Drum’s architecture. The scattered cairns and shelters are not villages but musical instruments, intentionally placed to alter and shape the resonant frequencies of the amphitheater. An Echo will spend a lifetime perfecting a single cairn, shifting a stone by a hair’s breadth to achieve a particular overtone, to catch a certain harmonic from the walls. Their communication is not spoken but *drummed*. By tapping their resonating carapace with their delicate finger-hands, or by using their pincer-tools on the stone, they converse in a language of pure vibration—tonal, rhythmic, complex enough to convey history, geometry, and emotion. Layers of meaning are embedded in the attack, the decay, and the harmonic overtones. A pointed punctuation is the sharp *tack* of a chitin-hammer being struck. A question is a slow, tremulous roll that asks for a resonant reply.

Their relationship with the herd of shelled, hammer-beasts is one of sacred symbiosis. The Echo do not herd them; they *harmonize* them. The smaller creatures are the percussion section, the eternal, stochastic heartbeat of the Drum. The larger ones, with their back-hammers, are the principals. The Echo will approach one of these hammer-beasts, not with force, but with a perfectly matched thrumming from their own core, a call-and-response that placates the creature. They will then gently guide its hammer with their pincer-hands, striking a specific resonance on a specific stone, composing the grand, slow symphony that underpins the chaos of the field. They are conductors of a biological orchestra.

Their nourishment is the heat itself. Their internal geode crackles with geothermal energy, drawing the warmth directly through the soles of their feet from the stone floor, up through their mineral-laced cartilage.
