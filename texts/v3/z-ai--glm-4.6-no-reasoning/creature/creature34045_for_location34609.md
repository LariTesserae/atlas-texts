To live in the Quilt-Mire is to be born into a throat. The locals—if one can use such a sterile word for them—are not inhabitants of an environment; they are expressions of it. They do not live *in* the Tangle; they are the Tangle, differentiated only by the temporary architectures of their bodies.

First, there are the Deep-Rooted, the closest thing this place has to a people, though they possess no language of words. They are humanoids only by the most generous definition, standing seven feet tall but seeming much larger due to the cascading manes of pale, photosynthetic hyphae that drape from their scalps and shoulders, blending seamlessly into the root-matrix around them. Their skin is not skin, but a tight-corded bark of federated rootlets, slick with the same weeping condensation that coats the archways. They do not eat; they absorb. Their torsos are hollow, lattice-work chambers where symbiotic colonies of bioluminescent algae churn, casting that cold, electric blue glow through the interstices of their ribcages. They navigate not by sight, but by the pressure of the hum, feeling the peristaltic pulse of the Singer-Puffs through the pads of their broad, fused feet. 

The Deep-Rooted are the philosophers of the Mire, and their philosophy is simple: *To be still is to be compost.* They do not build homes; homes are delusions of permanence. Instead, they practice "the Shed." Every few days, a Deep-Rooted will find a dense thicket of Shelf-Leafs, anchor their hyphae into the mass, and step out of their own outer bark. They leave behind a hollow, bark-shell that is immediately set upon by the scurriers and worm-things, becoming food. Beneath, a new, slightly altered bark has already grown, adapted to the immediate micro-conditions. They shed their skins like thoughts, never remaining the same person twice. When they die, they do not fall; they simply cease their shedding, and the Tangle claims them over the course of an hour, roots federating through their lattice-ribcages as naturally as water filling a cup.

Then there are the Scurriers, the nervous system of the Mire. They are small, warm, and frantic—furless mammals the size of rats, but with elongated, membrane-stretched limbs that allow them to glide from root to root. Their eyes are overdeveloped, wet, and black, consuming every flicker of the glow-worms' light. They are the opportunists, the scavengers, the ones who rush into the percussive shockwave of a bursting Singer-Puff, stuffing their cheek pouches with spore-matter before the Weavers can seal the breach. They sleep in rolling shifts, never all at once, curled inside the momentarily hollow stalks of Gilled Grottoes. Their lives are a mathematics of seconds; a heartbeat too slow, a glance too long, and the moisture molds them in their sleep.

And there are the Drifters, the hermits of the deep pools. They are amphibious, pale things, resembling something between a manatee and a limbless slug, five meters long and entirely blind. They dwell in the unseen depths of the subterranean pools, swimming through the tannin-dark water. They breathe the dense air through a single, flowering siphon that breaches the surface, a fleshy pink trumpet that opens into a grotesque, beautiful blossom to filter the spore-clouds. When the light of a rare sun-spear pierces the mist and turns the water to an incandescent pillar, the Drifters rise to bask in the heat, their massive, translucent forms casting shadows like submarines. They sing the lowest note of the Mire—a sub-harmonic *hoom* that resonates through the root-matrix, vibrating the very teeth of the Weavers working overhead. They are the memory of the water, unchanged for millennia, surviving because the pools are the only place where the Tangle's appetite is slightly slower.

But the true locals are not individuals; they are the moments. There is the populace of the Rot-Flare, the sudden, desperate citizenry that explodes into existence when a wall of Shelf-Leafs blackens and melts into slurry. In the handful of minutes before the Weavers' silk seals the wound, a micro-civilization emerges. Winged gliders, no larger than a thumb, descend to lay eggs in the cooling broth. Fungal sprigs, existing moments ago only as spores, rocket up an inch to fruit and disperse before the light changes. Pale, eyeless worm-things wriggle in by the thousands to drink the nutrient soup. It is a frantic, neonatal city of millions, born in sudden wealth, dying in sudden starvation, its entire history—birth, golden age, collapse, and assimilation—occurring in the time it takes for a Singer-Puff to exhale.

To be a local of the Quilt-Mire is to hold no grudge against being eaten. It is to understand that the boundary between "me" and "here" is a polite fiction, a temporary scab of silk and bark held together by motion. They do not fear death; they fear stillness. They fear the quiet. For a human to survive here, they would have to become like them: breathing water, eating mold, shedding their skin at the door, and moving, always moving, to the terrible, beautiful rhythm of the world's endless, peristaltic chew.
