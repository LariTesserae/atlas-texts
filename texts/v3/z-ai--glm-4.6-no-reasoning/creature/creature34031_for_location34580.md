To live in the Weave is to be a creature of the margin—the razor-thin, shifting boundary where the architecture’s cold, infinite logic meets the hot, screaming chaos of the biosphere. The inhabitants do not rule this place; they are squeezed into its interstitial spaces, surviving in the perpetual spray and spark of its functioning. They are not thinkers or builders. They are thermodynamic inevitabilities, shaped by the weeping glass and the electric mist.

At the foundation of the living hierarchy are the *Slicks*. They are not individuals but a continuous, seething mat of chitin and mucous that coats the lowest troughs of the Weave, submerged in the sluggish, oily puddles. Imagine a cross between a deep-sea isopod and a language of flatworms—a pale, segmented carpet of overlapping shells, perpetually sliding over itself. They are blind, possessing only clusters of thermoreceptive pits that track the fading warmth of the electro-static motes as they dissolve into the water. When a mote touches down, the nearest segment of the Slick rears up, jaws gaping in a silent, wet click, consuming the light before it can reach the deeper water. They are the carpet of the city, grinding their mandibles endlessly, digesting the waste heat of the world. To step over them is to hear a sound like thousands of wet leaves being crushed underfoot.

Above them, scaling the vertical planes of the slick, black walls, are the *Glass-runners*. These are the chrome-plated centipedes, but to call them simply arthropods is to underestimate their geometry. They are built for the turbulence and the three-dimensional labyrinth. Their bodies are long, articulating chains of mirrored segments, each leg ending in a microscopic geodesic claw—gripping not by friction, but by fitting perfectly into the fractal micro-etching of the Weave’s surface. They do not crawl; they compute. Their path up a wall is a flawless algebraic curve, shifting and looping to intercept the drifting currents of aerosol plankton. They move with a terrifying, mechanical precision, yet their numbers create a stochastic madness. When a draft reverses in the corridor, they do not fall; they simply invert their trajectory, running upside down along the ceiling, a river of clicking, splintering sound. Their shells are always filmed in the ambient condensation, leaving a temporary, glittering smear on the perfect glass as they pass—a trace that vanishes in seconds as the Weave heals its own surface.

In the higher caverns, where the ceiling arches away into the blue-white gloom and the corridors widen into auditoria, live the *Ray-slicers*. They are the apex of the airborne planktivores, pearlescent manta rays the size of doors, slicing through the saturated air. They have no eyes, only a vast, lattice-like nose that filters the ozone-heavy breath of the city. Their skeletons are visible, glowing faintly blue as the consumed motes digest in their hollow bellies. They ride the chaotic thermal drafts—those sudden, roaring reversals of wind—with a terrifyingly passive grace, wings flexing only at the last microsecond to avoid a collision with a healing wall. When a dense cloud of electro-static plankton gathers—sparked by some deep seismic realignment in the Weave—the Ray-slicers descend. It is not a hunt; it is a subsumation. They open their maws, and the cloud implodes into them in a flash of crackling light, leaving the air momentarily dark and silent until the hum reasserts itself. 

And then there are the *Thunder-hides*. Rare, colossal, and speaking in a frequency felt in the teeth rather than heard. They are the deep-dwellers, the macro-predators of the great, echoing chasms. Shelled in a bismuth-like, iridescent armor that sheds the heat and the water, they are too massive for the corridors, confined to the vast, spherical pockets where the fractal stresses of the Weave demanded empty space. They feed on the Ray-slicers, and on each other. Their movement is a tectonic event. When a Thunder-hide shifts its weight, the vibration travels through the obsidian mineral for a hundred meters, triggering a cascade of panic—a wave of clicking Glass-runners pouring down the walls like liquid metal, and a burst of desperate, flapping membrane from the Ray-slicers above. The Thunder-hide does not chase; it simply opens a segmented, lamp-like maw, and waits for the turbulence to funnel its prey inward. It is a creature of absolute patience, matching the slow, geological patience of the walls around it.

To be the fauna of the Weave is to be deaf and wet and incandescent. None of these creatures possess a brain capable of comprehending the city they inhabit. They do not know that the walls heal themselves; they only know that a surface that was rough a moment ago is now smooth, and they must adjust their grip. They do not know that the light comes from the structure; they only know that the light is food, and the dark is the absence of food. They live in a state of perpetual, screaming equilibrium. There is no sleep here. The humidity prevents it, the noise forbids it, and the constant, flickering twilight obliterates the very concept of rest. 

They are not haunted by the ghosts of the builders, for there were no builders in any sense the fauna could understand. They are the white blood cells of a dead god’s still-beating heart, swimming through its luminous veins. Their lives are a frantic, deafening ballet of consumption and evasion, playing out in the hot, weeping ruins of a technology that has long since forgotten them, even as it feeds them, shelters them, and, in its slow and mechanical way, reshapes the world around them without a moment's pause.
