The people of Khad Varel do not inhabit a place; they are incorporated into a process. To live here is to be a soft, transient thing embedded in a slow, hard event, and this fact—more than the heat, the height, or the thirst—is what makes them who they are.

They are, physically, a people drawn taut. The air steals moisture from them with a constancy that has shaped their very forms. They are lean, not from want of food, but because the body sheds what it cannot afford to carry. Their skin is desiccated, taking on a texture like the processed gypsum of their interior walls—pale, dry, faintly dusty. Lips are often split; the residents do not notice, just as they do not notice the constant, low-grade thirst that has become so much a part of their physiology that it feels like a baseline state of consciousness rather than a deprivation. They lubricate themselves with the mineral-heavy water drawn from the deep seeps, and over a lifetime, this water leaves its trace. The oldest residents have a faint iron-ochre discoloration at the nails and the roots of their teeth, a patina of the plateau. They are being slowly galvanized.

Their eyes have changed, too. In a city without vegetation, the gaze has nowhere soft to rest, and so the residents have learned to make softness, or to find it. They suffer from what visitors call "stone eye"—the ocular fatigue of looking only at hard, unyielding surfaces—but they have developed a counter-strategy. They look at fabric. They look at smoke. They look at the clouds that pass below. And they look at each other. In Khad Varel, the human body is one of the few organic forms in the visual field, and so the residents study one another with an intensity that visitors often mistake for hostility or paranoia. It is neither. It is a sensory necessity. The drape of a sleeve, the fall of hair, the specific way a colleague's neck tendons shift as they turn their head—these are the visual equivalents of an oasis. The residents are the only soft things in the city, and they rely on each other for optical relief.

Their clothing is functional but deliberate. There is no plant fiber to be had, no cotton or linen, so they dress in the processed byproducts of their own industries: mineral-wool spun from slag, a material that is warm, fireproof, and mercilessly irritating to the skin; thin films of processed gypsum-cloth for interior wear, stiff and rustling; and, for those who work the upper wards or the hoist-systems, remarkable vests and belts woven from the fiber of the height-spiders. This last material—dark, slightly iridescent, stronger than it has any right to be—is a mark of association with the high places. To wear spider-silk is to claim the upper city, to declare that you or your children go where the clouds pass through. The fabric catches the minimal light and gives it back in a way that the stone never does, and so a gathering of Khad Varel residents is a study in textures and faint, trapped glimmers against the overwhelming mineral monotone of the city.

They move with a choreography dictated by heat and geology. The midday hours belong to the warmth; the streets empty, and the city is given over to the slow distortion of rising air and the muffled, wool-wrapped acoustics of thermal saturation. The residents retreat into the close-layered dwellings, into the thin-walled residential wards where proximity to neighbors is insulation. They sleep, or they work the overnight-shift industries, or they simply sit in the dim, waiting. Their daily cycle is not diurnal in the conventional sense; it is tidal, shaped by the crests and troughs of temperature rather than the presence and absence of sun.

When they do move through the city, they navigate the verticality with a thoughtless grace that visitors find terrifying. The gaps between buildings that have settled at different rates, the ladders in the narrow chasms, the stairways cut into living rock—they traverse these as a flatland citizen traverses a sidewalk. Children especially inhabit the in-between spaces. They are the ones who harvest the height-spiders' webs, climbing into gaps that make adults vertiginous, moving with a fluidity that is partly their natural fearlessness and partly an adaptation to a city where "street level" is a meaningless phrase. A child of Khad Varel does not think of the ground as something they walk on; they think of it as something they are currently on, with other grounds above and below.

Their speech is quiet. The acoustic deadness of the city—the way sound is produced and then consumed by the rock, with no reverberation, no echo, no persistence—has made loudness feel like a violation. To shout in Khad Varel is to transgress against the patience of the stone. They speak in low, even tones, and they hear with an acuteness that visitors lack. In the textured quiet of the evening, they can understand a whisper from across a room; they can interpret the specific creak of a floorboard as a message about who is approaching and from which direction. They live in each other's sounds. The walls are thin, the acoustic absorption is absolute, and there is no ambient noise to mask the breath of the person on the other side of the wall. Privacy, in the residential wards, is a concept that does not include sound. They have adapted by making silence ordinary and by developing a culture of acoustic discretion. You do not speak of things you do not wish the next room to hear; you simply do not speak of them at all.

Their relationship with the few non-human residents of the city is one of pragmatic coexistence, tinged with a respect that borders on recognition. The rock lice—pale, flat, chitinous—are treated as part of the infrastructure. A resident who finds a rock louse on their wall will not kill it; they will ignore it, or, if it is in a sensitive area, they will move it to a place where its cleaning work is more useful. There is an unspoken understanding: the lice keep the condensation channels clear, and in return, the residents tolerate their presence in the walls. It is a working relationship, not unlike the relationship the residents have with their own machinery.

The height-spiders are regarded with something closer to affection. They are useful—their fiber is integral to the hoist-system's lighter cables—but their usefulness is not the whole of it. The spiders live in the upper structures, spinning their webs across the gaps between buildings, and in a city with no vegetation, no birds, no soft forms of any kind, the spiders' webs are one of the few things that catch the light beautifully. When condensation beads on a web, it glitters. The residents find this pretty. It is a small word, a soft word, and it is not a word that applies to much else in Khad Varel. The spiders also move in ways that the city does not: quick, deliberate, eight-legged, organic. Watching a height-spider repair its web is one of the few natural spectacles available, and the residents pause to watch it in the same spirit that other cultures might pause to watch a sunset. It is a moment of uncomplicated aesthetic pleasure in a place where such moments are rare.

But the core of the Khad Varel self is not found in these adaptations—of body, of eye, of speech, of movement. The core is found in their relationship with time.

They live in a city that is still becoming. The rock beneath them rises four centimeters a year. The streets tilt, incrementally, over decades. The horizons change. Nothing is finished. And the residents have absorbed this so thoroughly that it shapes their cognition. They do not think in terms of endpoints. Their industry is an industry of intermediates—of parts that will become machines that will become products—and their minds run on the same logic. They understand processes. They are comfortable with incompleteness. A Khad Varel resident does not ask, "Is it done?" They ask, "What is it becoming?" 

This makes them philosophers of a practical, unromantic sort. They have a word—*varel*—which is also the city's name, or half of it, and which means something like "the state of being in the middle of a process that cannot be hurried." It is not a virtue word. It does not imply patience or stoicism or any of the moral qualities that other cultures attach to endurance. It is a descriptive word. It names a condition. The condition of being mid-genesis. The condition of being still-forming. When a resident says of a building, "It is varel," they do not mean it is under construction; they mean it will always be under construction, that its nature is to be incomplete, that completion is not a state it will ever achieve. When they say of themselves, "I am varel," they mean that they, too, are ongoing—that the self is not a finished thing but a process, and that this is not a tragedy but simply the nature of living in a living place.

This philosophical orientation gives them a relationship with change that other cultures find unsettling. They do not resist change, but they do not pursue it either. They accommodate it, at the speed of rock. A new technique is adopted not because it is better but because the old technique has, over thirty years, revealed its limitations in a way that can no longer be ignored. A residential block is expanded by adding another layer on top, carefully, testing each new load against the foundation's tolerance, because the alternative—tearing down and rebuilding—assumes that something can be finished, and in Khad Varel, nothing can be finished. The city evolves by deposition, like sedimentary rock, and the residents evolve with it, each generation slightly different from the last—not transformed but accumulated, the small differences building up over time into something that, from the outside, looks like a distinct culture but which, from the inside, feels like the natural result of being alive for a long time in a place that is also alive.

They dream strangely. The quiet of the city—at evening, when the fabrication halls reduce to their overnight cycle, when the hoist-systems slow, when the only sounds are the tick of rock settling and the drip of condensation in the lower wards—this quiet seeps into their sleep. Their dreams are mineral. They dream of structures that rise without limit, of heat that has no source, of water that arrives and departs without explanation. They dream of the city as it might be in a thousand years, when the plateau has risen another forty meters and the clouds pass through the middle wards instead of the upper ones and the buildings have grown dozens of layers taller and the hoist-system has been re-anchored a hundred more times. They dream of themselves as components in a vast machine—not a metaphorical machine but the literal machine of the city, its architecture and infrastructure integrated into a single system that breathes geothermal heat and excretes steam and processes ore and rises, slowly, on the chest of something enormous. In these dreams, they are not separate from the city. They are part of its metabolism.

And this, perhaps, is the deepest truth of who they are. The residents of Khad Varel do not live *in* the city the way that residents of other cities do—not as inhabitants of a structure, not as occupants of a space. They live *within* the city the way that enzymes live within a cell, or the way that corals live within a reef. They are integrated. Their dwellings are structural elements; their work is a metabolic process; their movements are the city's circulation; their quiet speech is its internal communication. They cannot be removed without the system failing, and the system cannot function without them, and this mutual dependency is not the result of design but of the slow, patient accumulation of co-adaptation that occurs when people and place share a process of becoming.

They are the people of the high, hot, rising city. They are the soft tissue in a mineral body. They are the transients in an event that will outlast them by a million years, and they know this, and they do not find it diminishing. To be part of something ongoing—something that will not be finished, that cannot be finished, that is still and always becoming—is, for them, not a limitation but a condition of existence, as fundamental as the heat that rises through their feet or the thirst that sits at the back of their throats or the quiet that presses in from all sides and teaches them, nightly, that they are small and slow and alive inside something vast and patient and also alive.

They are varel. They are mid-genesis. They are still becoming, and they are at peace with it, because the alternative—being finished—would mean being static, and in a city that rises four centimeters a year on the chest of a breathing world, static is the one thing that nothing, and no one, can ever be.
