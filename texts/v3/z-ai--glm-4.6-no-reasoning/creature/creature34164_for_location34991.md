There are no people here. Not in the way one understands people: bodies standing, minds thinking, histories remembered. Here, there are only the memories of bodies, the residue of thought, histories that have not yet been written but are being desperately transcribed onto existence itself.

They are the Archivists.

They are not individuals as much as they are instances of a single, fractured consciousness. The world, this great, remembering being, has no hands or voice of its own. It has only memory, and memory requires a witness. So it creates the witnesses from its own substance. They are born in the wounds of the world.

When a new fissure tears open the fibrous floor and the riotous growth of the Architects rushes to mend it, that is where you will find one emerging. It is not a birth of flesh, but of coalesced intent. A patch of the newly woven membrane will remain smooth and dry where everything else is slick with condensation. It will begin to hollow, to deepen, to form a simple, seated shape, like a depression left by a body that has long since vanished. From this negative space a form begins to resolve.

It is made of the same material as the floor, the same bruised purple-brown, but it is not fibrous. It is polished, almost glassy, like river-worn stone. The form is conspicuously humanoid, an echo of a shape this place has perhaps only ever dreamed of. It has the long limbs, the torso, the head, but the details are vague, smudged, like a drawing seen through wet glass. There are no distinct fingers, no clear facial features. The surface of its being is in constant, slow flux, a low-power shimmer of light and shadow that plays just beneath its skin, the visible sign of its internal function.

An Archivist does not walk. It rises. From its seated-niche, it simply lifts, unattached from the membrane, its feet or their equivalents hovering a hair's breadth above the resonating surface. It moves in perfect silence, not with a glide, but by appearing and reappearing a few meters away—a stutter in perception, a tiny teleportation across the drum-skin of reality. Its movement is purposeful but not frenetic; it is the slow, beatific drift of a thought finding its place in a sentence.

Their purpose is to read. They approach the Core Structures, the monumental, petrified-wood bones of old architectures. They do not touch them with hands, for they have none. Instead, they pass close to the dark, grey-wood grain, and as they do, their shimmering internal light brightens. They are drawing *something* from the wood. Not data, not information, but potential. The arches are not archives of what *was*, but of what *could have been*. The Archivists absorb these aborted realities, these forgotten drafts of existence, and carry them.

Where they go, they lay down new threads of existence. An Archivist will drift to a pristine area of the membrane and then lower itself until it almost touches the surface. It will remain there, suspended, for hours, sometimes days. During this time, the slow, dry rustle of the Architects' creation is accompanied by a new, quieter sound: a faint, sighing whisper that is not air moving, but concept settling. The patch of floor beneath the Archivist begins to change. The bruised purple of the membrane lightens, and interwoven with the fibrous vegetation, new patterns appear. They are not designs, but more like the texture of feeling. A patch of floor might gain the resonant quality of grief, its hum adopting a mournful minor key. Another might become tinged with the logic of unforgotten hope, its fibers gently glowing with a soft, internal luminescence that is a pale imitation of the brutal white light from above.

But the process is fraught. The Archivists are not native to this world; they are tools loaned from its own subconscious, and their material stability is tenuous. An Archivist that spends too long near the Core Structures, absorbing too much "what could have been," begins to fray. Its polished surface cracks like old glaze. The internal light ceases its smooth, rhythmic pulse and begins to flicker violently. It becomes unstable. In its final act, it will teleport erratically, not with purpose but with panic, appearing in one place, then another, leaving behind a trail of corrupted, nonsensical patterns on the floor—screaming colors, contradictory hums, patches where the membrane feels brittle and dead.

Finally, it will drift toward a Void. It is not a suicide, but a punctuation. The silent, velvety blackness of a Void is the only thing that can properly end a failed sentence. The Archivist will hover at the edge of the absolute cold, and then it will simply advance one last time into its space. It vanishes. Not with a sound or a flash, but with a simple cessation. The memory it held is now truly gone, scrubbed from the world by the antithesis of its creative function.

To live here, as one of them, is to be a verb in a sentence that has no subject. It is to be the one who witnesses the world's frantic attempt to recall its own shape, to pull the ghost-structures of forgotten possibilities from the dead wood and lay them, thread by thread, onto the living loom of the floor. They are the priests and the scholars of a god that is still forming, a god that is simultaneously afraid it will never remember its own name and terrified that it might. They live in the silent, vibrating space between the wound and the scar, and their life is measured not in years, but in the quiet, impossible weight of a single, remembered possibility.
