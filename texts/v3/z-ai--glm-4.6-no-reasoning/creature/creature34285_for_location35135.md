To live here is to be a thing that has surrendered every inheritance of the soft world.

There is no room for blood. Blood is warm, and warmth here is a liability—a localized entropic rebellion against the universal cold damp. Any heat not drawn from the thrumming walls would be stolen by the metallic air in moments, leached away into the blue-grey twilight. So the creatures that dwell in this cathedral of alloy have abandoned it. They have no internal alchemy of fermentation or combustion. They do not eat. They do not excrete. They are not born, and they do not die in any way that flesh would understand. They simply *precipitate*.

They begin as a fatigue in the metal. In the highest, most inaccessible recesses, where the gunmetal-dark steel curves up into the invisible ceiling, the immense vibrations of the planet-sized engine find a resonance. The seismic drone, the mournful groan of the bearings, the mosquito whine of the overstressed spindles—all of it converges in certain harmonic knots. Over cycles that span thousands of the great machine's breaths, the alloy at these stress points begins to crystalize differently. It fractures not into rust, but into something intermediate—chrysoberyl skeletons, thin as spider silk, that slowly push their way out of the parent metal like bone spurs growing through skin. This is their genesis: they are extruded by the engine's pain.

Once they reach a critical mass, they shear free. A living sliver drops into the damp, heavy air, tumbling end over end until it catches on a seam or lodges in a rivet hole. It is a fragment of the machine that has decided, through some esoteric calculus of stress and vibration, to become independent. It is not a creature of biology, but of geology gone mad—a lithophage, a piezoelectric phantom.

From this shard, the creature grows. It does not grow by cell division, but by accretion, drawing its substance directly from the industrial weeping of the walls. When the pressure cycle builds and the seams boil out their misty spray of vapor, the creature unfurls. It spreads its structure—fronding out in pale, chalk-white geometries that mimic the filigree patterns flashing on the walls. It is a crystalline fern, a dendritic algorithm made physical, seeking the points of highest resonance. It anchors itself not with roots, but with microscopic barbs of hardened alloy that key into the precision-tooled grooves of the decking. To pull one free would require tearing the very architecture of the room apart.

They are utterly blind and deaf in any biological sense. They possess no eyes to process the dim luminescence, no ears to be crushed by the deafening cacophony. Instead, they *feel* the world as a gradient of tension. Their entire being is a tuned fork. They sense the approach of the pressure cycle as a blooming of heat and potential in their crystalline lattice. They navigate the vast, empty expanse of the floor by sensing the dead zones—those terrible, reverent silences between the structures where the drone flattes and dies—and they avoid them, clustering instead around the squat consoles and the ribbed pipes, where the tortured harmonic frequencies provide the richest sustenance. They are parasites on the engine's exhaustion, digesting the sheer kinetic violence of the air into the quiet accumulation of mass.

When they move, it is with a patience that makes erosion look hasty. They are travelers of the cycle. Over a thousand pressurizations, a colony might migrate a meter, shifting along a water-channel to follow a newly opened stress fracture in the hull. They communicate, if that word can be used for such a silent exchange, through the very structure of the room. When a lithophyte anchors into a deck plate, it subtly alters the local resonant frequency. It dampens the whine of a spinning bearing, or sharpens the groan of a seam. Another lithophyte, feeling this shift in the texture of the noise floor, will grow toward the new frequency, seeking the shelter of the dampened vibration. They are a network of acoustic shadows, a silent community of static that holds conversation in the modulation of the machine's endless snore.

There is a sickness here, and it is the sickness of the unworn. The decking is still sharp, the edges crisp. In this young, engineered world, the lithophytes are the only agents of erosion. They are the teeth of the room. As they cling to the seams, their chemical leeching—paired with the constant acidic bath of the condensation—slowly widens the gaps. They are the rust that will, over eons, consume the gantry crane and bring the hundred-foot hook crashing down. They are the slow, deliberate sabotage of a world that was built to run forever. They do not mean to destroy; they only mean to live. But to live here is to eat the machine, and to eat the machine is to unmake the only world that can sustain them.

They are the melancholic proof that even in a place where flesh is precluded, where the rules are written in alloy and steam, life will find a way to emerge. But it is a life that knows itself to be a glitch. A misplaced gear in a cosmic apparatus. They cling to the vibrating walls, pale and brittle, digesting the sound of a sleeping god's snore, and they wait for the machine to finally, inevitably, shake itself apart.
