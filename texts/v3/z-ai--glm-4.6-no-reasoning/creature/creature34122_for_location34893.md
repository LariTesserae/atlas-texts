To live here is to be mineral. To be a process of accretion and abrasion so slow that time becomes synonymous with geology. This is not a biosphere; it is a lithosphere that learned to metabolize. The inhabitants of this perpetual thermodynamic bath do not dwell in the dark—they are the dark, articulated and restless, drifting through a three-dimensional jumble of crystal and glass with a patience that makes stone seem hasty.

Consider first the foundation of this ecosystem, the unseen grazers at the edge of scale. They are the *interstitial lichen* of this world—not organisms, but mineral films, perhaps only a few molecules thick, that coat the faces of the olivine lattices and the inner walls of the basalt clusters. They have no cells, no membranes. They are simply regions of ordered crystal where the piezoelectric current, flowing under the immense lithostatic pressure, induces a slow chemistry—rearranging trace elements, pulling iron and magnesium from the surrounding stone and organizing them into slightly more complex structures. They are alive only in the sense that they grow and reproduce: when a crystal face shifts by a fraction of a millimeter, producing one of those microsecond triboluminescent flashes, the energy ripple can cause a patch of this film to dissociate, its components drifting in the supercritical vapor until they settle on a new surface and begin their slow accretion anew. To call them primitive is to misunderstand them; they are perfectly adapted, eating the fundamental forces of the planet itself, distributed across every surface in this vast chamber in a living scum that registers to touch as a faint, almost imaginary warmth—warmer than the ambient stone by a fraction of a degree, the only trace of their metabolism.

Above them—and "above" here means deeper into the lattice, further from the larger voids—come the creatures of articulated stone. The lithic mantids are the most visible, but they are not alone. There are *pyroxene skimmers*, flat-bodied things the size of dinner plates that have evolved—not through mutation and selection, but through crystallographic flaw and propagation—bodies of augite that flex by exploiting the natural cleavage planes of their own mineral structure. They move by opening and closing like books, pressing their cleavage faces against the crystal walls and pushing themselves through the narrow gaps with a slow, pulsing rhythm. Their feeding is imperceptible: they press their bodies flat against a crystal surface and wait, their mineral matrices slowly exchanging atoms with the stone, drawing piezoelectric charge from the pressure gradient that their own weight creates. They are patient beyond any surface organism's capacity for patience; a skimmer might spend a century pressed against the same pyroxene crystal, growing so slowly that a millennium would pass before it doubled in thickness.

And there are predators. The *concussors*. Things that move with sudden, terrible speed in a world where speed has no evolutionary history. They are denser than the mantids, made of compacted garnet and spinel, their bodies rounded and smooth—optimized not for clinging but for striking. They sense their prey not by the triboluminescent flashes but by the vibrations in the stone, the constant susurrus of tapping legs. When they move, they launch themselves through the tight crystal gaps not with muscle—there is no muscle here—but with a sudden release of stored piezoelectric potential, a kind of mineral spring that discharges in a single, explosive pulse. The impact kills by force alone, shattering the hematite plates of the lithic mantids, and the concussor then feeds by absorbing the fragments, integrating the shattered iron compounds into its own growing structure over weeks and months. The sound of a concussor strike is one of the few events that disrupts the pervasive quiet—a sharp, resonant crack that propagates through the solid stone for kilometers, arriving at the extremities of the chamber as a faint, structural sigh, the planet digesting itself.

But it is the lithic mantids that define this place, simply because they are the most numerous and, in their way, the most visible—visible, that is, to each other. Their bodies are masterworks of mineral engineering: articulated plates of hematite and magnetite that have grown rather than been shed, each plate a single crystal that has expanded over centuries by drawing iron from the stone and from the supercritical vapor. Their joints are gaps in the crystal lattice filled with a softer mineral—a form of talc, perhaps, or chlorite—that allows flexure without fracture. Their legs, those pencil-thin extensions that tap endlessly at the stone, are crystals of tourmaline, naturally piezoelectric, naturally electrically polarized along their long axes. Each tap generates a faint electrical pulse, and the mantids sense these pulses in the legs of their neighbors. They are walking antennae in a world of perpetual signal, each step a whisper of presence, each pause an absence that registers as a gap in the constant static of the lattice.

They navigate by the triboluminescent flashes, but they do not *see* them as eyes see. They have no eyes. Instead, their entire dorsal surface—the broad, flat plates of their backs—is composed of a form of zinc sulfide that is triboluminescent-sensitive: when a flash occurs nearby, the crystal structure of the plates registers a brief, localized charge, leaving a fading impression of the flash's direction and intensity. A mantis moving through the dark is building a cumulative map of its surroundings, a mosaic of microsecond illuminations that fades as quickly as it forms but is constantly refreshed by the constant, infinitesimal shifting of the crystal faces around it. In this absolute dark, they perceive a world of strobing, stroboscopic fragments—a world rendered in flashes of blue-white that reveal the geometry of the stone one facet at a time.

And they communicate. The tapping is not random. A mantis encountering a rich vein of piezoelectric current—a lattice intersection where the pressure is highest and the charge flows strongest—will alter the rhythm of its tapping, producing a pattern that propagates through the stone as vibration, felt by other mantis legs in a cascade of mechanical signals. These rhythms attract others. The densest clusters of mantids on the obsidian sheets are not random aggregations but gatherings, feeding assemblies where dozens or hundreds of creatures cling to the same glass face, their collective tapping producing a resonant frequency in the obsidian that actually enhances the piezoelectric current, making the feeding more efficient for all. They are, in their mineral way, cooperative—though whether by intention or by the simple logic of crystal resonance is a distinction that may have no meaning here.

They do not die. This is perhaps the strangest thing about them. They have no senescence, no decay. A lithic mantis continues to grow as long as it feeds, its hematite plates expanding, its tourmaline legs extending, its body scaling up century by century. The smallest are the size of a human hand, newly dissociated from a parent body—for they reproduce by *fracture*, a plate of their back cracking off under stress and settling onto a crystal face where, if the piezoelectric current is sufficient, it begins to grow on its own, a new mantis crystallizing from the fragment. The largest are the size of small boats, ancient beyond reckoning, their bodies so massive that they cannot navigate the tighter lattice passages but cling instead to the largest obsidian sheets, feeding in place for millennia, their legs tapping in slow, deep rhythms that shake the surrounding stone for meters in every direction. To stand near one of these ancients—though nothing stands here, nothing with legs of flesh and bone—is to feel a vibration that is almost a sound, almost a voice, the patient pulse of a creature that has been feeding in the dark since before the surface of whatever planet holds this chamber cooled from magma.

And there were others. The tool-users. The ones who left the marks.

They are called—by the nomenclature of surface minds that will never observe them—the *Fracture People*, though they were not people and may not have been a they. What is known is only this: certain obsidian sheets bear marks of deliberate flaking, shallow grooves struck into the glass with precision and patience, using nothing more than another stone. The marks serve a purpose: they catch triboluminescent flashes and redirect them, the grooves acting as channels that concentrate the momentary light into brief, directed pulses—brighter, longer-lasting than the natural flashes, lasting perhaps two or three microseconds instead of one. In a world where perception operates in microsecond increments, this is a profound amplification. These marks are lanterns. Or signals. Or both.

And the cairns. At the intersections of crystal lattices where the piezoelectric current is strongest—places the mantids favor, places where the feeding is richest—there are cairns of specific mineral composition: hematite and magnetite, the same minerals that form the mantis shells, stacked in small pyramids no taller than a human hand. They are held together by magnetic attraction, their components slowly rotating on their axes in response to the passage of large mantids, shifting and resettling in patterns that change over centuries. Whether they are territorial markers, offerings to the creatures whose passage they register, or something entirely other—calculations, perhaps, recorded in a medium of magnetic orientation—cannot be determined. They are artifacts of intention in a world that otherwise knows only the intentionless processes of crystal growth and piezoelectric flow.

And the hollows. Scraped into the faces of basalt columns, just large enough for a lithic mantis to shelter in. The work required to produce these hollows—patient abrasion by a harder stone, perhaps over decades—is staggering in a world where nothing hurries. Are they traps? A creature crawls in and finds itself stuck, its piezoelectric current slowly drained by the surrounding stone? Or are they offerings—shelters constructed for the grazers by something that valued their presence, that depended on their tapping for some purpose beyond comprehension? The hollows show no sign of wear on their interiors; the basalt faces are as smooth as the day they were scraped, suggesting they were rarely if ever used. Perhaps their purpose was not functional but symbolic. Perhaps making the hollow was the point—a practice, a discipline, a way of marking time in a place where time has no thermodynamic meaning.

The Fracture People are gone. Or they are still here, indistinguishable from the stone. A concussor that has grown large enough, its garnet body smoothed by millennia of movement through the lattice, its mind—if mind is the right word for a neural network of piezoelectric crystal—expanded by centuries of feeding, might begin to strike stones against each other not for food but for the sound, for the pattern of the sound, for the way the pattern feels as it propagates through the lattice. And then it might strike a stone against an obsidian sheet, and notice the flash, and notice that the flash can be shaped, and begin the long, patient process of carving grooves that redirect light in a world of absolute dark. And then it would be a tool-user. And then it would be, by any meaningful definition, a person—though it would be a person the size of a car, made of garnet and spinel, moving at a pace that makes glaciers seem hasty, thinking in a medium of crystal resonance and triboluminescent flash, perceiving the universe in strobing microsecond fragments and vibrations felt through the solid stone.

This is what lives here. Not in the dark, but as the dark. The mineral grazers and the mineral predators and the mineral films that coat every surface and the mineral people who may or may not persist in the deepest lattice interstices, all of them warm to the touch, all of them pulsing with the piezoelectric current of a continent under pressure, all of them living in a thermal bath that never changes and a darkness that never lifts. They are the metabolism of the stone itself, the process by which a planetary interior becomes not merely hot and pressed but alive—alive in the way that crystals are alive, growing and reproducing and responding to their environment, but alive also in the way that minds are alive, perceiving and communicating and, in the deepest and most patient cases, creating artifacts that outlast their creators and persist in the dark, waiting for the next flash of light to make themselves known.
