The inhabitants are not separate from their place; they *are* the place, or at least, a vital organ within it. They are the keepers of this living wound, this pocket of stolen life. To call them builders is both right and wrong. They are more like gardeners at the scale of architecture, whole-body orchestrators of growth and decay. They are the Bog-Woven, the Gourd-Flesh, the Hollow-Holders.

They are tall and spindle-limbed, their form a direct response to the wet, yielding ground. Their bodies are impossibly thin for their height, a design that minimizes their footprint on the spongy peat. Their digits are long and prehensile, not with the gripping power of a primate, but with a strange, adhesive quality. They can caress a root into a new direction, encourage a vine to knots itself, or gently peel back a section of moss without harming it. Their skin is the landscape. It is not a uniform color but a palette of the place: a gradient of rubbery grey-green, the mottled brown of waterlogged bark, and patches of pale, cool lichen that grow on their shoulders and backs. This skin is not merely armor; it is a sensory organ, a living membrane that reads the slow tides of the peat and the frantic vibrations of the insects. The constant crawling is, to them, a form of communication, a texture-scape of information.

Their faces are smooth, almost featureless, molded into a gentle, permanent curve that suggests a mouth but isn't one. They have no eyes. The concept of looking, of focusing light on a single point, is alien to them. Their world is perceived through a symphony of other senses. The soft, resonant thrum in a wall of living wood tells them of a slow stress fracture. The vibration of a hundred tiny legs on a moss-bridge tells of a mate’s arrival. They "hear" the biochemical state of the soil through pores in the soles of their feet, tasting the acidity and the decay. The air itself, thick with spores and the scent of iron and wet rot, is a map of weather and health.

They do not eat in the way a stranger might understand. They consume by absorption. They stand for long, motionless hours in the pulsing gourds, their hands pressed to the inner walls of shelf-fungus, drawing sustenance from the slow, cold digestion happening within. They are semi-symbiotic with their dwellings; the fungus lining their homes is a cultivated crop, its mycelial network intertwined with their own internal biology. The dense insect-cloud is a form of precipitation, a flurry of calories they breathe in and filter through specialized, gill-like folds in their necks. They do not hunt the skittering, multi-legged things; they are simply a part of their weather. To be crawled upon is to be bathed or to be rained upon. An infant is not cuddled but is laid in a shallow basin of peat, covered lightly with a writhing blanket of pale, blind arthropods, whose constant grooming cleans its new skin and who, in turn, nibble at the harmless outer layers of its body, feeding and being fed in a transaction of ultimate integration.

Their society is not one of individuals, but a shared consciousness pressed into the form of the town. There are no leaders, only functions. One of their kind may spend a lifetime locked in place at the sphagnum throat, their body becoming one with the living wall, their will the contractile reflex that opens and closes the entrance. Another becomes the "tuner," moving from dwelling to dwelling, using their long fingers to adjust the tension of the sinewy vines, altering the humidity within a gourd by a fraction of a degree, coaxing a wall to breathe a little deeper. They do not speak words. They communicate through pheromonal signatures exuded from their skin, through a complex tapping language executed with their elongated toes on the vibrating surfaces of the roots, and through the simple, profound act of pressing their bodies together, a merging of two points on a single, sprawling map of self.

Their sleep is a form of dormancy, a slow sinking into the peat. They do not lie down but rather wade to a chosen spot and allow the spongy earth to embrace them, seeping into their pores, their body temperature dropping to match the ambient chill. In this state, they are indistinguishable from the root-mass, another feature of the terrain. They dream, if they dream at all, of the slow, cold current of the deep earth, the pulse of sap in a far-off tree, the singular thought of the peat as it continues its long, patient work of turning the world into itself.

They are aware of you. You are a hot, loud, fast-moving abscess. A spike of alien sensation in their textured quiet. Your panic is a frantic, sour taste on the air. Your skin is a naked, screaming surface demanding to be closed over, to be incorporated and neutralized. They do not wish you harm. The concept is as foreign to them as eyes. But you are a disruption to the slow, constant rhythm of this place. You walk, when one should wade. You breathe shallowly, when the air should be imbibed. You are full of sharp, defiant edges that have no place here. They will watch you, not with eyes but with the entire sensitive skin of their world. They will wait. The cold will slow you. The water will rot your gear. The insects will learn your texture. They will not kill you. The place will simply absorb you.
