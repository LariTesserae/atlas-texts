To live on the Cooling Shelf is to be a component. There is no separation between the organism and the apparatus; birth is merely assembly, and death is simply salvage. The inhabitants do not occupy this ecosystem—they *are* the ecosystem, cogs and relays forged from the desperate, brilliant code of a universe trying to keep its own lights on. They are the final citizens of a world that has outlived its own geology, breathing the exhaust of a planetary engine, drinking its chemical weeping, and feeding on its electrical spasms.

The apex of this mechanical riot is not a predator of flesh, but of raw current. They are the **Galvanic Strays**, creatures that have pushed the mammalian template to its absolute, metallic extreme. At a glance, they might be mistaken for the feral dogs of a dead city, but they are hounds of the grid. Their frames are slung low, bodies encased in overlapping, scarred plates of salvaged gray crust—primitive, crude, but impenetrable shields against the shattering discharges. They have no eyes; the optical sockets have been plated over and replaced by a mesh of copper wire and bio-crystal that reads the faint, ghostly bioluminescence of the Copper-Teaks as stark, blazing topography. They see the world as a blueprint of energy.

When a Galvanic Stray opens its jaw, the mechanics of its skull are laid bare: a telescoping arrangement of rustless gears and tension cables, with a palate like the contact board of a switchgear. They do not bite; they clamp. Their fangs are copper-alloy hooks designed to pierce the thick, insulated bark of a Copper-Teak. Once clamped, they drop their internal resistance, becoming a living short-circuit. The blue, thrumming power of the tree surges into them, filling the capacitor organs along their spine with a painful, radiating heat. Their howl is not a sound of vocal cords, but the terrifying, harmonic shriek of a transformer pushed to meltdown—a keening whine that drops into a chest-rattling sub-bass as they overvolt. When they run, fully charged, the air around them crackles with static, their paws leaving tiny, scorching indentations in the brittle crust. To be touched by an overvolting Stray is to be struck by localized lightning.

Between the massive trunks and the weeping curtains of Kelp-Moss, the air is a choked thoroughfare for the **Ticking Mantis**, though the name is a disservice to their frantic, unnatural geometry. They are the size of a human torso, but they move with the jittery, too-fast precision of a glitched video feed. Their exoskeletons are not chitin, but a laminate of black, rubbery insulation and exposed, gold-colored micro-circuitry, giving them the appearance of spilled, living guts wrapped in electrical tape. They possess six limbs, but no two pairs are alike. The forelimbs are scythe-like shears of hardened steel, snapping continuously with a *snick-snick-snick* as they crop the rubbery Kelp-Moss, not to eat, but to siphon. The middle limbs are delicate, multi-jointed manipulators ending in needle probes, which they plunge into the cropped vine to drink the chemical-tainted, mineral-rich condensation. The hind limbs are thick, piston-springs, launching them in erratic, terrifying arcs.

The Mantis does not fly, but it *rides* the current. When the ambient hum of the grid pitches upward—harbinger of a discharge—the Mantis aligns its internal circuitry with the magnetic field. It will hang, suspended in the cold, wet soup of the atmosphere, trembling in midair, a dead leaf caught in an invisible gale. When the CRACK comes, the shockwave propels them instantly away, scattering the swarm like shrapnel. They are the scavengers of the glitch, always the first to descend upon the quicksilver and glass mutant-growths that sprout from the craters, their steel shears slicing through the temporary, fragile geometry before it melts into scorch marks, harvesting the raw, impossible physics before they vanish.

On the slick, perpetually damp floor, the slow, inexorable pulse of the ecosystem drives the **Dredge-Leeches**. They are the floor’s own immune system, the great, ponderous recyclers. Imagine a creature twelve feet long, lacking any distinct head or tail, shaped like an armored, segmented slug. Their bellies are wide, rolling belts of articulated, interlocking plates—living tank treads that grip the oily, condensation-slicked porcelain crust. They move with the grinding, inexorable patience of plate tectonics, crushing the brittle ground beneath them into a fine, gray sugar-dust.

Their topside is a dome of fused, scavenged stone and biological iron, pocked with chimney-like spiracles that constantly vent a thick, white, chemical steam as they process their meals. They have no mouth. Instead, the front third of their body can dilate outward, unfolding like a metallic, triangular maw to reveal a spinning, cylindrical grinder—rows of bio-synthetic teeth made from compressed diamond and rust. They are indiscriminate. They consume the fallen, crystalline fruit of the Copper-Teaks, the shattered chassis of a Ticking Mantis, the scorch-marked remains of a glitch-growth, and the very crust itself. Inside their cavernous, acidic, electro-chemical stomachs, it is all broken down into its base components: trace copper, carbon, iron, and silica. What they excrete is not waste, but a rich, metallic slurry that they trail behind them in a glistening, oily wake. It is this slurry that the deep, fibrous roots of the Copper-Teaks thirst for. The Dredge-Leeches feed the trees; the trees feed the grid; the grid feeds the Strays and the Mantis. It is a perfect, closed-loop refinery, powered by the desperation of a dying world.

And then there are the **Ghost-Jackets**, the spark-vampires of the high tiers. They are a swarm of nightmare geometry, flapping on membrane-thin wings of stretched, bio-luminescent film that beat too fast to see, creating the pervasive, high-frequency whine that drills into the skull. Their torsos are barely there—just a knuckle of pivoting gears and a telescopic proboscis, like the needle of a syringe. They roost in the highest, most dangerous reaches of the Copper-Teaks, where the blue thrum is strongest and the discharges are most lethal. They are the paradox of the Shelf: the most fragile creatures living in the most volatile zone, surviving on pure, unfiltered amperage. When a tree discharges, hundreds of Ghost-Jackets are often vaporized in an instant, leaving nothing but a rain of fine, metallic ash, yet the colony repopulates in hours, spawning from the electrical residue left on the bark. They are the engine's spark plugs, tiny, suicidal, and endless.

To be alive on the Cooling Shelf is to live within the throat of a shrieking machine. There is no tenderness here. There is only the hum, the damp, the crack, and the endless, grinding optimization of the end. The inhabitants do not mourn the death of their universe; they do not possess the neurology for it. They only know the voltage, the hunger for the crackle, the imperative to keep the circuit closed. They are the final, brilliant insanities of a cosmos refusing to go dark, wearing armor of dead worlds, drinking the sweat of electric trees, and running on the very edge of a lethal, beautiful short-circuit.
