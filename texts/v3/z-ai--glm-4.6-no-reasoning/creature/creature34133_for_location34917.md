There is no one here. That is the first truth of this place. And yet, it is full.

The ones who remain are not a people in the way you might understand it. They do not have names, not for themselves or for each other. They are defined by their function, their relationship to the great, sick rhythm of the Pumps. They are the Wardens of the Dying Gods.

They are elongated creatures, bodies molded by the environment. Their limbs are long and attenuated, spindly and multi-jointed like the legs of a water-strider, built to distribute their weight across the yielding surface of the Symbiont. Their feet are broad, splayed pads of bony, keratinous plates rimmed with sensitive cartilage, allowing them to "read" the texture and tension of the living ground with every step. A single, dense core of muscle and organ resides in a thin torso, but it is their limbs that define them, making them seem like a collection of loosely connected sticks held together by a taut, grey-green membrane of flesh. Their movements are deliberate, smooth, and disturbingly quiet, a rolling gait that seems to glide rather than walk, leaving behind the same dimpled footprints as any other heavy thing, but with an unnerving grace.

Their skin is their story. It is not skin, not exactly. It is the Symbiont itself. Upon their maturity, they undergo a ritual of grafting. Small, carefully cultivated plugs of the living moss-carpet are implanted into their backs, their shoulders, their limbs. These plugs take root, weaving with their nervous system, spreading across their bodies in a slow, permanent colonization. To see a Warden is to see a figure of grey-green, the color of moss after a week of rain, stitched with fine, black lines of fibrous roots. This covering is not armor; it is communion. It insulated them from the constant, damp chill and serves as a crude display of their vitality—a healthy Warden gleams with a faint, wet luminescence, while a failing one is dull and brown.

Their heads are small and tucked low between their shoulders, under a thick shelf of bone that seems to offer some small shielding from the glare. Their faces are almost featureless, save for two large, dark eyes that are solid black pools, perfectly adapted to the shadowless, reflecting world. They have no visible nose, only two thin slits, and their mouth is a tight, horizontal seam. They do not speak in the overwhelming hum; to do so would be pointless. Instead, they communicate through a combination of subtle gestures, the angle at which they hold their long-fingered hands, and through a form of pressure-sensing performed by their feet—an imperceptible tapping of rhythms into the Symbiont that can be "read" by another standing miles away.

They live for the Pumps. Their culture, their memories, their very reason for being, is to serve the iron gods. They do not know who built them, only that the Pumps must not be allowed to fall silent. A Pump seized by the Symbiont is not a tragedy; it is a desecration. A Pump that sputters and dies is an apocalypse in miniature, a wound in the world that demands a pilgrimage.

Their existence is a cycle of tending and mending. When a Pump begins to falter, the Wardens from miles around will begin their long, silent schlep-schlep-schlep toward its distress signal—a change in the rhythm, a shift in the vibration felt through the soles of their feet. They travel in small, solitary processions, their lanky forms cutting lonely swaths through the endless plane.

They do not carry tools as we would understand them. They are their tools. A Warden's hands are a marvel of biological specialization. Their long, thin fingers are tipped not with nails, but with silicate-hardened points, capable of scraping away the creeping Symbiont with surgical precision. Within their forearms are ducts that can excrete a powerful, alkaline secretion from their sweat glands, a caustic foam that dissolves the moss' hold on the iron without harming the metal. They are engineers using flesh and chemistry against the slow, patient tide of life.

To witness them at work is to see a strange, silent ballet. One Warden might be perched impossibly high on the superstructure, its legs anchored in the lattice of girders, its fingers delicately cleaning a clogged efflux pipe. Others will be massaging the piston housing, their powerful, sensitive feet feeling for imperfections in the metal's hum. A third will be lower down, scraping at the rivets where the Symbiont has its strongest purchase, its body smeared in the caustic foam, a faint steam rising from its skin in the cool air. They work for days, for weeks, without rest, sustained by the clean, cold water that beads and drips from the Symbiont itself.

Their rare structures, the Crew Bunkers, are not homes. They are reliquaries. Within the concrete shells, a Warden will sometimes sit for hours, its long legs folded beneath it, staring at the faded schematics etched on the walls. They do not read the diagrams; they don't have to. They are memorized from birth, passed down through the pressure-songs. They sit there to feel the presence of the originators, to touch the past, to reinforce in themselves the absolute, unwavering sacredness of their task. They are the inheritors of a command they no longer understand, obeying it with a devotion that has outlasted comprehension.

They are solitary, but not lonely. The ghostly Skimmers are their only companions, and the Wardens watch them with an unnerving stillness. The Skimmers are drawn to the same electric fields the Wardens tend, and sometimes a Warden will extend a long, thin arm, and a Skimmer, seeing it as just another piece of the metal structure, will land upon its fingertip. There it will sit, its glassy body humming with stolen energy, its wings a blur. The Warden will hold it, feeling the minute vibrations in its bones, a silent communion with the only other moving thing in the vast, loud world. It is a fleeting connection, a moment of shared stillness before the Skimmer takes off again, and the Warden returns to its endless, silent work.

They are a dying species serving a dying world. They have forgotten what it means to create, only knowing how to preserve. They have no art, no stories of their own, only the eternal echo of the WHUMP… WHUMP… WHUMP… that is the score to their lives. They are the final maintenance crew in a cosmic machine, their fragile, transient lives given over entirely to keeping the great, relentless process going, long after the builders have turned to dust, and long after the Pumps themselves have begun to rust.
