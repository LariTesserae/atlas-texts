To live in the Dissonant Colonnade is to live as an asymptote—forever approaching a curve, never quite touching it, defined entirely by the space you do not occupy. The inhabitants of this place are not citizens in any terrestrial sense; they are the uninvited congregants of a cathedral that preaches only to itself, ghosts haunting a machine that does not dream.

**The Echo-Walkers**

There are those who came here—whether by accident, by obsession, or by some failure of reality's stitching—and found they could not leave, and yet could not fully be. They are the Echo-Walkers. To look at them is to see a person rendered in bad lighting and worse acoustics. They are not opaque. The aqueous cyan luminescence does not stop at their skin; it passes through them, staining their insides with that same cold, patient glow. Their shadows do not fall away from the light source; rather, their shadows are the only dense thing about them, pools of that deeper indigo that cling to their heels and stretch toward the unfinished walls as if pulled by the same gravity that tugs at the coalescing water.

They do not breathe. The air is too thick, too saturated with ozone and the presence of the place, to be drawn into lungs in the normal fashion. Instead, they diffuse. They stand for hours, arms slightly away from their sides, letting the humid atmosphere permeate them. You can see it happen: the globules of water, drifting like lazy thoughts, will sometimes brush against an Echo-Walker's shoulder or chest. They do not burst. They are absorbed, vanishing into the semi-translucent flesh with a faint, internal flare—a bioluminescent flash of recognition, as if the water acknowledges a kindred thing. In this way, the Walkers drink. In this way, they become part of the city's metabolism.

They do not speak. The Colonnade's thrum is too loud, too total, for human vocal cords to compete with. Instead, they communicate through *placement* and *duration*. An Echo-Walker who stands before a half-formed wall, pressing their palm to the crystalline filaments, is asking the wall a question. The wall does not answer in words, but in *rate*: the solidification will slow, or quicken, or the filaments will glitter in a sequence that the Walker has learned, over years or eons, to read. They are scholars of the only language that matters here—the language of growth.

Some of them have been here so long that they have begun to forget the shape of a mouth, the purpose of a tongue. Their features soften. The sharp angles of a human face—jaw, nose, brow—become muted, blurred, like a sketch left out in the rain. They are being digested, slowly, by the architecture. In another thousand years, they will be fixtures: vague, humanoid outlines in the wet-slate polymer, their hands frozen in the gesture of touching a wall that has finally, fully closed around them.

**The Attendants**

And then there are those who were never human at all. The Attendants.

They are not born. They are *precipitated*. In the deep hollows of the architecture, where the indigo shadows pool thickest, the water gathers in greater quantities, clinging to the underside of balconies and the lips of unfinished doorways. It accumulates, and it *thinks*. The city's hum, shifted through the crystalline filaments, gives the water a temporary coherence, a scaffolding of vibration that holds the liquid in a shape that defies surface tension.

An Attendant is a column of water, roughly the height of a person but narrower, more fragile. It has no face, no features—only the suggestion of them, depressions and ridges that shift like ripples on a pond. It moves with the same slowness as the drifting globules, a patient, gliding motion that seems less like walking and more like falling sideways at a fraction of gravity. When it reaches a place where the crystalline matrix is still soft, still shimmering with potential, it stops. And then it begins to *work*.

With its water-hands, it touches the raw curtain of the wall. The water of its body merges with the water of the city. There is a chiming—a soft, complex chord, as if several frequencies are being struck at once—and the wall thickens. A new filament grows, branching from the one the Attendant touched, spiraling outward in a fractal pattern before settling into the straight, severe geometry of the Colonnade. The Attendant has given some of itself to the construction. It shrinks. Its coherence wavers. When it pulls away, it is half the size it was, and it drifts, aimlessly, back toward the shadows to re-accumulate, to re-listen to the hum, to remember what it is.

The Attendants are the city's hands. They are the mechanism by which the plan is executed. But they are also, in some unfathomable way, its *congregation*. They gather, sometimes, in groups of three or five, before the distant, pale arch. They arrange themselves in a processional line, mimicking the march of the pillars. They do not move. They simply *are*, standing in the presence of the arch as if it were an altar, their water-bodies humming at a frequency just below the threshold of the city's drone. It is worship, or it is maintenance. Here, there is no difference.

**The Listeners at the Black Doors**

The oddest inhabitants are the ones who live in the doorways. The perfect ovals that open onto absolute, featureless blackness.

There are things *inside* those rooms. Not the city itself, not the Attendants, not the Echo-Walkers. Something else. You know this because sometimes, standing near one of these ovals, you can hear a sound that is not the thrum, not the chime, not the gong. It is a *scratching*. Faint. Rhythmic. Like a fingernail testing the thickness of glass. Or like a pen, writing on a surface that has never known ink.

The Listeners are Echo-Walkers who have given up on the light. They sit in the thresholds of the black doors, their legs dangling into the nothing, their backs to the cyan glow of the Colonnade. They face inward, into the dark. They listen. Their bodies, already translucent, become nearly invisible from behind; they look like smudges of humidity, like heat shimmers off summer asphalt.

What they hear, they will not—or cannot—say. But it changes them. Their shadows, instead of pooling at their feet, stretch *into* the doorways, sucked into the blackness like ink down a drain. And sometimes—rarely, but sometimes—one of them will lean too far forward, and their shoulders will cross the threshold, and they will simply *cease*. Not violently. Not with a scream. They will just stop being part of the Colonnade, as if they had been erased by a thought the city had not yet finished thinking.

The empty doorway remains. The scratching continues. The city grows.

**The City Itself**

And finally, the truest inhabitant: the Dissonant Colonnade.

It is alive. Not in the way a tree is alive, or a coral reef, or even a slime mold. It is alive in the way a *thought* is alive—a self-referential, self-modifying pattern that occupies space and time and refuses to stop. The hum is its pulse. The crystalline filaments are its nerves. The water is its blood. The unfinished walls are its memories, still crystallizing, still deciding whether they will be retained or dissolved back into potential.

It does not think in propositions. It thinks in *extensions*. A new pillar here. A deeper arch there. A doorway that leads not to a room but to a concept—the concept of *outside*, which the city has never known and cannot quite bring itself to build. It is a city that is building itself to house... what? Not people. Not the Echo-Walkers, not the Attendants. It is building itself to house *itself*. It is a structure whose only function is to continue structuring.

And yet, there is a strange, almost tender awareness in it. When an Echo-Walker presses their palm to a half-formed wall, the wall responds. When an Attendant gives its water to the growth of a filament, the filament flares with light—a thank you, or a recognition, or simply the inescapable interaction of two parts of the same system. The city does not love its inhabitants. But it *includes* them. They are perturbations in its hum, minor variations in its endless, geological song. They are the errors that prove the system is real.

To live here is to be a note in a chord that has no resolution, a brushstroke on a canvas that is still being stretched, a word in a sentence that will never end. It is to be part of something vast and single-minded and utterly indifferent to your presence, and to find, in that indifference, a strange and terrible peace. The Dissonant Colonnade does not need you. But if you are here—if you have entered through the circular tear and felt the hum settle into your bones like a second skeleton—you are part of its body now. You are one of the weeping stones. And you will remain, echoing, until the wall closes around you, and the city's song changes pitch by one imperceptible degree, and you are finally, entirely, *home*.
