They do not have a single name for themselves. Or rather, they have many—lineage-names carved into the alcove walls, work-names earned by the shape of one's strokes, seasonal names that shift with the water table. When they must refer to themselves as a whole, they use a word that translates roughly to *the ones who are still hollowing*. It is not a noun so much as a present participle. They define themselves by the work that is not yet done.

---

## Bodies made by the place

They are small, and they are dense. Generations of carbohydrate-sparse living and the constant pull of damp cold have compacted them. A surface dweller might look at them and see stunting; the truth is efficiency. Every body is calibrated to the oxygen-poor air, the limited calories, the need to move through passages that narrow without warning. Their shoulders are narrow, their chests deep rather than broad, their hands—those are what you notice.

The hands are enormous. Not in length, but in breadth and thickness, the palms wide as trowels, the fingers shortened and knobby with cartilage that has calcified from decades of pressing bone picks into wet clay. The thumb on the carving hand—I say *the* carving hand because nearly all of them develop a dominant side with an asymmetry that would look deformed on the surface—is often pulled inward, the joint fused at an angle optimal for gripping a scraper handle. They do not think of this as a hardship. They think of it as becoming-fitted, the way the cured clay walls become fitted to their purpose.

Their skin is the color of the walls. Not metaphorically—literally, after a life spent pressed against the raw earth, the clay has entered them. It lives in the creases of their palms, the folds of their knuckles, the creases beside their eyes. The younger ones are lighter, almost the yellow-white of fresh-chalked walls; the elders are the deep brown-red of cured clay, as if they have been fired in the same slow air. Their hair, what remains of it, grows in low crests, kept close to the skull not by cutting but by rubbing—with stone, with clay, with the dust of the passages—until it wears away. It is practical. Hair catches in low ceilings. Hair catches in the seep.

They smell of the place: the mineral edge of wet stone, the fungal sweetness of the wall-growths, the faint sourness of sweat that never fully dries. It is not an unpleasant smell, but it is total. They carry the Undercity with them, exhaled through their skin.

---

## The way they move

Watch them navigate a passage and you will understand something essential. They do not walk. They flow—or rather, they *read and respond* in a continuous loop, their bodies interpreting the space faster than conscious thought allows. A narrowing passage: the shoulders drop and turn without breaking stride. A low ceiling: the head ducks, the knees soften, the body becomes briefly fluid. A wet patch on the floor where the seep has gathered: the foot redirects around it without the eyes ever looking down, because the feet know the topography of their chambers the way a tongue knows the shape of a mouth.

They move in the dark. Not by some mystical echolocation, but by the simple calculus of continuous contact. A hand trails along the wall—not reaching, not grasping, just *touching*, reading the texture, the dampness, the subtle temperature shifts that mark a carved wall versus a raw one, a passage opening versus a dead end. In the total darkness of the deep chambers, they navigate by the air on their skin: the slight coolness that means an opening ahead, the pressure-change that means a larger space, the dead-still pocket that means a chamber with no exits.

The children learn this before they learn to speak. A child in the Undercity does not toddle; it creeps, hands out, learning the walls. By the time a child can walk upright, it can also move through its home chamber in perfect darkness without hesitation. The passages are its body extended—the city is, for each of them, a second skin, an architecture of touch that they wear internally.

They do not run. They have never run. Haste is a surface-disease, the belief that time can be outrun. Here, time moves at the speed of clay drying, of water seeping, of stone yielding to bone. They move at the pace of the work, which is the pace of the earth, which is slow beyond the comprehension of anyone who measures their life in sun-circuits.

---

## How they live together

The unit is not the family. The unit is the chamber-cluster—a group of connected hollows occupied by people who share the work of maintaining them. The boundaries are architectural: your cluster is the set of chambers you can reach without entering another cluster's primary passage. But the boundaries are also fluid, because chambers collapse, seeps shift, and what was a separate cluster last year may have been carved into continuity this year.

Within a cluster, the arrangements are dense but not chaotic. Sleeping alcoves are carved at body height along the walls, narrow slots just wide enough for a person to lie curled—the position they sleep in, always, even when an alcove is wide enough to stretch out in. They sleep curled because the chambers are cool, because the body conserves heat this way, because it is the shape the body takes when it is underground and knows it. There is no shame in density; the alcoves are separated by the width of a hand, and the sleepers can hear each other breathing, and this is not intrusion but confirmation. *We are here. We are still here.*

The elders—those whose hands have fused to their tools, whose knowledge of the passages extends past what any younger person has explored—do not lead in the surface sense. They do not command. They *indicate*: a gesture toward a wall that should be carved next, a murmured warning about a seep that will shift with the season, a hand placed on a young person's shoulder and then moved to show them the correct angle of approach. Authority here is a matter of depth. The deeper you have gone, the more chambers you have carved, the more the earth has taught you, the more your words carry the weight of stone.

Children are not raised by specific adults. They are raised by the cluster, by the chamber itself. A child left to its own devices in a carved space will crawl to the wall and begin to scratch at it—this is instinct, or something so close to instinct that the distinction doesn't matter. The work is the first language they learn. Before they can shape a word, they can shape a hollow. A toddler's first carving, a scooped depression the size of a fist in a low wall, is kept. The wall grows around it. The child's mark becomes part of the city's body.

---

## The work as identity

Ask someone here what they do and they will not understand the question. The work is not separate from living. It is the medium in which living occurs, the way breathing is the medium in which existing occurs.

But the work has specialties, and these become identity. There are the *deep-cutters*, the ones who venture into the raw earth to open new chambers, who work at the frontier where the walls still weep and the clay is soft and yielding. They are respected and a little feared—they go where the seep is unpredictable, where the voids lie, where the scratch-marks on the walls suggest something large moves through the unused spaces. They return with clay under their nails and a look in their eyes that says *the earth moved while I was there*.

There are the *curers*, the ones who tend the finished chambers, who monitor the drying walls and know by touch when a surface has hardened enough to carve an alcove, who maintain the storage vessels and the cooking hollows and the worn passages that need resurfacing. Their work is slower, less dramatic, but it is the work that makes the city livable. Without the curers, the chambers would stay raw and dripping, and the walls would never harden, and the city would remain a wound instead of becoming a home.

There are the *water-readers*, the ones who know the seep. This is the most esoteric skill, the hardest to teach. A water-reader can look at a wall—can *touch* a wall—and tell you where the moisture is coming from, how fast it's moving, whether it will increase or decrease with the season, whether it's safe to carve or whether the hydrology will shift and collapse the chamber next month. They are the ones who locate new cisterns, who chart the wet and dry seasons, who know when to move stores and when to dig. Their knowledge keeps everyone alive. It is not written down. It cannot be written down. It lives in their hands, in the way a doctor's knowledge lives in their fingertips—tacit, embodied, impossible to fully transmit.

And there are the *void-tenders*, the ones who maintain the boundaries with the natural cavities, who place the offerings, who ensure that no one carves into the spaces no one made. This is sacred work, though they would not use that word. They would say: *we do not go there because we did not make there, and what we did not make is not ours to alter*. The offerings they place—the pale fungi, the thread-root flowers, the best grain—are not gifts to a god. They are acknowledgments of a neighbor. *You were here before us. We respect your priority. We will not encroach.*

---

## What they carry, what they keep

Possessions are minimal. The walls are the furniture; the city is the container. What a person carries on their body is what they need for the next hour: a bone pick, a stone scraper, a twist of dried fungus for eating, perhaps a small clay lamp if they are heading deep. What they keep in their alcove is only slightly more: the tools that are too valuable to lose, the stores of grain or dried thread-root that will carry them through the wet season, the salvaged metal piece that will be passed to the next generation when it breaks.

But there are *markers*. Small things, carved things, that seem to serve no structural or nutritional purpose. Smooth stones from the natural cavities, kept because their shape is pleasing. Bits of chert that fracture in interesting ways, accumulated in alcove-corners. Lengths of carved bone with notches and grooves that might record something—harvests, generations, the number of times a particular passage has been re-carved—or might simply be the work of hands that needed to carve even when there was no chamber to expand.

The most significant possessions are inherited: the specific bone pick that a deep-cutter's grandparent used, its grip shaped by a hand that has since fused and stilled. The water-reader's collection wall, a chamber where every seep they've tracked is marked by a small clay blob pressed into the wall at the exact point of emergence—a map of water written in water's own medium. These are not heirlooms in the surface sense, not status symbols or wealth. They are *continuations*. The work continues. The tool continues. The person is a link in a chain that extends back to the first hands that found the wound in the earth and decided to descend.

---

## What they know of the surface

Some of them have been there. The entrance—the wound they descended through—still opens onto whatever lies above, and in dry seasons, some go up: to trade cured-clay vessels for metal, to gather surface-grasses whose stalks can be woven into tool-handles, to see the thing called sky that they have heard of but cannot quite believe in.

They return changed, and not for the better. The surface is too large. The distances are wrong. A person who has spent their life in chambers where no wall is beyond arm's reach will stand in an open field and feel something like pain—the absence of enclosure, the way the air moves without resistance, the terrible infinity of a horizon. They speak of the sun as a wound in the sky, a glaring brightness that offers nothing but thirst. They speak of wind as a thing that takes heat from the body with a violence that the seep never approaches.

Those who have been to the surface and returned carry a new quietness. They do not speak of what they saw unless asked, and even then their words are careful, as if describing something that might not be real. *There is a space above us. It has no ceiling. It goes on until the eye tires.* The younger ones listen and do not understand. How can a space have no ceiling? What holds it up? Nothing holds it up. That is the point. That is what makes it unbearable.

Most of them will never go. Most of them will live their entire lives in the honeycomb, will carve their chambers and raise their children and be carried to the deep quiet when they die—the body placed in a finished alcove that is then sealed with raw clay, the person returned to the earth from which the chamber was carved, the wall closing over them the way a wound closes, slowly, as the clay cures.

## The thing they do not name

The scratch-marks on the high walls. The droppings in the unused chambers. The occasional passage that has been widened—not by human hands, not with the patient grooves of bone-on-clay, but by something that simply *compressed* the earth, squeezing the walls apart as if the clay were soft dough.

They know it is there. Or they know *something* is there, in the deep spaces, in the natural cavities they do not enter, in the porous dark where even the void-tenders do not place offerings. They know because the chambers they carve sometimes connect to spaces that show signs of recent occupation—not human occupation, but something that moves through the dark with a different relationship to the earth. Something that does not carve but *makes space by presence*, the way a root makes space in soil, by being and by the pressure of being.

They do not speak of it. When the signs appear—a passage widened overnight, a wall scored with marks that no human tool could make, a scent in the deep chambers that is fungal but wrong, too rich, too dense—they acknowledge it to each other with a look, a pause, a slight redirection of the work. *We will not carve here. We will carve elsewhere.* This is not fear, exactly. Fear implies an enemy, a threat, something that must be fought or fled. This is something else. This is *recognition*: the understanding that they share the earth with something that was here before them and will be here after them, something that moves through the substrate the way they move through the chambers, but with a body suited to the medium in a way that their bodies are not.

The void-tenders are the ones who manage the boundary. They place the offerings not to appease but to *communicate*: *we are here. We know you are here. We will not encroach. Please do not encroach.* It is a negotiation between neighbors who will never meet, who cannot meet, who occupy the same earth at different scales of existence.

Sometimes, rarely, the offerings are taken. Not destroyed or disturbed—simply *gone*, the niche empty, the thread-root flower and the pale fungi vanished without trace. When this happens, the void-tender who placed them returns to their cluster and says nothing, but their hands shake for the rest of the day, and that night they sleep in the innermost alcove, the one furthest from the deep passages.

It is not that the thing is malevolent. It is that it is *large*, and they are small, and the earth is the only thing large enough to hold them both, and the earth is still deciding its shape, and until it decides, they will live in the spaces it has given them and leave the spaces it has not.

---

## What they are becoming

They are becoming the city. This is not metaphor. The city is becoming them. There is no separation.

When a deep-cutter spends a lifetime carving chambers, their body takes the shape of the work—the fused hand, the curved spine, the calluses that are not on the skin but *in* the bone, the skeleton reshaped by decades of pressing against the earth. And the chambers take the shape of the cutter—the height of the ceiling, the width of the passage, the angle of the alcove, all shaped by the dimensions of a specific body. The city is not built to an abstract standard. It is built to *them*, to the reach of their arms and the width of their shoulders and the length of their stride. When they die and are sealed into the walls, the city retains their shape. A thousand years from now, someone will move through a passage and feel it fit, and they will not know that it fits because a specific person stood there and carved it to their own dimensions, but it will fit nonetheless.

They are becoming the earth. Their bodies carry the clay, the mineral, the water. Their waste becomes the chaff that carpets the passages, the organic matter that feeds the fungi, the input that the closed ecosystem requires. They eat what the walls produce and they produce what the walls need, and the cycle is so tight, so enclosed, that the boundary between person and place is not a line but a zone—a gradient where human becomes clay becomes wall becomes chamber becomes home becomes human again.

They know this. They do not speak of it in those terms—they do not have the philosophical vocabulary, or perhaps they do not need it, because the understanding is not intellectual but visceral, written into the rhythm of their days. *We hollow the earth and the earth hollows us. We shape the chambers and the chambers shape us. We are the thing that the place is doing to itself in order to become inhabited.*

When a child is born in the Undercity—and they are born here, delivered onto the cured clay floors by the hands of the cluster, the umbilical cord cut with a bone scraper and the afterbirth pressed into the wall of the birthing chamber as an offering to the void—the child's first breath is the breath of the deep. The air that fills their lungs has been circulating through the chambers for generations, has been exhaled by every person who has lived in this cluster, has passed through the porous walls and been filtered by the fungi and been cooled by the seep. The child's first breath is the city breathing into them, saying: *you are of this place. You will never be of anywhere else.*

And the child believes it, because there is no reason not to. The place is the whole world. The walls are the horizon. The seep is the weather. The slow dark is the sky. And the work—the constant, patient, unending work of hollowing out chambers in the raw earth—is the only thing there is to do, and it is enough. It is more than enough. It is a way of being in the world that the surface, with its urgencies and its distances and its unendurable sky, cannot offer.

They are the people of the Undercity of Embayment. They are still becoming.
