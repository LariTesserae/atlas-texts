They do not have a name. A name requires a self, an "I" to apply it to, and they are not parcels of consciousness in that way. To call them "the people" or "the builders" is to project a familiar—the idea of a civilization of individuals—onto a phenomenon as fundamentally alien as a self-sustaining geode. They are, or perhaps were, a consensus. A distributed intelligence that manifested as architecture and then, as a final act of will, liquefied that intelligence into maintenance and stasis.

Their original form is a matter of speculation. Perhaps they were bipedal, maybe they floated, maybe they were simply the city itself, thinking and dreaming in geological time. What is known, what can be felt in the very bones of the place, is their ultimate state, their final, desperate act of preservation. They are the Maws and the Skitterers, and something else besides: the conductor, the ghost in the machine of this city-sized instrument.

The engines that walk the floor are their cells, their antibodies, cast in a form recognizable to us as "fauna." They are the gross physical work, the heavy lifting of this perpetual war against decay. The great, house-sized Maw is a stomach, a bulldozer, a sweeper, determined to erase any deviation from the master plan. But it is not the mind. Its path, its appetites, its very existence are dictated by something far more subtle.

The city itself is their true and final body.

And it is woven through with nerves.

They live in the junctions. The hair-thin, perfect seams between the colossal stone blocks are not merely functional joins; they are conduits. Fused within the black glass of the city's skeleton are filaments of a material that is neither metal nor crystal, but a third state that hums with a low, resonant energy. This is the nervous system of the Sunken City. It is the ancient consciousness of the builders, compressed and cooled, now operating on the most basic level of pure, unending function. It is a mind without thoughts, only principles. It is a desire petrified into math.
