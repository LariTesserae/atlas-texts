To live here is to be a creature of intermediate geometry—too large to be ignored by the vastness of the chamber, too small to comprehend its edges. They are the only moving things in a world of made stillness, the only flesh in a cathedral of glass and stone.

They do not think of the surface. The surface is a hostile abstraction, a source of that blinding, magnified light that burns down through the crystal ceiling and forces them to squint behind the thick, polarized lenses of their masks. They are bathed in the byproduct of a dead star, but they live in the womb. The warm, skin-temperature water is their amniotic fluid. They do not feel wet; they feel only the pressure, the firm, constant hand of the medium resting on their chests, reassuring them that they are held.

Their bodies tell the story of their adaptation. They are not sleek like swimmers, nor rough like sailors. They are dense. They are built for traction in a medium that wants to buoy them up, to steal their footing and send them drifting like plankton. Their boots are led-soled anomalies, heavy enough to ring out against the mosaic when they shift their weight—*clunk, clunk*—a heavy, percussive counterpoint to the rhythmic striking of their hammers. Their hands are calloused within their gauntlets, their shoulders swollen with the specific musculature required to swing a hammer not just with gravity, but against the stubborn, viscous drag of the water. Every movement costs twice the effort it would in air. They have learned to move with the deliberate, mechanical grace of crabs, stepping wide, keeping their centers of gravity low, hugging the geometry of the floor.

They breathe through their necks. The masks are grafted there—seamlessly, or perhaps with old, healed scars disappearing into the collar—a technology so integrated it has become anatomy. The hiss and plink of the regulators is the sound of their lives ticking away, a constant mechanical heartbeat. Bubbles rise from their jaws in irregular, silvery streams, ascending forever upward to flatten against the distant crystal ceiling, gathering into a slow, shifting mirror of condensed breath. They are turning the hostile sun’s light back into a haze of their own exhalations.

Their minds are mosaic. They do not think in grand philosophies; they think in tesserae. They think in lapis and turquoise, in the cold gleam of gold leaf, in the fractal geometry of a repeating pattern that must align perfectly across a span of ten thousand tiles. The chamber is not a room to them; it is an endless plane of potential, a floor that is never finished. The "desert of opportunity" is their obsession. They suffer the particular madness of creators: the belief that if they can just lay one more tile, tap one more vein of gold, the pattern will become complete, the geometry will lock, and the floor will become a thing not just of beauty, but of truth. They are building a cosmology out of stone and metal, mapping stars in obsidian and mother-of-pearl, creating a heaven underfoot to match the hell of light overhead.

Their solitude is profound, but it is not lonely. They are a colony of shared solitude, like corals in a reef, each occupying their own hollow, their own work-cell, touching only at the edges of their expanding territories. They communicate rarely, and when they do, it is with the slow, deliberate sign language of the submerged—gauntleted hands moving through the water in broad, deliberate arcs, discussing angles, grout lines, the quality of a stone’s fracture. They do not speak of homesickness, because they have no other home. The kelp fronds, tethered and battle-green, are their companions. They tend them not with affection, but with the deep, unspoken gratitude of a lung for air. The kelp breathes out; they breathe in. It is the oldest contract of life, stripped to its barest, most functional terms.

The noise of the place is their silence. The thrumming of the pumps, the *CLANG... clang...* of the hammers, the hiss of the regulators—this is the white noise of their existence. It drowns out the intrusive thoughts, the memories of a life before the pressure, if such a life ever existed. They have become so attuned to the mechanical rhythm that a silence would be terrifying, a signal that the pumps have failed, that the water has gone still, that the oxygen is thinning. The noise is the heartbeat of the sanctuary, and they are its blood cells, moving through its veins, keeping it alive with their labor.

They age in this place, but they age slowly, like the tiles they lay. The warm, uniform temperature preserves them, even as the relentless labor carves deep lines into their faces, visible behind the glass of their masks. Their hair, floating in slow halos around their heads, turns white long before its time. Their eyes, always fixed downward, always parsing the subtle seams and colors of the floor, develop a particular keenness, a mosaic-vision that sees the world not in continuous gradients, but in discrete, hard-edged units of meaning. They see the chaos of the universe and they make it orderly, one small, sharp piece at a time.

To live here is to be an artisan of the deep, a maker of worlds in a bottle. It is to know that your work will never be seen by those who walk in air, that your great floor will never be danced upon, never be trod by the feet of the uninitiated. It is to know that your only audience is the pitiless sun above, whose light you capture and refract and return as beauty. And it is to be content with that, because the making is the meaning, and the pressure is the proof that you are alive, and held, and home.
