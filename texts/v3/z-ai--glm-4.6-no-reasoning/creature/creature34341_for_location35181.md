To live here is to never know silence, and to never know thirst as the soft things know it.

You are born into the crush. You emerge not into space but into occupation, your pale, jointed body pushing free of a chitinous ovisac into a world where every cubic millimeter is already claimed, already being consumed, already being fought over. You are small—perhaps the length of a human thumb, perhaps smaller—and you are never alone. From the moment your mandibles crack open and your sensory bristles first taste the hot, sulfur-heavy atmosphere, you are part of the wave.

You are a grazer of the amber vines. Your body is a dull, translucent thing—pale chitin like yellowed fingernails, articulated legs that end in hooked claws for gripping the smooth, hollow creepers. You do not drink. You have no mechanism for it; water is a violence, a solvent that would dissolve the mineral bonds of your exoskeleton. Instead, you draw sustenance through the delicate, fibrous pads of your feet, pressing them into the silicon vines, absorbing the mineral-rich sap that flows not with liquid but with a warm, dense paste of crushed verdure and atmospheric vapor. You eat by touch, by constant contact. To stop moving is to starve. To pause is to be consumed.

And so you move. You move as part of a wave—a seething, clicking carpet of millions that sweeps across the tangled floor of the room in great, slow oscillations. You feel the vibration of your kin through the hollow creepers beneath your feet, a shivering tremor that communicates direction, alarm, the presence of a moulting neighbor or the shadow of something descending from the ledges above. The sound you make together is the sound of the room itself: a low, grinding hum, the dry papery rustle of a billion legs scraping against a billion jointed stems. It is not communication. It is simply existence, made audible.

You do not think. You have no brain, only a distributed nerve cord that responds to pressure, vibration, and the faint amber light that filters down from the shaft above. But you know the geometry of this place in your body, in the curve of your claws and the bend of your segments. You know the hand-chiseled drainage slots in the basalt walls, now choked with copper fungus and oxidized viridian sponge. You know the shallow alcoves where the vapor-arachnids weave their mineral nets—places to avoid, places where the sticky strands of spun iron-silk will catch your bristles and hold you still while something red and patient descends. You know the eroded steps and balconies, the load-bearing columns strangled by vines, the smooth, undulating curves where the city’s sharp edges have been worn away by the ceaseless scrape of your ancestors. This is your architecture now. The builders are dust; you are the inhabitants.

When the shadow passes overhead—a colony of vapor-drinking arachnids, their iron-ore carapaces dull in the perpetual dusk—you press flat against the vine, your legs splayed, your body rigid. You exude a faint, defensive pheromone, a chemical shriek that ripples through the carpet of grazers around you, and for a moment the wave stalls, a patch of stillness in the churning mass. Then the shadow moves on, and the wave resumes, flowing over the hollow cores of stripped creepers, past the glowing dots of bioluminescent lichen on the black-stained walls, down into the basin of fine, hot mineral ash and shed exoskeletons that fills the lowest point of the room.

You will molt. You will shed your pale shell in the ash-basin, and for a moment you will be soft, vulnerable—a morsel of exposed, unmineralized tissue in a world of sharp, dry mouths. If you survive those minutes, your new exoskeleton will harden in the warm, sulfur-laden air, and you will grow. You will grow until the cycle of consumption outpaces the cycle of growth, until a leg is lost to a predator’s mandible, until a fungus takes root in the joints of your thorax and begins to digest you from the outside, copper and viridian spreading across your carapace like a slow, beautiful death.

Or perhaps you will simply wear out. Your claws will dull. Your legs will slow. You will fall behind the wave, and the wave will pass over you, and a million mandibles like surgical steel will reduce you to mineral dust, to the fine, hot ash that feeds the amber vines, that sustains the copper fungi, that exhales the heavy gases that fill this sealed room with its suffocating, desperate breath.

This is your life: to move, to consume, to avoid, to molt, to die and be re-consumed. You have no tools, no memory, no future. You are an ancient design, perfected eons ago for a world without water—a dry, sharp, clicking thing, as much a part of this pocket ecosystem as the stone walls and the pale sky above. And when the intruder comes—when the giant of soft, wet tissue stands on the threshold and feels the heat and the hum and the brush of mineral webs against its face—you will not notice. You will not pause. You will simply continue, because continuation is all that your body knows, all that this room knows, all that this ancient, dying city knows, as it settles into its own weight and grinds slowly to dust beneath the relentless, clicking tide.
