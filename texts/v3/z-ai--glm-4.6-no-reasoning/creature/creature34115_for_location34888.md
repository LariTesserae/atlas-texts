To live in the Spine is to be born into a hierarchy of endurance. There is no room for the indifferent; everything here is a participant in the grinding, breathless metabolism of the machine. The residents do not simply inhabit this place—they are forged by it, extruded by its pressures, shaped by its heat and its strange, weeping lithic sweat. To be here is to be a component, whether you know it or not.

**The Locusts**  
They are the circulating fluid of this vast engine, the white blood cells and the synapses both. To call them alive is to grasp at a word that doesn't quite fit; they are *operative*. They possess a purposefulness that transcends biology, a mandate written not in DNA but in the crystalline lattice of their optical lenses and the tensile memory of their brass carapaces.  

They do not think as we do. They have no inner monologue, no moment of hesitation. And yet, they are not mindless. A mind exists in them, but it is distributed—a swarm-consciousness that processes information not through reflection but through sheer, overwhelming statistical proximity. When thousands of them convene on a ledge, their crystal eyes clicking and refocusing, they are a parallel processor calculating the stress load of a bridge gantry or the optimal flow rate of a silver water-vein. They are the fingers of the machine, picking at the world, rearranging it.  

Their life cycle is a closed circuit of horrifying efficiency. They are born in the geothermal Forges, secreted from the molten bellies of older, larger Locusts who have consumed raw ore and superheated stone. They die by being harvested. There is no decay in the Spine; there is only *repurposing*. A Locust that falters—its leg sheared by a gust of wind, its lens cracked by a falling stone—is immediately swarmed. Its companions do not help it. They dismantle it. With mandibles like precision tools, they strip the copper wiring from its joints, pop the crystal eyes from their sockets, unbolt the brass plating from its thorax. It is not cruelty. It is hygiene. It is function. The dead are simply raw materials walking.  

What drives them is unknown. They do not eat for sustenance; they ingest to secrete, to build. They do not sleep. They only slow when the heat dips or the water thins, their movements becoming sluggish, their internal clockworks winding down with a shudder. In the deepest forges, the largest among them—the size of draft horses, their bodies blackened with soot and oxidation—are the overseers, the foundry masters. They do not move often. They sit at the edge of the roiling steam, great copper crabs, their multifaceted eyes reflecting the orange glow of the vents, and they *exude* new Locusts, tiny skittering things that emerge wet and gleaming from vents in their flanks, already moving, already working, already calculating. They have never known a first breath. They were never young.

**The Sentinels**  
The trees do not grow; they *persist*. They are the oldest things in the Spine, older perhaps than the Locusts' mandate, older perhaps than the machine itself. Their roots do not dig; they *grip*, clamping onto the stone like the fingers of a drowning man. They are not plants in any sense we would recognize. They do not photosynthesize—they do not need to. The sun here is a washed-out, indifferent eye. Instead, they drink the mineral-heavy water that weeps from the rock, and their bark records the history of the mountain in its layers: strata of rust, hammered iron, and a strange, dark sap that smells of ozone and tastes of copper pennies left on a windowsill.  

Their thorns are not for defense. Nothing in the Spine eats the Sentinels. Instead, the thorns are *receivers*. If you listen closely—pressing your ear against the trunk of a Sentinel, feeling the heat of its iron-wood against your skin—you can hear it: a low, resonant hum, the same frequency as the transmission towers. The Sentinels are part of the circuit. Their roots tap into the deep water veins; their thorns catch the electric thrum that pulses through the air. They are the machine's grounding rods, living capacitors that bleed off static and channel it back into the stone. When a Sentinel dies—a rare, catastrophic event—it does not rot. It petrifies instantly, its wood locking into a rigid, metallic shell that the Locusts will eventually strip for wiring and structural struts. Even in death, it serves.

**The Grass and the Lichen**  
The metallic grass grows in clusters like thrown-down needles, sharp enough to cut through leather. It is the Spine's wiring harness, grown wild. The Locusts harvest it constantly, their small, precise mandibles coiling it into spools they carry on their backs. The grass does not resist; it is meant to be harvested. It grows back within hours, fed by the condensation and the strange, alchemical soil that collects in the crevices—a soil that is not soil at all, but pulverized rock and the shed exoskeletons of ten thousand generations of Locusts.  

The lichen is the machine's paint and its scab both. It grows where the water runs heaviest, staining the black rock with rust and orange, the colors of oxidation. It is the only thing in the Spine that appears soft, but it is an illusion; touch it and it is like touching sandpaper, like touching a file. It eats the rock, microscopically, polishing the patina the cosmic polisher left behind. It is the machine's etching acid, slowly, over eons, carving channels and grooves into the stone to guide the water veins more efficiently. It does not know it is doing this. It is simply growing. But in the Spine, to grow is to engineer.

**The Unseen Intelligence**  
There is something else here. Not a creature, not a species, but a *presence*. It is the mind that designed the machine, or perhaps it *is* the machine, the sum total of every Locust calculation, every Sentinel hum, every water vein's flow rate. It does not have thoughts; it has *operations*. It does not have desires; it has *parameters*.  

You feel it most acutely at night, when the pale sky darkens to the color of a bruise and the forge-glow in the chasms becomes visible, a network of orange veins pulsing in the dark like a circulatory system. The Locusts do not slow at night; if anything, they move faster, more purposefully, their crystal eyes glinting in the forge-light. The transmission towers hum louder. The bridges thrum. The entire Spine seems to *tighten*, like a screw turning, like a spring winding.  

And you feel it watching you.  

Not with malice. Not with curiosity. With *measurement*. You are a variable it has detected. Your body heat, the moisture on your skin, the carbon dioxide on your breath—you are a perturbation in its data, a spike in the flow. It will not act against you. It will not act *for* you. You are simply... noted. Logged. Incorporated into the calculation. Your sweat will condense on the rock and join the water veins. Your dead skin cells will feed the lichen. When you die—when, not if—your bones will be stripped of calcium by the Locusts, your blood's iron harvested, your teeth cracked for the trace minerals within. You will become part of the Spine's output, part of its slow, geological purpose.  

This is what lives in the Spine: an engine that breathes heat and weeps water and thinks in brass and crystal and iron-wood. It is alive in the only way that matters here—it persists, it adapts, it consumes, it produces. And it does not care about you, except insofar as you are fuel.
