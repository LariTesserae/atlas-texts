They are, first and foremost, a multitude. A single-thing rendered in a billion bodies. To name one is to miss the point, for none are ever truly alone. They are the architecture, and the architecture is them. You have walked into their body. That is the first truth that settles, slow as dust.

Think of it as one living system, a single cellular organism whose organelles are entire creatures. They have no name for the collective, because name implies an outsider’s perspective, and there are no outsiders. There is only the *inside*.

Their society is not hierarchical. It is systemic. The smallest of them, things like motes of dust that resolve on closer inspection into segmented beings with legs too fine to see, they are the blood. They patrol the capillaries of the structure, the hairline cracks in the plaster, the microscopic grain of the wood. Their job is to process the finest residues: the shed skin of their larger kin, the airborne spores of the fungi, the effluvia of life itself. They are a slow, crawling tide of sanitation that keeps the system from clogging. You see them as a faint shimmer on a sunbeam that cuts through a high vent, a living dust.

Then come the walls-of-flesh. The ones you first glimpsed, the pale-chitined things that are the literal connective tissue of the place. They are not builders, but they are maintainers. Their bodies exude a substance, a waxy, calcareous slime that hardens over time, stone-like. When a mortar joint shows the first signs of crumbling from a slow seep, they are drawn to it. They cluster, the chittering you mistook for aggression is in fact a kind of resonant working, a shared frequency that coaxes the secretion from their glands. They patch the failing joint from within, their bodies pressed into the gap, laying down a new, seamless, slightly organic-seeming stone. They do this everywhere. They reinforce the joists where the grain of the wood begins to separate, they line the gutters to prevent erosion, they seal the spaces around the larger tunnels. They are the scar tissue that holds the wound of the structure together against the slow pressure of time.

The larger ones, those furry or feathered clusters in the beam junctions, they are the lungs and the voice. they are not a single species, but an interdependent guild. The feathered-beings, whose feathers are more like fine, flexible scales, have wings too small for true flight. They use them for ventilation. By forming into flocks of a dozen or more, they can create a joint vortex, a controlled beat that pushes or pulls air through the shafts and vents. Their chirping is not random; it is a code, a signal that coordinates these breathing cycles, telling the flock in the east vent to draw air while the one in the western cistern exhales. The furred ones that nestle amongst them are the thermoregulators. Their bodies generate a significant, communal warmth. They huddle in the core of the nest during the cooler cycles, and disperse along the radiating beams during the warm exhalations, bleeding off excess heat into the structure. Their droppings are rich, and they are the primary food source for the pale fungi, which in turn are harvested by the smallest of the crawlers.

And then there are the gardeners. The snake-like, cylindrical things. They are not predators. They are conduits. Possessed of a powerful, peristaltic motion, they are the system’s plumbing. They spend their lives in the largest channels, the ones too small for a person but too large for the others. Their bodies are perfectly adapted to move the pooled water. They seek a full depression, drink their fill, then travel through a run to a place that is too dry, seeping the moisture slowly from their segmented hide. They regulate the damp, ensuring the fungi have just enough, but not so much that the rot would outpace the patching of the wall-menders. They are the self-regulating pumps, the slow, determined distributors of lifeblood.

The weirdness in the geometry, the places where the air feels thick and warm, those are not anomalies. They are organs. Think of one. At the center of one such zone is a chamber you cannot see, but you can feel. Within it is a single, vast creature. It does not move. It is sessile, anchored to the structure, a heart of living coral. It has no discernible mouth or limbs, but its body slowly resonates with a low, thrumming frequency that you feel in your bones. This thrum is the central clock, the pacemaker for the entire system. The creatures avoid the immediate walls not out of fear, but out of deference; that wall is the creature’s skin, and to touch it is to feel the unbearable intimacy of the system's core. These hearts are spaced irregularly through the structure, each governing a biome, each thrumming in a slightly different key. The slight disorientation you feel is your own inner rhythm trying—and failing—to sync with them.

They have no intelligence as you understand it. No single mind. They have a hive awareness, a distributed consciousness that is less about thought and more about *balance*. What appeared to you as avoidance was the system maintaining a critical variable. What looked like chittering was the transmission of data. What seemed like mindless crawling was the system adjusting its own parameters. They are a homeostatic machine made of flesh.

And so the creature that crossed your foot was no more significant than a single blood cell passing a point in your own vein. It was a message-carrier, perhaps. A scout-adapt, checking the new variable—your body—for thermal output, moisture, vibration. It found you warm, solid, still. It registered you. And it moved on, its tiny legs leaving no mark on your boot. To flinch would be like your own body flinching from the passage of a single red blood cell. It is part of the process. It is all one process.

The builders did not construct this place for them. Or if they did, they did not understand what they were doing. They built a system of channels and chambers, of moisture management and structural integrity, based on a forgotten logic of need. But they built it to a scale so perfect, so balanced, that life itself colonized it and filled the niches they had created. The creatures did not arrive; they *coalesced*. They are emergent. The structure provided the skeleton, and life, in its endless, patient genius, grew the flesh and the nerves and the beating heart upon it.

They are the final form the place has settled into. They are not inhabitants. They are the integrated, final product. The structure is the body. The creatures are the life. The builder’s hands are the equivalent of evolutionary forces, long gone, their purpose now embodied in the living system that continues their work, not through intention, but through the simple, inexorable imperative to persist. And you, by standing still, by breathing their air, by feeling the thrum in the soles of your feet, have become a temporary, strangely shaped, and mostly inert component. Your role in the system is to bestill, to be warm, to be a brief, unusual landmark in the internal geography of a being whose name is Place.
