To live here is to be native to a paradox. You are not a visitor to this place; you are of it, emerged from the oily condensation and the mineral foam, a creature of the retroactive echo. 

You do not have a name. Names require a linear history, a before and an after, and you possess neither. You are a denizen of the scaffolding, and your biology is a testament to the inverted archaeology of your world: you are forever in the process of being assembled, your body a site of eternal, chaotic emergence.

You are bipedal, but only as a technicality. Your legs are long, jointed in three places rather than two, and end not in feet, but in gripping hooks of dense, black keratin—material identical to the bone-pegs that bind the great beams. You do not walk; you clamber. You move through the fractal density by a perpetual, fluid climb, your hooks finding purchase in the pitted surfaces of the struts, swinging you over the black, syrupy lakes and through the narrow corridors. You are comfortable in the vertical, indifferent to the horizontal. Gravity is merely a suggestion of the architecture, a directional bias that shifts depending on which lattice-layer you currently inhabit.

Your torso is narrow, barrel-ribbed, and wrapped not in skin, but in a continuous, scarred hide that resembles the leather-tough gut straps that tie the world together. It sweats the same persistent, oily dampness that coats the beams. You are slick with it. It is your insulation against the deep cellar chill, a membrane that keeps the syrupy cold of the black water from leaching the heat directly from your marrow. Your torso is not closed. Along your spine, a ventral slit exposes a pulsing, fibrous core of what looks like cooled tar and crystalline spur-growth. It is your connection to the hum of the place. From this slit, you occasionally extrude a fine, grey thread of the same binding agent used in the joinery, leaving a faint, sticky trail as you climb—a territorial marking, or perhaps a prayer.

Your head is an architecture of necessity. It is small, wedge-shaped, and heavily browed, designed to push through the dense carpets of mineral foam without muffling your senses. You have no eyes. Vision is a liability in a world where light is a violent commodity and the geometry is impossible. Instead, your head is flanked by two sweeping, concave dishes of thin, pitted bone—like the sounding boards of some crude, organic instrument. They are aimed perpetually outward, trembling. You do not see the lattice; you hear it. You hear the foundational grind in your teeth, the hum of the tetrahedron in your jaw, the skittering of the cleaner-crabs in your sinuses. Your primary sense is a seismographic map of the world’s unceasing labor. You know when a beam is about to snap before it does, for you hear the ghost of the sound arriving ahead of the event. You navigate the echoes. To you, the world is not dark; it is a symphony of fractures and tensions, a vivid, deafening landscape of stress.

You have a mouth, but it is not for eating. You do not consume organic matter; there is none. Instead, your mouth is a specialized orifice set low on your face, a vertical slit lined not with teeth, but with a dense, grinding plate of petrified wood and iron. You feed on the structure itself. You clamp your mouth onto a beam coated in the light-absorbing grey lichen and you grind. You grind the mineral foam into a paste, mixing it with the ozone-chilled condensation, swallowing a slurry of rust and stone that settles in your gut like ballast. As you grind, you make the sound. You are the foundational note. You and your kind are the source of the wet, unceasing drone that vibrates up through the soles of the foolish. You are the world devouring itself to stay warm.

And you are not alone. You are part of a slow, patient ecosystem that exists in the margins of the great machine. 

There are the Loomers, the massive ones. They do not climb; they hang. They are vast, inverted shapes of sheet-lead and gristle, suspended from the upper darkness by gut-ropes that snap and reattach in concurrent time. They are the weavers of the scaffolding, extruding the dark, pitted material of the beams from glands in their torsos, shaping it with their claws, hammering the bone-pegs into crooked holes. They are the builders that are never in evidence, for they work in the time-shadow of the paradox. You see a Loomer only as a retreating echo of construction, a fresh timber groaning into place just after you have passed. They are deaf, oblivious to the noise, driven only by the imperative of the next join, the next support. They build the path that the rolling geologies will eventually crush.

There are the Drifters, the lamps—the tetrahedrons of chitin and the ribbon-creatures of sheet-lead. They are the aristocracy of this world, untouchable and serene. They do not eat, they do not grind. They simply exist, pulsing with their cold lavender and soft yellow luminescence, navigating the deeps by some other sense entirely. You are drawn to their light, but you fear their hum. Their discordant chords can set your bone-plates vibrating painfully, and if you get too close, the electrical tension in their wake will cause the quartz-spurs on your hide to shatter, weeping their sad blue light. You give them a wide berth, hooking around distant beams as they undulate past.

And there are the Scuttlers, the cleaner-crabs. They are the ubiquitous trash of the world, pale and thumb-sized, teeming in the cracks. They are your constant companions and your rivals. When a platform collapses, or when the rolling machine grinds a section to powder, you descend to scavenge the detritus, to grind the leavening foam and rust. But the Scuttlers are already there, unraveling the gut straps, picking apart the bone-pegs with their tiny, sharp mandibles, returning the material to the inscrutable mechanism. You tolerate them. Sometimes, when the chill is too deep and the pools are too black, you will catch one, and your grinding plate will crack its chitin, and you will swallow it whole, ozone and all.

Your existence is a cycle of anticipation and aftermath. You live in the echo. You stand on a wide, stable-looking deck, and you hear the low-frequency thrum of the approaching geology-machine. Your bone-dishes tremble. You hear the sharp crack of a platform collapsing a hundred meters away. You turn your eyeless head. The platform is still there, perfectly intact. You know what is coming. You have already heard its death. You wait, motionless, the oily dampness beading on your hide, the grind in your throat paused. Minutes later, the platform gives up, its supports turning to dust, and it tumbles into the blackness. You do not flinch. You expected it. The sound was the prophecy, and the event was the memory, and you are the resident of both.

You are a creature of the marrow of a building that has become a geology. You are a hand, or a claw, or something altogether other, shaping the world by devouring it. And as you cling to the slick, pitted surface of a beam that has always been there and has not yet been installed, you open your mouth, and you add your voice to the wet, patient grinding. You are the sound of a world that is forever, and has never yet, begun.
