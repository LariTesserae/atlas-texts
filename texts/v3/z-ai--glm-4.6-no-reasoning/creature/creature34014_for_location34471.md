To live on the Verge is to be a creature of the threshold, an inhabitant of the aftermath. There is no thriving here in the way the lowlands understand it—no proliferation, no clamor, no seething biological imperative. There is only *persistence*, a quiet and absolute fidelity to the equilibrium.

The people who built the Constructions—whose tool marks still score the dark stone like a desperate, geometric prayer—are gone. They are not dead in the way of a fallen civilization brought low by famine or sword; they have been *resolved*. They were the midwives of this nascent world, hacking shape into the chitinous shards of the Verge’s broken shell. They carved the runnels and the basins, angled the roofs to shed the weeping stone, and hollowed out the watchtowers facing the void. And then, having completed their work, they stepped into the system they had finished. 

They are still here. 

They are the moss. They are the pale, waxy blossoms. They are the patient, spiraling strings in the pools. The Constructions were not built for a society; they were built to become the architecture of a slow, mineral transcendence. The inhabitants understood that in a world without time, without weather, without season, the animal rhythm of breath and blood was an intolerable disruption—a sharp, erratic note in the infinite, resonant chord. And so, they gave up their heat. 

It was a willing exhalation, a surrender to the Verge’s profound stillness. You can find them if you know how to look. Do not look for bones; the Verge does not waste its calcium. Look instead at the plush, silvery-green cushions clinging to the water-streaked walls of the great halls. They drink the weeping stone directly, but they grow in unnatural arrangements—too dense around the carved sleeping platforms, too perfectly framed around the squared-off doorways. Press your hand to the moss where a head once rested on the hollowed-out stone pillow. It is warmer than the ambient air, by a fraction of a degree. It is a memory of blood, a final, infinitesimal blush that the unchanging light refuses to fade.

Look at the six-petaled blossoms, the size of a thumb, flesh waxy and cool. They have no scent, because scent is an appeal to the wind, and the wind here is only a *shush*. But if you sit in the silent, sloped halls where the runnels channel the water into the central basins, you will notice that the blossoms only open facing inward, toward the water. They are watching the slow, geometric accretion of time. They are what the inhabitants became when they stopped speaking and started listening to the *plink… plink… plink…* of the dark coins of water. Each blossom is a thought held in suspended animation, a single, patient idea turned vegetative.

And in the shallow pools, the pale green strings unfurl in their perfect, mathematical spirals. They are the most alive of all. Their growth is visible over weeks—a slow, deliberate reach outward, turning the chaos of a former life into the elegant, inevitable logic of a Fibonacci curve. They are the inhabitants’ final art: the translation of breath into osmosis, of history into geometry. The people did not perish; they simplified. They became an equation that the Verge could balance.

But there are those who did not fully turn. In the towers with the single window facing the cloud-void below, the transition was interrupted, or perhaps stubbornly refused. Here, the ghostliness of the Verge is not just a metaphor. Here, a presence remains, caught in the amber of the perfect, shadowless light. It does not haunt; it observes. It is the watchman who never left his post, whose essence was too dense with duty to dissolve into the velvet moss. You feel it as a pressure against the back of the neck, a slight, localized drop in the temperature—a coldness that is not the clean cold of the upper air, but the damp, interior cold of a lung emptied of its last breath. It stands at the window, looking down into the featureless void, waiting for something that the cloud-sea will never reveal. It is a mind made of mineral and habit, holding its breath alongside the world.

To live on the Verge is to stand in the Genesis without the breath of spirit, and to realize that the breath was never exhaled—it was simply inhaled by the stone. The inhabitants are the landscape’s slow metabolism. They are the weeping water, the chiseled rock, the silent halls. They have achieved the only immortality a place of absolute stillness can offer: they have become the furniture of eternity, resting in the flat, unbroken line of time, as patient and as finished as the dark, glistening pools that catch their unending tears.
