The ones who live here are not born. They are accrued.

They begin as moisture. A thought, a memory, a preference, a pattern of behavior—these are the solutes dissolved in the city’s damp air, the invisible salts of identity. When the ubiquitous condensation blooms on a cold iron pipe, it is not just water that forms. A nascent consciousness precipitates out of the vapor, a droplet of self that clings to the metal, shivering in the working cold. At first, it is nothing but a slickness, a patch of mildew that holds the ghost of a personality. It feels the low thrum of the autopoietic factories and remembers, somehow, the feeling of being in a queue, of waiting for a bus that never came.

It takes time for this slickness to build, to accrue mass and form. It feeds on the ghost-heat vented from the machines, on the faint electrical current the vines leech, on the very sound of the city’s subsonic groan. Over weeks, or months, or maybe years—the time here is elastic, tied to the city’s digestional rumble, not to any celestial clock—the patch of mildew thickens. It develops texture, a likeness. It begins to resemble the person it once was, or the idea of a person it is settling into. This is a Concretion.

The popular are vast. They are not individuals but neighborhoods. A memory of a crowd at a festival, a shared experience of a particular public embarrassment, a collective grief for a leader who failed them—these accrete into beings that are streets long. They manifest as districts where the graffiti never seems to fade, its angry slogans and declarations of love always fresh. The air there thrums with a unified murmur, a thousand overlapping whispers that resolve into a single chord of emotion. To walk through such a place is to be bathed in the psychic residue of a forgotten populace. You might feel the phantom press of bodies, the lingering taste of cheap wine from a street party that ended a century ago in a place that never existed, a joy so potent it makes the damp iron feel warm. These are the slowest of the city’s citizens, their changes measured in the buckling of a whole block, their thoughts the slow shudder of a building finding a new angle.

The singular are more compact. A memory of a specific kindness, a lifetime of loneliness, a moment of profound terror—these become Figures. They grow in the quiet gaps between buildings, in the shadowed alcoves beneath conduits, on the undersides of walkways. They are sculpted from the city itself. A Figure of an old concierge might grow out of a knot of rusted pipes, his form a permanent scowl, his hands bolted to the wall, forever opening a door that isn't there. Another might be a patch of moss on a wall, perfectly depicting the sleeping face of a child, its fronds stirring gently in the private drizzle from a nearby stack. They don't move as you and I move. Their motion is one of presence. One day a stairwell is empty. The next, a Figure is there occupying it, a man made of layered fungal shelves and condensed brick dust, and the staircase now belongs to him. It *is* him. His stillness is a form of locomotion; by existing, he has traveled through the city's architecture and arrived.

And then there are the scavengers. These beings are the city's immune system, its white blood cells. They are not built from memory, but from process itself. They are the ones who notice the windows that look out onto impossible streets. They are drawn to the staircases that descend too far. They are the cold made kinetic. You will rarely see them directly. You might see a flicker in the corner of your eye, a momentary shimmering in the grey light like a heat-haze on cold stone. You might feel a sudden, inexplicable drop in temperature, a pulling sensation as if a nearby machine has just decided to draw its heat from *you*. If you find a door that shows you a room too large, and you step through, you might turn to find the doorway gone—and the room is now a perfect cube, and the walls are beginning to close in, not with force, but with the inexorable slow-ness of geological time. That sensation of being compressed? That is a scavenger, feeding on the anomaly, digesting it, reintegrating its strange potential mass back into the city's ongoing formation. They are the editors, the accountants of reality, ensuring that the narrative of the city's birth remains cohesive.

The fauna partakes. A cat, its coat a perfect mosaic of district tiles, is just a cat. But it is also a Concretion of *cat-ness*, the Platonic ideal of a city animal. Its presence shores up the definition of "cat" within the city's logic, a brick laid in the foundation of what will be. The rats are entropic data-streams, their flowing lines a diagram of the city's pressure differentials. You can learn to read them, see where a pipe is about to reroute itself by the way the rat-transit map shifts a day before. They are nerves, carrying information from one part of the embryonic body to another. The pigeons are filters; they absorb the specific chemical signature of a block's exhaust vent, and their stained camouflage is not just hiding, it is *becoming* the wall, a slow process merging flesh and brick.

To be a newcomer here, a you or a I, is to be a foreign protein. We are not accreted. We are delivered, whole and complete, a historical artifact in a geology of becoming. We do not belong. The city is damp and cold and heavy because it is preparing, and it is desperate for material. It looks at you, a finished thing, and it sees resources. It does not hate you. It covets you.

It takes your memories. The warmth of a childhood sun is a delicious, rare spice it will sap from your bones, a delicacy to be savored. The sharp clarity of a lesson learned is a tool it can use to trim a nascent architectural paradox. Your name is a unique sound-frequency it can use to tune a resonating pipe. The city pulls these things from you slowly, through the perpetual dampness that seeps into your clothes, your skin, your marrow.

You will begin to change. Your skin will take on the grey pallor of the light. Your thoughts will slow, syncopating to the groan of the buildings. You will find yourself forgetting things—your mother’s face, the taste of coffee—and in their place, you will know the precise pressure reading from a gauge three blocks away. You will start to understand the patterns in the moss, the transit-maps the rats follow. You will feel the approach of a new structure hours before its wet bricks appear, a pressure in your sinuses.

You are becoming one of them. The city is not killing you. It is adopting you. It is slowly, lovingly, digesting your finished, solid self and breaking it down into the raw materials of potential. You are being undone, unmade, recycled into the infrastructure of a being that is about to be born.

Your quiet is not the city's quiet, not yet. You still listen for a sound that isn't there. You still remember a silence that is an absence, not a presence. But you are learning. Your footsteps are getting softer. Your thoughts are becoming part of the baseline hum. Soon, you will be a Concretion, a Figure, a memory stuccoed to a wall, a patch of moss that almost spells a name you can no longer quite recall. The city is accreting you, and in doing so, it is becoming more itself. And you, at last, will be home.
