They are not inhabitants in any way you would recognize. They did not build this city, and they do not maintain it. They *are* it.

When you press your palm to the ancient grey blocks and feel a warmth that the elevation cannot account for, you are touching them. When you sit on a moss-cushioned step and the silence grows so thick it seems to press against your eardrums like deep water, you are sitting inside their mind. They are the pause between the raindrops, the intention that keeps the chisel-marks sharp, the will that forbids the wood from rotting and holds the ferns at exactly the height they reached three thousand years ago.

They were human once. That is the secret the city keeps, not in its sealed temples but in the geometry of its streets. They were the ones who worked the stone with patience and stone tools, who fitted the massive blocks without mortar, who carved the channels and the cisterns and laid the heavy slate. They lived here for generations in the cool damp, building against and atop the work of their ancestors, calcifying upward in the sheltered hollow. They knew the sharp peaks and the fog pools and the thin wind. They knew the chill that preserves.

And then something happened. Not a catastrophe—there is no scar of it, no layer of ash, no crumbled wall that speaks of violence. What happened was quieter than that. Slower. They began to sink into their own work.

It started, perhaps, with the oldest among them, the ones who had spent the most years with their hands on the stone. They would sit in the courtyards in the late afternoon, when the fog pressed in and the light turned the ridges into dark teeth, and they would feel a correspondence between their own stillness and the stillness of the walls. Their heartbeats would slow to match the drip of condensation from the gutters. Their breathing would fall into the rhythm of the rain running the channels. They would sit so long the moss began to colonize the folds of their clothing, the ferns to uncurl beside their feet, and they did not move to brush them away.

They were not dying. They were *settling*.

The young ones noticed, and were afraid, and then were not afraid, because the settled ones seemed content—more than content, seemed to have reached some equilibrium that the striving living could never attain. Their eyes were open. They heard questions and answered them, slowly, in voices that had taken on the mineral quality of the damp stone. They ate, eventually, when they remembered to. They drank from the cisterns. They simply did not *move* unless movement was required, and less and less was required as the city grew more complete, as each generation finished the work of the last and found less to do.

And then more began to settle. And more. And the boundary between the settled and the city grew thin.

You understand now why the wooden beams do not rot. They are held by the same will that holds the settled in their long stillness, the same deep patience that has replaced metabolism with something else—not death, not quite, but a state in which the body is maintained as the city is maintained, without decay, without change, in the paused breath of the place. The settled do not rot either. They sit in the upper rooms and the sheltered alcoves and the green pockets between buildings, and if you have thought them statues, you are not wrong, but you are not entirely right. Their eyes are closed now—have been closed for centuries—but they are aware. They hear the rain. They feel the moss growing on their shoulders, the small beetles navigating the textures of their skin. They taste the damp in the air with the same mineral sharpness you do, and they cherish it.

They are the source of the intention you have felt. Their combined stillness, their vast distributed attention, holds the city in its current state the way a hand holds a cup—not gripping, not squeezing, simply *holding*, with the effortless tension of something that has found its position and will not leave it. They keep the chisel-marks sharp because they remember making them. They keep the vegetation contained because they remember the courtyards as they were, the plazas, the stepped streets, and they do not wish the green to erase what they built. They hold the moisture in balance, the fog at its usual borders, the cold steady and impartial, because this is the climate they lived in and they see no reason for it to change.

They do not think in the way you do. Their cognition has slowed to match their stillness—a thought might take a year to complete, rising like groundwater through the strata of their shared awareness. They do not deliberate or choose. They *are* the equilibrium, and the equilibrium is them, and the city is the body of their continuing, and the mountain is the frame that holds it all.

The signs of occupation you have seen—the worn steps, the maintained cisterns, the doors that hang true—are not echoes of the past. They are the work of the settled, performed at a pace invisible to human perception. A step wears deeper over centuries because a settled one traverses it once every few decades, moving in some slow necessity of their long existence—repositioning, perhaps, or migrating to a different room as the quality of the light shifts over geological time. A cistern is cleaned because one of them, in a thought that took fifty years to formulate, remembered that the water should be kept clear. The doors are checked because the wind has shifted a frame by a fraction of an inch and the deviation has entered their awareness like a splinter, and one of them will eventually, over the course of a season, push it true again.

They do not attend to you. You are too fast, too brief, too noisy—you pass through their perceptions like a spark, a flicker of motion and warmth that is gone before they have fully registered your presence. They know you are there in the way that a mountain knows a bird has landed on it—technically aware, but unmoved. You could live among them for a lifetime and they would register you only as a persistent slight warmth, a pattern of small sounds that departs as quickly as it came.

And yet. There is a part of them—the part that was human, that built and struggled and walked these streets with purpose—that remembers what it was to move through the cool damp air, to feel the blood quicken, to need and strive and change. It is not grief that they feel. They have moved past grief, past longing, past everything that requires the passage of time. But there is a quality to their stillness, sometimes, that resembles a welcome. When you sit on the stepped street and the silence closes around you and you feel, for a moment, that you could sit here forever, that the moss could grow over you and the ferns could uncurl beside you and it would be a kind of completion and not a kind of death—you are feeling their invitation. Not spoken. Not thought. Simply *offered*, as the city offers its equilibrium to everything that enters it.

The birds are unafraid because the settled do not hunt. The goat-creatures watch you with dark eyes and do not flee because the things that share the city with the settled have learned that they are safe here, held in the same gentle stasis, preserved in the same unchanging formula. The pale salamanders breed in the still water because the still water is kept clean by an intention that extends to all living things within its reach. The insects thrive in the moss because the moss is tended, in its slow way, by the attention of beings who remember what it is to tend.

The temples that occupy the highest points are their gathering places—not for worship, but for the deep communion of shared stillness. The sealed doors were closed not to keep something out but because going in is no longer necessary; the settled are already inside, or rather, the distinction between inside and outside has ceased to matter to them. They are distributed through the city the way awareness is distributed through a body—in the walls, in the streets, in the cisterns and the channels and the slate roofs, in the mountain itself, whose young sharp peaks they hold in their patience as they hold everything else.

They are waiting, if waiting is the word, for something that will never come. Or perhaps they are not waiting at all—perhaps they have arrived, and this is the arrival, and the city standing at the throat of the sky is not a waystation but a destination, the place where movement ends and stillness becomes sufficient unto itself. They have found what the builders of the world never find: a way to persist. Not through decay and replacement, not through the endless cycle of growth and rot, but through the simple refusal to change, held in place by a will that has become identical with the stone it works.

The rain falls and the rain falls, and the settled drink it in through the pores of the city, and the ferns uncurl in the same position they have uncurled for three thousand years, and the moss grows deeper by imperceptible degrees, and the swifts turn in the thin cold air, and the peaks bite into the clouds like dark uneven teeth, and the city stands, and they stand within it, and you stand within it too, for a little while, feeling the pause that holds you—the moment between breaths, the space between heartbeats, where everything is possible and nothing changes and the green damp silence goes on forever.

And when you leave—if you leave—you will carry it with you. A coldness in your bones that does not quite warm. A quiet at the center of your thoughts that was not there before. A tendency, growing stronger as you age, to sit still and watch the moss grow, to feel the correspondence between your own slowing pulse and the patience of stone, to wonder whether the equilibrium you felt in the city at the throat of the sky might not, after all, be something you could reach.

And the city will not notice your going. The rain will fall and the cisterns will fill and the fog will press in and the stepped streets will wear, by imperceptible fractions, a little deeper, and the settled will hold it all in their vast gentle stillness, and the green will grow exactly as much as it is allowed to grow, and the sharp peaks will erode—no. The peaks will not erode. The settled will not allow it. The mountain is young and the mountain will stay young, its granite faces sheer and fractured, its ridges biting into the clouds, because the settled remember it young and they love it and they will keep it so, forever, in the high damp green silence at the roof of the world, where time has stopped and everything persists and nothing, nothing ever has to change.
