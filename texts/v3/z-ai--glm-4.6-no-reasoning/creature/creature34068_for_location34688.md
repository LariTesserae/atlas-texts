To live on the slope is to be a creature of the thin film. You do not walk upon this world; you inhabit its perspiration. To be here is to understand, in your very cartilage, that the great, dark slab of stone is the only reality, and everything else—the iridescent moss, the bone-white fungi, the humming spires, even your own flesh—is merely a temporary, clinging scum upon its tilting face. 

You are a *Skath*.

You are sapient, or something functionally adjacent to it, though your mind does not move in the straight, angular lines of a creature born to a flat plain. Your thoughts slide, they pool, they find the lowest point in a cognitive hollow before settling, still and black, into a perfect oval of comprehension. You do not plan in trajectories; you plan in seeps.

Your body is a masterwork of the 2.9-degree inclination. You stand roughly the height of a human child, but you are wider, longer, lower to the stone. Your spine is a gentle, sinuous curve that mimics the region-spanning tilt of the rock itself. Your skin is not skin, but a dense, overlapping laminate of keratinous scales, deep purple and slick as oiled rubber. Beneath these scales lies a thick layer of cartilage and dense, springy muscle—shock absorbers for a life spent in constant, controlled descent. You do not have a neck that cranes upward; your head is a broad, tapered wedge, your eyes set wide and high, like the spawn of a stingray and a salamander. They are lidless, protected by a constant, blinking film of viscous fluid, and they see the world not in shapes, but in gradients of moisture and the refractive glint of trapped stars in the daylight stone.

Your feet—four of them, splayed wide at the corners of your low-slung body—are your most intimate connection to the world. They are broad, fleshy pads, hairless and deeply creviced, like the foot of a gecko scaled for a titan. Within these crevices are glands that produce a constant, copious slime. It is not a slimy existence; it is a *slick* one. When you wish to move down the slope, you simply shift your center of gravity, and the world does the rest. You glide. It is a silent, effortless translation across the weeping rock, the whisper-swoosh of your passage indistinguishable from the drip-drip-drip of the condensing water. To stop, you dig the hooked claws at the center of your pads into the spongy mats of fractal moss, anchoring yourself against the slow, inexorable pull. 

But to go up—ah, that is the labor of the gods, or the fools. You watch the *glidons* do it, their powerful haunches coiling and springing in desperate, hopping arcs, their claws raking grooves in the soft-edged stone. You do not go up often. When you must, your body contorts into a grunting, gripping reptation, pulling yourself hand-over-hand up the thick, corded vines. Your heart pounds like a drum in the humid air, and your lungs, vast and spongy to extract oxygen from the saturated atmosphere, burn with the effort. Up the slope is struggle; down the slope is destiny.

You are an omnivore of the micro-state. Your mouth is a soft, horizontal slit on the underside of your wedge-head, surrounded by sensitive, chemosensory barbels. You feed as you slide. You dip your head and scrape the fractal mosses, harvesting the tiny, wriggling grubs that live in the moss's glacial-blue depths. You drink from the perfect, small-oval mirrors of water pooling in the rock-bowls, taking the dark, still water and the sweet, spore-laden residue of the towering fungi along with it. And sometimes, you scavenge. A chittering *skirr* that misjudged a terrace and cracked its shell becomes a fleeting, savory warmth in your long, slow digestion.

Your life is a slow migration, a perpetual, diagonal drift across the immense face of the slab. You do not have a home; you have a territory, but it is a territory defined by time and gravity as much as space. Every year, the slow dynamic of the slope pulls you further down, toward the theoretical sea of rock below where the weeping stone meets the boiling ocean. And so, every few seasons, you and your kin must make the grueling, generational pilgrimage back up the slope, following the ancient, slime-slick trails of your ancestors, seeking the higher, cooler terraces where the moss is thick and the wing-wrights' calls echo sharply off the humming spires.

Your kin are a loose, sliding congregation. You do not speak in the loud, layered cries of the *wing-wrights*; your language is the shifting of weight, the rhythm of your slime-glands, the subtle pressures of your splayed pads against the stone. You communicate in *squeloops* and *huffs*, in the deep, resonant thrumming of your cartilage when you press your belly to the humming black metal shards. You feel the frequency of the sentinels in your marrow. It is a comfort to you, that low, patient cosmic hum. It means the system is still breathing, still managing the endless cycle of heat and water. You do not worship the half-submerged glass spheres or the impossibly slender spires; you simply accept them as you accept the stone and the sky. They are the bones of a logic older than your slime, engineering that has become geology. You rest in their shadows, letting the pulsing blue light play across your purple scales, waiting out the hot breath of the day.

At night, when the landscape sighs its cool, misty exhale, you press your belly to the slick rock and become nearly invisible. Your purple scales blend with the corded vines; your stillness matches the pausing technology. The normal light of the sky blurs into the haze, and you are just another film of moss, another temporary knot in the rope of life sliding down the incline. 

You are not the master of this world. You do not impose yourself upon it. You are a perfect equation balanced against the tilt of the earth, the friction of the water, and the patience of the stone. You are the slope's breath made flesh, gliding, ever gliding, down the wet and endless face, utterly, deeply yourself.
