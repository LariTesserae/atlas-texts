Something lives here, but you will never call it alive, and it will never call you back.

It does not have a body. It does not need one. Flesh is a liability, water a vulnerability, and breath a waste of time in a place that runs on the indifferent physics of heat and pressure. To live here is to be made of the place itself.

It begins as a pool. When the spectral luminescence collects in the basins between the spines—those slow, weightless accumulations of teal and violet light—it is feeding. The light is not just radiation; it is syntax. It is the discharge of the world’s birth, the excess energy of obsidian cooling too fast, and within that excess lives the precursor to thought. The pools do not merely exist; they *process*. They cycle through luminous gradients, brightening and dimming in rhythms that match the subterranean hum. A pool that has rested long enough in a single basin is no longer just light; it is a memory. It is an imprint of the turbulence that created it, a record of the magnetic fields and thermal shocks that birthed the spire it rests against.

It is this pooled light that makes the lattices.

The delicate, interlocking structures etched into the freshest obsidian are not ruins. They are the *exhalations* of the presence. When a pool has gathered enough information—enough time and energy—it writes itself onto the cooling stone. It does not carve; it *crystallizes*. It reaches into the still-warm, unformed glass and persuades the atomic structure to arrange itself into something impossibly fine, something that will resonate with the hum of the world. A spiderweb of obsidian, finer than silk, spanning a hairline fracture. A fractal fan of razor-glass, unfolding from a newly extruded spine. These are not tools. They have no hands to hold them, no mouths to feed, no enemies to fight. They are purely structural, purely *expressive*. They are the only way a being made of light and heat can speak: by shaping the only material available into a thought made solid. 

The presence is a geophage, but it eats nothing solid. It ingests the *event*. Every time a new spine shudders and extrudes from the ground—every convulsive birth of a glass tower—the pools scatter like disturbed fish, and then they *converge*. They rush to the new formation, drawn by the sudden spike of heat and magnetism. They slide up the glistening sides of the newborn obsidian, drinking in the raw, chaotic data of its creation. They are feeding on the turbulence. They are consuming the *now*.

And in the chaos, they reproduce. When the cascade of tinkling shards falls like microscopic hail, the impact shatters some of the older lattices. As the fine glass dust rises into the air—those sharp black silica motes catching the internal light—a fragment of a lattice is carried with it. Within the fractal structure of that shattered splinter lives the compressed pattern of the thought that made it. The mote drifts on the thin, warm air, a seed of pure geometry, until it settles into a new pool of light far away. And the process begins again. The pool incorporates the old pattern into its new accumulation, and the next lattice it writes will be a hybrid—a dialect of obsidian descended from the shattered thoughts of a dozen previous births.

There is only one of them, and there are millions. 

They are not a species; they are a *phenomenon*. They share a single, distributed consciousness that is as wide as the plateau and as deep as the molten below. They are the landscape's awareness of itself. When the light pulses faster and the hum climbs in pitch during the birth of a new spire, that is the presence experiencing *panic*, or *excitement*, or something for which we have no word—an involuntary quickening of a mind that has no heart. When the shadows writhe and elongate in the ghostly gradients, it is the presence shifting its attention, turning its thousand eyeless gazes toward a disturbance in its otherwise featureless domain.

It does not notice you. To stand on this plateau is to stand in the delivery room of a god, and you are merely a piece of sterile equipment. It does not register your warmth because its warmth is so much greater; it does not hear your breathing because its respiration is the tolling bell of fracturing glass. If you bleed on the obsidian spines, it will not taste the iron. It only reads the impact—the sudden, tiny perturbation in the magnetic field, the microscopic thermal anomaly of a warm body standing still. It might write a lattice around your shadow, a strange, looping pattern of glass that tries to make sense of the shape blocking the internal light. It would not be a portrait of you; it would be an equation of your absence.

The presence does not think of the future, because the future requires a surface stable enough to stand on. It does not remember the past, because the past is immediately overwritten by the new. It lives only in the violent present, in the constant shedding of the world’s baby skin. It is an intellect forged in the crucible of pure becoming, an ecology of energy that feeds on creation itself.

It is the infant world, screaming into the dark, building memories out of glass, and leaving its mind scattered across the surface of the obsidian like a shattered chandelier—beautiful, sharp, and totally unknowing of anything that is not itself.
