What lives here is not a collection of species, but a single, continuous verb. It is an *ing*—a consuming, a seething, a dissolving and reforming. To descend into this caldera is to step inside a stomach that has learned to manufacture its own meals from the raw material of the earth.

The pale beetles—those fist-sized, eyeless glaciers of stomach-fat flesh—are the blood of this place, or perhaps its lymph. They do not think; they flow. They are a rheology of chitin, moving in rivers that obey not gravity but the pheromone gradients of distress and chemical heat. When they surge over a ledge, they do not fall; they cascade, locking legs momentarily to create living nets that catch the droppings of the flyers above. They are the great assimilators. Give them a carcass and it is not eaten; it is *resolved*, broken down into the caldera's endless metabolic churn. They are the janitors of an apocalypse that never ends, the white blood cells of a wound that refuses to scab.

But they are merely the substrate. The segmented cables—the centipedes, though that word is too terrestrial, too quaint for these ticking cables of mandible and motion—are the architecture of predation made linear. They do not hunt; they *span*. They bridge the chaotic gaps between shifting boulders, their thousand legs acting as living rivets holding the unstable geology together for the crucial seconds it takes to strip a carcass to its mineral components. They are the load-bearing walls of this ecology. When the floor trembles and splits, they do not flee the crevices; they dive into them, propping the raw earth open with their own segmented bodies, holding the doors wide so the swarms can enter, fed by the geology's fresh wounds. They are not residents; they are the structural reinforcement of the riot.

Above, the leather-winged flyers are the breath of the place. They are the exhalation of ten million tiny, ammonia-soaked lungs made manifest. They do not roost; they *combust*. They jostle and shriek and drip, filling the air with the acidic rain of their collective digestion. They are the mechanism by which the floor is made to feed the ceiling, and the ceiling bleeds back down. They are blind, navigating by the pressure waves of ten million skittering legs below, diving to pluck up the drowned and the crushed, returning to the heights to scream and chew and seed the stone with droppings that feed the pale beetles, that feed the centipedes, that feed the fissures. They are the storm that never breaks, only perpetually gathers.

And then there is the larval thrashing in the pools—the warm, standing condensation that boils not with heat, but with the friction of unformed life. This is the *desire* of the caldera. These are the things that have not yet decided what they are. They are tails and mouthparts and translucent skins fighting for the moment of articulation. They are the place's refusal to harden, its insistence on remaining embryonic. They eat each other not out of hunger, but out of a need to *integrate*—to take in a rival's momentum and make it their own. A single larva is a contradiction; it is only in the mass that they make sense, a single roiling thought that the caldera is thinking about itself.

But who built the cairns? Who lashed the volcanic glass with fraying bindings and smeared the ochre that the wet stone immediately drinks? Who left the tarnished copper wedged in the crack like a buried tooth?

They are here still, or they were here so recently that their absence is merely a different form of presence. They are the *un-assimilated*. They are the ones who looked at the endless riot and tried to impose a boundary—a line, a mark, a platform, a "mine" and "not mine." They are the hands that knew leverage. They are the ones who brought the hammered copper and the ochre and the wood—materials utterly alien to this place, materials that do not *flow*. 

They are the eaten who refused to be resolved. 

They are the stubborn. The residually furious. They are whatever species—or singularity, or tribe, or desperate congregation—decided that the way to survive a world that skips the garden and goes straight to the riot is to *plant yourself in it*, even if the planting is just a cairn of basalt that will be swallowed in an hour. They build not to endure, but to *interrupt*. The ochre on the wall is a declaration: *this is not just meat*. The platform over the pit is a statement: *I will stand here, and I will watch, and for the moments before the bindings chew through and the wood rots and the copper tarnishes into nothing, I will be a thing that this place cannot digest.*

They live at the very edge of the churning mass, at the boundary where the riot meets the raw stone. They are the exception that tests the rule of assimilation. They are thin, hard, and desperate—the opposite of the wet, yielding, boundary-less swarms. They are the ones who still believe in the individual, even as ten million legs vibrate in their teeth and the warm dark shifts beneath them like a single, hungry organ. They are the place's conscience, if a place of pure consumption can have a conscience—the tiny, flickering awareness that watches the riot and knows it for what it is, and stacks stones against it anyway.

What lives here, then, is the riot itself—the great, boundaryless *they* of chitin and flesh and mandible and wing, a single organism the size of a cathedral, breathing and eating and shifting, stuck forever in the act of becoming. And within it, like a splinter of copper in the meat of the earth, lives whatever stubborn, furious, lever-handed thing refuses to dissolve.
