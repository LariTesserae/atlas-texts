What lives here is not truly alive, for life requires becoming, and nothing in this hollow becomes. What lives here *occupies*.

They are the pale climbers—the crustacean multitudes that carpet the interstices of this frozen cathedral. To call them a species is to grant them a genealogy they do not possess; they have no ancestors, no progeny, no mating rituals softening the edges of their existence. They are produced, perhaps, in the same subterranean moment that coughed the steel bores up through the fresh basalt—extruded whole and clicking into the dimness, already mid-stride, already bound to the rhythm. They are white as the inside of a lip, white as cave-saliva, their chitin lacking every pigment that might suggest a sun has ever shone on any branch of their phantom family tree. This leucism is not adaptation. There is no environment here to adapt to, no selective pressure, no survival of the fittest. There is only the hum and the climb.

Their bodies are an engineering of obsessive repetition. Low and broad, they are built to cling to wet vertical glass—basalt that gleams like a black mirror, offering no friction, no pores for grip. Their legs are numerous, splayed in a starburst of pale, jointed spines that end in hooked tarsi—microscopic barbs that catch in the molecular condensation itself, finding purchase in the omnipresent film of water. They do not grip the stone; they grip the wetness *on* the stone. When a bore rotates, they ride it. Not with any apparent purpose or destination, but because the steel moves beneath them and their legs are calibrated to hold. They are the static on a turning wheel, the irrelevant but inseparable friction of the machine’s skin.

They have mouths—complex, overlapping mandibles designed for crushing and grinding—but these orifices hang open or clamp shut in mechanical idleness. There is nothing here to consume that they do not immediately re-consume. The hypertrophic mounds of indigo and bile-yellow vegetation offer pulpy barrels of stored moisture, but the climbers do not drink from them. The tepid condensation weeping from the rotating bores runs in rivulets down the pale carapaces, pooling briefly in the junctures of their segmented bodies before sliding away, absorbed back into the suffocating humidity. They are flooded with water they never swallow, surrounded by food they never eat, inundated by heat they never process. They are closed systems of kinetic waste.

Their sensory apparatus, if it can be called that, appears entirely inward-facing. They possess no visible eyes—no compound lenses to drink the perpetual twilight, no stalks to swivel toward motion. In their place are shallow, dimpled depressions in the cranial carapace, smooth as river-stones, sealed over with a thin, translucent membrane that pulses faintly in sync with the subterranean thud. They do not see the hollow. They do not hear the skittering of their thousands of neighbors. They feel—or rather, they *obey*—the vibration. The deep, rhythmic shake that rises through the fresh, chisel-chipped basalt and into the hooked tarsi of their feet is their only stimulus. It is a metronome embedded in the marrow of the world, and they are its expression in flesh.

What drives them is not instinct, for instinct implies a response to an external condition—a predator, a scarcity, a season. There are no seasons here. What drives them is *imperative*. They scuttle because the engine beats. They climb because the bores rotate. They drop from the steel pillars and return to their bases because the cycle demands a descent for every ascent, a release for every grip. They are analog output—meat and chitin rendered as audible frequency, their countless legs scratching the wet rock in an overloud, percussive chorus that rides atop the engine’s thud like harmonic distortion. To stand among them is to hear not a colony of creatures, but a single, massive, distributed instrument playing a single, endless, unvarying note.

They are, each of them, a failure of time. They do not age. A carapace that is slick with condensation on one circuit of the bore will be equally slick on the ten-thousandth; there is no wear on their joints, no micro-fractures in their chitin, no accumulation of injury or exhaustion. They are pristine as the oiled steel, machined to a tolerance that biology should not permit. And when the glitching stasis of the hollow asserts itself—when the localized temporal drag catches them mid-scuttle—they freeze. They hang in the air like ornaments pinned to invisible felt, legs splayed in mid-stride, mandibles locked open, suspended in a second that stretches into five, into ten. And when the drag releases, they resume. They do not startle. They do not hesitate or recalibrate. The leg that was descending simply finishes its descent. The claw that was opening simply finishes its opening. They do not know they have paused, because they do not know they are moving. They have no internal clock, no experience of duration. They are processes, not beings—subroutines executing in a loop that has no exit condition.

If you crouch in the damp dimness and watch them—and you will, because there is nothing else to watch, the painted cliffs offering no distance, the stationary clouds no weather—you will begin to lose your own thread of time. The overloud rhythm of their skittering will enter your skull and crowd out your pulse. You will reach out to touch a pale carapace as it scales a wet stone, and your hand will slow in the temporal drag an inch from its shell, and you will watch the creature’s legs continue their mechanical ballet at a fraction of their speed, each hooked tarsus catching and releasing the condensation with unbearable, granular slowness. You will see the membrane over its eyeless depression pulse once, twice, three times in the dragged second—and then your hand will snap back, the speed will surge, and the creature will be three feet away, already ascending the next bore, already forgetting you were ever there.

They do not notice you because they cannot notice you. They do not flee because there is nowhere to go—the hollow is a closed room, its boundaries a flat plane, its logic a glitch. They do not interact with each other because interaction requires acknowledgment of an other, and they are not individuals; they are a mass, a carpet, a single organism distributed across ten thousand clicking shells. When two meet on the wet verticality of a rotating pillar, they do not vie for position or communicate. They simply climb through each other, their legs interleaving for a moment in a complex, frictionless gear-pattern before they separate, one ascending, one descending, neither altered by the crossing.

They are the inhabitants of a cathedral that worships nothing, congregating around an engine that accomplishes nothing, nourished by a stasis that permits no growth. They are the sole animate presence in a pocket of reality where the verb *to live* has been stripped of its future tense and its past, leaving only a shrill and endless present. They will scuttle on the wet black glass long after you leave. They were scuttling on it before you arrived. And if the engine beneath were ever to cease its frozen, pointless thudding, they would not die—they would simply, instantly, stop, mid-step, mid-gasp, pinned forever to the air of a world that tried to start and broke, leaving them behind as the only proof that motion was ever intended at all.
