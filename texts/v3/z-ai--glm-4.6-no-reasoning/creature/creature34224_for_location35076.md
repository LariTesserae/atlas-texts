The ones who live here were not born for it. Their forebears were, perhaps, in that they were born to it, but the ones who *live* here now are not so much born as they are *made*, just as their world is made. They are the Custodians, the Keepers, the Unending Maintenance Crew. To call them a people is to miss the point; they are a function, a living process with the same patience as the rust.

They are slender and tall, but not in a way that suggests fragility. Their bodies are built for the vertical. Their limbs are disproportionately long, ending in three-fingered, three-toed extremities that are not quite hands and not quite hooks. The pads of these digits are a dense, rubbery cartilage, ridged and textured like the non-slip surface of a high-tech walkway. This gives them an incredible, innate grip on wet iron, smooth glass, and crumbling concrete. They do not slip. They *adhere*. Their movements, when seen from a distance, have the flowing grace of a vine unfurling in time-lapse, but up close, there is a hitch, a mechanical precision to each placement of hand and foot that tells you this is an evolved, deeply-ingrained skill.

Their skin is the color of the place: a mottled, cool-grey, like weathered concrete, shot through with faint, iridescent veins of copper green and rust-brown that seem to shift and intensify in the pervasive light of the arc-lamps. It is not an aesthetic trait, but a functional one. Their skin is leathery and largely impermeable, protecting them from the constant damp and the occasional errant gout of scalding steam from a flawed pipe. A fine layer of slick, moss-like fuzz covers their upper back and shoulders, a symbiotic colony that absorbs ambient moisture and muffles the sound of their movement against the metal, allowing them to move without disturbing the deeper resonances of the city-mountain.

They are profoundly quiet. Their vocal cords have atrophied, or perhaps they never developed in a way we would recognize as speech. They communicate not through sound, but through a complex, tactile language and a sharp, hierarchical series of gestures that can be read across vast distances in the echoing spaces. To convey simple information—a gas leak, a blocked runoff, a newly formed safe perch—they tap coded rhythms onto the nearest resonant surface. A long, low thrum on a support girder might be a location marker, while three quick, sharp taps on a pipe could signal danger. For more complex dialogue, they meet on the wider platforms, the "Congregations," and speak with their hands, their long fingers tracing intricate shapes in the humid air, describing stresses, pressures, and flow-rates with the fluidity of a musician.

Their eyes are their most startling feature. They are large, black, and lidless, like polished obsidian beads. They lack pupils, giving them a perpetually startled, alien look. They are superbly adapted to the harsh, flat glare of the artificial lights and the deep, shadowed gloom of the undersides. They see in gradients and textures, not colors and details. A vibrissum of fine, sensitive hairs protrudes from their cheekbones and brow, twitching constantly in the humid air, feeling for changes in pressure and vibration, for the unseen tremor that signals a failing pump or the subtle shift in air current from an open vent miles away. They feel the world as much as they see it. They live within a constant, internal map of the Great Machine’s heartbeat.

The Custodians have no individual names. They have designations, alphanumeric strings based on their sector and function (e.g., 7Gamma-Scrape, 3Delta-Flow, 9Bravo-Clearance), but they rarely use them except in moments of stark necessity. Their sense of self is diffuse, meshed with the community and the structure. A lone Custodian is an impossible, terrifying concept; they exist only as part of the circuit, a single wire in a vast loom. The "lowerers," who work in the warm, aromatic, and dangerous deeps among the engines and steam-pipes, are considered separate but vital kin by the " cleaners," who navigate the precarious heights, clearing overgrown gutters and ensuring condensation has a path to flow.

Their needs are minimal and are met entirely by the structure. They do not eat the rust-crabs or the bright birds. They ingest a nutrient paste that is a byproduct of the deep-engine water reclamation process—a gray, flavorless slurry that extrudes from designated spigots on a timed cycle. Their clothing, if it can be called that, is a part of them. They are born with a thin, metallic carapace covering their torso and thighs, which they augment over their lives by fusing plates, wiring, and mesh scavenged from defunct machinery directly onto their bodies with a chemical tackiness that their own skin secretes. They are walking pelicans of refuse, their outfits a collage of forgotten purpose, a personal history of maintenance etched in steel.

They have no art, no history, no mythology. Or perhaps, they are all three. The endless, practiced repair of a rusted girder is their art. The hierarchy of maintenance tasks is their history. Their entire cosmology is the understanding that to maintain the falling thing is to hold back the void, that their ceaseless work sustains the world not for themselves, but for the world itself. They do not question why the lights must stay on or why the pumps must keep churning; the question is irrelevant, a remnant of a time before the Treaty. Their purpose is simply the doing. They are the process by which the mountain settles, rather than collapses. They are the immune system of a dying god, endlessly fighting a losing war to grant it a slow, beautiful, green-tinged decay.
