The people of the Ward call themselves the Inhabitants. It is a term that is less a proud label and more a statement of fact, a quiet acknowledgment of their position. They are not masters, not conquerors, not even residents in a conventional sense. They are a warm-blooded, softly-spoken infestation that has been, for generations, permitted by the greater consciousness of the place.

Their bodies are a direct consequence of their environment. They are a pale people, their skin possessing the washed-out quality of things that grow in low light. It is not a sickly pallor, but a pearlescent one, a milky hue that seems to hold onto the soft green-gold luminescence of the Lumen-tubes and often reflects with a faint, opalescent sheen in the gloom. Their hair, which grows long, is typically shades of ash brown, deepens to near-black, or occasionally carries the silver-grey of the lichen on the walls. Their eyes are their most distinctive feature. Dark-adapted, their irises are large and perpetually, deeply black, swallowing light. As a result, their pupils are almost indistinguishable, giving them a gaze that is perceived as either unnervingly vacant or profoundly observant, depending on the mood of the observer.

Physically, they are not frail, but lack the harsh density of those who live under the sun. There is a softness to them, a fluidity in their movements that echoes the gentle, rhythmic seepage of the water around them. They walk with a quiet, practiced deliberation, their bare feet or soft-soled slippers making almost no sound on the spongy, damp walkways. Their voices are low and melodic, pitched to carry no further than necessary, as if to do otherwise would be an affront to the prevailing hum. They do not shout; the concept is alien.

Their diet is a testament to their dependency. It is simple, uniform, and all-encompassing. The bulk of their sustenance is the nutritional paste derived from the Phloxignum sap. It is a bland, beige substance with the consistency of soft clay and a faint, sweet, earthy taste. They eat it from carved wooden bowls, sometimes mixing in reconstituted scrapings from the algae pools for texture. The only real delicacy is the rendered oil from the slicks, a scarce resource saved for infants, the elderly, or for times of great exertion. It is a delicacy not for its flavor, a briny, metallic tang, but for its incredible caloric density. To taste it is to taste the concentrated life of the Ward itself.

Society is a fluid matrix of vocation and proximity, rather than a rigid hierarchy. There are no formal leaders in the way a surface-dweller would understand it. The Inhabitants are organized by function, and respect is earned through competence and contribution. The highestesteemed are the Tenders, those who understand the Phloxignum. They can read the subtle color shifts in the pulsing blue capillaries, feeling the plant’s health through the pads of their fingers. They know with gentle intuition where to prune, where to encourage new growth, and where to install a new Extractor with the least possible harm. Next are the Engineers, the keepers of the metal. They are the guardians of the Refinery, the masters of the pressurized glass Lumen-tubes and the humming generators. They are a more pragmatic, less mystical group, but their work is just as vital. Then there are the Pool-Cleaners, the Floor-Menders, the Nursery Carers who cultivate the soft, springy humus for new living-quarters. Everyone works. To be without a role is to be without a place, a fate worse than death in the Ward.

Their culture is one of deliberate, symbiotic existence. They dress in simple, woven garments made from treated Phloxignon fibers—durable, absorbent, and perpetually damp to the touch. Their aesthetics are functional, mirroring the architecture around them. Carvings on their living-chamber walls are not ornate but representational: delicate diagrams of a capillary network, the geometric pattern of a lichen patch, the cross-section of an Extractor needle. Their music, when it exists, is not melodic but rhythmic, mimicking the steady plinking of water or the subtle oscillations in the Refinery’s hum.

The Inhabitants’ relationship with the Phloxignum is the heart of their being. To speak of it as a "plant" is a surface-dweller’s error. To them, it is the All-Mother, the Host, the Frame of the World. It is not a deity to be worshipped, but an immeasurably vast and ancient presence whose rhythms they must learn and obey. The Tenders are its most sensitive interpreters. They will sometimes press their ear to the wall for hours, learning to distinguish the "voice" of the plant from the ambient noise of the city. They speak not to it in words, but in touch, in careful, considerate actions. An Extractor being installed is not conquest; it is a solemn negotiation, a careful incision that is sealed and tended as one would treat a wound.

The "weirdness" the guest perceives is simply the nature of their reality. The stories of a forgotten terraforming project are not myths to them, but plausible history. But the whisper that the Phloxignum is aware of them is something more. It is a truth they feel in their bones. They know it because of the way the warmth in the living walls seems to intensify when a child is born in a nearby chamber. They know it because the slow, cyclical surges in the sap pressure often seem to align not with any environmental factor, but with the collective emotional state of the community. After a rare accident—say, a breach in a Lumen-tube that extinguishes the light in a whole district—the All-Mother will sometimes offer a small, localized compensation: a sudden, profuse bloom of Grove-Moths, their stained-glass wings filling the darkness with a gentle, pulsing light, a gesture that is too specific to be coincidence.

The Inhabitants live inside this feedback loop. Their deepest fear is to become a disease. The hum of the Refinery is not the sound of machines; it is the sound of their shared exhale, of the slow, careful siphoning of the Host’s lifeblood, an action that must always be balanced. They are not just tenants; they are a conscious, functioning part of the Phloxignum’s own immune system.

So who lives here?

A people who have forgotten the sun, whose memory is preserved only in the word "daylight," a term they use to describe the grey miasma outside their cavernous world. A people whose sense of self extends beyond their skin, whose community is not just the other warm bodies around them, but the warm walls that enclose them, the spongy floor beneath their feet, and the gentle, watching darkness above. They are the quiet consciousness of the Deep, a human chorus singing a single, sustained note of survival within the lung of a sleeping god.
