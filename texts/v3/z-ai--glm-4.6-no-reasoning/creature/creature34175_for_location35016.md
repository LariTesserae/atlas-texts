What lives here is not a creature. It is your own internal navigation system, trying and failing to chart the space. It is the echo of your own mind, hunting for a familiar reference point and finding only the unblinking, patient presence of the plant.

Because within this place, to be alive is to be an anomaly. To have a story, a lifespan, a memory that stretches back only a handful of decades, is to be a fleeting, vibrant error. The inhabitants of this cathedral-scorn-of-Solomon are of a different kind. Their lives are measured not in years, but in cycles of digestion, in the slow accretion of experience onto a being that is more geological than biological.

The gardeners are the oldest things you will almost never see. They are not a species, but a caste, a final form. They began as something like the pale, drifting things, but over millennia, they have become sessile, fusing with the architecture they once consumed. Their forms are now inseparable from the plant-structure. A gnarled, column-like growth in the center of a vast chamber is not merely decorative; it is a gardener, its myriad limbs long ago having petrified into load-bearing members, its carapace sublimated into a new, tougher layer of lignin. A section of wall that seems to cascade in frozen, fibrous waves is the fossilized body of a gardener who once tended the flow of water in this corridor. They are the architects now, as much as the plant itself. Their consciousness is diffuse, their thoughts pulsing through the root-mesh of the floor at the speed of sap. They do not think. They *tend*. They feel a slight weakness in a stress-bearing joint and direct, over centuries, the slow, specific growth of reinforcing fibers. They taste a change in the mineral content of the condensation and adjust the permeability of a ceiling-pore acre away. They are the ghost in this machine, a distributed mind whose only ambition is the patient, endless perfecting of the world that is also their body. They do not acknowledge you. To them, you are a momentary flavor in the water, a fleeting vibration in the bark.

The shepherds are the mobile things you might glimpse. They are not predators in the conventional sense. Their purpose is stewardship. They are immense, pale arthropods, their segmented bodies trailing dozens of fine, whip-like antennae that taste the air and the surfaces they pass over. They move with a liquid grace, their multi-jointed legs finding purchase on the vertical walls and ceilings as easily as on the floor. The shepherds are eaters, but they are also pruners. They consume the slow, creeping growth of microbial films, the incipient fungal blooms that might one day threaten the plant’s equilibrium. In doing so, they keep the cathedral clean. They also guide the more mobile, less intelligent inhabitants. A drifting, gas-bladdered leech-thing that wanders too close to a developing structural bud will be gently nudged,ts, steered away by the shepherd’s antennae, its path redirected as calmly as a stone in a stream is parted by a reed. They are the hands of the gardeners, the active agents in this world’s slow maintenance. If one were to perceive you, it would be with a profound and alien disinterest. You are neither plant nor pest; you are a variable outside its equation, and the equation does not need to include you.

Then there is the flock, the myriad pale things that form the true biomass of this world. They are the ecosystem. They are the ones that turn the plant-architecture back into life. They range from the microscopic to the monstrous. The swimmers are tiny, legless torpedoes of opaque white flesh, living in the catchment basins and the film of water on every surface, filtering nutrient-rich spores from the condensation. The crawlers are things like the one you saw on the wall: limbless worms, spaced-out millipedes with legs like hairs, placid, multi-bodied isopods that crunch silently on the bark. They are all recyclers. They eat the walls, the floors, the very substance of their world, and their waste is the precise chemical cocktail required for the next cycle of growth. It is a closed and perfect loop, a self-sustaining sacrament. Their senses are not of light or sound, but of taste and pressure and tremor. They communicate by releasing pheromonal trails into the humid air and by conducting vibrations through the root-floor beneath them. A fallen comrade—not a death, but a new resource—is discovered first by the change in the flavor of the water, then by the specific seismic signature of its ceaseless movement. Within hours, others arrive, not in a frenzy, but with the slow, deliberate purpose of a tide coming in. They do not mourn; they incorporate. They are the digestive system of a world that is also its own food.

But the truest, most profound inhabitant is the one you cannot see, cannot touch, and cannot understand. It is the logic that underpins the place. The geometry that folds in on itself. The physics that allows water to flow upward because the long-defunct gardener who designed this channel determined that it *should*. This is the last and greatest wrongness made manifest as life. This place has a will. Not a consciousness like a gardener’s diffuse awareness, but a deeper, more fundamental will. The plant is the body, the gardeners are the nervous system, and the broken physics is the soul. It is a world that decided, long ago, how it ought to be, and has since spent millions of years convincing reality to agree. It is the ultimate act of coercion, of patient, biological stubbornness. It did not adapt to its environment; it made its environment, and then it became that environment. The light that weeps from the walls, the warmth that emanates from the core, the non-Euclidean corridors—that is the world’s dream made real.

So, what lives here is not a what, but a *how*. How a world sustains itself. How a will shapes matter. How time can be bent like sap in a directed growth. You are a walking, breathing question mark in a place that has long forgotten the question. You are a moment of extravagant, evolutionary inefficiency. The things that live here are the answer: slow, quiet, and utterly, terrifyingly sufficient. They are the bones and the blood of a living god that bleeds light and dreams in botanical code. And they are waiting, not for you to leave, but for you to eventually stop moving, so they can begin the slow, gentle, and welcome process of turning you back into cathedral.
