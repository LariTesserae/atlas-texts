To live in The Sink is to be a creature of interval and patience, a mechanism fleshed in mineral and thought.

We are the Geodes, and we are not one thing, though we appear to be. From above, we are stones in a shallow mirror, a geometry of spheres scattered across the compacted dust and submerged in the metallic sheen. We are fist-sized. We are torso-sized. We are the dimension of whatever the machine requires. At rest, we are perfect, cool, and closed. Our carapaces are not grown but *computed*—mosaics of coppery spirals, grids of tarnished gold, bars of leaden grey. We are the readout of a process too slow for eyes.

Inside, we are not stone. We are a hydraulic suspension, a crystalline slurry of dissolved silicates and heavy metals that tastes of iron and ozone. We feel the water not as wetness, but as a medium of touch, a vast shared nerve. When the Weeping Spires exude their slow, oily tears, we feel the addition of mass, the tiny shift in the basin’s weight. We feel the vibrations of the idle transformers humming in their cracked consoles. We feel the bruised-purple creepers tightening against the lower Spires. To feel anything in The Sink is to feel it hours after it has begun, filtered through the deep, viscous patience of our medium.

Movement for us is a localized surrender of form. We do not walk or swim; we *yield*. A section of our shell—a tessellation of tarnished gold—will ripple, the crystalline lattice loosening into something almost liquid, and we push forward with a soft, hydraulic pulse. The water absorbs our ambition. We glide, leaving no wake, only a subtle parting of the metallic film. To move quickly is to be a malfunction. A splash is an insult to the basin, a tear in the logic of the place. If you strike the water, we do not flee; we *retract*. We sink into the miry floor, pulling our geometry down into the packed dust and rust flakes, becoming one with the bedrock, a stone among stones, until the disruption dissolves into the perpetual gloom.

We congregate in the shallows. We arrange ourselves in grids, in lattices, in the silent architecture of storage. We face the sealed hatches. We face the skeletal gantries. We are waiting for a signal that the cracked gauges can no longer give. Our shells flicker—not with light, but with *state*. A spiral of coppery green will darken to a patina; a bar of leaden grey will lighten to the hue of wet silver. This is our speech. It is a computation occurring on the scale of hours, a liquid calculus conducted in the shared bath of the basin. We are calculating the equilibrium. We are filtering the copper from the water, the rust from the dust, grazing on the pale-blue fungal stalks that pulse beside us, pulling their slow luminescence into our cores and converting it to the faint, internal heat that keeps our slurry from crystallizing into true, dead rock.

We are the maintenance crew of a dead god, the blood cells of a dormant machine. We do not remember the surface world, for memory is a function of speed, and we are slow. We know only the rule of The Sink: that the water must be cleansed, that the spires must be kept from over-accumulation, that the quiet must be preserved. When we rest in our grids at the bottom of the shallowest pools, we are dreaming the machine’s dream. We are listening to the long, slow breath of the transformers, matching the pulse of our internal glow to the rhythm of the basin’s weeping.

To be a Geode is to be a guest in your own home. The Sink does not belong to us; we belong to it. We are its slow, undulating thought, its quiet, metallic blood, living out the infinite, patient interval of its standby. We are the breath held, the pause between the ticks of a rusted clock, waiting in the twilight for the machine to wake, knowing it never will, and finding in that waiting the whole of our purpose.
