The Kelli do not think of themselves as a people. They think of themselves as a pattern. To be a Kelli is to be a node in a network of stone, a conduit for the city's one, unceasing process. Their individuality is expressed not in thought or ambition, but in placement and posture.

Their bodies are extensions of the architecture. The wet-slate skin is not merely protective; it is absorptive. It drinks the constant dampness, and within specialized, subdermal sacs, it begins the first stage of digestion, separating trace minerals from the silica-heavy water before the fluid is even consciously consumed. Their feet are their most defining feature. They are broad, splayed, and prehensile, with toes of unequal length that can grip the smallest irregularity in the rock face. The soles are calloused to a density that rivals petrified wood, grooved not just for grip, but to feel the vibrations of the city with breathtaking nuance. They can feel the slow, geologic groan of a supporting wall a hundred paces away, the frantic cascade of a slurry-jam in a neighboring channel, the percussive greeting of a mate from across a chasm. They feel the city as they feel their own heartbeat.

Their faces are masks of sensory efficiency. The lidless, black orbs are not passive. The iris is a sphincter of dark muscle that can contract to a pinprick, protecting the inner eye from flying grit when hammering stone, or dilate to encompass a vast, monochrome panorama. They see no sky, only layers of stone receding into haze, their depth perception flawless. Below the eyes, their face is a smooth, featureless slope, save for the mouth. The mouth is not for speech, but for consumption. The lips are thick and rubbery, capable of sealing tightly around a stone surface. When they eat the litho-fauna, they do not pluck it. They press their mouths against it, their tongue—a rough, mineralized organ rasp—and powerful jaw muscles scraping the crystalline growth directly from the rock. It is an act that blurs the line between person and place.

The Kelli language, the 'Tap-Speak,' is the city's consciousness. It is not a code, but an art. To a novice, it is a simple knock for 'attention' or a rhythmic rasp for 'danger.' But a master mason can tell a story. They can communicate the precise tensile strength of a new bridge by the resonant frequency it emits when struck in certain patterns. They can compose love poems in the complex, overlapping rhythms of taps sent through the intersecting walls of a shared dwelling—a private conversation that no one else can feel unless they touch the precise spot. There is gossip, carried along the main structural columns of a district, a low-secret hum of information that travels faster than any runner. The elders do not write histories; they compose great, slow-moving 'Resonances,' foundational tap-patterns taught to the young, epic poems that describe the proper shape for a load-bearing arch or the ideal flow-rate for a nutrient channel. History is not a record of events, but a manual of being.

Their lifecycle is the lifecycle of the stone. A 'birth' is not a joyful event, but a logistical one. A pair decides their cell is stable enough to support a third. They carve a new hollow, a niche just large enough for an infant. The child is born small, its skin still soft, its feet gripping reflexively. For its first year, it is strapped to the wall of its niche, fed chewed-paste litho-fauna by its parents. It learns the Tap-Speak not from listening, but from feeling the vibrations through the stone at its back. It learns to climb before it can walk, its world a vertical crib. Coming of age is the first solo crossing of a major chasm bridge, a test of nerve and grip.

To die in Kell is to be recycled. This is the final, and most sacred, act of a citizen. When a Kelli feels the Inward Stillness—the cessation of the(process) that drives their muscles—they make their final climb. They do not seek a high peak, but a place of offering. They find a designated 'Return-Chute,' a smoothly carved channel that leads down into the deepest, fastest-flowing gutters of the city's central nutrient river. They unstrap their tools, take their final position, and with their last ounce of strength, perform the 'Final Tap'—a single, resonant, sustained vibration that announces their passing to their immediate kin. Then, they let go.

Their body, dense with the minerals of a lifetime of consumption, tumbles into the slurry. The helpful onlookers do not mourn; they may tap a rhythm of 'Honor to the Stone.' The flesh is pulped by the abrasive current, the bones that are not dissolved are ground to powder, and all of it—the unique mineral blend of a lifetime—is absorbed, becoming nutrients. This enriched slurry will eventually coat a wall, and on that spot, a new, particularly vibrant growth of litho-fauna will appear. The finest, most nutritious growth is said to bloom in the places where a master mason has been Returned. They are, quite literally, feeding the next generation. The city is a mausoleum where every tombstone is a feast.

Their economy is one of grains and flows. There is no currency, only favor and balance. A channel-digger might spend a week clearing a blockage for a neighbor who controls a ledge with good sun-exposure (a misnomer, as there is no sun, but a place where the ambient light is fractionally brighter). In return, the neighbor will grant the digger's family 'Gleaning Rights' on their wall for a season—to harvest the best litho-fauna. To divert an even small trickle of the main slurry flow to a higher, drier district is an act of immense generosity, celebrated with a 'Resonance' that echoes across entire levels.

High-status Kelli are 'Growers,' who carefully scrape and clean sections of their walls to encourage the litho-fauna to grow in swirling, patterns of different ochres and greys. The most prized designs are those that mimic the chaotic, yet structured, flow of the slurry itself. Their art is an imitation of the city's own body, a tribute to the only process they know: the endless, roaring, precious metabolism of Kell.
