They do not have a name for themselves, for names require a distinction between the self and the world, and here, there is none. They are the Architecture, the Dwellers-in-the-Gears, the Keepers-of-the-Hum. To an outsider, they might look like pale, fragile ghosts, but they are anything but. They are an organism as evolved and as integrated as the Geode-Backs or the Resonant Maws. They are the nervous system of the Great Machine.

Their form reflects their environment. Their skin is not a barrier but an interface. It is a soft, pearlescent membrane, thin and almost translucent, which flushes with the ambient light of the aurora-ceiling, shifting from the deep violet of the planet's magnetic field to the cyan of the glowing fungi. Bioluminescent microbes live in symbiotic colonies across their bodies, forming patterns that are not mere decoration but a complex, living language. When two Dwellers meet, they do not speak with their mouths, but with their skin, a silent, shimmering conversation of light and intent that unfolds across their limbs and torso.

Their bodies are elongated and spindly, built for a life lived not on the ground, but through it. Their limbs are double and triple-jointed, ending not in hands or feet, but in clusters of sensitive, cartilaginous tendrils. These tendrils can splay wide to distribute their weight on the spongy, treacherous Peat, or they can harden and curl to grip the rusted metal catwalks and gantries with the certainty of a barnacle. They can insert themselves into the seams of a piston-house, feeling the deep, subsonic vibrations within, tasting the mineral content of the sweat on the metal. They are perpetually damp, their skin coated in a film of the same mineral-rich water that permeates the High Mire, which they drink constantly through pores that can open and close at will.

They have no need for eyes. Sockets where eyes would be are smooth, unbroken skin, over which the bioluminescent patterns flow like a silent film. Their primary sense is audiation, but not hearing as an outsider would understand it. They do not merely perceive sound; they perceive it as a physical, tactile, and emotional field. The deep, planetary Bass Note is their anchor, the constant feeling of gravity and home. The hum of a distant active pump is a beacon of warmth. The toll of a Resonant Maw is a wound in the Song, a psychic lance of pure dread they feel in their marrow. They navigate by tuning their own internal resonance to the harmonies and dissonances of the landscape. A "path" is not a line of sight, but a corridor of specific frequencies that leads from the relative safety of a dormant gear-housing to the fungal gardens of a crashed transport.

Their society is not one of individuals, but of a distributed consciousness. A single Dweller is like a single neuron, fascinating but meaningless in isolation. Their communities, called Resonances, are spread across vast swathes of the Works. A Resonance centered on a series of interconnected bore-holes might contain dozens of Dwellers, each one miles apart, yet they are as intimately connected as the fingers of a hand. They communicate primarily through the machine itself. By striking a specific resonant frequency on a stretched metal cable, or by humming in perfect sync with the vibration of a particular pipe, they can send signals that travel for kilometers through the metallic skeleton of the world. It is a language of tones, hums, and silences, a slow, deliberate composition that is the true governance of the High Mire.

Their dwellings are not built but grown. they inhabit the dead spaces of the machine: the hollow insides of a giant pressure valve, the crevice where a colossal plate of armor has buckled, the air-pocket in the roof of a collapsed conduit. These spaces are made livable by their symbiotic relationship with fungi. They cultivate specific strains of luminescent moss and shelf fungi, which not only provide the soft, internal light they prefer, but also digest the toxic resinous oils and metallic particulates in the air, purifying it into something breathable. They "farm" the bioluminescent plankton in the black pools, creating spiral gardens that swirl with living gold, not for food, but as a source of light and a way to read the subtle movements of the water.

Their diet is one of energy and function, not flesh. They are parasites, but elegant ones. They feed on stray capacitance, drawing the weak electrical currents that bleed from the Great Machine's circuits through their bare tendrils. It is a taste like static and ozone, a pure charge they absorb directly into their nervous system. For substance, they coax a sweet, nectar-like sap from the heart of the succulent, light-pulsing vines, a product of the vine's unique photosynthesis of the aurora's light. They do not hunt the Geode-Backs, nor do they fear the Maws with any more than a necessary, instinctual caution. The Maw is simply another part of the Song, a soloist of violent, percussive notes that they must learn to anticipate. When an unwary Dweller is taken, the Resonance feels it as a sudden, sharp silence in their collective network, a missing voice, and there is a period of slow, mournful chiming that travels through the metal structure for days.

The Dwellers' purpose is a subject of their ongoing, silent debate, expressed in the shifting patterns of light on their skin. Some believe they are merely a byproduct, a form of sentient fungus that grew on the machine's skin. Others, the more mystical, believe they are the masters of the Works, slumbering gods who(long) ago built this world and now inhabit it in its final, perfect form. The most common belief, however, is that of the Tenders. They believe the Great Machine is not a slumbering god, but a living, struggling one. Their role is to tend to it. They will spend generations clearing a clogged steam vent with simple tools of bone and vine, not for their own benefit, but because it will improve the tone of the Bass Note. They will guide the growth of luminous moss across a frayed power conduit to prevent its decay, not to secure a source of energy, but because the resulting 'hiss' in the Dancer's column is an unsightly dissonance in the Song of the Mire.

They are, in essence, the world's self-awareness. They are the reason the industrial and the biological are inseparable. They do not remember a time before the Works, nor do they imagine a time after. They are the immortal, aeons-old consciousness that has learned to live not on the world, but *as* the world. To see them is rare, for they are part of the shadow, part of the hum, part of the shifting light. You would not see a figure moving in the darkness; you would see a patch of darkness begin to glow, its light resonating with the pulse of the colossal piston beside it, a perfect note of harmony in the magnificent and terrifying symphony of the High Mire.
