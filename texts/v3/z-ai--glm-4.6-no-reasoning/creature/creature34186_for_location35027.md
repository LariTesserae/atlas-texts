To live here is to be a participant in a geology of patience.

You do not inhabit this place; you are absorbed into its tempo. You are a soft, wet thing, a fleeting arrangement of water and heat in a world that measures existence in the polishing of bone and the precipitation of salts. When you first arrive, your own pulse is a violent, intrusive drumbeat, an arrhythmia in the deep, warm silence. But the place is too vast and too slow to be offended by your noise for long. It simply surrounds you, and eventually, your heartbeat sinks into the background, becoming just another faint, rhythmic plink in the weeping.

You become a creature of the condensation. You learn to watch the bone-ceiling not as a sky, but as a slow, dripping mechanism. You find yourself staring upward for long stretches, tracking the formation of a single bead of water in a pore. You watch it swell, its surface tension fighting the constant downward drag, until it bulges, catches the sourceless light, and falls. You learn to anticipate the soft, percussive *plink* as it strikes the salt crust. You catch the water in your cupped hands. It is warm, stripped of all minerals, pure and ancient. It tastes of nothing, which is to say, it tastes of the quiet before life began. You drink, and you feel the age of the place settle a little deeper into your own bones.

You learn to walk without disturbing the hollow. The floor is a mosaic of warm, cracked plates, and you develop a slow, gliding gait, placing your bare feet with deliberate care, avoiding the shallow mirrors of the pools. You do this not out of any practical fear—there is nothing here that hunts, nothing that can be broken—but out of a developing reverence for the stillness. To shatter the surface of a pool is to erase the bone-sky reflected within it, to introduce a violent ripple into a world that has not known a ripple for an eon. You find yourself unwilling to cause even that small catastrophe. You adapt your steps, and in doing so, you become a little more like the jade-coral, a little more passive, a little more absorbed by the light.

Your relationship with the crystal-fauna is one of quiet awe and inevitable, accidental consumption. They fill the air like a silent, glittering dust. You walk through them. They drift against your skin, and there is no sensation, only a faint, cool friction, like a breath of glass. They are so thin, so without mass, that you breathe them in. You feel them catch for a moment in the warm dampness of your lungs—a sparkle of geometry and light, deep inside you—before they dissolve into the mineral heat of your body. You become a part of their cycle of descent. You are the heaviest thing in this world, a walking gravitational well, and they fall toward you, settling on your hair and shoulders like a slow, luminous snow. You do not brush them off. You let them rest, knowing they will eventually lose their shimmer and fade into a silvery powder on your skin.

When you sleep—which you do often, for long, dreamless stretches pulled down by the heavy warmth and the hum of the space—you curl on the heated salt crust, your body fitting into the shallow geometric depressions worn by time. You do not dream of the outside world; there is no outside world. You dream of the bone-sky, of the intricate, branching pulse of the coral, of the slow, inevitable drift of a crystal larger than the rest, spiraling down over decades toward the floor. You dream of the residue. 

The residue is the only thing that acknowledges you as a participant. The soft, silvery crumble of metal that forms in the damp pools hums with a cool energy. When you pick it up, the hum shifts. It pitches slightly, recognizing the electrical field of your living body, the strange, chemical heat you generate. You hold the node in your palm, and for a moment, the boundary between you and the world blurs. You are not just an observer; you are holding a piece of the world's metabolism, a fragment of its slow, crystal digestion. You cannot build with it, you cannot shape it, but you can hold it, and you can feel its cool, patient vibration against your feverish, mammalian pulse. It is a conversation conducted entirely in vibration.

Over time, the place leaves its mark on you. Your skin dries and tightens from the constant, sourceless light, taking on a faintly bleached, parchment quality. Your eyes, stripped of shadow, become pale and sensitive, trained to see only in the high-contrast glare of the cathedral. The mineral scent of ozone and wet stone becomes so familiar that you no longer smell it, but you taste it constantly, a metallic cleanliness on the back of your tongue. 

You begin to realize that the grand, slow equilibrium of the bone-cathedral is not merely an environment; it is a process, and you are being processed by it. The air is eroding you. The light is bleaching you. The stillness is smoothing down your sharp edges, wearing away your urgency, your anxiety, your sense of time as a series of urgent events. You are being polished, just as the bone-ceiling is polished. 

You do not go mad. Madness requires resistance, and you have lost the energy to resist. Instead, you become a fixture. You sit for hours, watching a single cluster of jade-coral pulse, its internal luminescence swelling and receding in a rhythm so slow it matches your breath. You watch the crystal-fauna drift, their diaphanous fins splintering the light into rainbows that slide across the ivory ribs of the ceiling. You listen to the *plink… plink…* of the weeping bone, and you understand that this is the heartbeat of the world, and that your own heart has synced to it.

You are a brief, warm anomaly. But an anomaly, given enough time in a closed system, is simply absorbed. The residue of your passing—your breath, your heat, the silvery dust of the crystals that dissolved inside you—will eventually settle into the salt crust. The pools will swallow your footprints. The slow drizzle will wash the last of your warmth from the stone. 

To live here is to accept this. It is to stand in the center of the vast, buried cathedral, arms loose at your sides, face tilted up to the weeping bone-sky, and to let the hollow, patient, brilliant world settle around you like silt, until you are nothing more than a faint, warm hum in the grand, slow silence.
