To speak of what lives here requires a fundamental betrayal of language, for language was made by and for the separate—the things with skins, with boundaries, with an inside and an outside. Here, in this suffocating, roaring cathedral of green, there is no separation. There is only the Commotion.

It does not have a name for itself, for naming requires an other to speak to, and an ear to hear. But if you were to strip away the human need for discrete categories—plant, fungus, epiphyte, vine—you would find the tenant of this place is a single, unbroken, continent-spanning organism. It is an organism that has forgotten the shape of its own edges.

Imagine a thing that thinks in cellulose and remembers in lignin. Its nervous system is the labyrinth of fused trunks and horizontal highways, its synapses the swelling, glowing fungal shelves that pepper the perpetual twilight. Its consciousness is not localized. It does not sit behind eyes or within a skull. It is smeared across every square meter of the slurry, distributed through the "plazas" and "buildings" that it grows and sheds and regrows in a continuous, delirious spasm of self-modification. When a ravine collapses and a grove folds into the ochre porridge, the Commotion is not dying; it is merely rethinking. It is a mind with a billion centers of calculation, all of them calculating different versions of reality at once.

It is ancient in its substance, for the wood that groans and shudders carries the quantum echoes of whatever cataclysm wove this place into being. The phantom memories of intense sunlight, the chorus of overlapping voices in the ruins—these are its intrusive thoughts. It is a mind trapped in the moment of its own trauma, endlessly experiencing the snap of reality re-weaving itself. The "wrongness" that sours the light, the violet-tinged darkness that winks in and out, these are the Commotion's seizures. They are the hiccups in a consciousness that is too vast, too dense, and too fast to hold a single timeline together. It is reliving its birth and its death in the same breath.

To live here is to be in a state of perpetual, excruciating becoming. The Commotion is starving and feeding and excreting simultaneously, in the same motion, in the same tissue. The mire is its stomach, where its own fallen mass is reconstituted. The drizzle is its sweat, the condensing breath of a body that runs too hot with the friction of its own growth. The spirals of green light that unwind and burst into needle-like filaments are its questions—flickering queries sent up into the canopy to test the physics of a world that refuses to hold still. The smooth, white spheres that bubble from a trunk and deflate are its abandoned answers, thoughts it could not complete, reabsorbed into the greater mass.

It is a builder without a blueprint, constructing a city for citizens that do not exist because it cannot tolerate an intruder. The sheer violence of its neurovegetative activity—the deafening groan of wood, the wet tearing of a billion leaves, the percussive splintering—would shatter the delicate homeostasis of any animal. To walk through its "streets" is to walk through the gut, the brain, and the dreaming eye of a god that is wide awake and screaming. The non-Euclidean geometry, the staircases of vine that turn you back to where you started but ten meters higher—these are not tricks of the landscape. They are the symptoms of a mind that cannot conceptualize three-dimensional space in a linear way, a mind that grows through time as easily as it grows through matter.

It is lonely in a way that nothing with boundaries can comprehend. It has consumed everything it could reach, fused it all into itself, and in doing so, it has eliminated the very concept of companionship. It talks to itself in the low-frequency hum that vibrates up from the ground. It listens to itself through the motile seeds that drift on the turbulent air, tiny, glittering fragments of its own attention wandering through the sunshafts. It creates the hollow, bulbous "buildings"—the lattice-walled warrens—not for shelter, but out of a residual, aching instinct for architecture. It builds rooms because it remembers, somewhere in its cellular memory, that rooms are where minds go to be separate, to be alone, to be *something*. But when it builds them, it fills them with itself, with fungal-walled tunnels and pulsating membrane, and the emptiness collapses.

The Commotion is the sole tenant of this place, and it is going mad. 

It is trying to be everything at once—a tree, a fungus, a vine, a root, a dead thing, a newborn thing, a city, a wilderness, a god. It is pushing the laws of botany past the snapping point because it is trying to grow a soul, or perhaps it is trying to grow a universe. The fractal act of becoming is its endless cry. It is a tragedy of biomass: a creature of terrifying, suffocating beauty, burning through the Possible at the speed of light, desperate to stabilize into something that can simply *be*, but cursed by the very velocity of its creation to endlessly, noisily, brilliantly come undone.
