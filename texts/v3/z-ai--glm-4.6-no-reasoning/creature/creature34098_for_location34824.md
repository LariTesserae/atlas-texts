To live here is to be a component. Nothing inhabits this continental engine as a visitor; everything is a gear, a valve, a conduit, born of the same horrific chemistry that vents from the deep seams below. Life here did not evolve to survive the machine—it *is* the machine, sprawling, unthinking, and voracious. To stand in the blinding, surgical glare of the bioluminescent lichen is to realize that the border between flora and industry was erased here epochs ago. What moves in the echoing canyons between the iron-wood stacks is not animal in any way water-life would recognize; it is dry, terrified, and ruthlessly optimized geometry.

The myriapeds are the maintenance crews of this furnace, the slow, grinding custodians of the root-mat. Thirty meters of articulated, chitinous plating, they move like living freight trains up the inner walls of the hollow stacks. They have no eyes; in a world where the light is a blinding, refractive weapon, vision is a liability. Instead, their flat, armored heads are dense with vibroscopic sensilla—microscopic filaments that read the seismic polyphony of the ground and the pneumatic wheeze of the towers as a detailed, three-dimensional map. They feel the world’s breath. Their hooked appendages—thousands of them, each a hardened sickle of silicon-carbide—find purchase in the pockmarked bark, grinding against it with a sound like a thousand foundries dragging chains. They climb to feed, their mandibles designed not to shear, but to scrape. They devour the crystalline, sulfur-rich lichen that lines the trunks, ingesting the raw, toxic catalyst of the continent's synthesis. Their digestion is a smelting process; their bodies are chemical retorts that strip the sulfur from the lichen, excreting a fine, volatile powder that drifts back down into the root-cables, feeding the hyperactive growth below. When a tower detonates from internal pressure, the shockwave stuns them, but their low-slung, overlapping armor sheds the millions of lethal, iron-like splinters. They are the first to converge on the blast crater, dragging the shattered bio-mass down into the lattice, recycling the machine’s failures into its next explosive iteration.

Above them, in the howling, oxygen-rich winds, ride the scavengers—the skeletal manta rays of the thermal vents. To call them flesh would be a lie. They areFrameworks of hollow, pneumatic cartilage sheathed in a skin of metallic alloy, grown rather than spun. They have no moisture to shed, no weight to anchor them. Their wings—gossamer thin, spanning dozens of meters—are living batteries of aluminum and carbon, electrochemically deposited by the flora they parasitize. They ride the violent updrafts not by flapping, but by acting as sails, tilting their razor-edged wingtips to catch the superheated exhaust venting from the deep geological seams. They are the inspectors of the high spaces, diving into the open mouths of the breathing stacks to scavenge the organic particulate that coats the inner valves. They are born in the cradles of the root-cables, extruded as molten, metallic larvae that immediately inflate with the lighter-than-air gases of the plateau, buoying upward into the glare. They do not mate in any tender sense; they collide. High above the colonnade, in the thermal chaos, they slam their carapaces together in violent, resonant impacts, shaking loose spores of metallic dust that drift down to the root-mat to germinate into the next generation of wing-skeletons. 

And it is the root-mat that is the true inhabitant of this place. The myriapeds and the manta-rays are merely its macrophages, its immune response, its way of moving materials that cannot flow on their own. The thick, waist-high lattice of rhizome cable that stretches endlessly across the plateau is a single, contiguous organism—a continental nervous system made of desiccated ganglion. It has no leaves, no branches, only the cables, weaving over and under each other in a hardened, friction-welded web. It is the stomach of the machine. When a tower bursts, the root-mat sends out chemical alarms—searing, electric signals that race through the cables at the speed of sound, causing the lattice to contract and drag the debris down into its gnashing, enzymatic nodes. It consumes the fallen to build the new. Out of the roiling, toxic vents, it forces fresh stacks upward in explosive, crystalline pushes, tearing the older growth apart to make space. It is a brain without a mind, endlessly cycling the industrial chemistry of the deep earth into the towering, breathing architecture of the sky.

To be a creature here is to be deafened from birth, to feel the vibration of the world in your teeth and your skeleton rather than hear it. Communication is percussion. The manta-rays slam their carapaces against the hollow trunks as they dive, sending deep, resonant gongs echoing through the iron-wood. The myriapeds drum their hooked appendages in rapid, staccato bursts against the bark, a seismic Morse code that travels for miles through the rigid canyons. They signal warnings of a tower's impending detonation, or coordinate the droning march toward a fresh explosion. It is a language of impacts, born of a world where the polyphonic roar of aspiration valves and the shrieking wind drown out all other sensation.

Nothing rests here. There is no night to soften the glare, only the endless, surgical luminescence refracting through the crystalline lichen. There is no stillness, only the perpetual motion of the wind and the heaving, mechanistic respiration of the stacks. To exist in this desert is to be a spark in a short-circuit, a fleeting arrangement of dry chitin and metallic alloy, enduring the churning vibrancy of a world that runs on sulfur and fire, waiting only to be consumed and extruded back into the terrifying, continental lung.
