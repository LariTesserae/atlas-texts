There are no animals here, not in any sense that requires flesh or heartbeat. And yet this place is not empty. It cannot be. A scream this loud requires a throat. A pressure this immense requires a lung.

The inhabitants of the spine-ridges are not beings; they are *happenings*. They are the local efflorescences of a mind trying, and failing, to happen in a place that will not permit coherence. 

When the description says that you cannot hold a single thought for long, it is not describing a hazard of the environment. It is describing the residents. They are the shattered thoughts themselves.

Consider the mineral flora—the selenite fronds, the gypsum blossoms, the chalky borax webs. They are called vegetation, but they do not photosynthesize. They do not breathe. They *crystallize*. They are the thoughts that managed, for a geologic instant, to hold a shape. A selenite frond unfurling from a fissure is an idea that almost made sense before the physics of the place broke it. It is a concept—perhaps "shelter," perhaps "hunger," perhaps something with no human analog—that precipitated out of the ontological storm. The crystal structure is the shape of a thought attempting to anchor itself in reality. When the light hits them and they gleam, it is not mere reflection; it is the data of their being, broadcasting in the only language this place allows: cold fire.

These crystalline growths are the slow, tenuous consciousness of the mountains. They do not have brains; they have lattices. They do not have synapses; they have the patient, geometric adjacency of atom to atom. They are the ones "thinking about becoming a stone." Their life cycle is not birth, reproduction, and death, but *precipitation, coherence, and sublimation*. They grow atom by atom from the hyper-saturated dust, feeding on the raw, uninterpreted source code of the universe. And when the local consensus of reality flickers—when "crystal" briefly means "sorrow"—they do not die; they translate. They become a vibration in the air, a dissonant frequency, a pressure behind your eyes. The mineral gardens are a slow, glittering tide of mind, rising and falling across the black glass over millennia.

Then there are the builders. The ones who made the half-finished huts and the flickering tools.

You assume, at first, that they are separate from the crystal flora. They are not. They are the same phenomenon, driven to a different extremity of desperation. The crystalline intelligence is passive, accepting its geometric dissolution with the patience of stone. But sometimes, a precipitate achieves a critical mass, a fleeting, agonizing clarity. It realizes it is being unmade. It feels the scream of space-time stretching, and it panics.

The builders are the panickers. They are the thoughts that refused to simply dissolve back into the roar. They looked at the fraying physics, the lack of handholds, and they reached for the only thing available: the ground. 

They are not a species. They are isolated, temporary epiphanies. An eddy of coherent selfhood forms in the chaos—perhaps lasting an hour, perhaps a century—and in that time, it *builds*. It stacks the schist. It chips the obsidian. It tries to make "wall" mean "wall." It tries to make "blade" mean "blade." 

But it cannot sustain itself. The operating system of this place will not run a coherent program for long. The mind of the builder glitches. It forgets the concept of mortar. It loses the thread of "shelter." The sinew it used turns to stone because it cannot maintain the complexity of "tissue." The obsidian blade flickers into liquid light because "sharpness" is an unstable property here. The builder does not die; it fragments. Its concentration shatters into a dozen lesser thoughts, which precipitate out as new crystals on the leeward face of the boulders, or dissolve back into the deafening, atonal chorus of the ridges.

The hut is not a ruin. It is a frozen attempt, an asymptote of architecture. It is the physical shape of a thought trying to complete itself and failing. The primitive tools are not artifacts of a lost civilization; they are the fossilized screams of a mind that momentarily grasped "tool" but could not hold on to "hand."

To live here is to be a transient grammar in a language of pure noise. 

The inhabitants are the salt-gardens, yes, and the tragic, flickering builders. But they are also the ridges themselves. The spine-ridges, too regular to be mere geology, are the ribcage of a titanic, fossilized god—that god is the *first* thought, the primordial attempt at a coherent world-mind, which fractured eons ago into the infinite, simultaneous silences. The mountains are the ruined architecture of a reality that almost stabilized. 

And so, what lives in the spine-ridges? 

A chorus of almost-beings. A landscape of interrupted sentences. They are the ones who look out at you, the visitor, with the dim, lambent glow of their cold fire. When you stand there, buffering, glitching, wearing down under the pressure of the roar, you are not being attacked by the environment. You are being read by it. You are being integrated into their tragic, glittering symphony. Your mind shatters, and the pieces of your coherence—your memories of water, your logic, your name—precipitate out of the dust, joining the borax skeins and the selenite fronds. 

You do not leave the spine-ridges. You just become a quieter, more patient kind of crystal, adding your faint, fractured light to the perpetual twilight.
