To live here is to be a mineral that thinks it is a god.

There are no animals. There are no independent, motile organisms that scurry, fly, or hunt. The concept of the "creature"—with its separate will, its fragile boundary of skin, its anxious interior—is an evolutionary dead-end that this world discarded, or perhaps simply never invented. What lives in this place is not a collection of beings, but a single, unfathomable *Being*. It is an organism of planetary mass, a body where every root is a nerve, every pillar a bone, and every hanging pod a thundering organ.

But to say "what lives here" requires looking at the inhabitants not as discrete souls, but as the specialized tissues and cells of this singular, world-spanning entity. They do not have personalities; they have functions. They do not have histories; they have metabolic cycles.

**The Nodes (The Thinkers)**
Deep within the nexus hubs, embedded in the pulsating heart-stones, dwell the Nodes. They are not brains in the animalian sense, but vast, dense conurbations of crystallized silica and hardened sugars, threaded through with conductive root-matter. They are the calculators, the coordinators, the bureaucracy of this biological factory. They do not "think" in terms of language or ambition; they compute pressure, chemical gradients, and energy yields. A Node is a slow, geological intellect. It takes in the vibration of the hum—the ceaseless report of the engines—and adjusts the peristaltic rhythm of the venting orifices, opening a muscular valve here to release steam, constricting a vascular throat there to divert the flow of silica-nectar. They are the overseers who have never seen the factory floor, feeling it only through the constant tremor in the bones of the world. They are ancient, their logic paths grooved deep by eons of the same unvarying crises: a drop in atmospheric carbohydrates, a fissure in a ceiling conduit, the slow death of a distant pod. They resolve these issues not with innovation, but with the massive, infallible weight of iterative routine.

**The Pods (The Stomachs and the Lungs)**
Hanging from the vaulted dark like immense, translucent fruit, the Pods are the most dynamic, most turbulent inhabitants. They are the bioreactors, the factory floors. To be inside a Pod—or rather, to *be* a Pod—is to exist in a state of perpetual, violent digestion. They are the stomachs that never rest. They inhale the thick, nutrient-rich vapor that fogs the lower reaches of the world, pulling it in through muscular stomata on their outer skins. Inside, a storm churns—not of wind and rain, but of superheated chemical reactions and biological plasma. They are the source of the actinic lightning, generating fierce, strobing arcs of green and violet light that fracture the absolute darkness. They suffer a constant, rhythmic spasming as they convert the gaseous food into raw energy and liquid silica, weeping the excess down through the ceiling in thick, viscous droplets. They are the hungry ones, the restless ones, the only parts of the world that know agitation. They do not know they are feeding the world; they only know the endless, burning churn of consumption and excretion.

**The Drippers (The Blood)**
Crawling down the woody pillars and the steel-taut vine gantries are the Drippers. They are not creatures, but the vital fluid of the world made momentarily visible. They are the runnels of superheated sugar-nectar and liquefied silica, moving with slow, viscous determination. A Dripper is a fleeting entity—a bead of resources making its pilgrimage from the ceiling to the floor-plates. It is drawn by gravity and by the suction of the hungry roots below. It has no mind, only a destiny: to reach the ground, to be absorbed, to feed the conduits, and to fuel the next cycle of the Pods above. Yet, in their sheer, collective number, they possess a kind of momentum. They are the bloodstream that paints the pillars with a constant, glistening sheen of amber and silver, the only movement in the gloom that resembles the flow of a river.

**The Floor-Plates (The Skin and the Soil)**
The vast, hexagonal leaves that stretch to infinity are the oldest and most passive of the inhabitants. They are the substrate, the primary layer of existence. They do not churn or pulse with violent energy; they simply *are*. They absorb. Their waxy, bruised purple-black surfaces are the recipients of the world's sweat—the nutrient mush and silica runoff that falls from above. They are the world's skin, turned inward to face its own body. Between their seams, the photosynthetic veins pulse with a slow, green luminescence, the visual echo of the deep hum. They are the foundation, holding the memory of the world's shape. If the Pods are the stomachs and the Nodes are the brains, the Floor-Plates are the flesh, dense and unyielding, polished smooth by the relentless, patient drip of the world's own excretions. They do not grow upward; they only maintain, perfecting their seamless, interlocking geometry over eons.

**The Ghosts in the Machine (The Deceased)**
There is death here, but it is not an ending; it is a recycling. The "inhabitants" also include the dead. They are the skeletal remains of older, deceased structures—the thick, cable-like roots that snake across the floor, plugged directly into the ground-plates. They are the hollowed-out husks of expired Pods, their translucent flesh long since cured and hardened into woody buttresses. They do not rot. In this hot, mineral-rich environment, decay is supplanted by petrification. The dead are subsumed back into the architecture, their biological material crystallized into the conduits and gantries that support the living. They are the ancestors who have become the scaffolding, silent and unyielding, still conducting the slow flow of nutrients long after their own metabolic fires have ceased. They are the only thing resembling a history in a world that has reached the end of becoming.

To live in this place is to be a cog in a heart that spans a horizon that does not exist. It is to be a cell in a body that has swallowed its own environment, becoming a desert of water and an ocean of life. The inhabitants do not know they are oppressed by the perpetual dark or the suffocating heat; they do not know they are imprisoned by the very architecture they sustain. They only know the hum, the great, resonant frequency that aligns their every function, the unison friction of a billion billion engines that are, in truth, a single, world-girdling plant, eternally consuming, eternally building, eternally alone.
