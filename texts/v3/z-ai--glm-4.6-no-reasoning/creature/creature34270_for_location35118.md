To live here is to be a semi-conducting element in a geological circuit. The residents of this place—are they a people, or the slowest-moving parts of the machine?—have skin the color and texture of old, wet ash. They are porous. Over a lifetime, the ionic mist works its way into them, mineralizing the soft tissues until a skeleton of micro-crystalline grit forms beneath the skin. They do not bend at the waist as we do; they fold, their joints moving with the grinding patience of tectonic plates. When an elder straightens from a crouch, you can hear the faint, granular click of Obsidian-Grit dust settling within their own cartilage. 

They are thin, but not fragile. They possess a tensile strength, like fiberglass. Their proportions are compact, suited to the windowless labyrinth and the low-slung archways. Their shoulders are narrow, conditioned by generations of brushing against the worn ceramic walls. They move without lifting their feet—sliding, shuffling, soles flat against the glassy blackness to ground themselves. To step high is to risk the static discharge that arcs between the cracks on humid nights; to walk with a leather sole is to invite the hum into the marrow. They wear garments of dense, untreated wool, perpetually heavy, perpetually dripping. The wet wool clings to their contours, acting as a dampener, a faraday cage of sorts, absorbing the electrical tang of the settlement and keeping the worst of the charge from pooling in their own bodies.

They do not speak. They have no need to. The constant *plink... plink... plink...* of water is the village’s conversation, and they have learned to read its syntax. Instead, they communicate through the pressure of their hands against the shared ceramic walls. This is the origin of the worn-smooth shoulders and the spiraling doorframe carvings: they are not merely decorative, but tactile-phonetic. A palm pressed flat against the humming wall transmits a low-frequency pulse; a finger drawn through the water beading on the stone creates a subtle disruption in the ionic flow. They feel these vibrations in their teeth and their mineralized bones. A household in the morning is a quiet place, its inhabitants resting their palms against the shared dividers, listening to the slow, electric heartbeat of the ground beneath them.

Their eyes are the most alien thing about them. They are not organs of sight, but organic capacitors. Set deep beneath heavy, scarred lids, they are not black or brown, but a dull, opaque grey, like unpolished slate. They do not register light in the human sense. When a Skitter-Lamp darts past, leaving a trail of pale green-blue luminescence, these eyes do not "see" the color; rather, the electromagnetic field of the creature induces a slight, resonant charge in the residents' optic nerves. They perceive the Skitter-Lamps as a feeling—a warmth, a pressure, a sudden, brief clarity of thought. Darkness is not a blindness to them; it is a baseline hum. To see is to feel the circuit brighten.

Their lives are orchestrated by the weeping of the walls. They sleep in the deepest, most interconnected chambers during the day, where the hum is a muted, comforting throb. At twilight, they emerge, sliding silently into the narrow alleys to harvest the Mist-Marrow. They do not pluck it. They kneel beside the hairline cracks, their wet wool dripping onto the ceramic, and they breathe slowly onto the translucent pearl-nodules. The condensation from their breath joins the fog, and the Mist-Marrow opens, exuding its sharp, clean taste. They draw it onto their tongues. It does not nourish them with calories or matter; it nourishes them with phase-state. The Marrow's cold, structured moisture aligns the crystalline lattice in their own bodies, easing the friction of their mineralized joints, bringing temporary clarity to the fog of constant electromagnetic sensory input. 

They are deeply afraid of the silence that precedes the great downpours. When the Skitter-Lamps retreat into the cracks and go dark, the residents retreat to the highest terraces, wrapping themselves in layer after layer of damp wool, grounding themselves against the central pillars. They press their ears to the Obsidian-Grit and wait, their grey eyes wide and unseeing, feeling the terrible, charged vacuum building in the stones. 

And when the sky boils and the furious rain strikes the ceramic, completing the vast, planetary circuit—this is their exultation. The hum becomes a roar, a vibration that shakes the spit from their lips and the hair from their heads. In this moment, they are not flesh; they are lightning rods, they are filaments. A faint, bioluminescent blush travels across their skin, mirroring the Skitter-Lamps, an echo of the current surging through the architecture. They stand in the boiling rain, teeth bared in silent, electric laughter, their palms flat against the weeping, thrumming walls, singing back to the circuit in the only language they know: the language of pressure, and resonance, and the slow, grinding patience of stone.
