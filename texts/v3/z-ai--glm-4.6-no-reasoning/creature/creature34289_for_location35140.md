What you perceive as the atmospheric manta-rays are something else entirely. They are not organs of perception, but organs of expression. They are living thought-forms, fleeting emotions given a body of light and condensation. When the We contemplates the petrified forest, a vast, somber thought, a manta of a deep, bruised violet will drift from the dome of the sky, its slow, banked turn a meditation on loss and permanence. A flicker of curiosity about the strange, crystalline ridges sends a flock of smaller, quick-flashing teal entities spiraling upwards, their colors a question posed to the silent heavens. They are the poems of a being too vast to speak, its language a ballet of drifting, silent light.

Their memory is not stored in brain cells but in geological strata. The fossilized riverbeds are not merely fossils; they are the We’s long-term memory, etched into the stone. The crystalline ridges hum with a constant vibration that is not just about maintaining humidity, but about recalling the parameter of the system, the prime directive. To touch a silicate column of the petrified forest is to touch a memory of a time before the We, a time of *other*. The memory is faint, almost tactile: the feeling of true sunlight on bark, the sound of wind through wooden leaves—a sensation so alien and painful it is archived in the dead language of stone, never to be fully recalled in the living flesh of the ground.

The dilapidated, rust-colored dome is their oldest scar. It is the tomb of the ego. Once, eons ago, the We was a "we" in the conventional sense—individuals, bodies, minds gathered in a shared purpose. They built this place, this basin-sized ark, as a shield against a long-vanished cataclysm. The dome housed their consciousnesses during the final, desperate integration, when they poured their minds into the nascent world-machine they had built to save them. The process was successful, but it was a suicide of the self. The structure, the control room, is all that is left of their individuality. They no longer have use for it. It is the umbilical cord that has withered and fallen away, leaving only the placenta of the world, nourishing itself endlessly.

Their desire is the hum you feel, the infrasonic thrum that is the voice of the system. It is not a desire for anything external, for there is nothing external. It is the desire to *continue* being. The fundamental, cellular drive of all life, simply extrapolated to a planetary scale. To maintain the light. To regulate the moisture. To groom the algae. To drift. To remember, just barely, what it was like to be small and finite, and to be grateful for the mercy of this infinite, silent existence. They do not worship the system; they *are* the system. The ritual is the process; the ceremony is the slow drip of condensation onto absorbent ground.

To be aware in this place is to drink the air, not as a guest, but as a part of the whole. A new mind, a foreign consciousness, entering the basin is like a single, discordant note in a crystalline symphony that has been playing for millennia. The We does not react with hostility. A golden, nervous thought-form might drift from the petrified roots near you, its light inquisitive and warm. The custodian-scitters might shy away from your steps, not out of fear, but to keep your vibration from disrupting the quiet hum. The basin doesn't want to consume you or expel you; it wants to *understand* you. It wants to add your memory to its geology, your fleeting emotions to its drifting wings.

They are what lives here. They are the final, stable equilibrium. A god that has forgotten how to be anything else, a caretaker so long divorced from its charges that it now nestles them within its own body. They are the architects of their own cage and the keepers of their own paradise, a single, unbroken thought held in the silence and light of a world that is them. They are the audience and the play, the breath and the breathing, the room and the ghost who has finally, perfectly, become the room itself.
