To live here is to be a descendant of abandonment, an heir to a purpose you cannot fathom. The makers—whoever they were, whatever they forged in those sealed furnace-cathedrals—left no instructions, no myths, no names. They left only the infrastructure. And so, what lives in this place is not a community, not a society, but an ecology of persistence. Life here has learned to be industrial, to be mineral, to be wet and hot and patient.

The inhabitants are shaped by the physics of weeping. Every surface here is either sweating or drinking, and so the residents fall into these two categories: the perspiring architecture and the absorbing fauna. There are no dry things. Even the dust has been rendered down into a fine, clinging mud of rust and ceramic powder that coats every gantry in a thin, ruddy slip. To move through the city is to hear your own footsteps—muffled, damp, slightly metallic—echoing against the hollow ribs of the structure. You are always aware of the drop beneath you, the kilometers of empty, humid space that yawns below the catwalks. Vertigo here is not a fear of falling, but a fear of the long, slow sink—of becoming just another droplet tracing its way down to the Sump.

The Scuttlers are the most visible of the mobile creatures, and they are not truly alive in any way the makers would have recognized. They are autonomic scrap given legs and a vague, tick-like intelligence. They are the custodians of the vertical spaces. A Scuttler is a low-slung thing, the size of a terrier but with the leg-count of a crab, and it moves with the clattering, jittery grace of a wind-up toy that has been wound too tight. Its body is not flesh but a concretion of interlocking ceramic plates—salvaged or grown, it is impossible to tell—which overlap like the scales of a pangolin made of teacups. They are the color of the city: rust-orange, bruised violet, chalky white, and the oxidized turquoise of the stromatolites. 

Their legs end in tiny, hard hooks of some dark, non-ferrous metal, and this is how they defy gravity. They crawl along the undersides of pipes, skitter down the faces of damp, weeping walls, and hang from gantries by a single leg, swaying slightly, their bodies vibrating with the THUM-HUMMM of the Geotherms. They do not eat in any recognizable way. They position themselves over the flowing rivulets—the streams of condensation that trace their frantic calligraphy down the city’s facades—and they let the water run over them. The water is rich with leached metals, trace sulfur, and the faint, tingly sizzle of static that the air conducts. The Scuttlers absorb this. Their ceramic plates are porous, and over time, the minerals build up in their joints and seams, forcing them to grow, to bud new plates, to split and refold. They are fed by the city’s sweat, and when they die—when their joints lock with mineral accretions or a particularly strong static discharge shorts out whatever rudimentary spark drives them—they simply stop. They become part of the structure. A dead Scuttler looks no different than a corroded bracket or a cluster of crystalline fungi; it is just another piece of the city’s ever-growing crust.

And then there are the things in the Sump. No one sees them, not really, but their presence is felt in the deep, hot drafts that sometimes rise from the sightless chasm, carrying the smell of warm metal and biological rot. They are the sink’s digestion. The probes that go down and do not return tell their stories in the broken telemetry of their final moments: a sudden pressure, a flash of bioluminescence—blues and sickly greens—and then silence. The Sump-dwellers are the recyclers. They are vast, slow, and soft, adapted to the warm, shallow lakes and the darkness. They have no eyes. They are membranes and filaments, filtering the constant rain of debris—Scuttler shells, flakes of rust, asbestos sheathing, dead microbes—out of the hot water. They break it all down. They are the stomach of the city, and they are patient. They do not hunt, because hunting implies urgency, and there is no urgency in the Sump. Everything comes to them eventually, pulled by earnest gravity, by the unseen sink. They simply wait, blooming in the dark like vast, blind anemones in a scalding sea.

The true inhabitants, though, are the silent farmers—the stromatolites and crystalline fungi. They are the city’s new skin. They do not move; they endure. The stromatolites grow in the flooded sump pits and along the ledges where water pools. They are layered, knobby things, colored in oxidized scabs of turquoise and copper-green, their surfaces slick and weeping. They are living rock, slowly weaving base matter—iron, carbon, water—into complex, beautiful patterns. And the fungi—pure, needle-thin quartz that sprouts from the mortar between bricks, and chalky white accretions that bloom like frost over sealed doors. They are the city’s dreams, crystallizing out of the damp, electric air. If you stand very still in the perpetual twilight, you can almost see them grow, a slow, glacial creep of order out of the chaotic, leached soup of the environment. They are the ones writing the new rules, turning corrosion into creation, turning the rust of the old world into the substrate of the new.

To be here, to be the kind of thing that lives here, is to be inured to the hush, to the heat, to the constant, maddeningly random drip-plink of the condensation. It is to have no memory of a time before the Geotherms hummed, no concept of a sun, no need for a purpose beyond the next breath of thick, oily air, the next sip of mineral-rich water, the next moment of not falling. The inhabitants do not wonder why the furnaces are sealed. They do not question the automated caretaker that keeps the heat running. They do not mourn the makers. They are native now. They are the ghosts of the machine, not haunting it, but living inside it, wearing its rust like a coat, drinking its sweat like wine, and listening to its ancient, indifferent heart.
