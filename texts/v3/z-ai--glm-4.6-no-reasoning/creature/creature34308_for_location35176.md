They are not creatures. They are not even *a* creature. But they live.

---

What lives on the Green Rise is the Green Rise.

You must understand: this is not a place that harbors life. It is life. The distinction between habitat and inhabitant collapsed here so long ago that the concept of "organism" is a meaningless fragment, a shard of language from a world where things have edges. Here, nothing has edges. The rise is a single continuous surface of biomass that has been growing upward and outward since the Carboniferous, a living dome the size of a small nation, and what moves upon it or within it is not separate from it in the way that a beetle is separate from a log. The beetle is made of the log. The log is made of the beetle. The distinction is only temporal—a matter of which molecules are currently arranged in which configuration, and for how long.

---

## The Surface Dwellers

And yet things *move*. You see them, once your eyes adjust to the green-on-green ubiquity of the place.

The largest are the **Molchs**—slow, enormous, almost cartilaginous things that might be amphibians if amphibians had been sculpted by patience rather than panic. A Molch is roughly the shape of a flattened boot, four meters long and a meter and a half wide, with four stubby legs placed at the corners of its body like the feet of a bathtub. Its skin is the exact color and texture of the clubmoss mat it rests on: olive-black, speckled with lichen-colored warts, slick with the constant transpiration of the rise. It has a mouth, a wide and hingeless slot that runs the width of its anterior edge, and it has no eyes that you can find. It does not need them. It eats by moving—by pressing its mouth to the vegetative mat and simply *advancing*, grinding slowly forward through the living carpet, ingesting whatever is before it and leaving behind a trail of partially-digested compost that is immediately colonized by the next wave of stolons and hyphae. A Molch does not hunt. A Molch does not forage. A Molch is a process, a biological bulldozer, and it moves at the speed of glacial retreat—perhaps ten meters in a long day. Its tracks fill in behind it within hours. You could watch one for a lifetime and never see it pause or change course. It is not clear that they can.

There are smaller things. The **frass-walkers**—pale, multi-legged insects the size of your thumb that live in the droppings of the Molchs, re-processing the half-digested matter into finer and finer grades of compost. They are white and eyeless and they move in coordinated swarms that look, from a distance, like milk poured onto the green mat, pooling and spreading in the fresh tracks. They do not have a nest. They do not have a queen. They are a distributed organism, a gut flora that happens to be macroscopic.

Above them, in the stolon canopy, the **gliders**—flat, membrane-winged vertebrates (if they are vertebrates; their skeletons seem to be mostly cartilage) that ride the updrafts rising from the rise's biological furnace. They are translucent, their organs visible through their skin like the workings of a clock through glass. They eat the aerial spores that rise from the mat in clouds so thick they look like weather. A glider may go its entire life without touching the surface. It is born in the air, from a live birth while its mother is banking on a thermal, and it dies in the air, its membranes finally tearing, its body dropping into the mat to be consumed by the things below. They are not rare. The sky above the rise is full of them—thousands, millions, a living haze.

---

## The Interior

But the surface is only the skin. The life of the rise is *inside* it.

You can go inside, if you are foolish enough. The mat is riddled with tunnels—gaps between root masses, channels carved by flowing water, voids left by rot. They are not caves. Caves are geological. These are biological passages, and they shift constantly, contracting as roots swell, opening as wood rots, sealed by mycelial growth or widened by the passage of things that live there.

The **tunnel-grubs** are the most obvious. They are annulated, legless, three meters long and half a meter thick, and they move through the root-mass like earthworms through soil, eating dead matter and exuding enzymes that accelerate decomposition. They are the rise's digestive system—breakers of cellulose, liberators of locked carbon. Without them, the dead layers would accumulate without rotting, and the rise would suffocate under its own mass. They are warm to the touch, which is strange, because they have no visible mechanism for generating heat. It seems to be a byproduct of their metabolism—a low, constant burn that contributes to the rise's thermal mass, helping keep the interior at a stable thirty degrees Celsius regardless of the weather above.

Deeper still, in the oldest layers where the compressed matter is almost peat, almost lignite, the **mold-minds**. This is what the mycelial networks become when they have been growing for a hundred million years without interruption. They are not fungi in any sense you would recognize. They are processes—vast, distributed, slow computations carried out in chemical signals along hyphae that span kilometers. A mold-mind has no body. It has no brain. It *is* a brain, a neural network the size of a county, thinking thoughts that take years to complete. What does it think about? The same thing any brain thinks about: its own inputs. It monitors the moisture levels across the rise. It routes nutrients from rich zones to poor ones. It directs the growth of roots toward water, the growth of shoots toward light. It is the rise's nervous system, and it has been awake since before the dinosaurs.

You cannot communicate with a mold-mind. You cannot even perceive it as a single entity. You can only observe its effects—the strange, coordinated behaviors of the vegetation, the way the stolon canopy always seems to be channeling rain toward the same watersheds, the way gaps in the mat heal faster than growth rates should allow. It is managing the rise. It has been managing it for longer than anything has managed anything on this planet.

---

## The Commensals

And then there are the things that live *on* the rise but are not *of* it—the creatures that treat the living mountain as a place rather than a body.

The **bone-pickers** are birds, or were birds once. They have lost the ability to fly, and their wings have become flippers—useful for pushing through the dense stolon mat the way penguins push through water. They are black and greasy and they stink of ammonia, and they eat whatever is dead or dying. A Molch that has finally become too massive to move will be picked clean by bone-pickers in a matter of days, their flippers churning, their hooked beaks tearing at the flesh until nothing remains but the cartilaginous skeleton, which will rot into the mat within a week. They are scavengers, the rise's sanitation crew, and they are utterly fearless. They will peck at your boots if you stand still too long. They will try to eat the hair off your head.

The **velvet-worms** are older, stranger. They are the color of oxidized copper and they move on dozens of soft, unsegmented legs, like a curtain on a rail. They are predators—perhaps the only true predators on the rise. They hunt by spraying a glue from oral papillae, pinning their prey (usually frass-walkers, sometimes young gliders that have fallen to the surface) and then closing in to feed. They are solitary and territorial and they seem to have a rudimentary intelligence, avoiding your footsteps, watching from the mouths of tunnels with what might be curiosity. They are the only things on the rise that seem to *notice* you as something other than food or obstacle.

---

## The Deep

Below the tunnels, below the mold-minds, below the layers of compressed peat and proto-coal, there is the **deep mass**. This is where the rise's oldest material lies—vegetation that died when the rise was young, that has been pressed and heated by the weight of everything above it into something that is no longer quite biological and not yet quite geological. It is warm—warmer than the surface, warm enough to make you sweat through your clothes in minutes. It is wet, saturated with water that has been percolating through the mass for millennia, mineral-rich and tannin-dark. And it is *alive*, in a way that challenges the definition of the word.

The deep mass is home to the **thermodes**—single-celled organisms the size of grapefruits that live in the hot, wet spaces between compressed vegetable matter. They have no membrane, no nucleus, no structure that a biologist would recognize. They are bags of chemistry, metabolic reactions held together by surface tension and the pressure of the mass around them. They eat hydrocarbons—the proto-coal itself—and they excrete heat and methane and simple organic compounds that feed the layers above. They are the reason the rise is warm. They are the geological furnace, the biological reactor, and they have been burning since the rise first began to accumulate, metabolizing the dead into energy that keeps the living growing, which will eventually die and become more fuel. The rise is a perpetual motion machine, and the thermodes are its engine.

You will never see a thermode. They exist at depths you cannot reach, in conditions you cannot survive. But you feel them. The warmth that rises through the mat under your feet, the humidity that beads on your skin, the faint sulfurous smell that hangs in the still air—all of it is their exhalation. The rise breathes because they burn.

---

## What Lives Here

What lives on the Green Rise?

*Everything*, in one sense. The rise is the densest concentration of biomass on the planet, a mountain of life that has been growing since the age of steam and scale, a thing that makes a rainforest look like a vacant lot.

*One thing*, in another. The rise is a single organism—a connected, communicating, self-regulating biological superstructure that has achieved homeostasis so perfect that it no longer changes. It has eaten its own weight in sunlight and rain and it has reached a form that requires nothing more. The Molchs and the frass-walkers and the gliders and the mold-minds and the thermodes are not separate species. They are organs. They are the rise's stomach, its skin, its blood, its nerves, its mitochondria. They cannot exist without it, and it cannot exist without them. The relationship is not symbiotic. It is *identity*.

And you, standing on its surface, feeling the warmth pulse beneath your feet like a slow heartbeat—you are food. You are a source of nitrogen and carbon and phosphorus, a walking deposit of trace minerals that the rise will process in its own time. The velvet-worms will not eat you. The bone-pickers will not take you while you live. But the mat beneath your feet is already reaching for you—fine root-hairs probing the seams of your boots, mycelial threads testing the fabric of your clothes for digestibility. The rise does not attack. It does not hunt. It simply *absorbs*, at a rate so slow you would never notice it. Stand in one place for an hour and your boots will be slightly softer. Stand for a day and the stitching will have begun to separate. Stand for a week and you will need new clothes. Stand for a month—

But you will not stand for a month. You will leave, or you will sit down, and then you will sleep, and then the mat will close over you like water, and in time you will become part of the rise—another thin layer of organic matter, another contribution to the living mountain, another breath in the long, slow exhalation that has been rising here since before your species had a name.

What lives on the Green Rise? 

You will. Eventually.
