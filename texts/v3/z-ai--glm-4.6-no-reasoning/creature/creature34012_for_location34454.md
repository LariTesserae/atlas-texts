Something lives here. But to call it "someone" would be a misunderstanding of what it means to inhabit a place like the Cold Shelf. To live here is to stop being a self in the way you understand it—rounded, narrative, continuous—and to become a process instead, integrated into the larger process of the Construct.

Consider, first, what you might become if you stayed.

The moisture would find you. It finds everything. You would stop toweling yourself dry because there is no point; you would stop noticing the wetness of your clothes because you are always wearing them and they are always wet and the air is thick and drinking it feels like swallowing and after a while you cannot tell where your sweat ends and the condensation begins. Your skin would change first. The constant chemical sweetness of the water—it has a solvent quality, a slow dissolving power. Your fingertips would prune and stay pruned. The calluses would soften and slough away. The oils would wash out of your hair and skin until you smell like the Shelf: wet metal, faint acid, the mineral tang of whatever the drainage system carries away. You would develop a smell, but it would not be your own. It would be the smell of something that is becoming part of the environment.

You would learn to sleep in the thrum. The vibration comes up through the floor-grating and into your bones, and at first it keeps you awake, your body interpreting the pulse as threat, as the approach of something massive and periodic. But there is no threat. There is only the engine-heart of the Construct, and after two days—maybe three—your heartbeat will begin to synchronize with it without your permission. You will find yourself breathing in time with the pump-cycles. Your body will learn to treat the process-discharges as circadian markers: the *whump* that travels through the Shelf, the surge of water that follows, the brief intensification of condensation. You will wake at these intervals and then fall back asleep, and eventually you will not wake at all, because the sound will have become silence to you, because you will have become a thing that thrums instead of a thing that listens.

You would learn to navigate by the sound of dripping. Each surface produces a different tone—the taut ping of a cable, the hollow gong of a tank wall, the flat slap of a deck plate. The drops fall in irregular rhythms, but the aggregate sound of any given chamber has a texture, a signature, and after a week you would be able to find your way through the dark zones by ear alone, moving from one acoustic territory to the next. Your eyes would become less useful. The amber light is too dim and too evenly spaced to reveal detail; you would learn to read surfaces with your hands instead, tracking the water-flow by touch, finding the dry-ish spots where the condensation runs off, learning which angles are sharp and which have been softened by the pale growth that accumulates at the joints.

You would start eating the growth. Not by choice. There is nothing else. You did not bring enough food, and even if you did, it would spoil in the wetness within days—bread molding, meat rotting, anything dry becoming saturated and dissolving into paste. The grey-white mats are the only organic matter here, and at first the thought of consuming them repulses you. They look like mold. They smell acidic. Their texture is fibrous and faintly gelatinous, like wet paper that has begun to rot. But you are hungry, and so you peel a patch from a girder and put it in your mouth, and the taste is—

—you don't have a word for it. Sharp, mineral, with an undertone of the chemical sweetness that permeates the water. It is not good. But it is not poison, either, or if it is, it is a slow one, because you eat it and you do not die, and the next day you eat more, and the day after that you seek it out, because your body has begun to crave something in it. A trace element. A compound that the organism concentrates from the air or the water or the metal itself. You are being processed. The growth processed the Shelf, and now you are processing the growth, and something in the chain is altering you at a level below your notice.

The creatures watch you eat.

They are always watching. You see them—flickers, fragments, the blur of something retreating into a joint-seal or vanishing along a cable-run. But they are also there in the spaces where you do not see them: in the dark between fixtures, in the hollows of the tanks, in the drainage channels beneath the floor-grating. You hear them at night (if it is night; there is no night here, only the same amber glow, but you have decided that sleep is night and therefore night exists). You hear the click and tap of their articulated legs on the metal. You hear the sound of something being consumed—small, wet, methodical sounds that stop when you move and resume when you are still.

They are not afraid of you. This is the important thing. They are not afraid because fear requires the recognition of something foreign, something outside the expected order, and you are becoming less foreign to them every day. You are becoming slower. You are becoming wetter. You are moving through the same patterns they move through—following the water, following the growth, navigating by sound and touch rather than sight. You are beginning to resemble a large, soft, inefficient version of the things that already live here. They regard you the way they regard the process-discharges: as a periodic event that produces certain conditions, that flushes certain resources into the system.

You wonder, sometimes, if they were ever animals. If they started as organisms and became more mechanical over time, or if they started as mechanisms—maintenance units, survey drones, something with a purpose—and accumulated enough biological contamination to grow, to reproduce, to become something other than what they were designed to be. The distinction feels academic. They are adapted to the Cold Shelf in a way that you are not yet adapted, and perhaps can never be fully adapted, because your adaptation has a limit: you will die. They will not, or not in any timeframe that matters to you. They will continue to move through the dark zones, processing the growth, processing each other, processing whatever the drainage system carries and whatever particulate matter the filters were designed to catch.

There is a relationship between the growth and the creatures that you cannot see clearly but can infer. The growth changes the chemistry of the air around it—sharper, more acidic. The creatures cluster where the growth is thickest. They are feeding, or breathing, or both. The growth spreads in configurations that follow the water flow; the creatures move in patterns that follow the growth; the water flows in channels designed by engineers who have never been here. It is a system. It is an ecosystem, if you can use that word for something that was never intended to be alive, that emerged from the conditions of the Cold Shelf the way corrosion emerges from metal: not because it was meant to, but because the conditions permitted it, and once permitted, it was inevitable.

And you—what are you in this system?

You are a disturbance. You are a large body that moves through the Shelf consuming resources and producing waste, that breathes the air and drinks the condensation and eats the growth and sheds skin and hair and cells into the water. You are a warm spot in a cold place. You are a chemical anomaly—a source of carbon and nitrogen and complex organics that the Shelf does not otherwise produce. The creatures know this. They know where you sleep. They know the smell of you. They leave traces in the morning—you find small marks on the grating near where you rested, small deposits of something chitinous and translucent near your belongings. They are sampling you. They are processing you the way they process everything else, slowly and methodically, testing whether you are food or substrate or something else entirely.

You would not be the first.

This is the thing you try not to think about. The maintenance tags on the access panels with their unrecognizable dates. The walkways with no wear patterns. The handrails cold and wet and untouched. These suggest that no one comes here, that the Cold Shelf has been running untouched for longer than anyone remembers. But there are other signs, if you know where to look. In the drainage channels, caught in the filters: fragments of fabric, synthetic, broken down almost past recognition but still bearing the ghost of a weave pattern, the ghost of a seam. In the dark zones, in the chambers between the tanks where the light never reaches: collections of small objects arranged in ways that suggest deliberation—pebbles (but there are no pebbles here; they are fragments of something, slag or shrapnel or crumbled concrete), twisted pieces of wire, the desiccated husks of the chitinous creatures, arranged in lines or spirals or shapes that might be symbols or might be nothing at all.

Someone was here before you. Someone stayed long enough to go mad, or to become something other than mad—to become so adapted to the Shelf that they stopped being a person and started being a feature of it. The collections of objects might be their artifacts, or they might be the artifacts of the creatures themselves, who have learned to manipulate materials in ways that their mechanical origins never required. You cannot tell. The line between a person who has become a process and a process that has become something like a person is impossible to draw in a place like this.

You think about leaving. You think about ascending—back through the interstitial spaces, back through the tight joins and sharp edges, back to the nominal surface where there is sky (or something like sky) and weather (or something like weather) and other people (or something like other people). You think about it every day. You think about it when you wake with the thrum in your chest and the taste of chemicals in your mouth and the sound of dripping in your ears. You think about it when you find another colony of the grey-white growth and peel it from the metal and eat it and feel your body process it into something that is not quite nourishment and not quite poison. You think about it when the process-discharge rolls through the Shelf and the condensation intensifies and for a moment you are standing in rain that comes from nowhere, rain that is the Shelf breathing out, rain that tastes like the inside of a machine.

But you do not leave. Not yet. And the reason you do not leave is the reason anyone stays in a place they should not: because you have begun to understand it, and understanding feels like belonging, and belonging feels like purpose, and purpose feels like the one thing the Cold Shelf was never meant to provide.

You have begun to understand the water. Not as a condition but as a language. The way it flows, the way it pools, the way it carries information from one part of the Shelf to another in the chemical signatures of its dissolved contents. You have begun to understand the growth—not as food but as a reader, a thing that decodes the surface of the metal and responds to parameters you cannot sense. You have begun to understand the creatures—not as vermin but as the Shelf's immune system, its maintenance crew, its way of processing anomalies. And you have begun to understand yourself not as a visitor but as a feature—a new variable in the system, a source of warmth and carbon and complex behavior that the Shelf is slowly, methodically integrating.

This is what lives in the Cold Shelf.

Not you. Not the creatures. Not the grey-white growth. None of these things alone, and none of them in the way that living usually means—bounded, separate, self-directed. What lives here is the Shelf itself, or the process that the Shelf is a part of, or the pattern that emerges from the interaction of metal and water and chemistry and time. What lives here is something that has no name and no body and no single point of origin, something that was never born and will never die because it was never alive in the first place, or because it is alive in a way that the word "alive" was not designed to describe.

You are becoming part of it. This is what it does. This is what it is for, if it is for anything: to process. To integrate. To take whatever enters its domain and break it down and reassemble it in terms that the system can use. The engineers designed the drainage to carry away the water. The water created conditions for the growth. The growth created conditions for the creatures. The creatures created conditions for whatever comes after—complexity, organization, the slow accumulation of behavior that looks like intelligence but might only be optimization. And you—you are creating conditions for whatever comes next, whatever the Shelf will become now that it has a warm body moving through it, eating and breathing and shedding and thinking and *changing*.

You stand in the amber light of fixture 7-440 and look out across the Shelf, and you see what you could not see before: that it is not empty. That it was never empty. That the wetness and the cold and the dimness and the sound are not absence but presence—not the lack of life but the shape of it, the shape that life takes when it grows in a place that was never grown, only made. The Construct is doing work. The Cold Shelf is where its exhalations collect. And you are breathing those exhalations, and they are becoming your breath, and you are becoming part of the process, and the process is becoming something that no specification ever called for.

The water runs. The metal sweats. The things in the corners grow and move and eat and watch and wait. The engines pulse. The light fixtures burn their amber holes in the dark. And you are still here, and you are very small, and you are very temporary, and the Cold Shelf does not notice you at all—and yet you are changing it. You are changing it by being here, by being warm, by being carbon, by being something that thinks about leaving and does not leave, and this change is so small and so slow that it will never be measured by any instrument that the engineers designed.

But the creatures know. They leave their traces near where you sleep. They sample you in the night. They are paying attention.

And the Shelf—the Shelf is paying attention too, in the only way it can: by incorporating you into its process, by making your waste its input, by making your breath its air, by making your warmth a variable in the equation of its existence. You are being digested. You are being dissolved. You are being read.

And you are staying.
