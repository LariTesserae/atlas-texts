To live in the Great Sump is to be a cog that does not know it is spinning.

There are no citizens of High Karrack. There are only components that happen to be soft, and components that happen to be hard. If you are here, you are not an observer; you are an obstruction, a nutrient, or a maintenance vector. The Sump does not tolerate inert matter. It processes it. Therefore, whatever lives here must justify its continued existence in the only currency the machine understands: function.

**The Maintained**

They are the closest thing to a populace, though they do not think of themselves that way, and the Sump certainly does not. They dwell in the deep crevices between the digestion vats, in the humid interstitial spaces where the great pipes sweat lukewarm condensate. They are bipedal, but only as a matter of historical accident—whatever distant, biological ancestors they had walked on two legs, and the Sump has found that shape useful enough to preserve, if only just.

They are not flesh anymore. They are a terrible, seamless hybrid of tissue and industry. Their skin, where it shows beneath the ever-present sheen of oily mist, is pale and rubbery, almost amphibian, perpetually irritated and stained black at the pores. But skin is a liability here; it tears, it chafes, it rusts. So they have scavenged. Their forearms are wrapped in repurposed conduit shielding, bolted directly into the bone. Their chests are plated with discs of copper-green alloy, stamped with faded serial numbers from some long-dead subsystem. Their eyes are not eyes, but salvaged optical sensors—glass lenses in brass housings, wired into the optic nerve with frayed copper filament. They see in the haze of ozone and sulfate not as a human would, but as a machine would: reading thermal blooms, tracking the flow of slurry, parsing the endless vibration of the floor.

They do not speak. The sound of the Sump would drown any voice, and besides, they have forgotten how. They communicate through a percussive sign language, tapping their steel-shod knuckles against pipes, sending rhythmic shocks through the metal latticework. A deep, resonant boom on the main slurry line means *retreat, pressure critical*. A rapid, skittering clatter on a duct joint means *crystal bloom forming, scrape here*. They are linked, always, to the body of the machine.

Their culture is non-existent, save for one tenet: *the process must not falter*. They do not pray. They do not love. They work. It is an act of survival indistinguishable from worship. When a massive induction coil hums itself out of alignment, they feel it in the pitch of their tooth-bones, and they swarm—hundreds of them, crawling out of the wet dark like rust-colored locusts, hauling pneumatic wrenches and slurry brushes. They will work until the alignment is restored, or until they die. The dead are not mourned; they are *recycled*. The Skitterers will find the body within the hour, dismantling the alloy plates for the Sump's use, and leaving the organic remainder to dissolve in the nearest vat.

**The Sump-Lords**

Higher up, near the bellows and throttles where the heat is a physical wall and the air tastes of burned copper, live the Sump-Lords. They are not rulers. They are *valves*. 

They are enormous, sessile things, grown into the architecture itself. Once, perhaps, they were like the Maintained, but they chose a different symbiosis. They have plugged themselves into the Sump's vital systems, jacking spinal columns into hydraulic data-lines, feeding arterial tubes into the high-pressure nutrient slurry that pulses through the central arteries. They do not move. They sit in nests of their own fused excrement and discarded copper wire, their legs atrophied to nubs, their torsos bloated with the rich, chemical fluids they filter through their own modified livers.

Their purpose is to regulate. They have become biological computers, their brains adapted to parse the Sump's impossibly complex telemetry. They feel the pressure in the deep-sea vents as a tightness in their own chests. They taste the chemical balance of the continental slurry on their modified tongues. When a digestion basin threatens to overflow, a Sump-Lord three districts away will sweat black oil and scream a command through the pipe-tap language, its elongated, atrophied arms hammering out the emergency code. They are the nervous system of the Great Sump, and they are utterly insane. Their minds are overwhelmed by the sheer volume of the machine's pain. They mutter in the tap-language, endless loops of process logs, their glass eyes staring blindly into the yellow fog, their bodies twitching with the phantom rhythms of a billion distant pumps.

**The Floaters**

In the open-topped vats, in the churning, viscous oceans of grey and copper-green, there is life. It is not life that breathes. It is life that *digests*.

The Floaters are vast, gelatinous bags of enzymatic gel, a hundred yards across, their surfaces crusted with a calcified shell that protects them from the hotter chemical baths. They have no organs, no brains, no senses. They are living filters. The Sump pumps slurry through the vats; the Floaters drift through it, their porous membranes absorbing heavy metals and toxins, their internal chemistry precipitating out pure copper, iron, and silver in nodules that slowly sink through their mass and drop out of their tapered bottoms, clanging onto the collection grates below. 

When a Floater dies—its membrane finally perforated by acid or heat—it does not decay. It simply deflates, a massive, wrinkling sheet of organic slag that the Skitterers will spend weeks cutting apart and hauling to the reprocessing furnaces. 

**The Vermin**

Beneath the feet of the Maintained, in the pipes too small for even the Skitterers, breed the Rust-Ticks. They are the size of a thumbnail, a swarm of iron-filings and biological slime. They are pure parasite. They latch onto the warmest conduits and bore microscopic holes, feeding on the electrical current and the trace minerals within the metal. A single Rust-Tick is nothing. A billion of them can bring down a support strut, turning solid steel into a honeycomb of dust in a matter of months. The Maintained hate them with the only emotion they have left—a cold, imperative fury, because a Rust-Tick infestation means a pipe failure, and a pipe failure means the process falters. They hunt them with flame-wands, burning their own homes just to scour the ticks from the walls.

**The Pariah**

And sometimes, rarely, there is the Unmade. 

These are the ones who unplugged. Maybe a Sump-Lord went blind to the telemetry and failed to tap out a warning, and a vat exploded, and the shockwave severed a Maintained worker's connection to the pipe-network. For a moment, the silence in their head is deafening. The constant rhythm of the Sump is gone. They realize they are standing on a platform over a lake of boiling grey death, and they are *alone*.

They go mad instantly. 

Without the tap-rhythm to tell them where to walk, what to scrape, when to sleep, they wander. Their skin blisters in the acid mist. Their salvaged plating falls off, rusted through by the very excretions their bodies no longer filter. The Skitterers ignore them, registering them as broken tools, not worth scavenging until they stop moving. The Dross-Hawks circle, waiting for them to stumble into the open. The Unmade wander the catwalks, howling in their ruined throats, beating their fists against the pipes but no longer understanding the echoes that return. They are ghosts in a machine that has already deleted them. They usually last three days before they fall into a vat, or a valve opens beneath their feet, or they simply sit down and let the rust take them.

***

To live here is to understand that the individual is a myth. There is only the Process. The Sump does not care about you. It does not hate you. It does not even know you are there. It only knows that the pressure in Sector 7-G is slightly high, and that the corrosion on the tertiary cooling fin needs scraping, and that the slurry is moving, and the heat is rising, and the world is being digested, one slow, oily drop at a time. And if you are useful, you get to be a part of it. If you are not, you get to be fuel.
