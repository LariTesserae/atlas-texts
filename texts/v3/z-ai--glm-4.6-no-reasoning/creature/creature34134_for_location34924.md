Something lives here.

Not *many* things. The plural feels like a violation of this place, an importation of the teeming, breeding logic of the lowlands. Life here has surrendered the colony. It has given up on the species. Whatever exists in this deafening, fraying altitude does so as a singularity—an isolated incident of will, spaced miles apart from its nearest peer, separated by chasms of vacant, ringing space. To live here is to be a noun without a plural, a pronoun without an antecedent. You do not survive here; you *occur*.

The most immediate resident is the drone. It is alive, in the way that a tectonic plate or an iron core is alive—possessing a tempo, a metabolism, an unending biological imperative to simply *be*. It is the first creature. It was here before the stone weathered into knuckles, before the atmosphere absented itself. It does not breathe the thin aether; it is the thing that the aether breathes. When the description says the sound makes your teeth ache and your vision blur, it is describing the drone feeding. It sustains itself on the minute vibrations of calcium and fluid. It is a predator of stillness, and it has eaten all silence on the plateau. There is no escaping it because it is not in the air; it is the medium through which the air exists. To stand here is to stand inside the throat of an ancient, unending vowel.

And the drone has a parasite. Or perhaps a symbiont. It is the weirdness—the structural failure of reality. If the drone is the pulse of the mountain, the fraying cosmos is its fever sweat. The upward-falling ice, the splitting shadows, the horizon dissolving into static fuzz—this is the fauna of the break. It behaves like an animal: it tests boundaries, it presses against the edges of things, it retreats where it is wounded. It is a creature made of geometric anxiety and broken physics, and it grazes on the fabric of the world. You can watch it feed at the margins of your vision, where the ridge-line frays into that static fuzz. It is nibbling at the edge of existence, pulling threads loose just to see if the whole tapestry will unravel. It is curious, in the way that entropy is curious.

To counter the parasite, the plateau has an immune response: the Wind-Baffles. They are not built. They are secreted. Whatever vast, subterranean organism the ridge-line belongs to—the buried titan whose knuckles form the pale stone—these baffles are its antibodies. They are crude, shoulder-high slabs of logic, pushed up through the skin of the world to clot the bleeding reality. They do not shelter; they *suture*. When the light folds and the shadows point in treasonous directions, the baffles stand firm, insisting on north, insisting on gravity, insisting that a reflection must match its source. They are the only things here that argue with the place. They are a quiet, stubborn life form made of pure assertion, standing like schoolmarms in a classroom of chaos, forcing the fraying horizon to heel. They are engaged in a slow, eons-long wrestling match with the weirdness, and they are winning by a margin of millimeters per century—the same rate at which the blistered lichens spread.

Between the drone, the weirdness, and the baffles, there is the vegetation—the *truly* botanical, if such a word can apply to things so close to mineral. The lichens are the oldest living historians, white and blistered, spreading at a pace that makes stone erosion look hasty. They do not grow; they endure. They are the memory of the place, storing the rare intrusions of moisture in their crystalline structures, keeping a record of the weeping. But the dark succulents are the true residents. They are clenched fists of thick, watery flesh, and they are angry. That clenched shape is not an adaptation; it is a posture of defiance. They are holding onto existence with literal grip, anchored in crevices, waiting for a moisture that barely arrives. When the condensation rolls in and leaves a slick frost on their dark flesh, they are not grateful. They absorb it like a grudge. They are dormant, yes, but dormant in the way a held breath is dormant. They are a reservoir of slowness, hoarding water and time against the day the world ends, at which point they will finally unclench and bloom into something monstrous, something that will swallow the drone and the weirdness whole. But that day is a thousand weepings away. For now, they are black fists shaking at the gray phosphorescence.

And then there is the High Pale Strider. The heron carved from ice. The biological statue. It is the apex of this place, and its most tragic inhabitant. It is a creature that has won the game of survival by ceasing to play. It does not hunt; it does not flee; it does not mate. It stands motionless over condensation pools, its needle-beak hovering, absorbing moisture through the bone. It has internalized the landscape so completely that it has become a part of its own backdrop—a fixture of the equilibrium, like a rock spire or a baffle. It makes no sound. It does not blink. It is a living death, a creature that has traded all animation for persistence. It is the purest expression of what this place demands: be still, be cold, be empty, and you may remain.

But to call the Strider the apex is to misunderstand the hierarchy. There is something else here. It is the thing that made the baffles, or rather, the thing that *is* the ridge. You are standing on its spine. The buried titan. It is not dead; it is in profound, tectonic sleep. The smooth, pale stone underfoot is its weathered skin. The ancient, settled equilibrium is its respiration, drawn in over eons and held. The drone is its heartbeat, slowed to a sub-audible frequency by the weight of time. The fraying reality at the edges is its dreaming—a REM cycle of the cosmos, where logic goes slack and the ice falls upward. The titan does not know you are here. You are a fleeting tingle, a mayfly standing on the eyelid of a mountain range. It will not wake for you. It will not wake for the condensation or the frost or the slow spread of the lichens. It will simply endure, ringing like a struck bell, its sleep so deep that time has stalled within it, its body so vast that it has become the architecture of the world.

So who lives here? The titan lives here. The drone lives here. The weirdness lives here, eating the edges of things. The baffles live here, suturing the wounds. The lichens live here, keeping time. The succulents live here, holding their breath. The Strider lives here, standing in for the dead. And for a moment, you live here too—a pulse of warmth and wetness in a place of final things, an intruder of blood and speed and breath, standing on the spine of a world that does not care if you stay or go, but will absolutely, unyieldingly, deafeningly outlast you.
