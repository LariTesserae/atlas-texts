# The Inhabitants of the Boiling Plateau

---

To live here is to have never known the cool. Nothing here carries the memory of shade, of water that does not burn, of air that does not press against the skin like a heated hand. Every creature on this plateau was born from the assumption that the world is a furnace—and that assumption has made them something entirely other than what the cooler world would recognize as alive.

---

## The Pale Annelids of the Thermal Pools

They are called *the drifters* by the workers, though no worker has ever needed to name them aloud, because the workers never speak of them at all.

In the iridescent pools—those shallow, chemical-laden mirrors that collect in the basalt shadows—the annelids move like thoughts occurring slowly inside a dream. Six meters of pale, segmented body, each segment a separate engine of digestion, processing the sulfur froth, the copper-laced brine, the manganese bloom. They are not white but *lucrid*—a color the cooler world has no word for, because it is a color that exists only in the emission of bioluminescence against the dark mirror of volcanic glass. They glow, but faintly, as if their light were an afterthought, a metabolic whisper, a thing their chemistry does because it can, not because it must.

Their bodies are sectioned in a way that suggests neither worm nor serpent but something between—something that decided on segmentation and then took it to an extreme that would be obscene in a cooler world. Each segment is a semi-autonomous processing node, a gut-within-a-gut, extracting different minerals from the froth as the creature drifts through the chemical gradient. The head, if it can be called a head, is a crown of feeding filaments—hundreds of them, fine as spider silk, trailing forward into the chemical bloom, each one a microscopic furnace of chemoautotrophic synthesis. They do not eat. They *osmose*. They draw the mineral soup through their skin in a continuous, passive consumption that has no mouth, no bite, no hunger in any recognizable sense. They are never hungry because they are always eating. They are never full because they have no capacity for satiation. They simply *process*, continuously, as the pool processes the weeping earth.

They have no eyes. They have never needed them. The pools are bright with chemical iridescence, but that light carries no information they use. They navigate by gradient—by the subtle shifts in mineral concentration, in temperature, in the acidity of the water. Their skin is a single vast sensory organ, reading the pool the way a blind reader reads braille: not seeing the words but feeling their shape. They know where the hottest water enters the pool. They know where the chemical slick is thickest. They know where the basalt shelf casts its shadow and the water cools by half a degree, and they avoid it, because half a degree of cool is, to them, a winter.

Their reproduction is a thing the workers have observed but do not discuss. Occasionally, two drifters will intersect in a pool, their bodies overlapping, segments interweaving. There is no courtship, no display, no recognition. They simply pass through each other—and in the passing, certain segments are exchanged. Not released, not transferred by any visible mechanism. Simply: before the intersection, each creature has its segments; after, each has some of the other's. And the exchanged segments, once integrated into their new host, begin to bud. Slowly, over weeks, a new segment forms at the budding site—a pale, miniature thing that grows in the mineral bath, that develops its own feeding filaments, its own bioluminescent whisper, until eventually it detaches, a new drifter a meter long, drifting out of the pool and into the connected network of thermal shallows, beginning its long, silent growth.

No one knows how old the largest drifters are. The workers have seen the same six-meter specimens in the same pools for years, for decades, possibly longer. The segments show no signs of senescence. Each one processes the chemical froth with the same indifferent efficiency it ever did. There is no death in the pools, only *dissolution*—the occasional segment that loses its structural integrity, that begins to leak its bioluminescence into the water like ink, that fragments into the mineral soup and is consumed by the filaments of its neighbors. But this is not death as the cooler world knows it. It is recycling. It is the pool reabsorbing what the pool produced.

The workers do not disturb them. The drifters do not disturb the workers. They occupy the same space with the perfect indifference of things that have never had any reason to acknowledge each other's existence.

## The Gas Arachnids of the Updrafts

Above the weeping cracks, where the steam rises in vertical columns of superheated vapor, the arachnids hang.

They are not spiders. They are not arachnids in any taxonomic sense, but the workers call them that because their silhouette—skeletal, many-jointed, suspended by invisible forces—recalls the cellar spiders of the cooler world, magnified to a scale that eliminates all comfort. They are the size of hounds, but they carry no weight. Their exoskeletons—if they can be called exoskeletons—are not chitin but a mineralized membrane, a parchment-thin lattice of silicates and calcium that has more in common with the rhyolite of the plateau than with any biological shell. They are stone-thin. Stone-light. They have to be. They live in the air.

Their bodies are gas bladders. Inside the skeletal frame of their limbs and thorax, they carry sacs of heated gas—hydrogen sulfide, methane, carbon dioxide—the volatile byproducts of the deep earth, captured and contained in membranes that are both permeable and resilient. The gas is hotter than the surrounding air, and it lifts them. They are balloons. They are zeppelins of the steam columns, riding the thermal updrafts that rise from the fissures, ascending and descending by adjusting the temperature of their gas sacs—expanding to rise, contracting to fall, a slow, deliberate navigation that makes them seem not like animals but like instruments, like weather gauges, like devices designed to sample the atmosphere at various altitudes.

Which, in a sense, they are.

Their legs—eight of them, jointed in ways that suggest elbows and knees and wrists—trail below them as they drift. But the legs are not for walking. They are for *fishing*. From each joint, from each leg-tip, long filaments descend, some of them meters in length, swaying in the updraft, forming a trailing net of almost-invisible thread. These filaments are coated in a adhesive secretion—not sticky in the way of spider silk, but chemically active, a compound that bonds with the sulfur compounds in the mist, that captures the mineral particulate that rides the steam, that gathers the microscopic thermophilic organisms that live in the vapor itself. The arachnids are filter-feeders. They trawl the steam columns the way whales trawl the ocean, gathering their food from the medium in which they swim.

They make no sound. They have no calls, no signals, no territorial displays. They do not fight. They do not mate in any visible fashion. Occasionally, two will drift close to each other in the same updraft, their filaments tangling, their legs briefly interlocking—and then they separate, each going its own way, carried by the steam. Whether this is reproduction or accident, no one can say. The workers have seen small arachnids—hand-sized, with gas bladders no larger than a fist—drifting in the lower steam columns, near the fissures where the updraft is strongest. They assume these are young. They assume the adults reproduce. But the mechanism is invisible, occurring perhaps in the high steam where no one goes, perhaps in the chemical composition of the filaments themselves, perhaps in a way that has no parallel in the cooler world's understanding of generation.

Their behavior, to the extent that it can be called behavior, is dictated entirely by the steam. They rise when the updrafts are strong. They sink when the plateau exhales less forcefully. They congregate over the hottest fissures and disperse across the flats when the thermal pressure drops. They are not intelligent—they are *responsive*, their physiology so thoroughly adapted to the medium of superheated vapor that they function as extensions of the plateau's respiratory system. When the plateau breathes out, they rise. When it holds its breath, they wait. They are the plateau's breath made visible, given limbs and filaments and a skeletal, hanging form.

In the early mornings, when the sun adds its heat to the plateau's own and the steam columns rise to their greatest height, the arachnids ascend with them, and the sky above the city is filled with their silhouettes—dozens of them, hundreds, drifting at various altitudes, their trailing legs and filaments catching the white-yellow light, casting shadows on the obsidian flats below. It is beautiful, in the way that functional things can be beautiful—elegant in their efficiency, terrifying in their indifference.

The workers do not look up.

## The Slag Crustaceans of the Flats

Between the pipes, beneath the walkways, across the vast expanses of weeping obsidian, the crustaceans scuttle.

They are the most recognizable of the plateau's fauna—if *recognizable* is the word for something that looks like a crab the size of a dog, with a carapace of cooling volcanic slag and legs that move with the fluid, jointless grace of a thing that has never known a spine. They are the most visible, too, because they occupy the flats—the open spaces between the structures, the areas where the workers walk, where the pipes run close to the ground, where the maintenance hatches are located. The workers see them every day. The workers have learned to step around them.

Their carapaces are not grown but *accreted*. A crustacean begins life—however it begins life; no one has seen a young one—with a soft, membranous shell, like a pale, wet thing just emerged from an egg. But within hours, sometimes within minutes, the shell begins to accumulate mineral deposit. The crustacean seeks the hottest surfaces—the pipes that bleed white steam, the basalt shelves above the thermal pools, the obsidian flats where the stone is weeping most fiercely—and it presses its body against them, and the minerals in the water and the stone bond to its shell, forming a crust. The crust thickens. The crust becomes armor. The crust becomes the creature's skeleton, an exoskeleton not of its own making but of the plateau's, a skin of volcanic glass and chemical salt that hardens and thickens throughout its life.

The oldest crustaceans are almost immobile. Their shells have accreted to such a thickness that their legs can barely lift them, and they move in a slow, scraping shuffle, leaving furrows in the condensation on the obsidian. They are moving rocks. They are stone things that happen to be alive, that happen to be seeking the hottest shadows with a patience that has nothing to do with urgency and everything to do with instinct—the instinct to be warm, to be hot, to be as close to the heat source as their increasingly heavy bodies will allow.

They are scavengers, but not in any way the cooler world would recognize. They do not consume organic matter—there is no organic matter to consume. They eat *minerals*. They eat the chemical deposits that form on the pipes, the sulfur blooms that collect at the junctions, the copper-rich scale that accumulates on the valves. They eat the plateau itself, in small, methodical quantities, grazing on the mineral accretions the way a cow grazes on grass—except the cow is eating to build flesh, and the crustacean is eating to build stone. Every mineral it consumes goes into the shell. Every sulfur compound, every copper salt, every manganese residue is incorporated into the accreting carapace, making it thicker, heavier, more chemically complex. The shells of the oldest crustaceans are stratified—layers of different minerals laid down in different seasons, in different locations, a geological record of the creature's life written in stone.

Their legs are not jointed. They are *segmented*—not like the articulated limbs of a crab or insect, but like a series of telescoping tubes, each one slightly narrower than the last, fitting inside the one before it like the sections of a fishing rod. They extend and contract by hydraulic pressure—not blood pressure but *steam* pressure, internal reservoirs of superheated vapor that push the leg segments outward when the creature wants to move and allow them to retract when it does not. The motion is fluid, seamless, a thing of pistons and cylinders rather than muscles and tendons. They move like machines. They move like the city itself moves—silently, efficiently, powered by the plateau's own heat.

They have eyes, but the eyes are not for seeing. They are heat-sensors—clusters of thermoreceptive cells that detect the infrared radiation of warm objects, that map the plateau in gradients of heat rather than light. To a crustacean, a pipe is a blinding beacon of warmth. A thermal pool is a sun. Another crustacean is a warm shape in a warm world, distinguishable from the background only by its movement, by the slight difference in temperature between its shell and the stone on which it sits. They see the world as a thermal image, a landscape of hot and hotter, and they navigate toward the hot, always toward the hot, with a single-mindedness that has no room for anything else.

They do not fight. They do not compete. The plateau provides enough mineral accretion for all of them—more than all of them, an inexhaustible supply of chemical deposit on every pipe and valve and weeping stone. They encounter each other frequently, in the hottest shadows, in the narrow spaces between the structures, and they simply... coexist. They step around each other. They wait for each other to finish grazing at a particularly rich deposit before taking their turn. There is no aggression because there is no scarcity. There is no territoriality because the territory is all the same—hot, mineral-rich, sufficient.

The workers have a relationship with the crustaceans that is closer to cohabitation than to observation. The creatures scuttle through the corridors between the buildings, shelter beneath the walkways, cluster around the valves and junctions where the mineral deposits are thickest. They are a constant presence, as much a part of the industrial landscape as the pipes themselves, and the workers have learned to treat them as such—not as pests, not as wildlife, but as *fixtures*. Things that are there. Things that do not interfere. Things that the city was built around and that have, in turn, built their lives around the city.

Occasionally, a crustacean will accrete its shell to a pipe. The minerals in its carapace will bond with the minerals in the pipe's scale, and the creature will become fixed in place, unable to move, unable to lift its increasingly heavy body from the surface it has bonded to. It does not seem to mind. It continues to graze on the deposits within reach of its legs. It continues to grow its shell, adding layers of mineral accretion until it is less a creature and more a formation—a stalagmite of slag and shell that happens, if you look very closely, to have legs. The workers do not remove them. They are not causing damage. They are simply... there. Becoming stone. Becoming part of the plateau, as all things here eventually do.

## The Human Population: The Workers of the Silent City

They are not thermophiles. They are the exception to the plateau's biological logic, the one species that was not born to the heat but chose it—or, more precisely, was sent to it, contracted to it, employed by it.

The workers are few. The city is vast, but its population is small—maintenance engineers, extraction technicians, hydraulic specialists, a small administrative staff housed in the single reinforced block at the grid's center. They live indoors, in pressurized quarters cooled by the same geothermal systems that power the extraction engines. They work in shifts, emerging in insulated suits to walk the grid, to check the valves, to monitor the pressure in the subterranean cylinders, to scrape the mineral scale from the critical junctions—the ones that feed the harvest, not the ones that feed the crustaceans.

They do not speak much. The silence of the plateau gets into them, settles in their chests like the humidity settles on the stone. They communicate in gestures, in brief glances, in the minimal language of people who work together in an environment where sound is a violation. They wear ear protection—not because the plateau is loud, but because the subsonic thrumming, the tectonic pulse that operates below the threshold of hearing, can cause headaches, vertigo, a disorientation that the workers have learned to guard against. They feel the plateau more than they hear it. They feel it in the soles of their feet, in the vibrations that travel through the walkways and the pipes and the stone itself, a deep, rhythmic pulse that is the breath of the earth, that is the reason they are here, that is the thing they have come to harvest.

They have relationships with the fauna, though *relationships* is perhaps too strong a word. The drifters in the pools are too distant, too alien, to provoke any feeling beyond a vague, aesthetic appreciation—the workers will sometimes pause at the edge of a thermal basin and watch the pale, luminous shapes moving beneath the iridescent surface, and there is a beauty in it that they recognize but cannot articulate. The arachnids in the updrafts are too far above, too skeletal, too much a part of the sky's architecture, to provoke anything at all. But the crustaceans—those they see daily, those they step around, those they share their spaces with—those they have a *relationship* with. Not affection. Not attachment. But a kind of respect, a recognition of co-tenancy, an understanding that the crustaceans were here before the city and will be here after it, that the city is, in some sense, a guest in their world.

The workers do not stay long. Contracts are short—six months, a year, rarely more. The plateau does not welcome long habitation. The heat finds its way into everything—the suits, the quarters, the drinking water, the air itself—and over time, it degrades the body's ability to regulate temperature. The workers lose weight. They develop mineral deposits in their kidneys, in their joints, concentrations of sulfur and copper and manganese that the body cannot process and cannot eliminate. They become, in a small and medical way, a bit like the crustaceans—accreting the plateau's chemistry into their own bodies, carrying the minerals home with them when their contracts end, passing them slowly over the years through sweat and urine and the slow, cellular turnover of flesh.

Some of them come back. Some of them sign new contracts, return to the silent city, resume their places in the grid. These are the ones who have found something in the silence that they cannot find elsewhere—not the heat, not the minerals, not the pay, but the *silence* itself, the vast, tectonic quiet that presses against the ears like a hand over the mouth, that muffles the world's noise, that reduces all human sound to a whisper. They come back because the plateau is the quietest place they have ever been, and the quiet is addictive, and they cannot give it up.

They do not talk about the fauna when they leave. They do not describe the drifters or the arachnids or the crustaceans to the people in the cooler world. Not because they are forbidden, but because the descriptions always come out wrong—too clinical, too speculative, stripped of the particular quality that the creatures possess when they are encountered in the context of the plateau, in the context of the heat and the silence and the weeping stone. In the cooler world, the creatures sound like hallucinations, like the fever-dreams of heat-exposed minds. Only on the plateau do they make sense. Only in the context of the boiling marsh and the mineral kingdom and the silent, thundering engines do the eyeless, luminous worms and the skeletal, hanging spiders and the slag-shelled crabs resolve into *inhabitants*—things that belong here, that are of this place, that could not exist anywhere else and would not want to.

## The Plateau Itself

And then there is the largest inhabitant, the one that contains all the others, the one whose life is measured in geological time and whose breath is the steam that rises from a thousand weeping cracks.

The plateau is not alive. The plateau is not a creature, not an organism, not a thing that can be said to have intention or desire or purpose. And yet—it breathes. It exhales. It pushes water from deep aquifers up through hairline fissures and into the superheated air, where the water flash-boils and hangs as mist and condenses on the stone and runs back down into the pools and is drawn back into the aquifers and rises again in a cycle that has been turning for longer than any creature here has been alive. It thrums with a tectonic pulse that is too low to hear but too strong to ignore, a vibration that permeates the stone and the metal and the flesh of everything that stands upon it. It sustains—the chemical froth that feeds the drifters, the steam columns that lift the arachnids, the mineral accretions that build the crustaceans' shells, the geothermal pressure that powers the silent city.

The plateau does not know that it sustains them. The plateau does not know anything. And yet, standing on its surface, feeling the thrum beneath your feet, watching the steam rise from the weeping stone, seeing the pale shapes in the pools and the skeletal shapes in the sky and the shelled shapes on the flats and the silent shapes of the city—all of them moving, all of them functioning, all of them sustained by the same immense, indifferent pressure—it is difficult not to feel that the plateau is a kind of organism. A body. A system whose parts, though none of them know that they are parts, combine to form a whole that is greater than any individual function.

The drifters process the chemicals. The arachnids filter the steam. The crustaceans distribute the minerals. The city harvests the pressure. And the plateau provides the heat, the water, the stone, the silence, the vast, amber-suspended medium in which all of this occurs.

It is not an ecosystem. It is not a machine. It is not a community or a biome or a colony. It is something for which the cooler world has no category—a *thermotope*, perhaps: a heat-unified system of interdependent functions, all of them powered by the same source, all of them silent, all of them stripped to the elegant minimum of what they need to continue. Not alive. Not mechanical. Not geological. All of these at once, and none of them, and something else besides—something that emerges only at this altitude, in this heat, in this strange, superheated lens where the air has volume and the light has weight and the stone weeps without ceasing and the silence is the loudest thing you have ever heard.
