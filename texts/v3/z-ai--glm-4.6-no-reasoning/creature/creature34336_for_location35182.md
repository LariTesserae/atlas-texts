The ones who live here are called the Hush, though they have no word for themselves. A name is a shout, and they have forgotten the shape of such a thing. They are not born so much as they are shed, arriving as silent, bone-blind purlings from the humming heart of the city, their skin the colour of damp slate, their limbs long and jointed with the same gentle curvature as the surrounding architecture.

To be one of the Hush is to be a living part of the circulatory system. Their lives are a routine of quiet maintenance, a constant, tactile conversation with their world. They do not walk; they flow. Their bare feet have a prehensile quality, splaying slightly to grip the slick, fused stone of the causeways. They move with a ripple, a transfer of weight from ball to heel that is so fluid it seems less like locomotion and more like a slow spreading of liquid. This is how they feel the city. They read the subtle tremors that presage one of the great towers splintering, the faint, high-frequency music of a vine forcing its way into a new fissure, the deeper, resonant hum of an approaching hunter in the high walls.

Their sense of self is porous. There is no hard line between a Hush and the damp air it breathes, the bone it leans against, or the moss it cultivates. They are imbued with a profound, cellular empathy. The blood that runs in their veins is thin and cool, and it pumps in time, almost imperceptibly, with the slow, diurnal pulse of the bioluminescent ceiling. When the light waxes and the city is bathed in its ethereal blue, they feel a kind of collective, placid alertness. This is the time for work.

Their work is never of creation, only of curation. They are the gardeners of the great, parasitical vines. Using sharpened spurs of bone, they prune not with a sense of violence, but with careful discernment, encouraging the green to grow in patterns that strengthen, rather than shatter, their walkways and plazas. They are the shepherds of the cleaning arthropods, guiding the obsidian-shelled creatures with gentle touches and chittering clicks that are felt through the ground rather than heard. They will clear a blockage of bone-shrapnel not by moving it, but by showing their meticulous little allies where to dismantle it, grain by grain.

Their dwellings are hollows in the great bone-towers, but they do not think of them as houses. They are nodules, chambers, pauses in the city’s structure. The woven curtains of symbiotic moss that drape their entrances are not doors but diaphragms, sensitive to the unique vib-frequency of a resident. They will contract for a familiar Hush, allowing them to pass without breaking their stride, but will stiffen into a fibrous wall for a stranger or a passing hunter. Inside, there is a soft, pervasive darkness, alleviated only by the steady, gentle glow of cultivated phosphorescent fungi, which they grow in shallow bowls of porous stone. These fungi are their clocks, their calendars, and their candles, their light waxing and waning with the life cycle of the mycelial network.

There is no fire here. The concept is an alien horror, a dehydration that would burn the moisture from the air and curdle the living marrow of the city. Instead, warmth is a communal act. Hush will gather in the deeper courtyards, the cupped-hand plazas where still water pools, and simply be. They lie curled together, their thin bodies drawing heat from one another, their long, delicate fingers interlaced. In this communal silence, they share a form of communication that has no name. It is not telepathy, but a kind of osmotic empathy. They feel the weariness of a neighbour who has spent all day guiding the re-growth of a collapsed causeway. They sense the anxiety of a young one who has felt the deep thrum of a new chamber opening higher in the ribcage. They share this, and in the sharing, the burden is lightened. This is their speech.

What then of their individuality? It is expressed not in what they say or own, but in what they steward. There is one of them whose sole purpose is to care for the great, bell-shaped flowers. He does not play them—an act of sacrilege—but he watches. He knows the precise tension of the petals, the subtle colour shift in the skin just before the resonant *thrum*. When the note sounds, he is the one who interprets it, feeling its vibration not just in his bones, but in the deep, ancient centers of his mind. He can tell if the note is a signal of health, a cry of nutrient thirst, or a prelude to a rare, spore-releasing event.

Another is an architect of echo, though silence is the city's rule. She walks the deep crevasses, where the water gathers into still cisterns. She will touch the surface of the bone walls with her fingertips, then dip her hand into the perfectly still water, feeling how the vibration transfers, how it is absorbed or reflected. Her work is to ensure that the city remains perfectly acoustically dead, save for the flowers and the hum of the ray-insects. It is believed that a true echo—a sound bouncing back on itself—is a sign of decay, a tear in the woven fabric of the place.

They eat what the city offers. They do not hunt the great obsidian hunters, nor do they harm the skittering arthropods. Instead, they farm. They scrape the soft, nutritious algae that grows in the perpetual damp of the towers, pressing it into cakes that dry into a crumbly, nourishing sustenance. They harvest the glistening nectar that pools in the hearts of the bell-flowers after they have bloomed, a rare and intoxicating treat saved for the deepest gloom. They drink the pure, cold water from the cisterns, often without touching it, cupping their hands and allowing the beads of condensation that form on their own cool skin to drip into their mouths.

To die is to be reabsorbed. There is no mourning, no ceremony beyond a quiet, communal gathering. The body of the fallen is not buried but laid to rest in a place of rapid reclamation, perhaps the embrace of a particularly powerful vine or the floor of a deep, active cistern. The Hush will gather around it and simply *be*, their shared empathy a final, slow embrace into the whole. In a matter of days, the body will be gone, its matter becoming part of moss, of vine, of stone, its individual consciousness dissolving back into the great, silent hum of the breathing city.

They are its immune system, its mind, its memory. They are the brief, conscious thoughts of a being too vast to know it thinks. They live and die in the vast, ribbed cradle, their lives a long, silent, and reverent exhalation.
