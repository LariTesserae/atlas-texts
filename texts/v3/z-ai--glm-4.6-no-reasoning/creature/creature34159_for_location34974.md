To live in the Room is to be a symbiote of entropy, a parasite on a god that is slowly eating itself. Nothing here is truly born; everything is exuded, fractured, and reabsorbed. Life in this place is not a matter of vitality, but of patience—a willingness to ride the agonizingly slow wave of the Lattice's final recession. 

The primary inhabitants are the **Crystalline Nodules**, those mineral barnacles that cling to the vast, curving ribs of the Lattice. To call them alive is to stretch the definition of the word to its breaking point, yet they possess a kind of sentience—a chemical, crystalline awareness that spans millennia. They do not grow by consuming light or water; there is none. Instead, they subsist on the Lattice's dying memories. They are the gallstones of the ancient plant, formed from the volatile resins and solidified oils that the Lattice secretes as it unconsciously tries to bleed out the phantoms of the city it digested. 

Inside each nodule, structures of impossible geometric purity interlock. They are data-crystals, storing the compressed echoes of a vanished world—the mathematical imprint of a plaza's spiraling floor, the tensile memory of a tower before it hollowed, the faint, dry scent of chlorophyll that haunts the air. They grow in the dark, feeling their way along the Lattice’s smooth, dry skin, mining the last microscopic seams of potential energy. When a nodule grows too heavy for the surface that bears it, it does not die; it fulfills its purpose. The fracture is deliberate, a slow-motion leap. The fall to the obsidian-like floor is the only journey it will ever take. The sharp *tink* of its shattering is its final word, a brief, ringing exclamation of existence before the Room swallows it again. The supercooled dust it releases is its spore, its final breath, carrying the chemical memory of its structure into the air to settle on the Lattice, perhaps to seed the next nodule centuries from now. 

Then there are the **Threadbare**, the desperate, vestigial remnants of the scavengers who came seeking a foothold in the immeasurable. They are not a thriving people. They are the last, exhausted iteration of humanity, shrunk and hardened by an environment that offers no sustenance, only architecture. They are deeply integrated into the Lattice’s slow cycle of decay, acting as the Room's unwilling mechanics and cartographers.

The Threadbare do not walk the streets; they cling to the walls. They are emaciated, desiccated by the constant, arid vacuum of the air, their skin like the petrified ropes they weave. They have no water, and so they have learned to harvest the moments of shattering. When a crystal falls and releases its puff of fragrant, supercooled dust, the Threadbare are there, pressing their dry, cracked lips to the floor, inhaling the volatile oils. It does not hydrate them; instead, it preserves them, pickling their cells in a state of near-stasis, slowing their metabolism to match the glacial pace of the Room. They are as much fossil as flesh. Their eyes have grown vast and hollow, adapted entirely to the sickly greenish-grey luminescence, useless in the light of any sun. They do not speak; the foundational thrum of the Room vibrates in their hollowed chests, and they have learned to communicate through subtle shifts in pressure, tapping the iron spikes they have driven into the Lattice, sending seismic whispers through the woody ribs of the world.

Their existence is a perpetual, failing ascent. They hammer the hand-forged iron spikes into the unyielding Lattice—a futile, hereditary obsession. They are trying to reach the measureless dark above, believing that if they can just climb high enough, they will find the source of the thrum, the heart of the sleeping creature, and perhaps wake it, or perhaps beg it for water. But the Lattice is unclimbable, and the spikes rust and loosen over generations. The scaffolds they lash together from the Lattice's own sprouting limbs are rickety, treacherous things, clinging to the archways and rents of the ruined towers. They live on these platforms, suspended in the velvet dark, sleeping in hammocks woven from the Lattice's shed fibers. 

Occasionally, a Threadbare loses their grip. The fall is long, silent, and absolute. They do not scream; they have forgotten how. They simply become another punctuation in the vastness, a soft, dry impact swallowed instantly by the Room's heavy pulse. Over eons, the Lattice absorbs them, too—their iron spikes, their brittle bones, their petrified ropes, all slowly compressed into the obsidian floor, leaving only the faintest mineral trace of their struggle.

And then, there is the **Lattice** itself. It is not merely the architecture; it is the apex inhabitant, the world-body. It is a creature that has outlived its own ecosystem, feeding now on the ghost of its own energy. Its "life" is an abstract, geologic thing—a slow, tectonic creaking, a subtle expansion and contraction as it metabolizes the memory of light. It dreams in the language of structure, its sprawling, fingerprint whorls in the plazas acting as the neural pathways of a mind too vast and too slow to form a single coherent thought within a billion years. It does not perceive the Threadbare or the Nodules; they are sub-cellular, fleeting irritants, less than bacteria, gone before its slow immune response could even recognize them.

To be in the Room is to be among these inhabitants. It is to be a tiny, fragile thing caught between the crystal-shatterers who feed on memory, the climbers who are slowly turning to stone, and the sleeping architecture that digests them all. There is no community here, only overlapping solitudes. The Room is a necropolis, but it breathes. It is a vacuum, but it is full. It is a madness of scale and time, and everything that lives within it is simply waiting, in perfect stillness, to be reabsorbed into the final, hollow form of the world.
