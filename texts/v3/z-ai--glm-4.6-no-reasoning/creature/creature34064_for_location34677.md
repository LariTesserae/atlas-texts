To live on the Old Floor is to be a verb, not a noun. There are no inhabitants here in the way a warmer, younger world understands the term—no citizens, no static beings who wake and work and sleep. The plateau’s physics have dissolved the luxury of a fixed coordinate. To exist here is to be in a constant state of translation: from one state of matter to another, from one distorted geometry to the next, from presence into echo. 

The living here are the mosses and the lichens, and they are not simply growing; they are reading the decay. They are the scribes of the plateau’s dissolution. Their rootless, creeping advance is a biological mapping of a reality that is losing its math. Where space thins and tears, the indigo carpet of *Oscillatoria terminus* thickens, drinking from the condensation that falls upward into the sky-wounds, its pigments shifting from bruised magenta to a harsh, arterial white as it metabolizes the ambient unreality. The massive, leather-bracket fungi that erupt from the gneiss are not fruiting bodies; they are anchors. When the substrate flickers—when the cold stone momentarily becomes a blistering pan of hard light or howling desert—they are the biological deadweights that pull the plateau back into its primordial dampness. They cling to the local gravity with a desperate, ancient turgor, their freezing dew acting as a ballast of raw, liquid mass. But there are others. Not the original architects—they are long dissolved, their paths back down the cliffs unspooled into impossible loops, their bodies perhaps still walking the endless, four-day corridors, aging centuries in a single forward stride, forever unable to turn around. No, the current residents are the refugees of the collapse. 

They came from the fraying edges of the world below, climbing the staggering elevations to the Old Floor because they heard the silence. They are the last worshippers of the end. They are a people without a name, because names require a stable lineage, and here, lineage is a spatial casualty. They are tall, emaciated by the cold, and wrapped in layers of woven lignin stalks and gut-sinew, their garments blooming with the same dark, quiet moss that covers everything. They wear the condensation; it beads on their shoulders and drips from their hoods in the slow, arrhythmic syncopation of the plateau. Their eyes are wide and unblinking, adapted to the violet-tinged rarity of the atmosphere and the shimmering tears in the sky. They do not look at the horizon, for the horizon curves visibly here, a subtle bend to the world that induces a vertigo of the soul. They look only at the ground, watching the moss for the telltale flicker of a substrate shift.

These people do not live *in* the settlements; they live *through* them. A ziggurat that takes ten minutes to enter might take four days to exit, and so they do not conceptualize buildings as shelters, but as traversals. They navigate the localized weather systems of the deep chambers—the twenty-foot cubes of perpetual, localized snowstorm—as one navigates a river, stepping around the boundaries of causality with an instinct born of desperation. They have learned that standing at the crossroads of a load-bearing corridor is fatal; to see your own back walking away from you is to risk being pulled into the infinite league of distance that stretches between a person and their own echo. When they must cross such an intersection, they close their eyes and navigate by the sound of the dripping condensation, trusting the wet, expanding sound of growing things to guide them back to a geometry where they remain singular.

They have no agriculture, no industry. They drink from the complex, gravity-fed cisterns trapped within the porous walls of the breathing towns. They eat the bracket fungi, which they harvest with rituals of profound care, cutting only the outermost margins, taking the freezing dew on their knives as a sacrament. They are not studying the end of the world, as the ancient architects did; they are *communing* with it. They understand that the weirdness is not a visitor, but the underlying physics. They have felt the way time stutters in the presence of the mirror-still tarns, and so they treat the shallow pools with a terrified reverence, never disturbing the perfect, unsettling clarity of the reflected bruised sky, lest they disturb the reflection and find the sky itself falling into the water.

And then there are the ghosts. Not the spirits of the dead, but the residues of the living. In the deeper, fractured chambers of the stone towns, where the spatial breakdown is worst, there are those who have lost the thread of their own coordinate. A refugee, pausing too long near a tarn that reflects an upside-down ziggurat in the empty air, might find their shadow detaching, falling upward into the closed, wet eye of the sky-tear. Or, walking down a corridor, they might slip into a pocket of localized time, existing forever in a ten-minute stretch of damp, still air, unseen by their companions who age and move on. 

These lost ones are not dead; they are suspended, iterating endlessly through a broken loop of causality. They are the quiet, active mechanics of the plateau’s population. Sometimes, a living refugee, navigating the dark ziggurat by the sound of dripping water, will hear a footstep behind them. They will turn and see a figure standing at the edge of a snowstorm cube, wrapped in moss-blooming rags, perfectly still. They will reach out, and their hand will pass through cold air, because the figure is a temporal refraction—a version of themselves, or someone who passed this way centuries ago, trapped in a localized stutter. 

These refracted people are the true inhabitants of the Old Floor. They are the population of the margins, the citizens of the dissolution. They stand in the load-bearing corridors and the intersecting halls, their distances stretching into impossible leagues, their backs forever walking away. They are the silent, folding of space upon itself, given a cold, damp, human shape. They do not speak, for sound is a casualty of the thinning air. They simply are, enduring the final form of the world, where the flattening of stone has finally, beautifully, become the flattening of meaning.
