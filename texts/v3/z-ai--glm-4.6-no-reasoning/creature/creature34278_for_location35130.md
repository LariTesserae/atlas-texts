Those who live here, these people of Sere, are forged in the same crucible as their city. They are not built for the horizontal world; they are creatures of the vertical. Theirs is a body shaped by a million generations of clinging, a physiology that prioritizes a low center of gravity and a grip that can hold a man to a cliff face like a barnacle to a ship’s hull.

Their frame is compact and dense, with a broad, powerful chest housing lungs that can draw scant, thin air from the high altitude. Their legs are short, thick with muscle, ending in broad, flat feet that can find purchase on the smallest of stone irregularities, often clad in soft-soled, thin-worn leather that allows for a tactile connection to the rock. But it is their arms that tell the story. They are long, ropey, and disproportionately strong, the tendons in their forearms standing out like woven cords. Their hands are their primary tools and their salvation; broad, with thick fingers and hard, calloused pads that can feel the subtle texture of wet granite or the fraying of a knot by touch alone. You will never see a Serite with idle hands—they are always tying, checking, or holding.

Their faces are a study in endurance. High cheekbones sit above firm-set jaws, their features often weathered to a leathery smoothness by the constant, cold dampness and the biting, wind-scourged sand blown from higher peaks. Their eyes are the most striking feature: large, dark, and remarkably still, adapted to discern shape and detail in the flat, high-altitude light and in the perpetual gloom of the tangled city. They seem to miss nothing. They rarely blink, and when they do, it is a slow, deliberate motion like a lizard's. Their skin is pale, not from a lack of sun, but from a life spent mostly in the shadows cast by their own architecture and the granite walls, punctuated by the brilliant, painful glare of the open sky when they must traverse the arêtes.

They are a quiet people. Volume is a waste of energy in a place where the wind steals your words and intimacy is measured in feet, not yards. Their communication is a subtle palette of gestures, slight nods, and pitched murmurs. They speak in phrases full of knots and splices, of wind-speed and rope-tension. A parent doesn't shout for a child; they will pluck a guide-rope in a specific rhythm, a "calling-chord" that vibrates through the web of the city and leads the child home. Laughter is rare and infrequent, a soft, breathy sound more like a release of tension than an expression of mirth.

Their society has no leaders, only anchors and guides. The most respected person in Sere is not a chief, but the one who knows the most knots—the Master Weaver. This individual is responsible for the city's great lifelines, the massive ropes woven from Spinnerwurm silk that anchor entire sections of the city to the living ironwood trees embedded in the rock. Their knowledge is oral and tactile, a family lineage of remembered strengths and weaknesses in the city's skeleton that stretches back centuries.

The work of Sere is the work of survival, manifested in specialized roles. The Silk-Riders are the bravest, young men and women who glide on woven frames over the abyss of cloud to herd the Spinnerwurms, using long, hooked poles to guide them and harvest the flowing filaments from their spinnerets. They possess an almost preternatural sense of air currents, a deep intuition for the ever-shifting moods of the sky. Below them, the Ridge-Wranglers tend to their flocks of ridgewings, walking the highest, most exposed paths, their bodies tethered to guide-ropes, calling their screeching mounts with guttural clicks and whistles. Then there are the Carvers, who dwell in the deep, damp undercity, their craft is the patient work of chipping new cisterns from the living stone with bone and iron mallets, their eyes adapted to the cool, green light of the phosphorescent fungi that they cultivate.

Life is governed by a deep, pervasive fatalism. Everyone knows someone who has slipped, whose rope has frayed, whose platform has given way. They do not fear death; they see it as the final, gravity-assisted journey. What they fear is the Unmoored—the final, lonely fall into the mist, unknown and un-mourned. To be Unmoored is to be forgotten, to have failed in your fundamental duty to hold on. Because of this, their lives are entangled in a web of mutual responsibility. You are not just responsible for your own rope and your own footing; you are responsible for the rope of the person below you, the platform of the person above you. When a structure is built or repaired, it is a community act, a weaving of not just rope and wood, but trust and obligation.

Their highest art is not painting or song, but the knot. A complex, beautiful knot is not just functional; it is a family crest, a prayer, a record of a successful harvest. They tell stories through the patterns of their weavings, the braids in their hair, the intricate lashings of their homes. They are, in essence, a symbiotic organism. They have bound themselves to the cliff, not to conquer it, but to live within its harsh terms. They are as much a part of the vertical world as the Silent Drakes, as the granite itself. They are a people who do not look up at the sky with wonder, but with a practical eye, watching for the turn of the clouds that signals the coming condensation. They do not look down at the abyss with terror, but with a cold acknowledgment that it is the ever-present partner in their existence.
