To live here is to have never known the softness of a cell, the give of water, the mercy of a drop of rain. Life in this pocket did not evolve; it *mineralized*. It is not flesh adapting to metal, but metal remembering how to be hungry. 

The foundational life here is the Rust-mite, though to call them individuals is a misunderstanding of scale. They are a tissue. A single mite is a fist-sized node of pale, eyeless chitin, coated in the matte red of the oxidized iron it consumes, but they exist only as a contiguous sheet—a breathing, clicking epithelium stretched across the floor, walls, and ceiling. They do not perceive light; they perceive the magnetic bleed of the sparking coils, the micro-vibrations of the slag, the chemical gradient of sulfur. Theirs is a world of static and abrasion. When a mite feeds, it does not chew; it extrudes a microscopic, acidic filament that dissolves the iron at the molecular level, drawing the slurry back through its hollow legs. They graze in circular, shifting patterns, leaving behind paths of impossible smoothness, contributing to the primordial flatness of the pocket. Over eons, they have eaten the jagged edges of the world into submission.

Between the mites, in the suffocating interstitial spaces, live the Wire-slugs. They are the decomposers of the synthetic, the digesters of the macroscopic. A slug is a flat, pneumatic prism of exposed copper sinew and decaying synthetic sheathing, moving via peristalsis on a bed of non-aqueous black lubricant. They are the lungs of the pocket. They press their ventral seams against the hot ceramic veins and the sparking coils, absorbing the electrical static that arcs through the thin air. They inhale the sharp, sulfurous gas, their internal cavities crackling with slow, chemical combustion as they metabolize the mineral atmosphere. When they exhale, it is with a wet, pressurized hiss, venting a vaporized plume of black oil that instantly dissipates, leaving the air thick with the smell of ozone and burnt machine. They do not drink; they are terrified of moisture, which would short-circuit their internal chemistry and cause them to seize and crystallize. They leave behind the vitrified slag—the frozen, dirty puddles of glass—which is the indigestible silica of their strange, metallic diet.

Above this crawling substrate prowl the Stalkers. They are the apex predators, though "predator" implies a vitality they do not possess. A Stalker is a tall, spindly nightmare of jointed scrap and calcified wire, standing three meters high on legs like rusted rebar. They move with a terrifying, herky-jerky discontinuity, freezing for minutes as they process the seismic vibrations of the mite-carpet through their hollow, resonant legs, then lunging forward in a blur of scraping metal. Their heads are merely a pair of scissor-mandibles, operated by taut cables that snap with the deafening report of breaking tow-lines. They do not hunt for sustenance alone; they hunt to maintain the engine. A Stalker culls the mite-carpet, plucking up the slow and the overfed, preventing the substrate from suffocating itself. When a Stalker feeds, it does not swallow; it grinds the mite’s carapace between its mandibles and lets the iron-rich dust fall back to the floor, feeding the slugs, feeding the cycle.

But these are merely the current expression of the pocket. The true inhabitants are the dead. The realization creeps like a chill: the walls are not iron; they are compacted strata of generations-dead mites, their carapaces compressed into a pseudo-iron ore over a billion years. The floor is not stone; it is the fossilized excrement of a trillion slugs, layered and re-layered until it became vitrified slag. The lone, decapitated smokestack is not a ruined artifact of giants; it is the fossilized respiratory organ of a macro-organism that died when the pocket was young, now being slowly eaten by the descendants of the parasites that once lived in its gut.

The pocket itself is the creature. It is a sealed, mechanical stomach, digesting the ruins of its own ancestors. The sound—the deafening, unbroken roar of a trillion scraping legs, the hiss of vaporized oil, the subsonic thud of collisions—is not the noise of life. It is the noise of digestion. It is the sound of a world eating itself, infinitely, in the dark. 

To be alive here is to be a gear in a machine with no purpose, running on the fumes of a dead industry, in a place where water is a myth and iron is mother's milk.
