The ones who live here are not whom you expect. They are not a people who conquered this place, nor refugees who fled to it. They are the plateau's own answer to the question it poses: What grows when nothing can grow? What moves when everything is still? They are a slow-walking, quiet people, and for them, the city is not a construction; it is a continuation of the plateau itself, a way of shaping the water weeping from the stone.

Their bodies are adapted to the ceaseless damp. Their skin has the coolness and texture of the stone they live among—not cold, but perpet6ually at a temperature that feels like a mild, damp cavern in summer. It is a pale, slate-gray, smooth save for a network of fine, darker lines, the markings of their life and lineage. These are not tattoos, but deeper pigmentation in the dermis, mapping their personal history in abstract, flowing patterns that resemble the mineral stains on the walls or the deltaic patterns left in the drying silt of a shallow pool. Their stature is low, their shoulders broad and arms long, a geometry built for maneuvering the narrow corridors, for lifting and placing stone, for reaching into damp crevices. They are not heavy, but dense, with a low center of gravity that makes them seem rooted, as if they could not be easily blown from the walkways by the sweeping winds.

Their hands are their most telling feature. Fingers are long and splayed, but the tips are broad and spatulate, the nails thick and honed almost to a chisel’s edge. They are hands for feeling the texture of wet stone, for sensing the subtle shifts in water pressure, for prying apart the flat, limestone shards without the leverage of tools. To see them work is to watch a form of communication with the rock itself. They do not build; they tend. They find a wall where the water has found a new, faster path, and they reinforce it, stacking a new course of stone, a pale graft onto the old grey. They do not fight the seepage; they choreograph it, guiding the water from the walkways into the cistern-chambers, or from a leaking wall into a channel that will simply dissipate its energy back into the aquifer. Their work is a constant, quiet, generative maintenance.

They are a people the water has entered into. They move with a liquid grace. Their joints are unusually flexible, allowing them to fold and wedge themselves into the spaces between the settling walls. They feel the plateau through the soles of their bare feet. The vibrations of a deep-seep transferring its load, the subtle tremor of a distant rain raising the water table, the minute settling of an ancient block—these are their senses, their weather reports. The thin air is no hardship; they breathe it shallowly, their having learned to draw sustenance from the heavy mist, their lungs as efficient as a sponge. The sound of the place is their music—the *klok* of the lone bird is a punctuation, a mark of time, but the gentle *tss* of the seeping water is the rhythm of their own blood, a sound they were born into and will one day dissolve back into.

They do not speak with mouths and tongues as we might understand it. Their communication is a subtler thing, a combination of gesture, posture, and a very low, resonant hum that seems to emanate from their chests. A change in the angle of a held hand can indicate water pressure. A slight shift in weight can signal danger. The hum is for closeness, a shared vibration that conveys contentment, alarm, or the simple acknowledgment of another’s presence. To an outsider, they would seem silent, a community of mimes moving through the grey corridors. But they are in constant, intricate conversation with each other and with the place that is both their home and their god.

They eat the plateau. The silvery, succulent leaves that grow in the sheltered cracks are a delicacy, peeled with a thumbnail to reveal a watery, cool flesh. They cultivate the sedge, not in fields, but in select pools, encouraging it to grow thick and tender. Its fibrous roots are chewed, its stalks woven into soft, absorbent mats that line their sleeping niches. The pale, blind amphibians are not fauna to them but living pearls, a source of protein found and consumed with a quiet reverence. But most of their sustenance is a kind of alchemy they perform with the standing water. In certain deep cisterns, where the light is filtered through a hundred yards of corridor, they foster a slick, nutritious algae—a film they harvest with flat stones, scraping the green-gold bounty into ceramic bowls fired long ago from the plateau’s clay.

Their society is horizontal, just like their city. There is no hierarchy, no central authority. The city is divided into fluid "wards," based not on status, but on hydrology. One family may tend to the western drainage system, another to the algae cisterns, a third to the windbreak walls. Knowledge is inherited not by proclamation but by participation, a child learning the feel of a failing mortar joint by placing their hand on the wall alongside their parent. Their lives are measured not in years, but in cycles—the hundred-year rains that cause the lower levels to flood, the slow tilting of the Great Outer Wall, the seasonal blooming of the high-altitude mosses.

They are alone, but not lonely. Their world is small enough that a life can be spent knowing every stone, every pool, every trick of the light. The horizon is a concept they do not need; their world has walls. The endless sky is their roof, and its movements are their clock. They do not wonder what is beyond the plateau, for to them, there *is* no beyond. This is all there is: the damp, the stone, the quiet hum of existence. They are the city’s keepers, its fail-safes, the gentle, persistent current that moves through its veins. They are not living *on* the plateau; they are a conscious extension of the plateau’s will, a slow, grey, and profoundly alive people built from the same tears of stone as the endless walls that surround them.
