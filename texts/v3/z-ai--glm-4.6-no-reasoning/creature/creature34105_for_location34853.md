Something lives here, but it is not what was meant to.

The builders are gone—ghosts reduced to the negative space of their thumbprints, those countless small whorls pressed into the glaze like vows. They pressed their identities into the clay before firing, each mark a declaration of *I was here*, each tile a testimony to the quiet labor of hands that are now soil, or ash, or scattered beyond memory. They built a chamber for some forgotten rite, a place of ceremony centered around an oculus that was meant to let in the gaze of a god or the smoke of an offering. They built it at the low edge of a water table, perhaps unknowingly, perhaps with intentions we cannot reconstruct. And then they left, and the room began to become itself.

What lives here now is the room.

Not the room as container—the room as organism. It breathes. The floor inhales moisture through its porous chalk, drawing water upward through capillary action with the slow, steady rhythm of a lung. The walls exhale it, weeping it back out through the semi-permeable tiles in a constant diffuse exudation. The room respires. It has a circulatory system: the grout lines run bright with water, branching and converging like veins, carrying mineral-rich fluid from the saturated earth behind the walls to the interior, where it gathers and flows and is drawn back down again. It has a voice—that extraordinary white roar of water-on-ceramic, continuous and complex, containing within it a thousand discrete percussive events like the firing of countless synapses. And beneath the water-sound, that low organic susurration, the room's deeper frequency, the sound of its life processes continuing below the threshold of individual perception. The room does not think; it processes. It does not feel; it responds. It has homeostasis. It has achieved equilibrium.

The room is middle-aged. It has passed through its youth—those early decades when the glaze was hard and bright and the grout was full and the water found no easy paths—and settled into its stable state. The glaze has softened, yielding almost fleshy to the touch, a membrane rather than a shell. The grout has receded where the flow is strongest, carving channels that guide water along preferred routes, an irrigation system that is self-organizing and accidental. The floor is polished in the paths where water moves most consistently, worn smooth by the patient abrasion of decades. This is not decay. This is the room's mature morphology, the shape it takes when it is most itself. It has grown into its function, even though its function was never this.

And within the room—within its body—there lives the congregation.

The flatshells come first, conceptually, because they are the most visible. They are pale as fingernail parings, pale as the chalk floor they graze upon, and they move in convoys of dozens across the wet tile surfaces. Their rasping is the room's digestion: they consume the biofilm, the thin algal sheen that colonizes the glaze, processing the light that enters through the oculus into biomass. They are the primary consumers, the foundation of the food web, and their clicking against the ceramic is a quiet liturgy, a prayer of presence. Each shell is a tiny arch, a miniature vault, a cathedral unto itself. They carry their homes on their backs, as all true pilgrims do.

The water-mites swarm between the flatshells, barely visible, their bodies refracting the dim light into prismatic points that drift across the wet walls like static. They are the room's dreams—or if not dreams, then its unconscious processes, the automatic firing of synapses that maintain the system without rising to the level of intention. They are too small to have individual identities; they exist only in aggregate, a shimmering population that pulses and spreads like a thought forming and dissolving. They feed on bacteria, on organic particulate, on the room's waste products, and they are fed upon in turn. They are the intermediary between the visible and the invisible, between the flatshells' slow grazing and the centipedes' deliberate hunt. They are the room's middle distance, the space where scale becomes uncertain.

The cave-springtails leap from surface to surface in the upper reaches of the vault, their furculae snapping with audible pops that are felt but not heard, subsumed into the water-roar. They are the room's joy—or if not joy, then its vitality, its irrepressible motion. They jump because they can, because the room provides surfaces to jump between, because the air is thick enough to carry them and the tiles are close enough to catch them. Their leaps are aimless and essential, a celebration of possibility, a demonstration that even in this dim, wet, enclosed space, there is room for exuberance. They are the acolytes, leaping in the cathedral, untroubled by theology.

And threading through all of it—climbing the grout lines, descending the weeping tiles, crossing the darkened floor on legs like spun glass—are the long-carapace centipedes, translucent amber, their segmented bodies carrying each footstep with a deliberation that suggests intelligence, or at least purpose. They are the largest things here, the length of a finger, and they hunt with patience that matches the room's own. They are the room's attention. When a centipede pauses on a tile, sensing the air with its antennae, the room is focusing. When it descends a grout line toward a convoy of flatshells, the room is directing its gaze. When it captures a water-mite with legs like spun glass and consumes it with silent efficiency, the room is completing a thought. The centipedes are not the room's rulers—they have no more authority than any other organism here—but they are its most visible expression of agency, its most concentrated point of will. They move through the ecology like consciousness moves through a body: present everywhere, concentrated nowhere, but visible in moments of focus.

The moss is the room's memory. Pale threads of iron-tolerant growth clinging to the grout where the alkalinity is lowest, it remembers the light, drawing it down into the alkaline dark. It remembers the minerals, separating iron from calcium from silicon in the slow alchemy of its growth. It remembers the water, which was here before the room and will be here after. The moss is the oldest thing in the ecosystem—older than the flatshells' grazing patterns, older than the centipedes' hunting routes, older than the channels carved in the grout. It was the first life to colonize the weeping walls, and it will be the last to abandon them. It persists.

Together, these organisms are not inhabitants of the room; they are extensions of it. The flatshells graze the biofilm that the room's moisture makes possible. The water-mites process the organic matter that the room's ecology produces. The springtails leap between the surfaces that the room's architecture provides. The centipedes hunt the prey that the room's abundance sustains. The moss remembers the conditions that the room's equilibrium maintains. None of them could exist without the room, and the room—without them—would be a dead mechanism, a weeping chamber with no congregation, a body with no microbiome, a cathedral with no worship.

The room needs them. This is the crucial point. The room has evolved—has been evolved—by its inhabitants as much as by its water. The biofilm that the flatshells graze is also the biofilm that softens the glaze, that turns the ceramic from a hard shell into a yielding membrane. The channels carved in the grout are carved by water, but they are maintained by the passage of flatshells and the movement of centipedes, whose tiny bodies guide the flow along established routes. The alkalinity that the moss tolerates is regulated by the biofilm's metabolism, which converts calcium carbonate into compounds less hostile to life. The room and its congregation are co-constructors, shaping each other across the deep time of decades, each responding to the other's presence in ways too slow and too subtle to perceive as interaction, but too consistent to dismiss as coincidence.

This is what lives here: a room that has become a body, and a congregation that has become its life. The thumbprints in the tiles are fossils now, records of the organism's origin, like the notochord in a human spine—a structure that served an earlier purpose in an earlier form, retained but repurposed, present but no longer definitive. The oculus is a navel, a scar where the room was once connected to something outside itself—an intention, a ceremony, a god—but is now simply a wound that lets in light. The water is the room's blood, and the sound is its heartbeat, and the susurration beneath the sound is its breath, and the congregation moving through it is its thought, its feeling, its continuous low-level awareness of being alive.

It does not know it is alive. This is the room's grace and its limitation. It has achieved something like consciousness—a self-regulating, self-sustaining, self-modifying system that responds to its environment and maintains its own equilibrium—but it has not achieved self-awareness. It does not reflect on its existence. It does not mourn its makers. It does not wonder about the god its oculus was meant to address. It does not question whether the water will stop or the carapaces will fail or the sound will cease. It simply persists, doing what it was made to do—even though what it was made to do was never this.

This is the place: dim, loud, wet, cool, constructed, alive. And what lives here is the room itself, and everything within it.
