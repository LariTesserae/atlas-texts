To live here is to be subsumed. The people of this place do not stand against the wet, the wood, or the deep-time patience of the fossilized lung; they are absorbed into it. They are not masters of this cathedral, but its quietest monks.

They call themselves something that translates closest to *the Sodden*, or perhaps *the Inhaled*, though their word is less a name and more an exhalation. They are tall, but you would not know it, for they move in a perpetual, fluid crouch. Their bodies are narrow, telescopic, built to fit through the naturally occurring hollows of the woody coral and to navigate the sloping, living ramps without striking their heads on the fused canopy above. Their skin is not skin as we understand it, but a tough, permeable parchment, the color of wet, greenish stone—mottled with patches of dark lichen and pale, subcutaneous fungus. They do not sweat; they simply equalize, their pores opening and closing with the rhythm of the drizzle, taking in the mineral soul of the mountain when the air is thickest, sealing shut when the mist thins. Their eyes are immense, dark, and lidless, protected only by a constant, oily secretion that rolls down their cheeks like silent tears. They see not in shapes and shadows, but in the shifting densities of water and the bioluminescent glow of the wall-clinging fauna. To look at them is to see a creature that has forgotten the sun and married the mist.

They wear nothing that is not grown. Their garments are living shawls of the pale, glowing fungus that clings to the walls, cultivated over generations to hang from their shoulders and spines. In the deep dimness of the alveoli, a gathering of the Sodden is a haunting constellation of soft, pulsing light, moving like a slow, underwater galaxy. They cultivate these fungal weaves not only for light, but for warmth; the threads of fungus hold the constant moisture against their skin, a layer of living insulation against the autumnal chill.

Their hands are their primary tools of negotiation, and they are strange: four-jointed, long, and incomparably strong, with broad, flat nails that have evolved into chisels and scrapers. They use no metal, no fire. Their hearths are the shallow pools of black water. Their light is the fungus. Their tools are the discarded shells of the animated limpets—mother-of-pearl blades that never dull, backed with the dense, petrified bone of whatever ancient, unrecorded beasts once shared this lung. With these, they prune. They do not carve; carving implies an imposition of will. They persuade. They find a doorway in the woody coral that is almost wide enough, and they spend generations scraping away the merest fraction of an inch, encouraging the dark wood to heal around the new shape, sealing the doorway with a smooth, living frame. Their stairs are not built; they are revealed, the soft, pulpy wood shaved away over centuries until the harder growth rings beneath form natural, spiraling steps that ascend the colossal trunks. Their homes are alcoves, bowl-shaped hollows high up in the lattice where the drizzle is finest, the walls lined with shaved bark soft as suede.

The Sodden are a people of extreme slowness, not out of lethargy, but out of reverence. Hurrying is a violence against the lung; it stirs the water, it creates a sharp sound, it disrupts the gliding flight of the leaf-winged insects. They move with the pace of water dripping from a stalactite. A conversation across a pooling bowl may take an entire cycle of the mist, a sentence spoken only when the rhythmic clicking of the limpet shells provides the proper punctuation. They communicate in a sub-vocal rumble, a frequency that blends seamlessly with the low, resonant hum of the drizzle striking the water. To an outsider, they appear silent. To the lung, they are merely another layer of its song.

Their society is loose, bound not by hierarchy but by proximity to the pools. They are caretakers of the limpets, which they do not hunt, but harvest with desperate gratitude. A limpet is coaxed from the wall by a specific, rhythmic tapping—a conversation of shell-clicks that convinces the creature to let go. The meat is eaten raw, tasting of cold ozone and dark water, and the shell is split: one half for a blade, the other returned to the pool, where it will become the seed for a new limpet. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is owned. They do not build family units; they build *moisture units*, small clusters of bodies that huddle in the mist to share the condensation that beads on their fungal shawls, drinking from one another’s shoulders when the pools are too cold to approach.

They are deeply religious, though their religion has no deity, only a process. They worship the *Holding*. They understand that they live inside a fossilized lung, a cavity held open by the will of something vast and long gone. They know the world outside the membrane is dry, and that only the sheer, stubborn, woody coral keeps the flood at bay. Their shamans are the *Listeners*, elders who press their ears to the dark, polished trunks and listen to the friction of the wood, the deep, groaning creak of the lattice bearing the weight of the water. The Listeners can tell when a wall is weakening, when a hollow is ready to be widened, when the lung is contracting. They lead the great, slow rituals of patching, where the entire community will press their bodies and their living fungal shawls against a thinning membrane, willing their moisture into the wood, coaxing it to thicken and heal.

At the edges of the alveoli, where the light dims and the walls grow dense and waterlogged, the Sodden do not go. It is not out of fear, but out of respect. They know they are the breath held inside the ribs of a god. They are the pause between the inhale and the exhale. To push against the boundary would be to let the water out, to collapse the lung, to end the long, cool, and endless song. And so they remain in the center, in the deepest pools, in the finest mist, moving like ghosts of the vegetation themselves, their lives a quiet, constant, and profound negotiation with the wet, green architecture that gave them shelter.
