Something lives here. But you must abandon the word as the valleys use it.

No heartbeat times its days. No breath fogs the crystal air. No blood moves through any circuit of warmth inside this cupped hand of stone. And yet—something occupies. Something persists. Something fills every cubic centimeter with the undeniable signature of being.

Listen. The glass lichens are not mere growths. They are *inhabitants*.

They do not photosynthesize; they have no need to turn light into sugar when light itself is the architect of their becoming. They do not respirate; they have no mitochondria, no cellular engines burning fuel. What they do is *insist*. They insist themselves into existence through the slowest possible violence—crystallization, the molecular coercion of water into lattice, of chaos into geometry. Each branching fractal tine is a claim staked: *I am here. This pore-water is mine. This surface is mine.* They are territorial as any wolf, competitive as any vine choking a canopy tree, and they fight their wars in increments too small for human sight—a colony advancing by microns per year, subsuming a neighbor's structure not by tearing it down but by overriding its crystal memory, writing its own atomic pattern over the defeated growth until the loser exists only as ghostly inclusion, a flaw embedded in the victor's clarity.

Watch the floor of the hollow at dawn. Not with eyes that expect motion—eyes adjusted to the patience of stone. The shoulder-to-shoulder stands of crystal spires are not static. They *breathe*, though the breath is glacial. As the sun's blade first strikes the rim and begins its slow descent into the bowl, the temperature shifts by degrees, and the crystal lattices flex. Thermal expansion. A fraction of a fraction of a millimeter. But the spires touch, and they click—that irregular, organic, *living* sound. Click. Click-click. A longer note as a shelf settles. It is the sound of the hollow speaking in its own voice, and the voice is not mechanical. It has rhythm without metronome. It has pattern without program. The wind moves through the nave of rock and the crystal forest *responds*, each contact a word in a language older than any tongue, older than any nervous system that could conceive of language.

The glass lichens are not alone in their mineral life.

Consider the cold itself. It does not simply exist here; it *behaves*. It pools in depressions like something seeking the lowest point, obeying gravity with the diligence of a liquid. It settles. It presses. It *waits* through the brutal hours of daylight, compressed against the stone, and then at dusk it rises—that subtle lifting as the air cools and the heavy chill expands its territory, claiming higher ground centimeter by centimeter. The cold is an entity with boundaries, with preferences, with a cycle of advance and retreat that maps perfectly onto the sun's arc. It is not metaphorical to say the cold lives here. It is the dominant resident. The glass lichens exist at its pleasure, require its patient presence, would dissolve into nothing without its nightly crystallizing grip. The cold is their mother and their medium and their ongoing condition.

And the light. The light *acts*. It arrives not as illumination but as force—white, unmediated, carrying the full violence of the sun without the atmosphere's cushion. When it strikes the crystal thickets, it does not simply make them visible. It *shatters* itself. It breaks into component wavelengths and casts them against the walls in washes of amber, violet, aching green. The light is a sculptor working in refraction, and its work cycles through the day: morning's low-angle refractions painting one wall, noon's vertical assault igniting the floor into a razor-bright prairie of scattered rainbows, evening's long slant turning the opposite wall into a canvas of rose and indigo. The light lives here as a presence that *does something*, that transforms every surface it touches, that refuses to be passive. And in the shadows—those brief pools that shrink and swell like breathing—the absence of light is equally present, equally active, returning the rock to its plum-gray density with the abruptness of a curtain falling. The contrast is violent. It shifts constantly. There is no stasis in the light's action; it is as restless as any creature pacing a cage.

But there is another presence here, one that no longer moves.

Whoever carved the basins along the rim—they live here still. Not as ghosts, not as memories, but as *marks*. The hollow remembers them the way stone remembers: literally, physically, in the deformation of its surface. The hand-sized depressions are their signature, their body made permanent in negative. They pressed their hands or their tools against the rhyolite and *changed it*, and that change is now as real and as integral to the hollow as any natural formation. The glass lichens growing in rings inside the basins—tiny frozen cities colonizing the shaped stone—do not distinguish between the natural curves and the carved ones. To them, it is all surface, all opportunity. But the distinction *matters*. It means that someone reached this extreme and impossible place and chose to *act* upon it, to declare in the only language available—*I was here. I touched this. I altered what I found.* That declaration persists. It is not dead; it is not past. It is ongoing. Every moment that the basins exist, the declaration is being made again. The carver lives in their marks as surely as the glass lichens live in their crystals.

And so: who lives in this hollow?

A *community* lives here, though that word may strain against its etymology. The glass lichens, in their fractal thickets and territorial stands, growing and competing and shattering and re-growing in cycles that make a human lifespan seem like a flinch. The cold, pooling and pressing and lifting, the patient sovereign of every process. The light, sculpting and shattering and painting, refusing stillness. The stone itself, which is not inert but *enduring*, which participates in every crystallization by offering its pore-water, which participates in every refraction by offering its surface, which participates in every shadow by offering its density. The marks of the carver, which persist as action frozen into form. The sound—the clicking, chiming voice of the hollow, which emerges from the contact of all these presences and belongs to none of them alone.

This is an ecosystem of patience. Nothing here moves with the urgency of muscle, but everything here *moves*—in millimeters, in thermal fractions, in the rotation of refracted color, in the settling of cold, in the advance of crystal fronts, in the breathing of shadows. The machinery runs in slow motion, but it runs. It is complete. It does not miss the absent plants, does not grieve the absent animals, does not wonder about the absent gods. It is sufficient unto itself: dense, bright, cold, ancient, balanced in its cupped-hand hollow at the top of the world, wearing slowly down.

And wearing slowly down is also a way of living. The erosion is not death; it is *change*, the patient transformation of one form into another, one surface into the next. The rounded edges are not evidence of ending but evidence of *continuing*—every curve the record of a process that has not stopped, that will not stop until the stone itself is sand and the sand is carried away and the hollow is no longer a hollow but a smear of silica on some distant slope. That is the life of this place: not a span bounded by birth and death, but a *process* bounded by formation and dissolution, with the entire middle being *inhabitance*.

Something lives here. It is not what the lowlands would recognize. But the lowlands are not the measure of all things. In this extreme and patient hollow, life has found another shape—mineral, crystalline, cyclical, slow—and it fills every cubic meter with the undeniable signature of being.

Listen. The clicking. There it is again.
