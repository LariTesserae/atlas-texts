To live in Karsos is to live inside a drum.

The people of this city do not have a name for themselves that translates cleanly into the languages of the lowlands. They are the Karsine, though they would not recognize the spoken word. They are a people defined by an absence—of silence, of speech, of sudden motion. They are the custodians of the slow exhaustion, the inhabitants of the weeping machine floor.

**The Somatic Reality**
The low-frequency roar of the Regulator Pipes does not merely drown out sound; it restructures biology. Over generations, the Karsine have adapted to a world where hearing is a liability. Their outer ears have shrunk to tight, cartilaginous ridges against the skull, often tucked behind folds of skin, useless for anything but funneling the perpetual vibration inward. What hearing remains is not the reception of nuance, but the monitoring of catastrophe: they do not hear tones, they feel the *shift* in the chest cavity when a borehole’s pressure fluctuates by a fraction of a percent. Their true alarm system is seismic, rooted in the soles of their feet and the bones of their sternum.

Because the acoustic world is dead to them, the visual and tactile domains are hypertrophied, exquisitely tuned. A Karsine worker can read the age of a rust bloom by its exact shade of ochre, or diagnose a micro-fracture in an iron ridge by the way the encrusting lichen interrupts its growth. They navigate the wide, puddled avenues not by sound, but by the subtle reflections in the inch-deep water—the way the diffuse light catches the standing sheets, turning the flatlands into a bruised mirror. They feel the city’s pulse through the damp soles of their boots: the thrum of the aquifers, the sigh of the condenser coils, the slow weep of the stone.

**The Language of Pressure**
Speech is a dead art. The mouth is used for eating, for breathing the heavy, humid air; the vocal cords have atrophied into thin, vestigial bands. The Karsine speak in geometry and vibration.

Their primary language is a fast, fluid sign, but it is not the sign of the lowlands. It is compressed, economical, designed to be read in the terrible, shadowless light. A tilt of the chin, a flick of the knuckles, a widening of the nostrils—these carry lexemes. Whole sentences are exchanged in the span of a second, a blur of fingers near the chest where the rust-stained work aprons hold the most contrast.

For complex or intimate thought, they use the *batons*. These are dense, cured rods of ironwood, worn in loops at the belt. To speak with a baton, one presses the end against the collarbone of the listener, or against the meat of their forearm, and taps out a Morse-like staccato of pressures. It is a language of rhythm and weight. Lovers communicate in the dark by pressing batons to each other's ribs, spelling out endearments in the delicate percussion of bone against wood. When a mother soothes a child, she does not sing; she draws a baton across the infant’s spine in long, slow, attenuated waves, mimicking the steady, unvarying bellow of the Regulators—a sound of absolute, mechanistic certainty.

For the permanent record—for the blueprints of the manufactories, the tally of the aquifer pressure, the lineage of the foremen—they use wax and stylus, pressing grooves into soft, mineral-scaled tablets. But even this is tactile; the archives of Karsos are not libraries of reading, but libraries of tracing, run over by the calloused pads of fingers.

**The Architecture of Survival**
The Karsine are sparse because the city demands a brutal economy of presence. There are no crowds in Karsos. To gather without purpose is to waste the calories required to stand against the vibration, to endure the damp heat. The population is a maintenance population, a colony of attendants serving the vast, geothermal leviathan.

They wear garments of treated canvas and oiled leather, heavily starched to resist the omnipresent water. Everything is rust-brown, sulfur-yellow, or the pale grey of undyed rootless horsetail. They move with a distinct, rolling gait—slow, deliberate, placing their weight with the care of someone walking on the crust of a half-settled foundation. Speed is an insult to the plateau. The city measures time in the occlusion of a window by mineral scale; a human life is but a blink in the century-long subsidence of a block. The Karsine understand this. They do not rush. They endure.

Their dwellings are thick-walled bunkers of poured concrete, inset with double-paned glass that is perpetually fogged with condensation. There is no aesthetic of comfort; there is only the mitigation of the damp. Furniture is bolted to the floors. Beds are narrow cots suspended from the ceiling by iron rods, keeping the sleeper an inch above the warm water that inevitably seeps across the floorboards. They sleep not in silence, but in the embrace of the roar, which to them is the sound of safety—the sound of the plateau not exploding.

**The Spirit of the Machine Floor**
They are not a spiritual people in the way of those who worship the sun or the rain. They have no temples, no gods of the harvest. But they possess a profound, quiet animism. They know the earth is alive. They feel it fighting the shale, pressing upward, desperate to fracture the karst. The Regulator Pipes are not machines to them; they are wounds, necessary bleedings that keep the plateau from rupturing. The foremen who oversee the boreholes carry an air of solemnity indistinguishable from priesthood. They monitor the pressure gauges with the fervor of surgeons keeping a patient on life support.

The Karsine feel a kinship with the blind isopods that scuttle the weeping cliffs, and the pale nematodes that wriggle in the warm runoff. They, too, are blind in the traditional sense, deaf to the world of birds and wind, living only in the narrow band of sight and vibration. They, too, are feeding on the residue of a slow, mineral apocalypse.

To be a resident of Karsos is to stand in the humid void, the warm water soaking through your boots, the diffuse light flattening the world into a rust-colored cutout, and to feel not despair, but a strange, mechanistic peace. The world below the cloudline—the world of storms and seasons and the chaos of organic exuberance—is a fiction to them. Here, there is only the bellow of the earth, the weeping of the stone, and the endless, patient rust. They are the people of the wet, high desert, living out their short, quiet lives inside the deafening heart of the machine.
