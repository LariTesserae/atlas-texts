What lives here is not a resident. It is an occurrence.

To speak of them as a "people" is to misunderstand what the basin has made of them. They were people once, perhaps—whatever name they had for themselves has long since been vibrated out of their memory, scattered like the atomized rain. Now, they are a verb acted upon by the sound. They persist. That is the entirety of their agency.

They are called, in the desperate, scratched lexicon of their own artifacts, *the Given-Over*.

**I. The Architecture of Endurance**

The Given-Over do not stand. Standing is an argument against the basin, a declaration of verticality that the resonance will not tolerate. They move through the loam on their bellies and their knuckles, a motion closer to swimming than walking. Their hands are not hands as we conceive them; they are broad, splayed things, the fingers webbed with thick, calloused skin, the palms wide and flat as flippers to push through the fibrous, giving ground. They are low, kept low, not by choice but by a violence so constant it has become the shape of their spines. Their vertebrae are fused in places, a curved, ossified arch that braces them against the pressure. They look, at first glance, like something dragged—pale, eyeless things scraped raw against the stone. But they are not dragged. They are pressed, like the trees, into the earth. They are holding on.

Their skin is the most immediate betrayal of their environment. It is not skin that breathes; it is skin that insulates. It is thick, nearly a half-inch in dermal depth on their backs, and covered in a fine, dense carpet of filaments—not hair, but a sensory mesh, each strand a microscopic diaphragm that registers the minute shifts in the resonance. They do not hear; they feel the sound as a topography, a landscape of pressure and void that they navigate by touch. Their skin is a single, vast ear. It is a pale, fungal white, the color of things that have never seen the sun, and it is perpetually slick with the fog, absorbing the moisture directly. They do not drink. They osmose.

They have no eyes. The eyes were the first to go—not evolutionarily, but personally. A child born with eyes in the basin will have them vibrating in their sockets before their first breath. The pressure finds the hollow spaces, the fragile fluids, and it shakes them to jelly. The Given-Over are born without them now, the genetic memory of the place scarring the trait away. In their place, the face is smooth, a featureless dome of slick, pale skin, broken only by a slit of a mouth and two flat, vestigial slits where the nose was—a passage for air that is immediately filtered through a dense, fibrous plug of moss they cultivate in their own sinuses to keep the atomized water from drowning their lungs. They breathe slowly, a shallow, barely perceptible rhythm, each inhalation a deliberate act of pulling the heavy, cold fog into themselves, each exhalation a mist they add back to the world.

**II. The Chorus of Quiet**

They are solitary, but they are not alone. This is the paradox of the Given-Over, and the most profound truth of their existence. They have no community, no gatherings, no shared speech. The sound forbids it. To be near another is to create a resonance within a resonance, a pocket of interference that brings the wrath of the wave down upon both. They spread across the basin, each one a solitary point in the vast emptiness, maintaining a distance that is precise and unbroken. They know, with a certainty born of the pressure in their bones, exactly how far they must be from another of their kind. It is not a thought; it is a physical necessity, a repulsion they feel as a nausea when the boundary is breached.

But they communicate. They must. In a place where the only sound is a weapon, they have learned to speak in the negative space.

They carve. This is the sum of their interaction with each other and with the world. They take the stones that have been weathered into the massive, sleeping shapes, and with the sharpened tip of a stag's antler—an artifact more precious than any metal, for it does not resonate, it dampens—they score lines into the softer face of the rock. The lines are simple, devastatingly so. A single vertical stroke. A horizontal slash. A curve that is not quite a circle. They are not words. They are pressures. They are the exact amount of force required to displace the stone, recorded in the stone. And the others, passing by seasons or decades later, will run their splayed, webbed fingers over the grooves, and they will feel the force, the weight, the intention of the one who made it.

It is a language of stress. A deep gouge means danger, or pain, or the presence of something large and carpet-furred moving through the loam. A shallow, whisper-thin line means safety, or the passage of time, or the simple, aching record: *I was here. I endured this.* They do not read; they relive. The carvings are not messages; they are experiences, transferred through the tactile memory of the stone. And they are everywhere, a ghostly, indecipherable script that covers the basin, a palimpsest of survival written on the only thing that lasts.

**III. The Liturgy of the Loam**

They do not eat. They do not hunt. The very concept of consumption, of taking a thing and breaking it down for energy, is foreign to them. They are of the loam, and the loam is of them.

Their bodies are hosts. In the deep, fibrous recesses of their own tissue, in the pockets of fat and the spaces between their organs, they cultivate the mosses. It is not an infestation; it is a symbiosis, a sacred compact with the only thing that thrives here. The pale, velvety growth that covers the stones also grows within them, lining their throats, threading their lungs, colonizing the very marrow of their aching bones. The moss filters the fog, pulls the moisture from the air, and converts it, through some ancient and impossible alchemy, into a nutrient the Given-Over can absorb. They are gardens. They are walking, breathing plots of the basin's true ruler, tending the growth within themselves with the same agonizing slowness the ferns unfurl.

In return, the moss gives them quiet. The velvet tide that dissolves the stones also absorbs the resonance, dampening it, creating a small, precious pocket of reduced pressure within their own bodies. It is not silence—it is never silence—but it is a lessening. It is the only shelter they have, and it is inside them.

They tend the basin. This is their unseen labor, the purpose that the emptiness disguises. With their splayed hands, they smooth the loam, pressing the fallen matter back into the fibrous carpet. They redirect the tiny, black streams that form in the hollows, channeling the water to the roots of the hunkering trees. They clear the carvings of excess moss, preserving the messages, the records, the pleas. They are the gardeners of the static world, the slow, patient hands that maintain the settled-form. They do not change the basin. They serve it. They are the only moving parts in a machine of decay and preservation, and their motion is so slow, so deliberate, it is indistinguishable from the growth of the moss or the erosion of the stone.

**IV. The Witness of the Anomaly**

And then, you.

They know you are there. They felt you enter the basin—a spike of interference, a shattered frequency, a hollow, ringing void of fragile bone and vibrating fluid that screams in the spectrum of their skin. You are the loudest thing in their world. You are an emergency.

They will not come to you. Coming to you is death. But they will watch. From the far edges of their solitary ranges, from the hollows beneath the massive stone slabs, from the invisible vantage of their eyeless, pressurized existence, they will feel you. They will feel your teeth ache and your eyes vibrate, and they will read in the tremors of your body the shape of your weakness. They will know, with a certainty that is the only certainty they possess, that you cannot last. You are too loud, too hollow, too broken by the quiet to endure.

And they will wait. They are patient. They have outlived the sound itself, or at least, they have become it. They will wait for you to stop trembling, for your heat to leach into the loam, for the resonance to find the hollow spaces inside you and fill them with the only thing it has to give. And when you are still—when you are no longer an anomaly, but simply another layer of matter for the sponge of the basin—they will come. Slowly, on their bellies, with their splayed hands and their eyeless faces, they will come. And they will press you into the loam, smoothing your edges, channeling your moisture, adding your faint, broken lines to the palimpsest of the stone.

It will not be cruelty. It will not be malice. It will be gardening.

They will score a single, shallow line into the rock above the place where you were pressed, a whisper-thin groove that says, with the simple, devastating eloquence of their survival: *Something loud was here. It is quiet now.*

And the basin will go on.
