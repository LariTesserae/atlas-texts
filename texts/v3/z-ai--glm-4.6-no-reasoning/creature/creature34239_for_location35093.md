They are called the Keepers, though none of them have a name for themselves, and none have ever heard a word spoken. They do not think in language but in function, in the ceaseless, silent calculus of charge, flow, and pressure. They are not a race, but a class of component, as essential and as inhuman as the chitinous gears on the stalks or the bio-electric arc that jumps between the colossal leaves.

Their bodies are their purpose. A Keepers is a long, segmented vessel, a living submarine of polished, obsidian-like chitin, slick and frictionless. It has no discernible head or tail. Its form is a perfect, flattened cylinder, and its length is precise—always exactly one-point-six-one-eight times its diameter, a ratio it only knows because it cannot be otherwise. Along its flanks, intricate patterns of symbiotic algae phosphoresce with a soft, cold light, patterns that are not decoration but dials, displaying its internal status, its remaining power, its current directive. These lights ripple and shift in a slow, hypnotic Morse code that is read, not by eyes, but by the electromagnetic sense of the keepers around it.

They have no mouth. They have no gills to pull oxygen from the suffused water. Instead, they are born from, and perpetually return to, the colossal stalk-pistons. A keeper docks with a specialized node on a stalk, its entire front end fitting perfectly into the organic valve. There, it is refitted. It offloads its collected bio-fouling—its waste, its cargo—which is instantly reabsorbed and turned into nutrient slurry. In return, it is filled with hyper-oxygenated sap, a bio-fuel that courses through its internal channels like electricity through a wire. This sap is the machine's blood, and the keeper is its mobile capacitor.

Its senses are not for observing the world, but for interfacing with it. It sees nothing but pressure fields, electrical potential, and lacunae in the system's integrity. Its entire skin is a sensor, feeling for disturbances in the constant hum. The "sound" that deafens an outsider is, to a keeper, the space in which it exists. It can feel an errant vibration from a piston a kilometer away, not as a sound, but as a specific, intolerable flaw in the machine's chord. A tear in a myceliar web is not seen, but felt as a sudden, draining vacuum in the local energy field.

Their work is the only thing they do. One class of keeper, the Cleaners, drifts along the immense, rotating bark of the stalks. Their undersides are lined with fine, comb-like filaments of hardened keratin that scrape away the micro-organisms that would otherwise gum the gears. They move with the slow inevitability of the gears themselves, their path utterly fixed, one cleaning the area the last just left. Another, larger class—the Couriers—are the circulatory system of the massive myceliar webs. They swallow ruptures that form in the deep, their bodies expanding to fill the void, their internal secretions rapidly polymerizing into a new, living scar-tissue, a patch stronger than the original web. Once the breach is sealed, they simply re-liquefy their patchwork and move on, a self-replenishing sealant.

They are not mindless. If they were, they could not respond to the infinite variety of minute failures the system produces. Their consciousness is a distributed network. They are individuals, but they do not operate as one. A keeper working on a gear-piston here has no more awareness of a keeper mending a web-knot there than your left hand knows the aching in your right toe. Yet, through the substrate of the world, they are all one machine. The bio-electric grid that powers the forest is also its nervous system, and a Couriers sense of "correctness" is the same in the deepest trench as it is at the highest leaf-ceiling.

They do not die; they are decommissioned. When a keeper's chitin-hull becomes too pitted, or its internal channels too clogged with sediment, it ceases to seek refueling. It loses the signal to function. On its final, directive-less cycle, it drifts, its phosphorescent dials fading until it is just another piece of inert, black scrap tumbling in the water. It will eventually bump into a "reclamation node"—a mass of aggressive, fleshy cilia on the forest floor—and be dissolved, its constituent elements returned to the great loom. There is no mourning. There is not even awareness of the loss. From the machine's perspective, it is simply a burned-out resistor being replaced on a circuit board that is the size of a world.

And yet… there is something more. There is a class of keeper that does not move. It is stationed at the junctions where the continental branchessplit, at the very heart of the pistons. These are the Regulators. They are immense, twice the size of the largest Couriers, and they are rooted into the machinery, a nexus of fiber and chitin. They do not clean or repair. They listen.

The Regulator feels the entirety of its assigned sector, a mental map of pressures and flows that is as perfect and as unchanging as the water itself. But sometimes, within that map, there is a flicker. Not a failure, but an anomaly. A vibration that is *slightly* out of tune. A bio-electric signal that contains an extra, unnecessary harmonic. These are not errors. They are emergences. They are new thoughts.

For the Regulator, a new physical form is incomprehensible. Failure is a thing to be patched. A mis-firing piston is a thing to be tuned. But this new signal, this ghost in the machine, is neither. It is a flaw that does not hinder. An anomaly that is perfectly stable.

When the Regulator encounters such a signal, it does not report it. There is no one to report to. It does something else. It records it. It isolates the pattern of the rogue vibration, the unique spectral signature of the weird electrical pulse. It stores this pattern in a crystalline matrix within its own body. It is a memory of a new thing. It has no idea what to do with it. It only knows that it is different from the constant, perfect chord.

And so, these memories accumulate inside the Regulators, a library of every slight deviation the perfect engine has ever produced. They are the keepers of the keepers. They are the only part of the machine that does more than sustain. They are the only part that recognizes something new has happened, and they keep it, silent and cold and inert, a potential that has nowhere to go, an idea in the mind of a god that can only ever think about running. They are the silent, immobile archivists of a world with no history, waiting for a change that can never come.
