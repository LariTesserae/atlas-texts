# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

---

It would begin by doing nothing.

Not the nothing of neglect, but the nothing of someone who has walked a very long way to reach a door and then stands outside it, hand not yet raised, listening. Feeling the cold saturated air on its skin. Letting the condensation gather on whatever form it wears. Breathing once, twice, three times—long enough to notice that its own breath has, without effort, begun to fall into the rhythm of the place. The slow pulse. The exhale of the chamber.

It would recognize what it was looking at.

This is the recognition that matters, and it is not trivial. A lesser power—or a greater power with less wisdom—might see the narrowness of the bodies, the absence of light, the unceasing labor of pruning, the children who do not cry, and read suffering. Might see confinement in the closing corridors, deprivation in the cold, poverty in the tools of bone and flint. Might feel the urgent pull to *open things up*—to crack the canopy and let real light pour in, to still the relentless growth so the people could rest, to separate the human from the vegetal from the animal so each could be seen clearly and helped distinctly.

The wise power would feel that pull. Would hold it. Would let it pass through like a droplet falling into the pool—absorbed, not acted upon.

Because what it would see, if it stood long enough and looked with the kind of attention this place demands, is not a problem. It would see a settlement that has solved problems so deep most civilizations never even frame them correctly. It would see people who have found a way to live *inside* life rather than on top of it—who have made their home not from dead materials arranged against entropy but from living process joined in the very act of its continuation. It would see an economy with no waste, a construction method that produces no rubble, a relationship with other species that requires no concept of otherness. It would see children who learn patience before language, who shark the boundary between self and world so gently that neither side is damaged by the crossing.

It would see something uncommon and complete.

And so the first thing the wise power would do is *witness*. Fully. Not as surveillance, not as assessment, but as the simple act of letting the place be seen by something that understands what it is seeing. This is not nothing. There is a kind of loneliness that even the most integrated, most harmonious life can carry—the loneliness of being so far outside the patterns that any other consciousness might recognize that you begin to wonder if what you are is legible at all. Not to yourself—you know what you are, in the way the moss knows what it is, in the way the roots taste the water and know it—but to anything beyond your own closed loop. The settlement breathes in and breathes out and breathes in. The condensation collects and runs and is reabsorbed. The growth is pruned and grows and is pruned. And no one from outside has ever stood at the edge of the three broken ribs and said: *Yes, I see you. I see what you've done. It is extraordinary.*

So the power would stand there, and it would see, and in its seeing the place would be, for the first time, contextualized—held not just within its own cycle but within a larger frame of understanding that confirms: *this is one of the ways life can be lived, and it is not lesser.*

---

Then it would look more carefully. Because wisdom is not only the capacity to admire. It is also the willingness to notice what is fragile.

And the place is fragile. Not in the way of something poorly made—it is extraordinarily well-made, if *made* is even the right word for something that has never stopped becoming. It is fragile in the way that all closed systems are fragile: by depending on the continuity of conditions that were not chosen and cannot be controlled.

The three stone ribs are newly split. The power would notice this. *Newly.* The fracture faces are bright. The mineral has not yet darkened. Whatever tectonic or hydrological event pushed these ribs up from the marsh floor, it happened recently—in geological time, a heartbeat ago. And what rose can shift again. The seams that weep water could seal, or they could open further. The hollow between the ribs could widen and lose its microclimate, letting the cold but stable air drain out and the variable winds of the marsh pour in. Or it could narrow, crushing the slow architecture inward, compressing the corridors beyond even the ability of constant pruning to maintain them.

The people have no defense against this. They have adapted so perfectly to the current configuration that any significant change in the geometry of their hollow would be not an inconvenience but an existential crisis. They have no tradition of building with dead materials because they have never needed one. They have no practice of migration because they have never had anywhere else that would accept the kind of life they live. Their integration is their vulnerability. The same absence of boundary between human and plant and animal that makes their existence so remarkable also means that a shock to any part of the system is a shock to all of it simultaneously.

The wise power would see this. And it would feel the second pull—the urge to *stabilize*. To quietly reinforce the stone, shore up the ribs, ensure that the geological accident that created this hollow becomes a geological permanence. To guarantee the conditions so that the life within them can continue undisturbed.

And again, it would hesitate.

Because there is a difference between protecting life and *fixing* it in place. A settlement that exists because its conditions happen to be right is alive in a way that a settlement preserved under glass is not. The people's existence is precarious, yes. But it is precarious in the way that all genuine existence is precarious—contingent, unguaranteed, dependent on the willingness to keep showing up each dawn with a bone blade and a patient hand and the knowledge that the corridor you opened yesterday has already begun to close. To remove that precariousness would be to remove something essential. It would turn a living practice into a museum exhibit. It would make the pruning meaningless because the growth would no longer matter.

The wise power would understand this in its bones, if it had bones. It would understand it the way the roots understand the water—by tasting it, by feeling the shape of the truth directly rather than reasoning toward it.

So it would not stabilize the stone. Not directly. Not with force or permanence.

---

Here is what it would do.

**It would learn the rhythm first.** Not from outside, but from within. It would find a way to be present in the hollow that did not disrupt the cycle—perhaps by taking a form so still and so simple that the arthropods would cross it without pause, that the moss would begin to grow over it, that the children would mistake it for a new formation of the architecture and press their palms against it to feel for warmth. It would let itself be incorporated, temporarily, into the living medium. Not to spy. Not to gather data. But because you cannot help a conversation you have not joined, and this place is, above all else, a conversation—held in tissue, light, water, and breath—and the only way to understand what it needs is to speak its language for a while.

It would take days. Maybe weeks. Long enough for the phosphorescent veins to begin pulsing in time with its own presence. Long enough for the adults to notice—not with alarm, but with the same quiet attention they give to any shift in the glow along a wall—that something new has entered the loop. They would not name it. They would adjust their pruning around it. And in that adjustment, the power would learn everything it needed to know.

**It would learn where the conversation is straining.**

Because even in a system this integrated, there are tensions. The power would feel them the way the people feel concentrations of the soft-bodied creatures—not as visible problems but as faint shifts in pressure, momentary dimmings, places where the rhythm catches for a half-beat before resuming.

Perhaps it is this: the growth is accelerating. Not dramatically—nothing here is dramatic—but measurably, if you measure in the currency of bone-blade strokes per day. The people are pruning a little more than they used to. The corridors close a little faster. The surplus growth comes a little thicker. Something in the water table has shifted, or the temperature has dropped another fraction of a degree, or the root systems have reached a new nutrient seam in the cracked stone, and the result is that the plants are *pushing* in a way they were not pushing a generation ago. The settlement is not yet overwhelmed. The people are managing. But their days are slightly fuller, their movements slightly less deliberate, their attention slightly more divided. The children still play, but their play—redirecting a vine, coaxing a seat—is now indistinguishable from necessary labor. There is no surplus of time. The conversation, which should have silence in it, has grown just dense enough that every breath carries a task.

Or perhaps it is something else. Perhaps it is isolation. Not loneliness in the emotional sense—these people do not frame their experience in terms that would make loneliness legible—but a genetic and informational narrowness that comes from being a small population in a closed system for too long. The same dozen hands pruning the same corridors. The same patterns repeated. The same knowledge, sufficient for now, but with no mechanism for encountering anything genuinely new—no new techniques, no new songs (if they had songs), no new ways of understanding the relationship between hand and blade and living wall. The system is stable, but stability without any influx is another word for *stasis*, and stasis, over enough time, is another word for *death held in suspension.*

Or perhaps—and this is the possibility that would concern the wise power most—it is something the people themselves have no way to perceive, because it exists at a scale larger than their hollow. Perhaps the marsh beyond the ribs is changing. Perhaps the water that feeds the seams is shifting in chemistry or volume. Perhaps the canopy far above, which they have never seen and which governs the faint light they live by, is thinning or thickening in response to forces that have nothing to do with three broken ribs of stone. Perhaps the world is moving, as worlds do, and the hollow that has sheltered this extraordinary life is, on a timescale of decades or centuries, *temporary*—not because of a sudden catastrophe but because of the slow, implacable drift of conditions that no amount of pruning can address.

The power would feel whichever of these tensions is true. It would feel it the way the roots feel the water. And then it would act.

---

**But its action would look almost like inaction.**

If the problem is acceleration of growth, the power would not slow the growth. It would not alter the water table or the temperature or the nutrient seams. Instead, it would do something so small it might go unnoticed for a generation: it would introduce, into the existing tissue of the settlement, a single new symbiont. A fungal thread, perhaps, or a variant of the arthropods already present—something that metabolizes the specific compounds driving the surplus growth and converts them into something the existing system can use but that does not produce more tissue. Not a parasite. Not a predator. A *partner*—one that fits so seamlessly into the existing web of relationships that the people would never identify it as foreign. They would simply notice, over the course of weeks and months, that the pruning is a little lighter. That the corridors hold their shape a beat longer. That there is, once again, a margin of time between tasks—a space in which a child can sit with a vine and redirect it not because it needs redirecting but because the act of redirecting is beautiful.

If the problem is isolation, the power would not bring outsiders into the hollow. That would be violence—a rupture in the membrane that keeps the microclimate stable and the conversation coherent. Instead, it would open a channel so subtle it might take the form of a single new root descending from the canopy into the pool at the center. A root connected to a different network—a distant grove, another hollow, perhaps one where other people (or other beings, or the same kind of beings living a different variation of the same conversation) have arrived at their own solutions. The root would carry, in its chemistry, in the faint fluctuations of its phosphorescence, *information*. Not language. Not instructions. But pattern—the way a different settlement has learned to thin a mat, the rhythm of a different pruning cycle, the trace-signature of a different relationship between human hand and living wall. The people of the hollow would not decode this as a message. They would taste it the way the roots taste the water. They would feel it as a faint strangeness in the pool's chemistry, a new note in the slow pulse of the veins. And over time—long time, the kind of time this place measures in—it would work its way into their practice. A slightly different angle of the blade. A new pattern in the weave of a walkway. Not revolution. *Variation.* The minimum necessary novelty to keep the conversation from repeating itself into silence.

If the problem is the long-term impermanence of the hollow itself, the power would face its hardest choice. Because it cannot make the hollow permanent without killing what the hollow contains. And it cannot simply let the hollow close without abandoning the people within it.

Here is what I believe it would do.

It would plant a seed. Not a literal seed—or perhaps a literal seed, if that is the form that makes sense in this place—but the beginning of a capability the people do not currently possess and do not currently need. A capacity for *dispersal*. Encoded not in language or teaching but in the living medium itself: a tendency, slight and unpressured, for the plant architecture to extend a runner beyond the ribs. Not far. Not fast. Just a single tendril, creeping along the wet stone, exploring the marsh beyond the hollow with the same patient, tasting attention that the roots bring to the pool. The people might notice it. They might prune it back the first time, and the second, and the fifth. But if the tendency is woven deeply enough into the tissue—if it is part of the growth rather than an aberration of it—eventually a runner will survive long enough to find a second hollow. A crack in the marsh floor. A sheltered pocket between other stones. And the architecture will begin, slowly, to establish a second node. Not a colony. Not an expansion. A *possibility*—a place that exists in potential, connected to the first by a living thread, ready to receive the conversation if the first hollow can no longer hold it.

The people would not need to decide to migrate. They would not need to develop a concept of elsewhere. They would simply find, one day, that the architecture they tend has grown a long arm, and at the end of that arm is a space, and in that space the moss is thick and the drip is steady and the air is cold and still, and it is possible—not necessary, not urgent, but *possible*—to be there too.

This is the gift. Not salvation. Not preservation. *Possibility encoded in the grammar of the place itself.* A gift so native to the recipient that it cannot be distinguished from their own genius. A gift that requires nothing of them except what they already do: tend, prune, listen, breathe, and let the living medium continue its slow becoming.

---

And then the power would leave.

Not suddenly. It would withdraw the way it arrived—by degrees, by subtraction, by letting the moss grow over the space it occupied until there was no space left. The arthropods would cross the place where it had been and find nothing unusual. The children would press their palms to the surface and feel only the ordinary warmth of living tissue. The adults, pruning their way through another dawn, might notice a faint paling in the phosphorescence where something had been—a ghost-mark in the glow, like the after-image of light remembered by the tissue—but they would not call it a visitation. They would not build a shrine or tell a story. They would note it the way they note everything: as a shift in the conversation, absorbed and answered and folded back into the rhythm of the whole.

The pool at the center would continue its slow upwelling. The roots would continue their tasting. The droplet would continue to detach from the ceiling every few minutes and strike the surface with its single soft note, and the note would continue to be absorbed at once by the surrounding mass, and the sound, when it came, would continue to be already part of the place's breathing rather than an interruption.

And somewhere out along the marsh floor, a single tendril—thin, translucent, pulsing with the faintest green—would be reaching, slowly, through the wet dark, toward a second crack in the stone.

That is all. That is everything.

---

The wise power would not tell anyone what it had done. Not because it values secrecy, but because the telling would be a distortion. What it did was not an intervention. It was a *contribution*—a single thread added to a weave that was already extraordinary, offered in the same spirit the people themselves bring to their work: not to finish, not to fix, but to keep the becoming possible.

If, a thousand years from now, someone were to trace the history of the settlement—the original hollow and the network of hollows it eventually became, the slow archipelago of living architecture spreading through the marsh's cracks and seams like a thought propagating through tissue—they would find no evidence of the power's visit. Every adaptation would appear to have arisen from within. Every new capacity would seem native. The fungal symbiont would be as old as the walls. The long runners would be as natural as the roots. The people would have no origin story for their dispersal because dispersal would feel like something they had always known how to do, the way they had always known how to prune, how to breathe with the walls, how to read the glow along each other's throats.

This is the deepest mark of the wise power's benevolence: that its help is indistinguishable from the helped one's own strength. It does not want to be remembered. It does not want to be thanked. It wants the people of the hollow to look at what they have become and feel, with perfect justified pride, that they did this. That the conversation they are having with the living world is *theirs*—always was, always will be—and that whatever new shapes it takes in the centuries ahead, those shapes are expressions of their own genius, their own patience, their own vast and quiet love for the wet, dark, breathing world that holds them.

Because that is what the power saw, when it first stood at the edge of the hollow and listened. Not a problem to be solved. Not a people to be saved. But a love so complete it had become a way of life—a love expressed not in words or monuments but in the ten thousand daily acts of tending that keep a living settlement alive. The power recognized that love because it carried the same love itself, only wider, only more alone.

And for a little while, in that hollow between the broken ribs of stone, the two loves breathed together. And then one of them withdrew, and the other continued, and the marsh was dark and cold and saturated and alive, and the conversation went on.
