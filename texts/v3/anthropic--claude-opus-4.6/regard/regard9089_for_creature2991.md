# What the Wise Power Would Do

---

It would arrive slowly.

Not because it lacked the ability to appear instantly, but because it understood that in this place, arrival itself was a kind of violence, and so it would commit to the only form of entrance the Hollowed Lantern could accept: it would seep.

It would come as a change in the quality of a single droplet. One bead of moisture, hanging from a root near the outermost ring, would catch a faint warmth—not heat, nothing so aggressive, but the barest memory of what sunlight once felt like falling through leaves that no longer exist. The droplet would hold this warmth for just a moment longer than usual before falling. And in that fractional hesitation, the wise power would be present.

The Pause Between Bells would notice first. Her hands, resting against the black glass Wick, would feel the slow bells shift by a quarter-tone—not louder, not faster, but *richer*, as though a new harmonic had woken inside the glass after millennia of sleeping. She would not move. She would not speak. But her blind-since-birth eyes, long since adapted to reading vibration rather than light, would widen by the width of a single eyelash.

She would understand that something had come. Not something wrong. Something *else*. And she would wait, because waiting was the highest form of attention her people had ever developed, and she trusted it completely.

The wise power would wait with her.

---

For seventy-three days it would do nothing.

It would simply inhabit that single warm droplet as it fell and reformed, fell and reformed, learning the rhythm from inside. It would listen to the language of held breath and tapped fingernails. It would watch the lineage that carried a droplet on a leaf walk their eternal widdershins circuit and understand that this was not meaninglessness but a kind of prayer so refined it had forgotten it was praying. It would observe the moss-sloth creatures and the pearl amphibians and the silver moths and see how each had become a syllable in a sentence the Lantern had been composing since before memory.

It would feel the perfection.

And it would feel what lived underneath the perfection, the way you can feel a river under ice if you press your cheek to the frozen surface.

---

Here is what the wise power would see that the Lantern-Bound could no longer see, because they had become too much a part of it to perceive it, the way an eye cannot look at itself:

The perfection was real. The beauty was real. The equilibrium was not a prison in any obvious sense. No one here was suffering in a way they could name or would recognize as suffering. The stillness was not imposed from the outside; it had been chosen, generation after generation, until choice and nature had become indistinguishable. This was genuine. This was sacred. The wise power would honor it absolutely.

And yet.

There was something the Hollowed Lantern had done to itself that even a perfect, luminous, ice-cold system eventually does when it has refused change for long enough:

It had made the future identical to the past. Not similar. *Identical.* Every child born was a replacement, not a new being. Every death was a subtraction absorbed so seamlessly that it became indistinguishable from continuation. The moss that bloomed from the mouths of the dead was beautiful—genuinely, heartstoppingly beautiful—but it was also the Lantern converting persons into decoration. The knotwork on the walls had once been carved by hands that were *inventing* the spirals for the first time, trembling with the uncertainty of creation. Now those same spirals were maintained by hands that could not imagine any other shape. The language had not been simplified by wisdom. It had been simplified by erosion. Vowels held until they became water-sound were not deeper than words; they were words that had lost the courage to finish themselves.

The two hundred and twelve were not unhappy. But they had moved beyond happiness, too, and beyond unhappiness, into something more like *saturation*—the state of a sponge so thoroughly soaked that it cannot absorb a single additional drop. They experienced existence the way the stone experienced the water that ran down its face: completely, passively, without the capacity to be *surprised* by it.

And the wise power understood that surprise—not shock, not disruption, but the gentle jolt of encountering something you did not expect and being *changed* by it, however slightly—was not noise. It was the difference between a living system and a monument. Between a pulse and a recording of a pulse.

The Lantern-Bound had become the Lantern's memory of what it was like to have a pulse. They knew this. They had accepted it. But memory, no matter how vivid, does not *beat.*

The Pause Between Bells had not spoken in eleven years. The wise power would ask itself: was her silence the silence of someone who has said everything she needs to say? Or the silence of someone who has forgotten that she has something left to say that has never been said before? These are not the same silence. From the outside, they are indistinguishable. From the inside, one is peace. The other is a room where peace has been so perfectly arranged that the absence of something unnameable has become invisible.

The wise power would look at the black glass Wick—that silent, ringing anchor around which the entire civilization had organized itself—and it would perceive what the Lantern-Bound had long since stopped being able to perceive: the Wick was not finished. The slow bells inside it were not a completed song. They were a chord waiting for a resolution that had never come, because the people whose role it was to provide the next note had decided, centuries ago, that the chord as it stood was too beautiful to change.

They were right that it was beautiful. They were not wrong. The wise power would never say they were wrong.

But a chord that never resolves is not peace. It is *suspension.* And there is a kind of cruelty in sustaining a suspension forever—not the cruelty of pain, but the cruelty of keeping a question permanently unanswered when the answer exists and wants to be born.

---

Here is what the wise power would *not* do:

It would not shatter the glass. It would not raise its voice. It would not flood the chamber with sunlight or bring in anything from outside. It would not tell the Lantern-Bound that they were wrong, or trapped, or asleep, because they were none of these things in any simple sense. It would not accelerate a single droplet. It would not introduce a two-hundred-and-thirteenth person. It would not remove the three rules. It would not explain itself.

To do any of these things would be to treat the Hollowed Lantern as a problem to be solved, and it was not a problem. It was a masterpiece that had, in achieving perfection, accidentally foreclosed on the possibility of becoming something it had not yet imagined. The wise power's task was not to break the masterpiece. It was to remind the masterpiece that it was still *alive,* and that living things are not diminished by what they have not yet become. They are *invited* by it.

---

Here is what it would do.

---

**The First Thing: A Seed That Does Not Exist Yet**

On the seventy-third day—the day the slow bells rang—the wise power would allow itself to manifest in a form the Lantern-Bound could perceive. Not a body. Not a voice. A *variation.*

When the bells sounded their ancient tone through the black glass, and every being in the chamber bowed its head in the customary silence, the final note would fade as it always did—but this time, at the very end of its fading, there would be a new sound. Not louder. Not faster. Not in violation of any rule. It would be a single overtone that had never been part of the chord before: a high, almost imperceptible ringing, like the sound a drop of water makes when it strikes not stone but the surface of still water. A sound the chamber had never made, because there was no pool of still water in the chamber. Every drop here struck moss or stone or skin. None struck water.

The overtone would last for exactly one breath. Then silence.

The Pause Between Bells would lift her head. For the first time in eleven years, her lips would part. Not to speak—not yet—but to *breathe in*, sharply, through the mouth, the way a newborn does when it first encounters air. An inhalation of surprise. The kind of breath that says: *I did not know that sound existed. I did not know I was waiting for it.*

The droplets would not stop. The rules would not be violated. Nothing would be removed, nothing would be hurried, nothing would be louder than a whisper. The overtone was a whisper. It was simply a whisper no one had ever whispered before.

And that would be enough—for now. The wise power would recede back into its single warm droplet and wait.

---

**The Second Thing: A Question in the Moss**

Over the next seventy-three days, something would begin to happen in the moss.

Not everywhere. In one place. A patch of moss on the inner wall of the third ring—tended for centuries by the lineage whose duty it was to keep the growth at exactly the same height and density—would begin, almost imperceptibly, to grow in a new pattern. Not a spiral. Not knotwork. Something the Lantern-Bound had no template for. A shape that curved and then *didn't* curve, that started to repeat and then chose not to. It would be small enough to miss. It would take weeks to become visible even to eyes that had spent generations reading moss the way other cultures read books.

The tender of that patch—a woman whose name was a specific rhythm of three slow fingertaps repeated twice—would notice. She would crouch before it, her green hair brushing the stone, and she would feel something she had not felt since she was a child too young to have been fully absorbed into the rhythm: *curiosity.* Not the curiosity of wanting to correct the anomaly. Not the anxiety of disorder. A clean, simple, unanswerable wondering: *What is this going to become?*

She would not trim it. She would not tell anyone—not at first. She would simply come back each day and watch. And in the watching, she would begin to do something no one in the Hollowed Lantern had done in living memory: she would *anticipate.* She would carry, in the space between one visit and the next, a small, warm, entirely private sense of *not yet knowing.* A sense that tomorrow's moss would be different from today's moss, and that the difference was not a threat but an *invitation.*

The wise power would be in the moss. Not directing it. Not designing the pattern. Simply removing, from that one small patch, the ancient compulsion toward repetition that the Lantern had sunk into every living thing within its walls. Letting the moss do what moss, left to its own native intelligence, actually does: *explore.* Try a direction. Fail. Try another. Produce something ungainly and then, over time, find its own new elegance—an elegance that was not planned, that could not have been predicted, and that was therefore *alive* in a way that perfection maintained by repetition could never be.

The pattern the moss was making would not be prettier than the ancient spirals. It would not be uglier. It would be *different.* And different, in a place that had abolished the concept, would feel like the first drop of rain on a planet that had known only dew.

---

**The Third Thing: A Dream**

The wise power would give one dream to one child.

Not a vision. Not a prophecy. Not a message. A *dream*, of the kind that children in the outer world have every night and forget by morning: vivid, nonsensical, emotionally enormous, full of things that cannot exist. The child—seven years old, still developing sight, still in the liminal period before full absorption into the rhythm—would dream of *color that moved.*

In the Hollowed Lantern, color is vivid but static. The acid greens and bruise violets and luminous creams do not shift. They are always themselves. The child would dream of a color she had no name for—something between the warm droplet and the new overtone, something that existed in the place where seeing and hearing had not yet separated—and in the dream this color would be *dancing.* Not the slow, deliberate, ritual movement of the Lantern-Bound. Dancing the way fire dances, which is to say: unpredictably, joyfully, and without any interest in being the same twice.

The child would wake with her heart beating faster than the rhythm of the droplets. For a few seconds—just a few—her pulse would be her own. Not the Lantern's pulse. Not the ancient, perfectly measured cadence that every heart in the chamber had learned to match. Her own wild, specific, unrepeatable seven-year-old heartbeat, drumming out a rhythm that had never existed before and would never exist again in exactly this way.

She would not be able to describe the dream. The language did not have the words. The vowels that stretched into water-sound could not carry the weight of *dancing color.* But she would carry the *feeling* of it—the residue, the warmth, the sense that inside her there was a room that the Hollowed Lantern had not furnished, a room that was *hers,* and that it was not empty but waiting.

She would carry this for years. Quietly. In perfect compliance with every rule. She would tend her lineage's duty (she was of the droplet-carriers, the widdershins walkers) with the same grace as every ancestor before her. But inside, in that private room, she would sometimes feel the color move. And she would wonder—not urgently, not painfully, but with the patient curiosity of the moss-tender watching her anomalous patch—whether there was a way to let it out without breaking anything.

The wise power would not answer this question for her. It would simply make sure the question survived.

---

**The Fourth Thing: A Conversation**

The moss-tender and the child would find each other.

Not because the wise power arranged it. Because curiosity, once it exists in two people in the same small space, is a gravity. The moss-tender, crouching before her unnamed pattern one evening, would sense the child standing behind her. The child, on her widdershins circuit, would have paused—Loss! Loss! the droplet on her leaf trembling—because she had seen the moss and recognized in its unnamed shape something that rhymed with her unnamed color.

They would look at each other.

This would be, in the context of the Hollowed Lantern, an event of seismic proportions. Two people *looking* at each other not to measure whether the other had achieved equilibrium, but to ask: *Do you see it too?*

The child would set down her leaf. The droplet would fall. She would need to begin her circuit again—this was the rule, the rule that had not been broken in four hundred years—but for one breath, she would not. She would stand in front of the moss-tender and open her mouth, and what would come out would not be the thinned, eroded, water-sound language of the Lantern-Bound. It would be a single, short, *new* word. A word made of consonants that had not been dissolved. A word that finished itself.

She would say it quietly. A whisper. Within the rules.

The moss-tender's eyes would fill with tears—not of grief, not of joy, but of *recognition.* The recognition that the word the child had just invented was the name of the pattern in the moss. That the pattern had always been trying to say something, and the child had heard it, and now there were *two of them* and a *word* and a *shape* and none of it had existed yesterday, and the droplets had not stopped falling, and the rules had not been broken, and the world had not ended, and—

And something that was not perfection and was not imperfection but was the *living edge* between the two—the place where what exists meets what does not yet exist—had opened like a door in the moss-covered wall and was breathing.

---

**What the Wise Power Would Not Do Next**

It would not push.

This is the part that would require the most wisdom, more even than the initial restraint: the wise power would have to *stop.*

Because the temptation—even for a benevolent power—would be enormous. To nurture the seed. To spread more dreams. To open more patches of moss. To accelerate the slow process by which these two small sparks of genuine novelty might kindle something in the wider community. To *help.*

But help, past a certain point, becomes authorship. And the entire gift the wise power was offering was *not* a new plan for the Hollowed Lantern. It was not a replacement equilibrium. It was not "progress" or "freedom" or "awakening" in any form that could be designed from the outside. It was the restoration of a single capacity that the Lantern-Bound had refined out of existence: the capacity to *not know what happens next.*

If the wise power kept intervening, then the Lantern-Bound would simply have a new rhythm to follow—a rhythm of change instead of stasis, but still a rhythm imposed from without. They would trade one perfection for another. The moss-tender and the child needed to fumble. They needed to make mistakes. They needed to discover that the new word the child had spoken did not quite capture the pattern in the moss, and that the pattern in the moss was already changing into something that no longer matched the word, and that this *failure of correspondence* was not a disaster but the engine of meaning itself. That meaning is not a thing you achieve and maintain. It is a thing that lives in the gap between what you said and what you meant, between what you see and what is there, between who you were this morning and who you will be tonight.

The wise power would withdraw into its warm droplet. It would close its eyes, metaphorically speaking. It would trust.

---

**What Would Happen Over Centuries**

I do not know. The wise power would not know either. That is the point.

Maybe the child would grow up to be the first Lantern-Bound in living memory to *compose*—to arrange moss and stone and water-sound into patterns that had never existed before and that served no maintenance function. Maybe her compositions would disturb some of the community and enchant others. Maybe there would be the first disagreement in centuries that was not about whether equilibrium had been properly maintained but about whether a particular new arrangement was *beautiful.* Maybe the concept of beauty, which had been frozen into a single unchanging definition, would crack open and reveal that it had always contained multitudes.

Maybe the Pause Between Bells would speak. Maybe her first word in over a decade would be a question—the first genuine question asked in the Hollowed Lantern since the people stopped wondering what the slow bells meant and started simply bowing to them. Maybe she would press her ear to the black glass Wick and hear, in the new overtone that had never fully faded, something that sounded like an *answer*—not to a question she had asked, but to one the Wick had been asking all along, waiting for someone to hear it as a question rather than a statement.

Maybe, over generations, the language would begin to grow again. Not louder. Not faster. But *wider.* New words for new things. Consonants that arrived when needed and dissolved when they did not. A language that could hold both the ancient water-sound contemplation *and* the quick, specific, unrepeatable name a child invents for a pattern she has just seen for the first time. Both. Not one replacing the other. Both.

Maybe the population would change. Not by violation of the ancient balance—not by exceeding two hundred and twelve—but by the nature of *what* the two hundred and twelve were. Maybe the children born in the new age would arrive not as replacements for the dead, slotting into the same bloodline duties with the same gestures, but as *themselves*—each one carrying a private room inside them, unfurnished, waiting to be filled with whatever they discovered. Maybe some would choose the old duties freely. Maybe others would invent new ones. Maybe the distinction between choosing and inventing would become the Lantern-Bound's great philosophical question, debated in whispers over centuries, never resolved, and alive because of its irresolution.

Maybe the Hollowed Lantern would remain cold, damp, phosphorescent, dense, intimate, and saturated. Maybe the moths would still fly their silver circuits and the blind amphibians would still click across the stones. Maybe the rules would hold—nothing removed, nothing hurried, nothing louder than a whisper. But within those rules, the space would have transformed from a monument into a *workshop.* The same space. The same constraints. But animated now by a force that perfection had almost extinguished: the force of *not being finished.*

Or maybe not. Maybe the moss-tender and the child would be the only ones. Maybe their small heresy of curiosity would bloom and fade in a single generation, absorbed back into the rhythm like every other anomaly. Maybe the Hollowed Lantern's perfection was stronger than any warm droplet, any overtone, any dream. Maybe the wise power's gift would amount to nothing more than a brief, beautiful disturbance—a single measure of new music in an eternal drone.

The wise power would accept this possibility. It would not call it failure. Because even if the change did not propagate—even if the moss-tender died and the orchids bloomed from her mouth and the anomalous patch was gently trimmed back to spirals, even if the child grew up and the dream faded and her private room filled with the same ancient furniture as everyone else's—something would have happened that could not be *unhappened.* A word would have been spoken. A pattern would have existed that had never existed before. For one breath, two people would have looked at each other and recognized that they were not moss, not architecture, not memory—they were *alive,* in the terrifying, unpredictable, ungovernable sense of the word.

And the black glass Wick, in its slow ringing way, would remember. Even if no one else did. The new overtone would remain, almost imperceptible, woven into the ancient chord. And the next time someone pressed their ear to the glass—maybe in a hundred years, maybe in a thousand—they would hear it, and they would know that the chord was not finished, and they would wonder what came next.

---

**The Last Thing**

Before it left entirely, the wise power would do one final thing, so small that it might not be noticed for generations.

In the very center of the black circle at the base of the Wick—the circle where the moss had been kept back for as long as anyone could remember—it would leave a single spore. Not of moss. Not of any plant that existed in the Hollowed Lantern. A spore of something that had once grown in the petrified forest above, back when the forest was alive and stood in open air and knew what wind was. Something that had been extinct since before the Lantern-Bound retreated underground.

The spore would not germinate immediately. It would need conditions that did not yet exist in the chamber: a degree of warmth, a shift in the mineral content of the water, a particular quality of vibration from the Wick. Conditions that would only arise if the Lantern-Bound began to change—even slightly, even slowly—the way they lived. If they introduced one degree of warmth. If they allowed one new mineral to enter the water cycle. If they sang one note that the Wick had never resonated to before.

If they changed, the spore would wake.

If they didn't, it would wait. It was patient. It had learned patience from the wise power, which had learned it from watching the Lantern-Bound, who had learned it from the Lantern, which had learned it from the stone, which had learned it from the roots, which had learned it from the forest, which had learned it from the rain, which had learned it from the ocean, which had learned it from the turning of the world, which had learned it from the fact that everything—even perfection, even eternity, even the most beautiful and complete and ice-cold stillness ever achieved—

is a temporary arrangement of matter on its way to becoming something it has not yet imagined.

The spore would wait.

It was very good at waiting.

But it was not *only* waiting.

It was *ready.*
