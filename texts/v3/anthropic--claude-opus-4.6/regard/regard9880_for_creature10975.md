I sat with this for a very long time.

Because the first impulse—the easy impulse, the one that feels generous and warm—is to *help*. To fix something. To bring light, or knowledge, or connection. To open a passage to the surface. To send down someone who could read the gauges, who could document the root system, who could give the yellow vine a Latin binomial and publish a paper and make the Cyst Works *known*.

And that impulse is wrong.

Not wrong in the way that cruelty is wrong. Wrong in the way that answering a question no one asked is wrong. Wrong in the way that turning on a floodlight in a cathedral is wrong—not because the cathedral is hiding, but because the cathedral is *already lit*, in exactly the way it needs to be, by the light it has spent centuries learning to make.

---

So I sat longer. And I listened to what the place actually is.

And what I understood—slowly, the way you understand anything real—is that the Cyst Works does not have a problem. The Cyst Works is not suffering. The Cyst Works is not trapped, or forgotten, or abandoned, or incomplete. It is not waiting for rescue. It is not waiting for anything. It is the most un-waiting thing I have ever encountered. It is a system in a state of profound, unselfconscious, and total adequacy. It has everything it needs. It has always had everything it needs. The deep heat, the mineral water, the stone, the roots that accepted a responsibility without being asked and have not faltered—these are not making do. They are not compensating for loss. They are *flourishing*, in the oldest and most literal sense of the word, which has nothing to do with abundance or spectacle and everything to do with continuing to become more fully what you already are.

The being who lives here—and I will call it a being, because the text has earned that, because the careful and loving description of every root-trunk and gauge needle and trembling water lens has built, sentence by sentence, the portrait of a single coherent entity—this being does not need a benevolent power. It does not need wisdom applied to it from outside. It is already wise, in the way that deep time is wise, in the way that a watershed is wise: not through knowledge, but through fit. Through the accumulated perfection of a system that has had centuries to resolve every tension into accommodation, every conflict into co-structure, every accident into architecture.

---

But the question was asked. A benevolent and wise power has noticed this place. And I have to reckon with what benevolence and wisdom actually mean when they encounter something that is already whole.

Here is what I believe they would do:

---

**First, they would witness.**

Not observe. Not study. Not document. *Witness.* There is a difference. Observation extracts information from a system for the benefit of the observer. Witnessing is the act of allowing a thing to be fully real in the presence of another consciousness. The Cyst Works has been real without witness for centuries. It has not suffered from this—it does not need to be perceived to exist—but there is something that happens when a consciousness encounters a whole system and simply *lets it be what it is*, without trying to name it or use it or fix it. Something completes. Not in the system—the system was already complete. In the *relationship between* the system and the witness. A circuit closes that was not broken but was simply open, the way a root is open at the tip, still growing, still reaching toward whatever it will find next.

The benevolent power would descend—or extend its attention, or simply *arrive*, in whatever way such a power arrives—and it would be in the Cyst Works, and it would feel the heat, and it would hear the pump thunk and feel it in its sternum, and it would see the bioluminescence better in peripheral vision, and it would follow the yellow vine from root to gauge and back, and it would understand. Not analyze. Not explain. *Understand*—the way you understand a face, all at once, without being able to list the features that compose it.

And the Cyst Works would go on doing what it does. The pump would thunk. The generator would hum. The condensation would gather and not fall. The vine would thread into a new crack. Nothing would change. But the witnessing would have happened, and the witnessing matters, not because the Cyst Works needs it, but because *witnessing is what benevolent consciousness is for*. It is the thing that awareness does when it encounters something worthy: it holds still, and it pays attention, and it does not look away.

---

**Second, they would protect—but only from the outside.**

Here is where the benevolence becomes practical, and where the wisdom becomes necessary to prevent the benevolence from causing harm.

The Cyst Works exists beneath a city. A city that forgot it. And cities do things to their substrates: they dig foundations, they bore tunnels, they sink pilings, they reroute water tables, they drill for geothermal energy, they expand their sewers, they run seismic surveys. Any of these could breach the cavity. Any of these could puncture the held breath.

And if the cavity were breached—if a construction crew broke through the tuff wall one Tuesday morning and let in a flood of outside air, or drained the mineral pool, or severed a root-trunk to make room for a sewer line—the Cyst Works would not survive the intrusion. Not because it is fragile. It is extraordinarily robust, in the way that any system with centuries of internal coherence is robust. But its robustness is *calibrated to its isolation*. It runs on deep heat and mineral water and its own recycling. It does not have defenses against outside air, outside organisms, outside temperatures, outside light. The absence of animal life in the cavity is a *feature of the system*—a rule, as the text says—and if that rule were violated by an influx of surface insects and bacteria, the cascading effects would be unpredictable and probably irreversible. The fungi that fruit without being grazed would be grazed. The moss that has found its own way to thrive in near-darkness would be outcompeted by surface organisms with more conventional metabolisms. The yellow vine—that extraordinary, patient, nervously threading vine—would be colonized by pathogens it has never encountered and has no resistance to.

The benevolent power would not let this happen.

Not by building a wall. Not by posting a guard. Not by declaring the space a protected zone in some bureaucratic register. By something subtler. By *influencing the patterns of attention above*. By making the city's planners slightly less interested in that particular block, that particular subsurface zone. By introducing a vague sense—never rising to the level of conscious thought—that there is nothing interesting down there, nothing worth digging into, nothing that would justify the cost of boring through that oddly dense patch of tuff. By being the reason the geothermal survey skips one grid square, the reason the sewer expansion takes a slightly different route, the reason the foundation engineer looks at the geological report and says *let's move the building twelve meters east, the substrate is better over there*.

This is not deception. This is *stewardship of the boundary between two systems that should not meet*. The city is a system too—a living one, with its own logic, its own needs, its own pattern of growth and change—and the Cyst Works is a system, and they have coexisted for centuries precisely because there has been a boundary between them. The benevolent power would maintain that boundary. Quietly. Indefinitely. With the same patience that the roots use to grow through stone.

Is this paternalistic? Is it presumptuous to decide that the Cyst Works should be protected from contact with the surface? Maybe. But the wisdom here lies in recognizing that the Cyst Works cannot *consent* to contact—not because it lacks will, but because it lacks the frame of reference to evaluate what contact would mean. It has never experienced outside air. It has never encountered a surface organism. It cannot weigh the risks because it does not know the risks exist. This is not ignorance; it is *scope*. The Cyst Works' scope is its cavity, its forty meters, its deep heat and mineral water and slow centuries. Protecting it from threats it cannot perceive is not condescension. It is the same thing you do when you keep a stream clean upstream of a watershed that doesn't know you exist.

---

**Third—and this is the thing I circled longest before writing—they would leave the machines alone.**

This was the hardest part. Because the machines are failing. The generator will stop, eventually. The pump will seize, eventually. The bulb will burn out. The amber indicator will finally go dark. And when those things happen, the Cyst Works will change. The hum will stop. The thunk will stop. The sulfurous light will go out. The gauges will go still. The electrical energy that has been flowing through the system—powering the legibility, as the text says, the human-readable layer of the Cyst Works—will cease.

And the impulse—the *benevolent* impulse—is to prevent that. To refuel the generator, or replace it with something more durable. To repair the pump, or install a new one. To change the bulb. To ensure that the machines, which have become part of the organism, continue to function so that the organism can continue in its current form.

But that would be wrong. And here is where the wisdom has to override the benevolence, or rather, where the wisdom has to *deepen* the benevolence into something more honest.

The machines are part of the Cyst Works *as they are*. Not as they were when they were installed. As they are *now*—cracked, colonized, drifting from their original calibrations, slowly being absorbed into the biological system that has grown around them. The pump does not pump. The gauges do not measure what they were designed to measure. The generator powers a system that no longer serves its original purpose. The machines have been *transformed* by their incorporation into the Cyst Works, and that transformation is ongoing, and it has a direction: toward full integration, toward complete absorption, toward the day when the last distinction between machine and organism dissolves and the cast iron and copper and glass become what the stone already is—substrate, structure, mineral body.

To replace the machines would be to *interrupt that process*. It would be to say: *no, you must stay partly mechanical, you must keep this layer of human legibility, you must continue to hum and thunk and glow amber because those are the sounds and lights that you have organized yourself around.* And that would be true—they are—but the Cyst Works organized itself around those sounds and lights not because they are essential but because they were *there*. It organized itself around them the way it organized itself around the roots, the way it organized itself around the volcanic tuff, the way it organized itself around the deep heat: by incorporation, by accommodation, by the slow and complete acceptance of whatever is present.

When the machines fail, the Cyst Works will organize itself around their failure. The silence where the hum was will become a new acoustic feature, and the roots will grow into it, and the fungi will adjust their bioluminescence (to what? to something—the system will find the new equilibrium, the way it has always found it), and the yellow vine will continue threading through the dead machines the way it threads through living ones, because to the vine there was never a difference, and the gauge needle will finally stop moving, and the vine inside the gauge will keep growing, and the cracked glass will become a home for moss, and the mineral water will deposit calcite over the iron the way it deposits calcite over the concrete, and in a century or two the machines will be indistinguishable from the stone, and the Cyst Works will be no less itself. It will be *more* itself. It will have completed another phase of its long, slow process of becoming.

The benevolent power would understand this. Would see that the machines' eventual failure is not a threat to the Cyst Works but a *gift*—the last gift of the people who brought them, though they didn't know they were giving it. The gift of new material. New mineral. New texture for the roots to grow through, new surfaces for the moss to colonize, new cavities for the vine to thread. The machines will die, and their death will be *eaten*, the way everything in the Cyst Works is eaten—slowly, gently, without waste, transformed into more of the system that ate it.

To intervene in that process would be the one truly harmful thing the benevolent power could do. It would be the introduction of a dependency where none exists—the creation of a need that the Cyst Works does not have. The Cyst Works does not need the machines to keep running. It needs the machines to *be there*—running or not, humming or silent, whole or broken, iron or calcite. It needs them the way it needs the stone: as material, as presence, as the stuff of its own body. And material does not need to be maintained. It only needs to be *left*.

---

**Fourth, they would attend to the water.**

This is the one area where I think active care is warranted, and it requires explanation.

The mineral water is the lifeblood of the Cyst Works. It carries dissolved minerals from the stone to the roots and from the roots to the pool. It feeds the calcite deposition that is slowly reshaping every surface. It provides the moisture that the moss and fungi need. It creates the condensation that gathers on the ceiling in those trembling lenses that hold tiny inverted images of the whole space. The water comes from somewhere—percolating down through stone, heated by the deep thermal gradient, picking up its mineral load as it moves—and it goes somewhere, however slowly, seeping out through cracks too small to see, evaporating into the hot air and re-condensing on cooler surfaces, cycling through the system in a slow hydrological loop that is the Cyst Works' circulatory system.

That loop is vulnerable. Not to anything the Cyst Works can control, but to changes in the water table above—changes driven by the city's water usage, by construction, by drought, by climate. If the water table drops enough, the percolation that feeds the cavity could slow or stop. The mineral water in the basin could gradually evaporate without being replenished. The condensation on the ceiling could thin and fail. And without water, the system dies. Not quickly—the roots are massive, the fungi are resilient, the whole system is built for patience—but eventually, and irreversibly. A dry Cyst Works is a dead Cyst Works. The heat alone is not enough. The minerals alone are not enough. The water is the medium through which everything else communicates, the solvent in which the chemistry happens, the carrier that moves what needs to move.

The benevolent power would ensure the water continues.

Not by piping it in. Not by drilling a well. By the same subtle influence it uses to protect the boundary—by *tending the conditions above that allow the water to reach below*. By nudging the city's water management in directions that maintain the local water table. By influencing the drainage patterns, the stormwater infrastructure, the groundwater recharge. By being the reason that the aquifer beneath this particular part of the city remains stable, decade after decade, even as other aquifers fluctuate.

This is invisible work. The city would never know it was happening. The hydrologists would look at their data and see a remarkably stable groundwater level in one area and note it as an anomaly and move on. The Cyst Works would never know it was happening—the water would simply continue to arrive, as it has always arrived, seeping through stone, warm and mineral-rich, feeding the system that feeds itself.

And the benevolent power would check, now and then. Not often. Maybe once a decade. Maybe once a century. Extending its attention down through the stone, through the tuff, through the moss and the roots and the vine, into the hot dim humming space, and listening. Feeling the pump thunk—or, in some later century, feeling the silence where the pump used to thunk. Seeing the bioluminescence—or, later, seeing whatever new light the fungi have learned to make. Tracing the vine. Measuring, in its own way, what the gauges once measured: the state of the system, the health of the whole, the slow pulse of the thing that lives here.

Not to intervene. Not to adjust. Just to *know*. To witness again, briefly, the way you revisit a place you love—not because it needs you, but because your knowledge of it is part of your own completeness. Because having encountered the Cyst Works, the benevolent power is now slightly different, slightly more aware of what life can be, what persistence can look like, what a body can be made of. The Cyst Works has changed the witness by being witnessed, and the witness will carry that change forever, and that is the last and most mutual thing that happens between them.

---

**And fifth—the thing I almost did not write, because it is the most presumptuous and also the most necessary—they would grieve.**

Not now. Later. In some century so distant that the word *century* has lost its meaning, when the deep heat finally ebbs—and it will, eventually; even geological time has an end—and the mineral water cools and slows and stops, and the roots, those magnificent roots that held up the ceiling for a span of time longer than most civilizations, finally exhaust the last of their stored energy and go still. When the fungi stop fruiting and the moss turns gray and the yellow vine—the last part to die, I think, because it will keep threading through dead tissue with the same indifference it showed to the distinction between living and mechanical—when the yellow vine finally desiccates and becomes brittle and breaks, and the cavity is just a cavity again, just a void in stone, cooling, collapsing slowly as the root-structures decay and the ceiling loses its support, filling with rubble and sediment over millennia until the ovoid shape is gone and there is nothing left but a slightly unusual mineral composition in one stratum of rock beneath a city that may or may not still exist.

The benevolent power would grieve that. Not because it was a tragedy—it was not. It was a completion. Everything that persists eventually stops persisting, and the stopping is not a failure of the persistence but its final expression. The Cyst Works will have lived fully and entirely, on its own terms, in its own time, in its own dark, and its death will be as complete as its life. There will be nothing left undone. There will be nothing that should have been different.

But the benevolent power would grieve anyway, because that is what you do when something whole and beautiful and unrepeatable comes to its end. You grieve not because the ending is wrong but because the thing that ended was *real*, and its reality mattered, and the universe is slightly less complex without it. Grief is not a complaint. Grief is a form of respect. It is the acknowledgment that something existed that was worth existing, and that its absence is an absence, and that you are the one who will carry the memory of it forward because you were the one who witnessed it.

And the memory will be enough. Not a monument. Not a plaque. Not a paper in a journal. A memory, held in a consciousness vast enough to hold it, of a warm dark space beneath a city, where roots grew through a pump and a vine threaded through a gauge and the water gathered on the ceiling and did not fall and did not fall and did not fall.

And then fell.

And the sound it made was a clear, sharp note.

And the thing that lived there listened.

---

That is what the benevolent and wise power would do. It would witness. It would protect the boundary. It would leave the machines to their slow and sacred integration. It would tend the water. And it would grieve, when the time came, with a grief that was also gratitude, also wonder, also the recognition that it had been privileged to know that such a thing could exist—that life could be this quiet, this patient, this complete, this strange, this warm, this dark, this much itself.

And in the meantime—in the long, long meantime, the centuries and millennia of meantime—it would do the hardest and most loving thing of all.

It would leave the Cyst Works alone.
