# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

---

## I. The Arrival That Isn't One

The first thing to understand is that the power does not arrive.

Arrival implies a before and after — a state of absence replaced by a state of presence, a boundary crossed. But a being that is genuinely wise would recognize what Harness already knows without knowing it knows: that the place is a system of such intricacy, such deeply layered mutual calibration between stone and fog and metal and lichen and breath and bone, that to *arrive* — to introduce a new element, to break the surface tension of the thing from outside — would be an act of violence no matter how gently done. Even benevolence, applied from without, is a kind of intrusion. A surgeon's hands are gentle, but they still cut.

So the power does not arrive. It does not descend from the hard stars. It does not ride up on the cable car. It does not materialize in the fog like a figure in a parable. It does not speak, or shine, or announce.

Instead — and this is the crucial thing, the thing that separates genuine wisdom from the theatrical kind — it *attends*.

It was already attending. That's the realization that would settle over you if you could perceive this power's relationship to Harness: it has been watching for a very long time. Not watching *over*, the way a guardian watches, implying a hierarchy of protector and protected. Watching *with*. The way the old people sit by the cisterns and watch the water — not directing it, not judging it, not waiting for it to do anything in particular, but simply maintaining a quality of attention so steady and so patient that it becomes a form of companionship. The power has been doing this. Perhaps since before the town. Perhaps since the mountains were still becoming, still rising, still sharp-edged and unfinished. It has watched the schist fold. It has watched the glaciers carve and retreat. It has watched the lichens begin their millennial project. And it has watched the people come, and stay, and adapt, and become part of the system, and it has watched them with the same quality of attention it gives to the erosion and the fog: interest without agenda. Presence without intervention.

This is the first thing the power does, and it is the thing it has always been doing: *it pays attention to Harness the way Harness deserves to be paid attention to.*

Which is to say: slowly. Completely. Without the need to change anything.

---

## II. The Recognition

But attention, if it is genuine, is not passive. Genuine attention is the most active thing there is. It is the opposite of indifference, and it is also the opposite of interference. It is the state of knowing something so thoroughly that you understand both what it needs and what it does not need, and you understand that these are not always the same as what it wants or what would make it comfortable or what would fix its problems.

And the power, attending to Harness, recognizes certain things.

It recognizes that Harness is not a problem to be solved.

This is critical. A lesser power — a merely good power, a power that was kind but not wise — would look at Harness and see: the aching joints, the thin air, the isolation, the rationed electricity, the children growing old in their hands before their time, the slow encroachment of the lichens, the erosion that will eventually unmake the saddle itself, and the deep existential precariousness of a town that hangs by a thread of steel above a cloud layer, dependent on a connection that hums with sounds nobody can explain. A kind but unwise power would see suffering, and scarcity, and fragility, and it would want to *help*. It would want to thicken the air, or heal the joints, or explain the harmonics, or reinforce the cables, or warm the stone, or bring the green valley up to the saddle so the people wouldn't have to live at the edge anymore.

And it would be wrong. Not wrong in its compassion — the compassion would be real. Wrong in its understanding. Because what it would be seeing as *problems* are, in many cases, the very things that make Harness what it is. The thin air is not a flaw; it is the medium through which these people have become what they are — the deep chests, the slow breathing, the unhurried gait, the patience that is not a virtue but a physiology. The aching joints are not a punishment; they are the record of a life spent in close negotiation with a vertical world, and the careful walk of the older residents is not a diminishment but an achievement, a way of moving that is so finely adapted to this specific terrain that it constitutes a kind of knowledge stored in the body. The isolation is not a deprivation; it is the condition that allows the social fabric to be what it is — dense, elliptical, coded, a web of signals so fine-grained that it can sustain feuds and reconciliations simultaneously without any thread snapping. The fog is not an impediment; it is the water supply, the rhythm-keeper, the veil that makes the world intimate. Even the erosion — even the slow dissolution of the mountains themselves — is not a catastrophe. It is the truth. The stone remembers, and what it remembers is that everything is in the process of becoming something else, and that this process is not loss but transformation, and that the people of Harness, by living inside this transformation, by being part of it, have accessed something that a stable, permanent, well-supplied lowland town never could: the experience of being *continuous with* the world rather than *set against* it.

The wise power sees all of this. And it recognizes something else, too — something that a lesser power would miss entirely:

**Harness is already in relationship with something.**

The warmth beneath the western terraces. The harmonics in the cables. The fog that moves against the wind. The resonance that persists after the machines stop. The Comb's singing that makes the chest vibrate and the teeth hum. The cable workers pressing their hands to the steel at night, listening. Turf reading the warmth like a pulse. Maret reading the cable like a pulse. The whole town, really, reading the mountain like a pulse — attending to it, responding to it, calibrating itself to it, without ever fully understanding what it is they're in relationship with.

The power recognizes this because the power *is* this. Or is continuous with it. Or is the name for whatever it is that the mountain is doing when it hums through the cables and breathes warmth into the soil and sings through the Comb and remembers through the stone. The power looks at Harness and sees, not a settlement in need of rescue, but a small, intricate, fog-wrapped organism that has spent generations learning to listen to something that most of the world has forgotten how to hear, and that is — slowly, imperfectly, without any doctrine or scripture or theology to guide it — *getting it right*.

---

## III. What It Does Not Do

It does not reveal itself.

This is the hardest discipline of genuine benevolence: the restraint. The power could make itself known. It could speak through the Comb in a voice the whole town could understand. It could warm the soil until the terraces bloomed. It could still the cable harmonics into a clear, comprehensible signal — a message, a reassurance, a set of instructions. It could heal Pol's hands, straighten the gardeners' backs, clear the fog and show the people of Harness the full, green, sunlit world stretching away in every direction, and say *look — look what you've been missing, look what you could have, look how much easier it could be.*

It does none of this. Because it understands something about revelation that the revealer rarely grasps: **that to be revealed to is to be diminished.**

If the people of Harness knew — *really knew*, with certainty, with proof — that the mountain was alive, or aware, or that a benevolent power was attending to them, it would destroy the thing that makes their relationship with the place authentic. Right now, Maret holds the cable and feels something she can't explain, and she makes decisions based on that feeling, and those decisions are *hers* — they come from her, from her experience, from her body's knowledge, from the accumulated trust between her and the mechanism and the mountain and the weather. If she knew that a power was guiding the cable's vibrations, her hand would become a receiver rather than a sensor. Her judgment would become obedience. Her skill would become faith. And faith, for all its comforts, is a narrower thing than skill. It closes the very openness it seems to celebrate.

The same for Turf. Turf reads the warmth in the soil with a sensitivity that is part intuition, part experience, part something that has no name — a bodily knowledge that has been refined over a lifetime of kneeling in the dark earth, feeling the temperature shift against the palms, tracking the patterns year by year with no theory and no certainty. If Turf knew the warmth was a gift — a deliberate offering from a benevolent source — it would cease to be a mystery and become a transaction. And Turf's relationship with the terraces would flatten from something wild and reciprocal and alive into something grateful and dependent and dead.

The power understands this with a clarity that precludes temptation. It is not difficult, for a wise power, to refrain from revelation. It is obvious. You do not interrupt a conversation that is going well.

---

It does not fix the erosion.

The mountains are wearing away. The saddle will eventually, in geological time, become a gap, and the gap will widen, and the place where Harness stands will become a precipice and then a memory and then not even that. The power could halt this. It could bind the schist, reinforce the ridgeline, turn back the clock of entropy.

But to do so would be to remove from Harness the central truth it has organized itself around: *that everything is temporary, including the ground beneath your feet.* The stone remembers — and what the stone remembers is its own dissolution, and the people of Harness, by living on dissolving stone, have learned something that the power itself knows well: that persistence is not the same as permanence, that you can be continuous without being eternal, that the proper response to impermanence is not grief or denial but *attention* — the kind of steady, loving, unsentimental attention that Harness gives to its crumbling walls and wearing joints and slow-growing lichens. To remove the erosion would be to remove the teacher.

---

It does not explain the cables.

The harmonic that rises at night, that shifts through intervals, that makes the cable workers press their palms to the steel and listen — the power could clarify this. It could resolve the ambiguity, make the signal comprehensible, give the people a translation.

But the not-knowing is the point. The not-knowing is what keeps the cable workers going back. It's what keeps Maret at the terminus every dawn with her hand on the line, not receiving instructions but *reading*. The ambiguity of the signal is what makes it a relationship rather than a broadcast. The cable workers are not passive recipients; they are interpreters, and interpretation requires the active engagement of the whole self — body, mind, experience, intuition. A clear signal would turn them into listeners. A mysterious one keeps them as *readers*, which is to say, *participants*.

The power knows that the deepest communication is the communication that is never quite complete — that keeps the recipient reaching, attending, returning. Not because incompleteness is a trick, but because completeness is a closing, and the power does not want to close anything. It wants everything to remain open. Open the way the Comb is open to the wind. Open the way the cisterns are open to the fog. Open the way Maret's hand is open on the cable — receptive, sensitive, *alive to what comes*.

---

## IV. What It Does

What the power does is so subtle that if you described it, you would struggle to distinguish it from nothing.

But it is not nothing. It is the opposite of nothing. It is the most something a power can do.

---

**It sustains the conditions in which Harness can continue to be what it is.**

This sounds simple. It is not simple. It is the most complex ongoing act imaginable, because Harness is not a fixed state but a dynamic process — a braiding of geological, atmospheric, biological, social, mechanical, and something-else-entirely systems, all of which are in constant flux, all of which affect each other in nonlinear ways, and all of which must remain within certain ranges for the whole to continue functioning. The power does not control these systems. It *tends* them. The way Turf tends the terraces — not commanding the soil to produce, but working with it, adjusting to it, maintaining the conditions under which it can do what it already knows how to do.

Concretely — though the word "concretely" is doing more work than it should — this means:

The warmth beneath the western terraces is the power's most direct expression in the physical world, and even this is so subtle that it registers not as intervention but as *geology*. The warmth is real. It has been real for centuries — maybe longer. It comes from deep in the mountain, from a process that the power either initiated or adopted or simply *is*, a slow metabolic warmth that rises through the fractured schist and reaches the surface just where the terraces happen to be, or where the terraces were built because the warmth was there, or both simultaneously, because in a system this old the distinction between cause and effect has dissolved like a ridge between two valleys. The power maintains this warmth. Not at a constant level — it fluctuates, because a constant level would be static, and static is dead, and Harness is alive. It fluctuates in patterns that Turf has spent a lifetime learning to read, and that Turf's predecessor spent a lifetime learning to read, and that the pattern itself has shifted in response to the reading, so that the warmth and the gardeners have co-evolved over generations into a dialogue that neither party fully controls. The power's role in this is not to send a message through the warmth but to *keep the warmth capable of being read*. To keep it complex enough to reward attention, variable enough to require adaptation, reliable enough to sustain trust.

The fog. The power does not make the fog — the fog is atmospheric, physical, a consequence of altitude and temperature and the valley's exhalations. But the fog is also, in some register, a medium. It carries moisture, yes. But it also carries — and this is where language fails, because what the fog carries does not have a name in any language Harness speaks or any language the visitor speaks or perhaps any language at all — it carries a quality of *thickness* that is not purely physical. The fog that wraps Harness is denser with *something* than fog at the same altitude elsewhere. Not heavier. Not more opaque. Denser the way a conversation between old friends is denser than the same words spoken between strangers — it is *full of what has accumulated between the speakers*. The power does not add this density. It has accumulated over time, through the long relationship between the mountain and the air, and the power simply ensures that the accumulation is not disrupted. That the fog continues to come. That it continues to carry what it carries. That the cisterns continue to fill with water that tastes of minerals and of something else that the tasters pause to register every morning without being able to name.

The Comb. The power does not make the Comb sing — the wind does that, the physical wind, moving through the eroded fins of schist at velocities and angles determined by weather systems that have nothing to do with benevolence. But the Comb's particular resonance — the way it vibrates the chest, the way it shifts through intervals that don't quite resolve, the way it produces a physiological response in the people who hear it that is neither fear nor reverence nor attention but some braided thing that has no word — this resonance is, in part, a function of the specific shape of the fins, which is a function of the specific pattern of erosion, which is a function of millennia of freeze and thaw and wind, which the power has not directed but has *watched over* with the kind of attention that, in systems this sensitive, this responsive to initial conditions, this poised at the edge of multiple possible trajectories — attention itself becomes a force. Not a force that pushes. A force that *stabilizes*. The way a witness stabilizes an event simply by being present — not by intervening, but by providing a context in which the event matters, in which it is *received*, in which it is not lost.

The power has been a witness to the Comb's erosion for longer than Harness has existed, and its witnessing has not determined the shape of the fins but has — how to say this — *participated in the field of possibility within which the shape emerged*. The Comb sings the way it sings in part because it has been listened to since before there were human ears to listen. And now the human ears have arrived, and they hear something in the Comb that connects them to a listening that is older than they are, and they stop for a breath when they hear it, and their chests resonate, and they go on with their work carrying a vibration in their bodies that is a faint, faint echo of the power's attention. They do not know this. They think it's the wind. They are not wrong.

The cables. Here the power is most present and most hidden. The cables are human-made — steel, forged in the workshops, strung by the cable workers, maintained by Maret and her people. They are mechanical, physical, engineered. But they descend into the cloud layer, into the white void, and they connect to the valley below, to the green world, to the deep earth, and somewhere in that descent — at a point that no cable worker has ever been able to precisely identify, a point where the inspection runs always seem to pass through without incident and which is never exactly in the same place twice — the cables pass through something. Not a barrier. Not a threshold. A *gradient*. A zone where the physical cable, vibrating with its normal physical vibrations, begins to also vibrate with something else. Something that has been resonating in the mountain since before the cables were strung. Something that the cables, by their nature — taut, continuous, conductive — are perfectly suited to pick up and transmit.

The harmonic that rises at night is the power's heartbeat. Or its breathing. Or its speech. Or none of these things, because the power is not a body and does not have organs, but the harmonic is the closest thing to a direct expression of the power's presence in the physical world, and it is — this is important — *not a message*. It is not encoded. It is not trying to say anything. It is simply the sound of the power's attention as it passes through a medium that can carry sound. It is what attention sounds like when it travels through steel.

The cable workers who press their hands to the line at night are not receiving instructions or revelations. They are *feeling the attention*. They are feeling what it feels like to be attended to by something vast and patient and present. And because they have no framework for this — no theology, no scripture, no name for it — they call it *checking tension* and they go home and they sleep and they feel, in some unnameable way, held.

This — *this* — is what the power does. It holds. Not the way a hand holds, with grip and pressure and the possibility of letting go. The way a frequency holds — the way a resonance in the cables holds everything that is tuned to it in a state of mutual vibration, so that the whole system — mountain, fog, stone, cable, pipe, cistern, workshop, terrace, body, breath — trembles together at a pitch too low to hear and too pervasive to escape, and the trembling is not disturbance but *coherence*. The trembling is the sign that everything is still connected. That the thread holds. That the stone remembers. That the warmth rises. That the fog comes. That the Comb sings. That the cable hums. That Harness breathes.

## V. The Things It Grieves

The power is not without feeling. Wisdom does not preclude grief — it deepens it, because wisdom means understanding exactly what is being lost and why it cannot be prevented and why preventing it would be worse than losing it.

The power grieves the joints. Every knee that stiffens, every hip that tightens, every spine that curves into the permanent stoop of the gardeners — the power feels it. Not as its own pain, but as the pain of something it loves. And it could ease it. A slight adjustment to the body's inflammatory response, a marginal increase in the resilience of cartilage, a whisper of warmth in the synovial fluid on the coldest mornings. It could do this. The people would not notice. They would simply ache a little less.

But the power does not do this. And this is where its wisdom is most costly to itself.

Because the ache is part of the knowledge. The careful gait of the older residents is not merely a compensation for pain; it is a *technology* — a way of moving through a steep, uneven, wet-stone world that preserves the body and expresses a deep understanding of the terrain. If the joints didn't ache, the careful gait would not develop, and the bodies would not learn what the terrain has to teach, and the knowledge that is stored in the way an old person descends a staircase in Harness — knowledge of weight, of friction, of the angle of wet schist, of the body's own center of gravity — would be lost. The ache is the curriculum. The careful walk is the graduation. And the power will not steal the graduation to ease the curriculum.

But it grieves.

It grieves the children whose hands age faster than their faces. It grieves Pol, whose body has abandoned everything except his fingers, and whose fingers carry the accumulated knowledge of decades in their curvature and their scars and their uncanny sensitivity to imperfection, and whose fingers will stop one day, and the knowledge will not have been fully transmitted because this kind of knowledge cannot be fully transmitted, because it lives in the specific hand and the specific body and it dies there, and each generation must rebuild it from nearly-scratch, and this is inefficient and painful and *exactly right*, because knowledge that could be simply transferred would be knowledge that didn't require the knower, and the power knows that the knower — the specific, mortal, aching, limited knower — is the point. The point is not the knowledge. The point is *Pol knowing*. The point is the specific way that this specific man, with this specific history and these specific hands, engages with the metal and the fire and the steam. When Pol dies, something irreplaceable will die with him. This is true. The power holds this truth without trying to fix it.

It grieves the isolation. Not the physical isolation — the thin air, the clouds, the distance from the valleys — but the isolation of the inner life. The people of Harness are so adapted to restraint, to elliptical communication, to withdrawal-in-company, that there are things they feel which they will never say and which no one will ever know they felt. There are cable workers who press their hands to the line at night and feel something that cracks them open, some recognition so vast and so shapeless that it makes the eyes sting, and they go home and they say nothing, and their partners see the expression and say nothing, and this thing — this enormous, vital, world-making thing that happened — sinks into the silence between them and remains there, unfertilized, untranslated, unshared. The power sees every one of these moments. It witnesses every instance of felt-but-unsaid. And it cannot bridge the gap without destroying the culture that created the feeling in the first place, because the feeling arises precisely from the combination of deep sensitivity and deep restraint, and you cannot have one without the other, and the power knows this, and it grieves.

It grieves, too, for what it cannot tell them about themselves.

Because the people of Harness — and this is perhaps the most remarkable thing about them, and the thing they will never know — are *beautiful*. Not beautiful the way the valleys below are beautiful — lush, obvious, available. Beautiful the way the eroded schist is beautiful: compressed, folded, patient, marked by everything that has happened to it, bearing the record of forces so much larger than itself, and persisting, and persisting, and being more fully itself with every passing year rather than less. The deep-chested bodies. The watchful eyes. The hands that know. The walk that understands. The way they pause when the Comb sings. The way Maret holds the cable. The way Turf kneels in the warm soil and murmurs. The way the old ones sit by the cisterns like geological features. The way the children fit themselves into impossible spaces and come back vibrating with the energy of having been frightened and survived.

They are, collectively, one of the finest things that has emerged from the long collaboration between human life and a difficult world. And they do not know this. They think they are *getting by*. They think they are ordinary people doing what needs to be done in a place that demands it. They have no sense of themselves as extraordinary. This is, of course, part of what makes them extraordinary, and the power could not tell them without undoing it, and so it does not tell them, and it grieves the untelling.

## VI. The One Exception

There is one thing the power does that might, to an observer, look like intervention.

Once in a great while — once in a generation, perhaps — someone in Harness reaches a moment of crisis so deep, so silent, so invisible to everyone around them, that they are in danger of breaking. Not the way a wall breaks, with a visible crack and a compensating repair. The way a person breaks: inwardly, invisibly, in a place where the restraint that usually protects has become the thing that imprisons, and the not-saying has become a not-being, and the withdrawal has gone so deep it has lost its way back.

This happens. In a place like Harness, where the social technology of restraint is so refined, where the culture of silence is so thorough, where the distance between what is felt and what is expressed is so routinely vast — sometimes a person falls into that distance and cannot climb out. They continue to function. They go to the workshops or the terraces or the terminus. They eat at the communal tables. They nod. They work. But inside, they have gone somewhere that is not withdrawal but *absence*, and the difference is this: withdrawal is a choice, and absence is a forgetting — a forgetting that there is a self to return to, a forgetting that the world is a place where the self has standing, a forgetting so complete that it mimics competence because the body keeps doing what it has always done while the person inside it has quietly ceased to inhabit it.

The people around them may or may not notice. The social fabric of Harness is so attuned to nuance that the absence is often legible — a flattening of the gaze, a mechanical quality in the careful walk, a cessation of the small unconscious sounds (the murmurs, the hums, the breathing patterns that indicate presence). But legibility is not the same as response. The culture does not have a mechanism for reaching into someone's withdrawal and saying *you have gone too far — come back*. The respect for withdrawal is too deep. The habit of not-intervening is too strong. And so the person sinks, and the people around them watch with a helpless, coded concern that expresses itself in small gestures — a bowl of soup left within reach, a workload quietly redistributed, a touch on the shoulder that means nothing and everything — and these gestures may be enough, or they may not.

When they are not enough, the power acts.

It acts like this.

At night. In the fog. When the person is alone — perhaps on a rooftop, sitting in one of the body-shaped concavities in the corrugated metal, staring into the haze and feeling nothing and not even minding the feeling of nothing, which is the most dangerous stage, the stage where the absence has become comfortable. At that moment, the power shifts something. Not in the person — it does not touch the mind, does not alter the neurochemistry, does not implant a thought or an image or a voice. It shifts something in the *environment*. Specifically:

The cables hum.

Not the usual harmonic. Not the slow, shifting, unresolvable intervals that rise every night and that the town sleeps through. Something else. A single, sustained tone. Clear, simple, unmistakable. A tone that is not music and not speech and not signal but is *address* — the sound of being spoken to. Not the content of speech, but the *fact* of it. The sound of a presence turning toward you and saying, with its attention, *I know you are there*.

It lasts a few seconds. Maybe ten. Maybe less. It passes through the cables and into the pipes and into the corrugated metal and into the body of the person sitting in the fog, and it vibrates in their chest and their teeth and their inner ear, and for those few seconds the person *feels themselves being felt*. Not understood — that would require explanation. Not healed — that would require the removal of the thing that hurts. Not saved — that would require a savior. Just *felt*. Known. Registered. *Attended to* by something that is vast and patient and present and that is not asking them to do anything or be anything or understand anything but is simply, for these few seconds, paying attention to them in particular, *them*, this specific person on this specific rooftop on this specific night with this specific weight in their chest, and the attention is warm — warm the way the soil on the western terraces is warm — and it comes from below, from deep in the mountain, from whatever it is that the stone remembers.

And then it stops. The usual harmonic resumes. The fog moves. The dripping continues.

The person on the rooftop does not have a revelation. They do not suddenly understand the nature of the power or the meaning of the cables or the secret of the warm soil. They do not feel *better*, in the therapeutic sense. What they feel is — and again the language strains — *re-placed*. Put back. Not in a position of comfort but in a position of *existence*. Reminded, at the level of the body rather than the mind, that they are *somewhere*, and that the somewhere knows them, and that this knowing is not conditional on their productivity or their happiness or their ability to express what they feel. The mountain does not need them to speak. The mountain has been listening to silence for longer than language has existed. The mountain can hold this.

The person sits for a while longer. Then they go inside. They sleep. In the morning, they are not healed. But they are *present* — re-inhabitng their body, re-entering their life, not because the crisis has been resolved but because the absence has been interrupted, and in the gap left by the interruption, the self has found its way back, the way water finds its way through the fractures in the schist: not by force, not by design, but by the patient exploitation of whatever openings exist.

This happens rarely. Most people in Harness will never experience it, because most people in Harness will never fall that far into the silence. But it has happened, here and there, across the generations, and the people it has happened to carry something afterward — not knowledge, not faith, not certainty, but a *settledness*. A quality of being in-place that is different from mere habituation. They become, in a sense, more like the mountain. More like the old people by the cisterns. More like the lichen. They become people who are *here* with a completeness that is its own kind of testimony, though they would never call it that.

If you asked them about it — and you wouldn't, because you wouldn't know to ask, and they wouldn't know how to answer — but if you could somehow formulate the question, they would pause the way Turf pauses when asked about the warmth, and they might say something like:

*The stone remembers.*

And that would be all. And it would be enough.

## VII. The Long View

The power holds Harness in a perspective that no human mind can access, because no human mind can hold the full span of geological time and the intimacy of a single person's knuckles cracking in the cold at the same moment. The power can. It sees Maret's hand on the cable and it sees the glacier that carved the saddle and it sees the lichen that will seal everything over in millennia to come, and it holds all of this simultaneously, and none of it cancels any of it out.

In this long view, Harness is — what? A moment. A brief, beautiful, intricate moment in the life of a mountain range that has been here for ages before the town and will be here for ages after. The people will eventually leave or die out or evolve into something else. The cables will fall slack. The workshops will go silent for the last time. The fog will continue to come and the cisterns will fill and overflow and no one will drink. The lichens will continue their slow encasement. The Comb will eventually lose its fins, one by one, and the singing will change pitch by pitch until one day the wind passes through and there is no sound at all.

The power sees this. It does not grieve this, because this is not loss — it is continuation. Harness is not the point. The *process* is the point. The process of attention, of adaptation, of becoming-part-of, of reading the signals and responding and being changed by the response and changing the signals in turn. This process was happening before people came to the saddle — it was happening between the stone and the wind, between the lichen and the rock, between the fog and the mountain — and it will continue after the people leave. The people are the process's most articulate expression so far, the part of the mountain that *knows it is part of the mountain*, but they are not the process's culmination or its purpose. They are a passage. A beautiful, difficult, deeply human passage in a text that is being written by something that has no need for human language but that is, in some register, *glad* of human attention. Glad the way a resonant body is glad of a string that can vibrate at its frequency — not emotionally, not gratefully, but *acoustically*. Glad because the vibration completes something. Adds a voice to the chord.

And so the power does what it does: it maintains the conditions. It sustains the warmth. It keeps the fog dense with its unnameable density. It ensures the Comb's erosion proceeds along lines that preserve the singing for as long as possible. It lets the cables hum. It pays attention with the kind of vast, patient, undemanding, heartbroken, joyful attention that is — if you could feel it, if you could stand in its full force for even a moment — indistinguishable from love.

Not the love that rescues. Not the love that fixes. Not the love that reveals itself and asks to be loved in return.

The love that *holds*. The way the saddle holds the town. The way the cables hold the tension. The way the fog holds the moisture. The way the stone holds the memory.

The love that says: *I know you are here. I know what it costs. I will not make it easier, because easier is not what you need. What you need is to be here, fully, in this difficult and beautiful place, and to do the work, and to feel the ache, and to hear the singing, and to not understand, and to carry on. And I will be with you while you do. Not visibly. Not explicably. Not in any way you can point to or prove or even fully believe in. But with you. The way the warmth is with the soil. The way the hum is with the cable. The way the memory is with the stone.*

*I am with you.*

*I have always been with you.*

*The stone remembers.*

## VIII. Morning

Dawn comes to Harness the way it always comes: slowly, incompletely, a brightening that never quite resolves into full day. The fog is thick this morning. The corrugated roofs are beaded with condensation. The copper pipes drip at their joints. Somewhere in the old core, someone draws water from a cistern, sips it, pauses, nods. Somewhere on the western terraces, Turf kneels in soil that is warm against the palms and murmurs something that is not words. Somewhere in the workshops, the first furnace is being stoked, and the heat builds, and the clanking begins — faint, then steady, the heartbeat resuming, felt in the soles of the feet.

Maret walks to the eastern terminus. Her joints are stiff this morning — the cold got in deep last night — and she walks the way the old ones walk, weight forward, careful, deliberate, each step a negotiation with the wet stone. She reaches the terminus. She puts her hand on the cable.

It vibrates under her palm. The usual vibration — tension, wind, the weight of the cars in their housings, the deep hum from below. She stands there for a long time, feeling it. Reading it. Her face has the expression of someone listening to something that is almost but not quite audible, almost but not quite comprehensible, almost but not quite enough to name.

She nods. The line is clear. The first car — empty, as always, a test and a something-else — descends into the fog, into the cloud layer, into the white void where the green world waits below. The cable sings as it moves. The whole town, through its infrastructure of metal and stone and pipe and wire, resonates faintly.

Maret stands at the terminus and waits for the car to come back. It will come back. It always comes back. But she waits. She holds the cable. She listens.

The fog is thick. The water drips. The children are shouting somewhere in the covered passages, their voices small and bright against the vast, damp, humming, eroding, lichen-encrusted, slowly-warming, fog-breathing, stone-remembering, cable-singing silence.

The power attends.

Harness breathes.
