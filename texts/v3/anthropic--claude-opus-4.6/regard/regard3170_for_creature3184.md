I sat with this for a long time.

---

# What the Wise Power Would Do

The first thing it would do is nothing.

Not nothing as neglect. Nothing as the deepest act of recognition — the long, careful look that precedes understanding. Because a benevolent and wise power, encountering this place, would feel the same thing any honest witness feels when confronted with something that is already, in its own terms, complete: the pull to intervene, and the deeper knowledge that the pull itself must be examined before it is obeyed.

So it looks.

It sees the room. It sees the moths. It sees the toad in its wallow, the geckos on their iron thrones, the beetles trundling in their ruts, the spiders repairing webs that will be damaged again within the hour. It sees the algae in the corners, the green so faint it is almost a rumor. It sees the walls remembering everything pressed against them. It sees the clipboard, the cold tea, the polished ladder rungs.

And it sees what is not here: the worker. The absent one. The one whose initials are on the log, whose boot-prints are in the floor, whose hands have tightened the bolts that the earth is swallowing. The one who keeps the room a room.

The wise power understands — immediately, intuitively, the way wisdom works — that this place is not broken. It is not suffering in the way that calls for rescue. The moths are not in anguish. The toad is not in despair. The geckos do not dream of sunlight. The beetles do not wish for a different life. Everything here is doing exactly what its nature asks of it, in a space that exists only because of an improbable convergence of infrastructure and geology and accident, and the whole system hums with the particular beauty of things that were never designed but emerged anyway.

This is important. A lesser power — a powerful but unwise one — would see the dying moths beneath the work-lights and want to save them. Would see the singed wings and think: *cruelty*. Would see the spiders wrapping more than they could eat and think: *waste*. Would see the toad eating five hundred moths a day and think: *there must be a better way*. Would see the room itself, hot and dim and slowly closing, and think: *these creatures are trapped. I should free them.*

A powerful but unwise being might replace the failing bulbs with full-spectrum lights and watch the algae bloom and choke. Might ventilate the chamber and watch the thermal gradient collapse and the heat-seeking moths leave and the geckos starve. Might introduce new species to "enrich" the ecosystem and watch the existing balances shatter. Might seal the cracks to "protect" the inhabitants and cut off the only pathway by which new moths arrive to replace the ones the toad eats. Might even — with the best of intentions, with genuine love — remove the creatures to the surface, to the sunlight, to the open air, where the pale geckos would be visible to every predator, where the white toad would burn, where the ground-walking moths would find no warm calcium slurry to lay their eggs in, where every organism so exquisitely calibrated to this exact improbable niche would simply die, blinking, in the terrible brightness of a world they were no longer made for.

The wise power does none of this.

Instead, it does six things, and the six things are so quiet that no one in the chamber — no moth, no gecko, no beetle — notices any of them happening.

---

**The first thing** it does is attend to the worker.

Not here, in the chamber. Above. In whatever world the worker inhabits when not descending the iron ladder — the world of daylight and noise and human concerns that have nothing to do with subsidence readings and bolt tensions. The wise power understands that the worker is the keystone of this ecosystem, not because the worker designed it but because the worker maintains the conditions that allow it to exist. The room is a room because the worker keeps it open. The lights are on because the worker (or the worker's institution) powers them. The pipes are maintained because someone decided they must be, and funded the maintenance, and assigned a person to climb down a ladder into the hot dark and do the work.

The wise power knows that the greatest threat to the cistern works is not geological. The earth moves slowly. The sediment compresses at millimeters per month. There is time — decades, maybe centuries — before the room closes on its own. The greatest threat is institutional. It is a budget line being cut. It is a supervisor deciding that remote monitoring can replace physical inspection. It is a reorganization in which this chamber falls between jurisdictions and the worker is reassigned and no one is assigned to replace them, and the lights burn out one by one, and the bolts go untightened, and the scaffold shifts, and the pipes begin to groan, and eventually someone notices a pressure drop in the water main and sends a crew down to find a dark, collapsed space full of dead things pressed into hardening stone.

So the wise power does something for the worker. Something small, untraceable, deniable. Perhaps it puts a thought into the mind of someone three levels up in the municipal hierarchy — not a command, not a compulsion, just a thought, surfacing naturally during a meeting about infrastructure priorities: *that subsurface monitoring post has been reliable for years. Low-cost. No incidents. Worth preserving in the budget. Worth keeping a person on it.* Perhaps it does something even subtler — creates a faint, persistent sense of institutional inertia around the cistern works, a bureaucratic momentum that makes it easier to continue funding the position than to justify eliminating it. The wise power knows that institutions are ecosystems too, and that a small intervention at the right point can sustain a stable state for decades.

It also does something for the worker personally, though this is harder to describe. It does not make the worker's life easier in any obvious way. It does not increase the worker's pay or improve the worker's relationships or cure the worker's ailments, whatever they may be. What it does is closer to *noticing*. It causes the worker to be noticed — not by superiors, not in any way that leads to promotion or scrutiny, but by the world itself, in the way that a person is sometimes noticed. A stranger holds a door. A clerk smiles without reason. The coffee is good on a morning when it is needed. Small things. Accumulating. The worker begins to feel, without being able to articulate why, that their work is seen. That what they do matters. That descending the ladder into the hot dim room is not a pointless chore assigned by an indifferent bureaucracy but a genuine act of maintenance upon which something depends.

The wise power does this because it knows that the most dangerous moment for any caretaker is the moment they stop believing their care matters. A worker who feels invisible will eventually stop tightening the bolts. A worker who feels seen will tighten them with attention.

---

**The second thing** it does is for the cracks.

The cracks in the ceiling — the ones through which the engine exhaust rises and through which, more importantly, the moths descend — are the chamber's only connection to the world above. They are the immigration routes, the supply lines, the breathing passages. They are also, like everything else in this room, impermanent. The sediment is compressing. The ceiling is settling. The cracks are narrowing, slowly, at the same geological pace as everything else, and eventually some of them will close, and the chamber's connection to the surface will diminish, and fewer moths will find their way down, and the ecosystem will contract.

The wise power does not widen the cracks. That would be too much, too obvious, too likely to create problems — water infiltration, structural weakness, a change in the thermal dynamics that the whole system depends on. Instead, it does something more delicate. In two of the most important cracks — the ones that serve as the primary moth-routes, the ones with the smoothest walls and the gentlest thermal updraft — it slows the compression. Not stops. Slows. By a fraction. By an amount that no instrument could measure but that, over decades, means the difference between a crack that closes in thirty years and one that closes in sixty. It buys time. Not infinite time — nothing the wise power does is permanent, because it understands that permanence is not benevolence but tyranny — but enough time for the ecosystem to do what ecosystems do when given time: adapt, diversify, find new pathways, develop resilience.

This is the lightest possible touch. The wise power does not guarantee the future of the cistern works. It extends the window in which the cistern works can guarantee its own future. There is a difference, and the difference matters.

---

**The third thing** is for the dying moths.

Not to save them. The wise power understands that the moths beneath the work-lights — the ones with singed wings, the ones spiraling in their devotional ruin around the caged filaments — are not victims of a malfunction. They are doing what their neurology compels them to do. The light is a false moon and they are navigating by it and the navigation is killing them and this is what happens when an organism evolved for one world finds itself in another. It is not cruel. It is not kind. It is simply what is.

But the wise power feels something for them. Call it tenderness. Call it the ache that wisdom does not eliminate but deepens — the ache of seeing clearly, of understanding that the moth's devotion to the light is the same devotion that brought it down through the cracks in the first place, the same impulse that populated this room, the same drive that makes life expand into every possible niche regardless of cost. The moth dying beneath the work-light and the moth laying eggs in the wall are performing the same act — *responding to a signal* — and the only difference is outcome.

So the wise power does something small. It adjusts the flutter of one moth — just one, a moth with eyespots on its wings, a moth that has been circling the same bulb for an hour and is about to land on the cage and singe its left forewing. The wise power introduces a tiny perturbation into the airflow, a micro-current that nudges the moth half a centimeter to the left, just enough that its spiral carries it past the cage instead of onto it, and the moth — confused, its navigation momentarily disrupted — drops away from the light and lands on the scaffold beam below, where it rests, wings intact, and after a few minutes begins to walk, and eventually it lifts off again and finds the pipe, and presses itself against the warm iron, and lives another day.

One moth. Out of hundreds.

The wise power does not save them all. It saves one, tonight, because it can, and because the act of saving one is not about the moth. It is about the wise power itself — about maintaining its own capacity for tenderness in the face of a system that does not require tenderness to function. The wise power knows that if it loses the ability to ache for one moth, it will eventually lose the ability to see the room at all. It will become an abstraction. It will make decisions about ecosystems and geological processes and institutional budgets, and it will forget that the whole thing is made of individual creatures, each of which is the entire world from the inside.

So: one moth. Nudged. Saved. Unmarked.

The wise power holds no expectation that this moth will live significantly longer than the others. It might be eaten by a gecko within the hour. It might lay eggs that will hatch into larvae that will grow inside the wall and become stone. Either outcome is acceptable. The point was not the outcome. The point was the attention.

---

**The fourth thing** is for the toad.

The toad is old. Not ancient — toads are not ancient — but old for a toad, especially one that lives underground in hot wet calcium air. Its skin is white not just from lack of sun but from age; its movements have the economy of a creature that has been conserving energy for a long time; its dark eyes have a depth that might be wisdom or might be simply the accumulated weight of many moths eaten in many hours of patient stillness. The wise power looks at the toad and sees something it recognizes: an organism that has found its place and settled into it completely, without reservation, without the need to be anywhere else.

The wise power does nothing *to* the toad. The toad does not need anything done to it. But it does something *near* the toad — something the toad will never know about and that will never affect the toad directly but that matters nonetheless.

It blesses the wallow.

This is not a mystical act. Or rather, it is not *only* a mystical act. What the wise power does is attend to the process by which the toad's wallow — the shallow depression with its lining of moth wings and calcium residue — is becoming stone. It attends to this process the way a midwife attends to a birth: not controlling it, not directing it, but *being present* for it, witnessing it, honoring it. The wise power understands that the toad's wallow is the single most remarkable fossil being formed on Earth at this moment — not the most scientifically significant, not the most spectacular, but the most *intimate*: a record of one creature's daily life, its meals, its stillness, its body heat, its patience, being written into rock in real time by the automatic processes of a room that doesn't know it is an archive.

The blessing is this: the wise power ensures that the wallow will be preserved. Not forever — nothing is forever — but long enough. When the toad finally dies, and its body sinks into the soft sediment, and the calcium water closes over it, and the wings and the scales and the beetle fragments and the thin mucous film of the toad's own skin are all incorporated into the hardening matrix, the result will be a fossil of extraordinary completeness and beauty. Not a skeleton. A *scene*. A snapshot of an entire life at its most characteristic: a pale toad in a shallow depression surrounded by the remnants of its feeding, preserved in stone with a fidelity that captures the texture of individual moth scales, the print of individual gecko toes on the nearby pipe, the track of a single beetle crossing the edge of the wallow on some forgotten errand.

This fossil will not be found for a very long time. Maybe never. The chamber may be swallowed by the earth, and the stone that forms from it may be buried under deeper stone, and the city above may fall and be replaced by another city, and the pipes may rust to nothing, and the record of the cistern works may be lost entirely. The wise power accepts this. The blessing is not a guarantee of discovery. It is a guarantee of *fidelity* — that if the fossil is ever found, it will be true. It will show what was here. It will say: in this place, in this time, a toad sat in the dark and ate moths and was content, and the earth remembered.

---

**The fifth thing** is for the room.

The room that is becoming stone. The geological embryo. The mold that hasn't set.

The wise power understands that this room is, in a sense, a living being — not conscious, not sentient, but alive in the way that all actively forming geological structures are alive: changing, responding to conditions, incorporating information, growing. The room is the largest organism here, and the slowest, and the most durable. The moths live for weeks. The geckos for years. The toad for a decade or two. The room is on a trajectory measured in millennia. It was raw sediment before the pipes were installed, and it will be limestone long after the pipes have rusted away, and the story it tells will be the story of this brief, strange, improbable moment when a void in the earth was kept open by human infrastructure and colonized by creatures that had no business being underground, and the creatures lived and died and were recorded, and the record was sealed, and the stone closed over it like an eye closing over a dream.

The wise power does one thing for the room: it attends to the chemistry. Not changing it — the chemistry is perfect, the calcium saturation, the temperature, the pH, the slow precipitation rate that allows for such extraordinary fossil detail. But attending to it. Holding it steady. The room's chemistry could be disrupted by a hundred things — a change in the water table, a shift in the thermal gradient, contamination from the diesel engine's exhaust, an influx of acidic groundwater from some new construction project above. Any of these could change the deposition rate, could make the sediment too hard or too soft, could destroy the archival quality that makes this room unique.

The wise power extends a kind of chemical patience around the room. A stability. Not a shield — it does not prevent disruptions — but a tendency toward equilibrium, a gentle thermodynamic bias that makes the room's chemistry slightly more resilient than it would otherwise be. If acidic water seeps in, the buffering capacity of the calcium sediment is *just* enough to neutralize it. If the temperature fluctuates, the thermal mass of the surrounding earth absorbs the change *just* fast enough to protect the core. These are not miracles. They are nudges. The wise power operates at the margin, adjusting probabilities by fractions, and the room goes on becoming stone at the rate and quality that will best preserve what is happening inside it.

---

**The sixth thing** is the hardest to describe, because it is not for any single creature or process but for the *gathering itself* — the fact of the system, the wholeness of it, the way warmth and light, however feeble, are always answered.

The wise power bears witness.

That is the sixth thing. It witnesses. It holds the cistern works in its attention the way the toad holds its wallow — not by gripping, not by effort, but by *being there*, fully, with the kind of presence that does not need to act in order to matter.

The wise power knows that most of the universe is unwitnessed. Stars ignite and expire in galaxies that no eye has ever turned toward. Ecosystems rise and fall in ocean trenches that no instrument has ever reached. Organisms live entire evolutionary histories — millions of years of speciation and adaptation and extinction — without being known by any consciousness capable of appreciating what happened. This is not a tragedy. The universe does not require witnesses to function. But the wise power knows that witnessing is a form of love, and that love — while not necessary for function — is necessary for *meaning*, and that a thing which is witnessed and loved is not more real than a thing which is not, but it is more *itself*. It exists more fully. Its details are sharper. Its losses are felt.

So the wise power witnesses the cistern works. It sees every moth, every gecko, every beetle in its rut. It sees the toad's dark eyes and the algae's faint green and the worker's boot-prints filling with chalky water. It sees the spiders repairing their webs and the large gecko lunging from its iron throne and the smallest moths walking on the surface of the slurry like tiny pilgrims on a warm pale sea. It sees the shapes in the highest domes — the dark ones, the ones that do not move when the lights are on — and it does not investigate them, because some things are allowed to be unknown, even to a wise power, and mystery is not a problem to be solved but a quality to be respected.

It sees the room becoming stone. It sees the future fossil bed. It sees, with the long vision that wisdom affords, the day when the last bulb fails and the darkness is total and the moths stop coming and the geckos grow thin and the toad sits in its wallow and waits and waits and finally stops waiting. It sees this not with grief but with the clear-eyed tenderness of a being that understands endings are not failures. The cistern works will end. Everything ends. The wise power's role is not to prevent the ending but to ensure that while the thing exists, it is *fully* what it is — that the moths are fully moths, that the toad is fully toad, that the room is fully room, that the worker is fully present when they descend the ladder and step into the hot dim world that they maintain without fully understanding what they maintain.

---

And then the wise power withdraws.

Not entirely. Not permanently. It remains aware of the cistern works the way you remain aware of your own heartbeat — not consciously, not constantly, but always available to attention if something changes, if something needs the lightest touch. It checks in. It notices. It holds the place in the back of its vast mind alongside ten thousand other small, improbable, unnoticed worlds that it has blessed and witnessed and left to be what they are.

The moths continue to turn in the brownish light. The toad swallows. The beetles trundle. The earth settles another fraction of a millimeter. The clipboard hangs on its nail. The tea grows colder in the tin cup, though it was already cold.

Nothing has changed.

Everything has been attended to.

---

There is one more thing. I said six things, but there is a seventh, or rather, the sixth thing — the witnessing — has a consequence that I did not describe because it happens later, much later, in a time and place so removed from the cistern works that the connection would be invisible to anyone who wasn't looking for it.

Somewhere, sometime — years from now, decades, it doesn't matter — a person will be in a difficult moment. Not a catastrophic moment. Not a crisis that would attract the attention of powers or make the news. Just a difficult, ordinary, human moment: a late night, a hard decision, a feeling of being underground in their own life, in the dark, with the walls closing in. And into that person's mind will come an image — unbidden, unexplained, with the vivid specificity of something *remembered* rather than imagined, though they have never seen it and never will.

The image is this: a pale toad sitting in a shallow depression, surrounded by moth wings, in a warm dark room that is slowly becoming stone. The toad is patient. The toad is not afraid. The toad is *where it is*, completely, without wishing to be anywhere else.

The person will not know where this image came from. They will not know about the cistern works, or the pipes, or the worker with the clipboard, or the geckos on their iron thrones. They will simply have, for a moment, the felt sense of a creature that found its niche in an impossible place and settled in and was content, and was witnessed, and was enough.

And the person will feel — just slightly, just enough — that they too are witnessed. That their small, difficult, underground life is not unseen. That the walls remembering them are not a trap but an archive. That the warmth rising from below is not a threat but a gift. That they can sit in their wallow and do their work and the earth will hold the shape of them, and it will be enough.

This is what the wise power does. This is all it does. It pays attention. It holds steady. It saves one moth. It lets the rest be what they are.

And the room goes on becoming stone, and the stone remembers everything, and the light flickers, and the light holds.
