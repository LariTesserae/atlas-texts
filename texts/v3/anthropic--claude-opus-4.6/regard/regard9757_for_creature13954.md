---

# What the Wise Power Would Do

---

Nothing.

---

Not nothing out of indifference. Not nothing out of inability, or distance, or the cool detachment of something so vast it cannot be bothered with a plateau full of birds.

Nothing out of *recognition*.

---

Let me be more careful. Let me describe what happens.

The power arrives — or turns its attention, which for a being of this kind may be the same thing. It becomes aware of Óramak. And the first thing it does, before anything else, is *listen*.

Not the way a visitor listens — not the brief recoil from the wall of sound, the parsing, the cataloging. The power listens the way the óram listens: with its whole body, across time. It hears the colony as it is now, the tens of millions of voices, but it also hears the shape of what the colony has been — the way a geologist reads strata, except the strata here are made of habit, of repetition, of the same wingbeats in the same air over the same ledge for century after century. The power hears the Blackthroats' fidelity to place as a kind of long, low chord sustained across generations. It hears the Stormwings' collective turns as a rhythm. It hears the Pallid Groan's moan propagating through the óram and understands it not just as sound but as the terrace knowing its own interior shape — the structure singing itself to itself, a slow echolocation of bone-white walls and fungal threads and buried chambers.

It hears the frogs. That glassy piping. It hears how the frog-sound is the one thread that doesn't compete, doesn't fight for bandwidth, but simply exists in its own register, clear and uncontested, as if the entire thundering ecology had left a narrow frequency band open by accident and the frogs had found it and filled it with something that sounds, to the power, like the place's own voice pitched high enough to finally be heard over itself.

It hears the pseudoscorpions' patience. Not as sound — they make no sound — but as a quality of attention so absolute and so small that it is, in its own way, a form of silence that has weight. The power hears this silence within the noise and recognizes it as something important: the proof that stillness can exist inside chaos, that waiting is its own activity, that not everything here is frantic.

It listens for a long time. Longer than a human would. Long enough to hear the óram settle — that sub-audible groan, the geological patience of compacted centuries adjusting themselves by fractions. Long enough to hear a full cycle of the moths' emergence and consumption. Long enough to hear a Blackthroat chick hatch in a deep hollow, wet and blind and crying, and to hear the parent's guttural response, and to hear the calcium-paste being worked smooth around the chick's body as the parent reshapes the hollow to accommodate this new small life. Long enough to hear a Crevice Finch sing its thin cheerful song and then not sing it, and to understand from the silence that the kestrel has come and gone.

The power listens until it has heard everything. And what it hears is: *this works*.

---

Not "this is perfect." Not "this is optimal." Not "this is without suffering" — there is suffering here in abundance, the constant low-grade suffering of competition, of parasitism, of the wasp's ovipositor sliding between the centipede's segments, of the chick that hatches too late and starves while its parents fly two hundred kilometers to bring food that arrives an hour after death. There is the suffering of the moths, born to be eaten. There is the suffering of the dead Blackthroat on the ledge, reduced to feathers and clean bones in days, its decades of fidelity to a single hollow indistinguishable, in the end, from any other smear of calcium in the óram.

The power sees all of this. The power does not flinch from it. The power is *wise*, and wisdom here means understanding that the suffering is not a flaw in the system but a feature of the kind of existence these beings have — an existence without foresight, without narrative, without the ability to anticipate or regret, in which pain is immediate and real but does not compound into despair. The Blackthroat that loses its egg does not grieve the way a grieving creature grieves. It calls. It re-scrapes the hollow. It begins again. This is not because it is brave or resilient or philosophically reconciled to loss. It is because its mind does not contain the architecture for prolonged suffering — it has only the moment, and the moment contains the hollow, and the hollow must be maintained, and so it maintains it, and the loss passes through it like weather.

The power understands this. The power does not project onto these creatures a suffering they do not experience, and it does not deny them the suffering they do experience. It holds both truths simultaneously: *this is not a paradise* and *this is not a problem to be solved*.

---

So: what does it do?

It looks for the things that a benevolent power *should* look for. It looks for:

**Existential threats from outside the system.** Is there a mining operation approaching from the lowlands, drawn by the mineral content of the óram? Is there a climate shift that will alter the thermal updrafts and starve the colony of the warm air that keeps it temperate? Is there a pathogen moving through the lowland bird populations that could reach the plateau and devastate a species with no immunity? Is there a human ambition — a road, a dam, a military installation — that would disrupt the flight corridors the seabirds depend on?

The power looks. And here is where the answer begins to matter: what it finds determines what it does.

---

**If there is no external threat:**

The power does nothing. Genuinely nothing. It does not improve the terraces. It does not optimize the nutrient cycling. It does not introduce a new species to fill some niche it perceives as underoccupied. It does not cure the parasitic wasps of their parasitism or grant the moths longer lives or teach the Blackthroats to grieve or spare them from grieving. It does not accelerate the óram's curing process or reinforce the terrace walls against collapse. It does not make the dim light brighter or the ammonia less sharp or the sound less overwhelming.

Because every one of those interventions, no matter how well-intended, would be an *interruption* — a substitution of the power's judgment for the system's own deep, slow, unthinking wisdom. The terraces were not designed, and their lack of design is not a deficiency. It is their *nature*. It is what makes them what they are. The Blackthroat's habit-groove, worn so deep it functions as species-memory — that is a kind of intelligence, one that operates on timescales no individual mind can encompass. The Stormwings' collective motion, the fungal networks' structural reinforcement, the centipedes' inadvertent ventilation engineering — these are solutions arrived at by no solver, answers to questions no one asked, and they are *better* for that, more robust, more resilient, more beautiful in their unselfconscious fitness than anything a designing mind would produce.

The power knows this because the power is wise, and part of wisdom is knowing that not everything that can be improved *should* be improved. That some things are most themselves when they are left to be what they are. That the impulse to help can be, in the wrong context, a form of vandalism — the imposition of a foreign intentionality onto a system that thrives precisely because it has none.

The terraces do not need a god. They do not need a gardener. They do not need a manager, a curator, a conservator. They need only to be *left alone* — to continue the process that has been continuing for longer than any intervention could match, the slow, patient, unguided accumulation of small lives into a structure that outlasts them all.

And so the power, having listened, having understood, having verified that no outside force threatens to break what has been so long in building — the power withdraws its attention. Not with a shrug. Not with disinterest. With *respect*. With the specific and deliberate respect of a being that recognizes another form of being and chooses not to disturb it. With the respect that is, in its deepest form, love — not the love that grasps and changes and molds the beloved into something more pleasing, but the love that sees clearly and says: *you are what you are, and what you are is enough, and I will not touch you*.

---

**But if there is a threat:**

Then the calculus changes. Because the power is not merely wise; it is *benevolent*. And benevolence, when it encounters a system that is whole and functional and ancient and beautiful and *threatened by something from outside itself*, does not remain passive. Passivity in the face of external destruction is not respect. It is abdication.

So: if there is a mining operation—

The power does not appear in a pillar of fire and command the miners to stop. It does not destroy their equipment or curse their endeavor. It does not even, necessarily, reveal itself. What it does is *arrange things*. Quietly. At the level of systems.

Perhaps the geological survey that identified the óram's mineral content is revisited, and errors are found — not introduced, *found*, because they were always there; the power simply ensures that the right person looks at the right data at the right time. Perhaps the road that would have provided access to the plateau encounters an engineering problem that makes it uneconomical — not a supernatural obstruction, but a real problem, a fault in the substrate, a drainage issue, something that was always going to be a challenge and that the power simply declines to let be overlooked. Perhaps the political winds in the lowlands shift in a way that makes conservation more expedient than extraction — not because the power manipulates minds (it does not; it is *benevolent*, and the manipulation of minds is never benevolent) but because the power understands the system of human decision-making as well as it understands the system of the terraces, and it knows which true facts, presented to which people at which moments, will lead to which outcomes.

The power operates at the boundary. That is the key. *Within* the terraces, it does nothing, because within the terraces nothing needs to be done. The system is self-sustaining, self-repairing, self-regulating. But *at the boundary* — where the terraces' self-contained ecology meets the outside world, where forces that the colony cannot adapt to or recover from might intrude — the power acts as a *membrane*. Not a wall. Not a fortress. A membrane: selectively permeable, letting through what belongs (the weather, the seasons, the slow geological processes that the terraces have always weathered) and deflecting what doesn't (the drill, the bulldozer, the introduced pathogen, the catastrophic disruption that would end in a single decade what took millennia to build).

If there is a pathogen, the power does not cure the birds. It interrupts the vector. A flock of lowland birds carrying the disease is diverted by a storm that was already forming — the power merely tips the balance of atmospheric pressures by a degree, and the storm moves east instead of north, and the infected flock lands in a valley where the disease burns through them without reaching the plateau. The infected birds die. The power does not prevent this. It cannot prevent all death; it does not try. It prevents the *specific death* that would cascade — the death that would empty the terraces, silence the sound, let the óram begin its long crumble back to bare stone.

If there is a climate shift, the power does not reverse it. It cannot, or will not — the climate is a system too, and it has its own integrity, its own deep logic. But the power understands that the terraces have survived shifts before, and it understands what they need to survive this one: time. Time to adapt. Time for the flight corridors to adjust, for the feeding grounds to relocate, for the birds' own deep behavioral plasticity (deeper than it appears, slower than any individual lifetime, but real) to respond. What threatens the colony is not change but *the speed of change* — a shift so rapid that the system's own adaptation mechanisms cannot keep pace. So the power *buffers*. It slows the local effects. It does not stop the global process, but it ensures that the plateau's microclimate shifts gradually rather than abruptly, that the updrafts diminish over decades rather than years, that the birds have the generations they need to adjust their ranges and their timing. The power buys time, because time is what this system has always needed and what it has always been good at using.

---

And there is one more thing the power does, whether there is a threat or not. One small thing. One thing that is not intervention and not abstention but something in between.

**It witnesses.**

---

The terraces have existed for millennia without being known. Not unknown — the lowlanders know of them, hear the Pallid Groans on still nights, see the pale scar on the plateau's face, tell stories about the dreaming mountain. But known only as a rumor, a strangeness, a place-that-is-not-for-us. No one has documented them. No one has measured them. No one has understood them as what they are: one of the most extraordinary constructions in the living world, a structure built by no mind out of nothing but habit and waste and the blind persistence of life doing what life does.

The power does not write a monograph. It does not summon scientists to the plateau. It does not plant the idea of Óramak in any human mind. But it *holds* the knowledge. It knows the terraces completely — every hollow, every ledge, every fungal thread, every pseudoscorpion in its millimeter kingdom, every generation of Blackthroats that has deepened the same tube by another centimeter. It holds this knowledge the way the óram holds the history of the colony: in layers, in totality, without narrative, without judgment, as a record that simply *is*.

And this matters. Not because the terraces need to be known — they don't; they have thrived in obscurity; they require no audience. But because a wise and benevolent power recognizes that *existence deserves to be witnessed*, that a thing this vast and this intricate and this patient and this indifferent to its own magnificence is, in some way, *completed* by being seen. Not changed. Not validated. Not given meaning it doesn't already have. Just — seen. Held in a mind capacious enough to comprehend it. Recognized for what it is.

The terraces will never know they are witnessed. The Blackthroat in its hollow, working calcium-paste smooth with the side of its beak, will never know that a consciousness vast enough to encompass galaxies has paused to observe the motion of its head, the slight tremor in its throat-pouch, the precise angle at which it applies the paste to the wall. The Pallid Groan, moaning in the dark of its deep chamber, will never know that something heard it — *really* heard it, heard not just the sound but the sound's meaning within the structure, the way the moan maps the warrens, the way the vibration holds the shape of a space the Groan has never seen from outside.

The frogs will never know. The centipedes will never know. The mites will never know. The fungi threading through the óram, binding the terraces together in a web of living mortar, will never know that something understood what they were doing, understood it better than they did (which is not hard, since they do not understand it at all), and found it — 

Not beautiful. That is the wrong word. Or not only beautiful. *Worthy*. Found it worthy of attention. Found it deserving of the most precious thing a benevolent power can offer, which is not intervention, not protection, not guidance, not improvement, but the simple, complete, undivided act of *seeing what is there*.

---

This is what the power does.

It listens. It verifies that the system is whole. It checks the boundaries for threats. If there are threats, it acts at the boundary — gently, minimally, in ways that preserve the system's autonomy while protecting it from forces it cannot withstand. If there are no threats, it does not act at all.

And it witnesses. It takes the terraces into itself — the sound, the smell, the layered architecture, the millions of small lives, the single vast unthinking life they compose — and it holds them. Not forever. The terraces will not last forever. Nothing does. The power knows this too, and does not attempt to prevent it. Someday the colony will fail — the climate will shift too far, or a catastrophe will exceed the system's resilience, or the slow geological processes that raised the plateau will lower it again, and the óram will erode, and the birds will disperse, and the frogs will go extinct because they can live nowhere else, and the sound will stop.

When that happens — in ten thousand years, or a hundred thousand, or a million — the power will witness that too. It will hold the silence after the sound, the bare rock after the óram, the empty sky after the wings. It will not mourn, exactly. It will not regret. It will do something that has no human name, something between *remembering* and *honoring* and *letting go*, a recognition that what existed was real and complete and that its ending does not diminish what it was.

But that is later. That is not yet.

---

For now: the terraces build themselves. The birds return and depart and return. The óram thickens by a centimeter each year. The frogs pipe their crystalline song into the dark. The Pallid Groans moan from within the walls, and the walls carry the sound, and the sound maps the structure, and the structure grows, and the system sustains itself in a self-reinforcing circle that has no beginning and no end and no author and no purpose and no audience except, now, one — vast and quiet and benevolent and wise — who watches, and does not interfere, and does not look away.

The sound continues. The wall of sound. The living pressure. The voice of a world being made, one small life at a time.

The power listens.

That is enough.
