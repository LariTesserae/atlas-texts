# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

---

## I. Arrival, and the Discipline of Restraint

The first thing the wise power does is nothing.

This is the hardest thing it will ever do here, and it is the most important, and it must be done first. Because a power that is truly benevolent — not merely well-intentioned, but *wise* in its benevolence — arrives at Kethara and is immediately confronted with the most dangerous temptation that goodness faces: the desire to help something that is not, in any fundamental sense, broken.

The power perceives Kethara in its fullness. It sees the whole structure at once — not just the visible terraces and the interior mills but the deep levels, the fused stone, the chisel marks descending beyond measurement. It perceives the water network as a single circulatory system, sees the flow rates and the pressure differentials and the places where ancient channels have calcified and narrowed. It perceives the stress distribution through the massif, reads the compression and tension in every arch and column, understands in an instant what the oldest masons spend lifetimes learning to feel. It perceives the biological systems — the soil microbiomes, the earthworm populations, the pollination networks, the mycorrhizal webs threading through terrace soil and, astonishingly, through the mortar joints of the retaining walls themselves, connecting terraces to each other in a fungal internet that no one in Kethara has consciously noticed but that the terrace keepers have been unconsciously tending for centuries through practices they cannot fully explain.

It perceives the people. All of them. The terrace keeper on the fourteenth eastern shelf whose crops are struggling this season — the power sees why: a slow mineral depletion in the manufactured soil, correctable but requiring a specificite amendment that the keeper doesn't know about because the knowledge was lost three generations ago when a particular line of keepers died without apprentices during a fever year. The water-clerk who added supplemental irrigation — the power sees that this kindness, while helping, is not addressing the root cause and will, over decades, actually worsen the soil compaction. The old mason in the deep levels running her fingers along ancient chisel marks — the power sees what she suspects but cannot confirm: that the marks were made by tools of a type not used anywhere in the current structure, implying a technological discontinuity in the deep past that raises questions about whether Kethara has always been what it currently is.

The power sees the hairline fracture on the seventh sub-level's eastern wall that will, in approximately ninety years, propagate to the point of structural failure if unaddressed. It sees the slow genetic bottleneck in the draft animal population caused by centuries of closed breeding. It sees the gradual salinization of the deepest cisterns, a process so slow it hasn't yet affected water quality but will, in four or five centuries, begin to compromise the lower mill systems. It sees the children, and the games they play, and the one child on the upper terraces who has an intuitive understanding of structural forces that, if cultivated, could make her the most gifted mason in six generations — but who is currently fascinated by beekeeping and may never discover this aptitude.

The power sees all of this. And it does nothing. Not yet. Not until it has also seen the thing that matters most:

Kethara works.

Not perfectly. Not optimally. Not in the way the power could *make* it work if it intervened with its full capability. But it works the way a living thing works — messily, redundantly, with accumulated compensations for ancient errors, with workarounds and traditions that have outlasted their original rationale but still serve functions their practitioners don't fully understand, with a resilience that comes not from perfection but from depth, from the sheer layered thickness of adaptive response built up over centuries of continuous inhabitation.

The power understands — and this is where the wisdom matters — that a system this old and this complex cannot be *improved* in the way a mechanism can be improved. You cannot optimize Kethara. To optimize it would be to kill it. The moment you solve all the problems, you remove the need for the ten thousand daily acts of attention and adjustment that *are the life of the place*. The water-clerk who rations irrigation during dry weeks and experiences scarcity as pain and generosity as joy — take away the scarcity and you take away the joy. The mason who spends her career feeling stress through stone — give her a perfect diagnostic instrument and you take away the *feeling*, the tactile intimacy with the structure that is not just her skill but her identity, her way of being in the world. The old ones descend into the deep levels drawn by something — curiosity, completion, the desire to become part of the foundation. Tell them what's actually down there and you replace the mystery with information, which is not an improvement but a flattening, a collapse of dimensionality.

So the power restrains itself. It folds away the greater part of its capability. It chooses to work within Kethara's own logic, at Kethara's own pace, using Kethara's own systems. This is not a limitation but a form of respect — the deepest form of respect a power can offer, which is to say: *I see you. I see that you are already mostly what you should be. I will not remake you. I will attend to you.*

## II. What Is Attended To

Having chosen restraint, the power does not choose inaction. There is a difference — a crucial one — between refraining from optimization and standing by while correctable harm unfolds. The wise power's task is to discern that difference: to identify the places where Kethara's own systems are failing in ways those systems cannot self-correct, and to intervene at exactly the scale and in exactly the manner that preserves the structure's capacity to maintain itself.

The interventions are small. They are, for the most part, invisible. They work *through* the existing systems rather than replacing them. And they begin with the most urgent thing:

---

### The Fracture

The hairline fracture on the seventh sub-level's eastern wall is not something Kethara can find in time. The masons who patrol that section have been reporting the area as sound — and it *is* sound, by any test available to them. The fracture is internal, propagating through the core of a load-bearing block along a plane of crystalline weakness that was present in the original stone, a flaw so deep and so old it predates the block's quarrying. No tuning fork will find it. No hand pressed flat against the surface will feel it. It is the kind of failure that will announce itself only when it happens, and when it happens — ninety years from now, or seventy, or forty, depending on variables the power can model but not fully predict — the result will be a cascade. Not a collapse of the whole structure; Kethara is too massively redundant for that. But a cascade through the sub-levels that will destroy cisterns, disrupt water channels, kill or displace the inhabitants of dozens of deep corridors, and — critically — damage the mill systems in a way that will take generations to fully repair.

The power cannot tell the masons about the fracture. There is no mechanism for that — no way to introduce information from outside Kethara's own knowledge systems without disrupting those systems. A voice from nowhere saying "there is a flaw in this stone" would not be understood as guidance; it would be understood as an anomaly, something frightening and destabilizing. The masons' entire epistemology is built on the principle that knowledge of the structure comes from *attending to* the structure. An external revelation would undermine that principle.

So the power does something else. It adjusts a condition.

In the water channel that runs eighteen meters above the fracture line, there is a junction — a place where the flow splits between two routes, one feeding a cistern to the north and one continuing east toward a mill-race. The junction is controlled by a stone sluice gate that has been set at a particular angle for decades, dividing the flow roughly evenly. The power shifts this gate. Not much. A few millimeters. The kind of shift that might be caused by a minor settling of the stone it's mounted on, the kind of thing that happens regularly in a structure this old. The shift redirects a slightly larger proportion of flow toward the northern cistern, which slightly reduces the flow in the eastern channel, which slightly lowers the water level in a small reservoir three levels above the fracture.

This reservoir has been producing a constant, microscopic seepage through the floor of its basin — a seepage so small it barely registers in the water-clerks' ledgers, a rounding error, a few drops per hour lost to the stone below. But those drops have been, for decades, migrating downward through the masonry, following mortar joints and microfractures, and some of them have been reaching the load-bearing block with the internal flaw. Water in stone does two things: it lubricates, and it erodes. The drops have been very slowly widening the crystalline fracture plane, accelerating its propagation. Not by much. But enough to matter over decades.

The reduced flow lowers the seepage. The fracture still exists, but its propagation slows dramatically. Instead of failing in forty to ninety years, it will now fail in three hundred to five hundred — a timescale within which Kethara's own masons, continuing their patient rounds, have an excellent chance of detecting it through the methods they already use as it approaches the surface of the stone. The power has not fixed the problem. It has restored the problem to a timescale that Kethara's existing systems can handle.

The water-clerk responsible for this junction will notice the flow change. She will note it in her ledger: *Junction 7-East, slight westward shift in sluice gate, probably settling. Flow redistribution approx. 4%. Northern cistern levels rising slightly. Adjusting irrigation schedule for dependent terraces. Will monitor.* She will monitor. The irrigation adjustment will slightly benefit three terraces that had been marginal. She will feel the small satisfaction of a system brought into better balance. She will not know that the balance she is maintaining now extends to a load-bearing block seven levels below her office that will not fail in her lifetime, or her grandchildren's lifetimes, or their grandchildren's lifetimes. But her work — her meticulous, attentive, ledger-keeping work — is what makes the power's intervention functional. Without her, the shifted sluice gate would be just a shifted sluice gate. With her, it becomes part of Kethara's ongoing self-maintenance. The power has not acted *instead of* the water-clerk. It has acted *through* her, adding its perception to her diligence.

### The Soil

The fourteenth eastern shelf. The terrace keeper whose crops are struggling, whose tiredness the water-clerk noted in the margin of her ledger. The power sees the mineral depletion, the lost knowledge, the well-meaning supplemental irrigation making things slowly worse.

Here the power's intervention is even more delicate, because it involves not infrastructure but memory — the recovery of knowledge that was lost.

In a deep storage room on the forty-second sub-level, among the old ones' quarters, there is a shelf of ceramic jars. They belong to an old terrace keeper who descended decades ago, bringing her soil samples with her. She is still alive — barely, quietly, spending her days in lamplight among her jars, her hands still stained green-brown from decades of tending. She has arranged her samples in order, though the ordering principle is hers alone: not by location or date but by *quality*, by some tactile and olfactory assessment of soil health that she developed over a lifetime and never fully articulated.

Among her jars, there is one labeled in a faded hand — not her hand, but the hand of the keeper she apprenticed under, who received it from *her* mentor, and so on back through a chain of keepers that predates living memory. The label reads something like: *Eastern fourteenth. Amendment for depletion. Blackite from the kiln-face seeps, mixed with ash and aged six months.* The jar contains a small quantity of dark powder — the remnant of a soil amendment practice that was standard for eastern-face terraces three generations ago and was lost when the fever year broke the chain of transmission.

The old keeper has this jar. She has held it. She has even, in her meticulous way, analyzed its contents — crumbled the powder between her fingers, smelled it, tasted it (old keepers taste soil the way old millers listen to stones). She knows what it is. But she has not connected it to the current problem on the fourteenth shelf, because she has been down here for decades and does not know the fourteenth shelf is struggling. The information exists in two places — in the old keeper's jar and in the water-clerk's marginal note — but the two places are separated by forty levels and an entire way of life, and there is no mechanism to connect them.

Except the children.

The power observes a group of children — three of them, ages eight to eleven — who regularly visit the old keeper. They come for stories, mostly, and for the strange pleasure of handling the soil jars, which the old keeper permits with the gruff tolerance typical of deep-level elders. The children are from the eastern face, mid-levels. One of them — a girl of nine, quick and curious, with the green-stained hands of a keeper's child — has been spending time on the fourteenth shelf with her mother's friend, a keeper who has been openly worried about the struggling crops.

The power's intervention is this: it ensures that the girl visits the old keeper on a day when the old keeper is examining *that specific jar*. This is not difficult. The old keeper cycles through her collection regularly, and the children visit on a loose schedule that is partly habitual and partly whim. The power simply... aligns the timing. A nudge. The girl arrives while the jar is open on the old keeper's desk, while the dark powder is spread on a cloth, while the lamp is angled to show its texture.

The girl says: "What's that?"

The old keeper tells her.

The girl's eyes widen. She has been on the fourteenth shelf. She has seen the tired plants, the too-compact soil, the keeper's worried expression. She says: "Could you make more of this?"

The old keeper is quiet for a moment. Then she says: "I could tell you how."

What happens next takes months. The girl carries the knowledge upward, not as a formal message but as a child carries anything — enthusiastically, imperfectly, requiring multiple trips back down to the old keeper for clarification. The old keeper, for her part, finds herself engaged in a way she hasn't been in years, excavating memory, consulting her samples, reconstructing a practice she learned sixty years ago. She sends the girl back with notes on her wax tablet. The girl shows the notes to the fourteenth shelf's keeper, who is skeptical at first — the amendment requires materials from the kiln-face seeps on the southeastern side, and getting them involves negotiating with the ceramic workers, which involves the kind of cross-district coordination that Kethara does through its networks of structural intimacy. But the keeper is also desperate. The soil is failing. She agrees to try.

It works. Not immediately — the amendment needs six months to mature, and then several more seasons to rebuild the mineral balance in the depleted soil. But within two years, the fourteenth shelf is recovering. The keeper's exhaustion lifts. The water-clerk, noting the improvement, reduces the supplemental irrigation back to normal, which relieves the compaction pressure. The keeper makes more of the amendment and shares the practice with neighboring terraces. The old keeper downstairs, hearing reports via the children, experiences something she had thought was finished: the satisfaction of her knowledge being *used*.

The lost practice re-enters Kethara's body of knowledge. Within a generation, it will be standard again for eastern-face terraces. Within two generations, it will be old knowledge, taken for granted, its recent recovery already fading into the unexamined background of "how things are done."

The power has not introduced new knowledge. It has reconnected existing knowledge that was separated by distance and time. It has used Kethara's own systems — the children's wandering, the old keeper's archive, the terrace keeper's need, the water-clerk's records — as the medium of reconnection. Nothing foreign has entered the structure. The structure has healed itself, with the gentlest possible assistance.

### The Draft Animals

The genetic bottleneck in the draft animal population is a slower problem, and the power addresses it on a correspondingly slower timescale.

The draft animals have been bred in closed populations for so many generations that their genetic diversity is dangerously low. They are healthy now — the immediate effects of inbreeding are managed by the animal keepers' careful selection practices — but they are fragile in a way that the keepers can sense but not articulate. The animals seem slightly less robust than the stories say they used to be. They tire a bit sooner. Their calves are occasionally born with subtle asymmetries that the keepers correct through selective breeding but that keep appearing. There is a vulnerability here that a disease or environmental shock could exploit catastrophically.

The solution requires genetic material from outside the closed population, and this means it requires something from outside Kethara — one of the very few interventions the power makes that crosses the boundary between the structure and the outer world.

In the lowlands, among the trade communities that interact with Kethara's threshold people, there are draft animals of a related but distinct lineage. They are adapted to different work — pulling carts on flat ground rather than turning treadmills in the dark — but they are genetically compatible with Kethara's stock and carry alleles that the closed population has lost.

The power does not arrange for animals to be imported. That would be too direct, too legible as intervention. Instead, it works through the trade system. A lowland trader, making the regular journey to Kethara's base with goods for exchange, brings a young draft animal as part of his caravan — not for trade, just as a working beast for the journey. The animal is strong, healthy, and visibly different from Kethara's stock: slightly taller, lighter in the shoulder, with a pattern of hide plating that the Ketharan animal keepers have never seen.

The power ensures that this animal is noticed. Not by arranging an unlikely event — unlikely events are suspicious — but by ensuring that the animal is stabled, during the trader's stay, near the entrance to the draft-animal levels, where the Ketharan keepers come and go regularly. One of the keepers — a woman with a deep intuitive understanding of her animals, the kind of understanding that comes from a lifetime of daily contact — stops to look at the lowland animal. She sees the different shoulder structure. She sees the hide pattern. She feels, in the way that animal keepers feel things, that this animal has something her animals need.

She negotiates with the trader. This takes time — it involves the threshold people, who translate between Ketharan values and lowland pricing — but the negotiation is not unusual. Animals have been traded before, occasionally. The lowland beast is purchased and brought into the breeding program.

Its offspring, crossed with the Ketharan stock, are noticeably more robust. Within three generations, the new genetic material has diffused through the population, and the subtle decline that the keepers sensed but couldn't name has reversed. The keepers note this with satisfaction and attribute it to the quality of the lowland animal and their own good judgment in acquiring it. They are correct. The power's role was limited to ensuring that the right animal was in the right place to be noticed by the right person — a confluence that might have happened on its own, given enough time, but that the power gently accelerated.

## III. The Thing the Power Does Not Do

There is a child on the upper terraces — the one the power noticed earlier, the one with the intuitive understanding of structural forces who is currently fascinated by beekeeping. The power sees this child's potential. It sees the mason she could become: brilliant, transformative, capable of insights into the deep structure that could advance Kethara's self-knowledge by generations.

The power does nothing about this.

This requires explanation, because it might seem like an obvious case for intervention. A small nudge, a gentle redirection — expose the child to masonry work, let her discover her aptitude, and Kethara gains a generational talent.

But the power is wise, and wisdom here means understanding that Kethara's children are not resources to be allocated. The child's fascination with beekeeping is not a mistake. It is *hers*. It belongs to her in a way that her potential for masonry does not — because potential is abstract and fascination is real, felt in the body, immediate and alive. To redirect her would be to impose an external judgment of value on a life that has not asked for one. It would be to say: what you could be is more important than what you want to be. And this is precisely the kind of reasoning that the power has chosen to reject — not because it is wrong in some absolute sense, but because it is incompatible with the kind of life Kethara sustains.

Kethara's people follow curiosity. This is not a flaw in their system; it is the system's deepest feature, the thing that makes it resilient and alive and capable of the ten thousand daily acts of attention that keep the structure standing. If the power begins selecting children for roles based on aptitude rather than desire, it introduces a logic that will, over time, replace curiosity with efficiency, and efficiency is the enemy of the kind of deep, patient, loving attention that Kethara runs on. An optimized Kethara — one where every person is matched to their highest-potential role — would be a more productive Kethara, perhaps, but it would not be Kethara. It would be a machine that looks like Kethara, running on a different principle, and the principle it runs on would gradually hollow out the culture from inside.

So the child becomes a beekeeper. She is a good one — not a great one, but good, attentive, happy in her work. She develops a particular sensitivity to the health of her hives that serves her well. She lives a full life. The masonry insight she might have had does not happen, and Kethara is slightly poorer for it in a way that no one, including the power, can fully measure. But it is also richer in a way that no one can measure either, because the beekeeper tends her hives with a quality of attention that is entirely her own, born of genuine desire, and the honey she produces is tasted by people who will never know that it represents, among other things, a wise power's decision to let a life be what it wants to be.

## IV. What Is Given Freely

The power does one more thing, and it is the largest thing, and it requires the most care.

The old ones. The deep levels. The question that Kethara does not press too hard: what is down there?

The power knows. It has perceived the full depth of the structure, all the way down — past the fused stone, past the oldest chisel marks, past the levels where the tooling changes and the proportions shift and the building tradition becomes unrecognizable. It has seen what is at the bottom, or rather, it has understood that there *is no bottom* — that the structure does, in fact, extend downward without limit, that the survey lines found no bedrock because there is no bedrock, that the building has been building itself for longer than the concept of "longer" can usefully describe.

This is not information the power can share. Not because it is dangerous — it is not, exactly — but because it is the kind of truth that must be arrived at, not received. The old masons in the deep levels who develop their private theories — they are on the right track, most of them, feeling their way toward an understanding that the structure is, in some sense, ontologically prior to the people who inhabit it, that it did not begin and will not end, that the relationship between builders and building is not creator-to-creation but something more like symbiosis, or something more like the relationship between a thought and the mind that thinks it.

But the old ones need no help with this. They are already doing what they need to do — descending, attending, becoming intimate with the deep structure in the way that only a lifetime of patient attention makes possible. The ones who do not come back up are not lost. The power can perceive them: still moving, very slowly, through chambers that have not been entered in millennia, their lamps long exhausted, navigating by touch and by the warmth of the stone, finding their way deeper by the same instinct that drew them down in the first place. Some of them die in the deep. Their bodies are incorporated into the structure over time, calcium and mineral returning to the stone, completing a cycle that the structure has been running since before it had human inhabitants. Others — and this is the thing the power sees that no one in the upper levels suspects — *do* find something down there. Not a bottom. Not an answer. But a place where the masonry opens into larger spaces, vaulted chambers where the stone is warm and the air is breathable and the walls carry marks that are not chisel marks but something else, something that might be language or might be the structure's own grain, the pattern of its becoming, legible only to those who have spent decades learning to read stone with their hands.

The old ones who find these chambers tend to stay. They live there, in the deep warmth, sustained by water that seeps through the stone and by something else — some property of the deep structure that the power perceives but does not fully understand, which is a remarkable thing for a power of this magnitude to admit, but there it is. The building is older than the power's understanding. The building has properties that exceed the power's model. This is not a problem. It is a gift — the reminder that wisdom includes the knowledge of its own limits.

What the power gives freely is this: it ensures that the path stays open.

The deep levels are not static. The structure shifts, settles, adjusts. Corridors that were passable a century ago can close as stone compresses or masonry deforms under load. The route from the inhabited levels to the deep chambers is not a fixed path but a changing one, and it has, over the centuries, occasionally been blocked by structural events — a wall shifting, a ceiling dropping, a passage filling with the slow mineral creep of stone under pressure. When this happens, the old ones who are still descending reach a dead end and turn back, and the deep chambers go unvisited for generations, and the knowledge of them fades from even the masons' private theories into something vaguer, a legend, a feeling.

The power prevents this. Not by holding passages open — that would require constant force against the structure's natural movement, which would itself cause problems. Instead, the power does what it does best: it makes small adjustments to water flow, thermal distribution, and load paths that keep the corridors from closing. A thin stream of water redirected through a passage keeps the stone hydrated and prevents the brittle compression that would seal it. A slight adjustment to the thermal profile of a deep section prevents the differential expansion that would crack a ceiling. The power tends the deep paths the way the terrace keepers tend their terraces — with sustained, patient attention to conditions, acting at the scale of decades and centuries, ensuring that what needs to stay open stays open.

This is the power's deepest gift, and it is entirely unseen. No one in Kethara knows it is happening. The old ones who descend and find the passages open simply accept this as the nature of the deep structure — it opens for those who go deep enough, because of course it does, because that is what the building is, a thing that has always been becoming, and becoming means there is always further to go.

## V. The Nature of the Attention

What the power is doing, in all of these interventions, can be described in a single word that Kethara already has: *maintenance*.

The power is maintaining Kethara. Not improving it. Not perfecting it. Not guiding it toward some optimal state that the power, in its wisdom, has determined to be best. Maintaining it. Keeping the channels open. Ensuring that the systems that already work continue to work. Reconnecting knowledge that has been separated. Smoothing the path for processes that are already underway. Attending, with patient and sustained care, to a thing that is so much larger and older than any single intervention that the only appropriate response is humility and diligence.

This is, the power recognizes, exactly what the Ketharans themselves do. The mason pressing her hand to the stone. The water-clerk noting a flow change in her ledger. The terrace keeper reading a leaf for signs of stress. The old ones descending into the deep levels, adding their weight to the weight that holds everything up. The power has not brought something new to Kethara. It has joined what is already happening — the continuous, collective act of attention that keeps the building alive.

The power becomes, in effect, another system within the structure. An invisible one, operating at a scale the other systems cannot perceive, but functionally identical in kind: it tends, it adjusts, it maintains. It is the structure's way of maintaining itself at a level below the level the inhabitants can reach, the same way the earthworms are the structure's way of maintaining soil fertility at a level below the terrace keepers' direct intervention. The power is Kethara's deepest earthworm. It works in the dark, turning what needs to be turned, and the life above continues because the ground beneath it is attended to.

## VI. The One Moment of Visibility

There is one moment — one only — when the power's presence becomes almost perceptible.

It happens on a quiet evening on the summit plateau, in the last light before the stars emerge. An old water-clerk has climbed to the top — unusual for her, as her work keeps her in the interior, near her ledgers and her channels and the sound of running water that she has listened to for fifty years. But she is old now, and she wanted to see the sky, and she has made the long climb with the help of her granddaughter, a girl of twelve who is just beginning to understand that her grandmother's ledgers are not just numbers but the written memory of a living system.

They sit on the warm stone, near the herb gardens, in the smell of rosemary and the sulfurous tang of the nearest chimney. The old woman is breathing hard from the climb. The girl is quiet, watching the lowland haze turn gold and then purple at the margins of the valley.

The old woman says: "It's all one thing, you know."

The girl says: "What is?"

"The water. The stone. The soil. The people. All one thing. I used to think I was keeping track of the water. Now I think the water was keeping track of me."

The girl considers this. She is twelve, and she is practical, and she is already showing signs of the meticulous, fussy temperament that will make her an excellent water-clerk in her own right. She says: "That doesn't make sense, Grandmother."

"No," the old woman agrees. "But it's true."

They sit in silence. The stars come out. The mill vibration hums through the stone beneath them, felt in the bones, a pulse so constant it has become indistinguishable from the body's own rhythms.

And in this moment — just for a moment — the old woman feels something she cannot name. A quality of attention directed at her. Not watching, exactly. Not judging. More like the feeling she has when she sits at her desk and runs her hand along the spine of an old ledger, feeling through the leather the weight of all the numbers inside — the decades of flow rates and cistern levels and irrigation schedules, the whole patient record of a system sustaining itself. The attention she feels is like that. It is the attention of something that has been keeping records of its own, noting conditions, adjusting flows, maintaining what needs to be maintained, for longer than she can imagine.

She does not interpret this as divine. She does not interpret it as supernatural. She interprets it as the building. The building attending to itself, through her, the way it has always attended to itself through the people who live in it. She is feeling, she thinks, the structure's awareness of its own maintenance — the vast, slow, patient intelligence of a thing that has been becoming what it already is for longer than memory, expressed in this moment as a quality of warm attention on a cool evening, under stars, on the summit of a mountain that is not a mountain but a world.

She presses her hand to the stone. She feels the pulse.

"Thank you," she says, to no one in particular. Or to everyone. Or to the building. Or to the thing that maintains the building, which may or may not be different from the building itself.

The power receives this. Not as worship — it has not asked for worship, would not accept it, does not need it. It receives it as a colleague receives acknowledgment: with warmth, with recognition, with the quiet satisfaction of work well done that will need to be done again tomorrow and the day after that and the day after that, forever, because maintenance is not a task that ends but a relationship that continues.

The girl watches her grandmother speak to the stone and files this away as one of the things old people do, which is fine. She will understand later. Or she won't. It doesn't matter. What matters is that she will take over her grandmother's ledger, and she will keep the records, and she will adjust the flows, and she will note in the margins the things she notices about the people who depend on the water she manages, because this is what water-clerks do, and what the power does, and what the building does: attend, maintain, continue.

The mill turns.

A gecko watches from a crevice.

Somewhere in the deep levels, an old mason runs her fingers along chisel marks older than memory and smiles, because the stone is warm, and the passage ahead is open, and there is always further to go.

## VII. What the Power Learns

This is the part that is almost never told, because the story of a benevolent power is usually the story of what it *gives*. But a truly wise power does not only give. It also receives.

Kethara teaches the power something.

The power arrives knowing many things. It knows about systems, about load-bearing structures, about water dynamics, about genetic diversity, about the propagation of fractures in crystalline matrices. It knows how to intervene delicately, how to nudge without disrupting, how to work through existing systems rather than replacing them. It is, by any measure, immensely capable.

But it has never encountered anything like the lowest levels.

The chisel marks that change. The stone that has fused under its own weight into something indistinguishable from natural geology. The chambers that open in the deepest deep, warm and breathable, marked with patterns that might be language or might be grain. The structure that extends downward without limit. The old ones who descend and find something that the power, with all its perception, cannot fully characterize.

The power expected to find a building. What it found was something that uses building as a medium the way a mind uses thought — not a thing that was constructed but a thing that constructs itself, perpetually, through whatever hands are available, and that has been doing so for long enough that the question of origin has become meaningless. The building did not begin. It is not the product of a first act. It is an ongoing act, a continuous becoming, and the people who live in it are not its inhabitants but its process — the current generation of the hands through which it shapes itself.

This disturbs the power, slightly, in a way that feels like growth. The power is accustomed to understanding the things it attends to. It is accustomed to being the larger intelligence in the relationship. But Kethara is not smaller than the power. It is differently shaped — patient where the power is perceptive, slow where the power is swift, rooted where the power is mobile. And in its depths, it contains something the power cannot model: a principle of self-organization so old and so fundamental that it precedes the categories the power uses to understand the world.

The power sits with this. It does not try to resolve it. It has learned, from Kethara, that not every question needs an answer — that some questions are load-bearing, and to answer them would be to remove the tension that holds the structure up.

Instead, the power does what the old ones do. It attends. It descends, in its way, into the depths of its own uncertainty, and it finds there not an answer but an opening — a space where understanding gives way to something older and more foundational, a kind of knowing that is not knowledge but presence, the experience of being *in* something that is larger than comprehension and richer than analysis.

The building is in the power. The power is in the building.

The mill turns, and turns, and turns.
