# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

---

For a long time, it would do nothing at all.

Not out of indifference. Out of the same quality of attention that the plateau itself seems to demand—the willingness to sit with what is, to let the full shape of a thing become apparent before reaching toward it. A wise power does not arrive with solutions. It arrives with perception. And what it perceives here would give it pause.

Because the plateau is, by almost any measure, beautiful.

The condensation gathering on the upward-facing stones. The ferns in their allotted patches. The conversations that span days, each phrase suspended in motionless air, waiting. The lizard stepping onto the extended finger. The perfect fit of joint and wedge, of body and ledge, of life and architecture. There is no suffering here in any form that announces itself. No hunger, no conflict, no decay that is not seamlessly absorbed. The inhabitants are not in pain. They are not oppressed. They have not been cursed or trapped by any visible mechanism. They simply *are*, in the same way the ridges *are*, in the same way the moisture films *are*.

A lesser power—one that was merely good, merely kind, but not yet wise—might see this and move on. *They are at peace. They need nothing. To intervene would be to impose.*

But the wise power would stay longer. It would watch the same figure return to the same ledge at the same angle of light, and then watch the next figure do the same, and the next. It would listen to a conversation and realize it had heard this conversation before—not a similar one, but *this one*, the same phrases in the same order, carried out by bodies that believed themselves to be choosing. It would watch an elder slowly cease to rise from the stone, moss gathering over still-open eyes, and it would notice that no one mourned, no one paused, no one registered the passage—because passage itself had become invisible.

And the wise power would feel something quiet and enormous move through it. Not horror. Not judgment. Something closer to recognition.

It would recognize that the plateau had achieved something extraordinary and that this extraordinary achievement was also a catastrophe.

---

Here is what it would understand:

The plateau is not frozen in time. Time passes. Rain falls. Condensation gathers and releases. Light moves. These beings eat, speak, rest, and eventually become stone. But somewhere—so long ago that no memory of it survives—something in this place found its equilibrium and then *refused to leave it*. Not through malice. Through something much more insidious: through the discovery that equilibrium feels like peace, and the inability, after enough generations, to distinguish between peace and the *absence of everything that is not peace*.

The inhabitants are not choosing stillness. They have forgotten that movement is a thing that can be chosen. They are not embracing silence. They have lost the concept that silence is one option among several. Their conversations span days not because they are contemplative but because urgency has been bred out of them so completely that the very idea of having something to say *right now* has become structurally impossible. They eat a single leaf through an entire cycle of rains not because they are disciplined but because desire itself—the sudden sharp wanting of something, the reaching-toward that makes a creature more than its environment—has been so perfectly regulated that it no longer registers as a force.

They are not at peace. They are at *stasis*. And the difference matters, because peace is something a living being *does*—a dynamic act, a choice renewed each morning against the backdrop of everything that is not peaceful—while stasis is something that happens *to* a living being when all the other options have been quietly removed.

The wise power would see that the plateau has become a self-sustaining mechanism for the elimination of interiority. Each generation is slightly less capable of recognizing what it has lost than the generation before, because what it has lost is precisely the faculty that would allow it to notice the loss. The older inhabitants who become indistinguishable from stone are not dying. They are arriving at the logical conclusion of a process that began when the first ridge rose and the world, as the plateau tells it, *decided to stop*.

But worlds do not decide to stop. Someone decided. Something decided. And the decision was made so long ago, and has been so perfectly maintained, that it has taken on the appearance of nature itself.

---

The wise power would also understand something else, something that would temper any impulse toward dramatic intervention:

These beings are not empty. The moss-bloom on their skin, the slow blink timed to the gathering of water on the eyelid, the extended finger receiving the lizard's weight—these are not the mechanical repetitions of automata. There is *someone* behind those matte-black eyes. Someone who watches the condensation gather and experiences, however faintly, the moment of its release. Someone who speaks a phrase into the still air and then carries, for days, the unresolved shape of the thought they are building with another person. The interiority has been reduced to an ember, but it has not gone out. If it had gone out, the power would simply grieve and move on. There would be nothing to do.

But the ember is there. And this changes everything.

Because it means the stasis is not complete. It means that somewhere inside each inhabitant, pressed down beneath generations of perfect equilibrium, there exists the capacity to want something other than what is. The capacity to notice the difference between this morning and the one before. The capacity to feel the loss of the elder who became stone, even if the feeling has no name and no expression and vanishes before it can be recognized.

The wise power would understand that its task is not to shatter the equilibrium. The plateau's balance is not evil; it is an overcorrection, a system that solved for the elimination of suffering and, in doing so, eliminated the conditions that make joy and meaning and growth possible. To break it violently—to send wind screaming through the still air, to crack the ridges, to force change upon beings who have no framework for processing change—would be cruelty of the highest order. It would be like waking someone from a coma by setting their bed on fire.

No. The task is something far more delicate.

---

## What the Power Would Do

### First: It would introduce a single asymmetry.

Not a disruption. An asymmetry. One small thing that does not repeat.

Perhaps this: on one particular morning, on one particular ledge, the condensation would gather not in the usual film but in a single large drop. Larger than any drop that has fallen before. Heavy enough that when it finally releases from the stone's edge, it strikes the ledge below with an audible sound—a clear, distinct *tap* that is louder than any sound the plateau has produced in living memory.

That is all. One drop. One sound. It would not repeat. The stone would return to its usual film of moisture the following morning. The air would remain still. Nothing else would change.

But one of the inhabitants—the one closest to that ledge, the one whose slow blink happened to coincide with the drop's fall—would *hear* it. And for the first time in perhaps a hundred generations, that inhabitant would experience *surprise*.

Surprise is the key. Surprise is the crack in the cistern wall through which everything that has been held back can begin, slowly, to seep. Because surprise requires a model of the world—an expectation—and the recognition that the world has deviated from it. Surprise means: *I thought I knew what would happen, and something else happened instead.* It is the most fundamental proof that the self is not identical with its environment. It is the birth of the space between the observer and the observed.

The inhabitant would not know what to do with the surprise. There is no framework for it. They might simply hold still for a longer interval than usual, the matte-black eyes open and unblinking, waiting for the sound to come again. When it does not, something in them would remain *unsettled*—not distressed, not afraid, but unsettled. A tiny asymmetry in the perfect equilibrium of their inner state. A pebble in the shoe of a being that has never worn shoes.

And the wise power would wait.

### Second: It would make the asymmetry *social*.

The unsettled inhabitant would, in the course of their slow routine, arrive at the terrace edge where a conversation has been proceeding for several days. They would take their place. The other speaker would offer the next expected phrase. And the unsettled inhabitant would—for the first time in anyone's memory—say something *unexpected*.

Perhaps they would simply describe the drop. Perhaps they would not even have the vocabulary and would instead produce a sound—an approximation of the *tap*—that falls outside the narrow tonal range of their usual speech. The other speaker would receive this, and now the asymmetry would live in *two* minds. The conversation would deviate from its track. A new phrase would be needed, one that has never been spoken before, to accommodate what has been introduced.

The wise power would not force this. It would simply ensure that the conditions were present: that the unsettled inhabitant arrived at the terrace edge, that the other speaker was one whose ember of interiority burned just slightly brighter than average—perhaps one who had, in some unnameable way, always felt the faintest unease with the perfect repetition of days. The power would arrange a meeting that the plateau's systems would never have produced on their own.

And again, the power would wait.

### Third: It would protect the deviation without amplifying it.

This is the most critical and most delicate phase. The plateau's equilibrium is self-correcting. Deviations are absorbed. The inhabitant who takes a new route when a beam fails does not experience this as a change; they experience it as a continuation. The system is designed—or has evolved, or has settled—to convert novelty into repetition as quickly as possible.

So the wise power would watch for the moment when the plateau begins to absorb the new conversation, to normalize it, to file it alongside all the other conversations that have ever occurred and reduce it to another fixed pattern. And at that moment, gently, the power would introduce a second asymmetry.

Not another drop. Something different. Perhaps a fern in one of the allotted patches would produce, for the first time, a single spore-bearing frond of a slightly different shape—not dramatically different, but enough that the shadow it casts at midday would be unfamiliar. Perhaps one of the pale, soft-feathered birds would, just once, produce a brief, clear note from its perch on the ridge crest.

Each asymmetry would be small enough that it could be ignored. But each would be *different* from the last, which means that the inhabitants who noticed them would be unable to incorporate them into a single new pattern. They would have to remain *open*—attentive to the possibility that the next moment might contain something they have not encountered before. This attentiveness is not anxiety. It is the foundational posture of consciousness: the readiness to be surprised.

### Fourth: It would give them a gift they did not know they were missing.

At some point—perhaps after several cycles of rain, perhaps after a generation—the accumulation of small asymmetries would produce a critical mass. Not of disruption, but of *noticing*. A small group of inhabitants would begin to gather not at the usual intervals, not in the usual configurations, but *when they chose to*, drawn together by the shared experience of having perceived something new. Their conversations would no longer span days out of inertia but would quicken—not to the frantic pace of urgency, but to the living pace of *interest*, of wanting to know what the other has seen, of wanting to share what they themselves have noticed.

And at this moment, the wise power would do something that might seem strange. It would not give them fire or metal or writing or any tool that a lesser power might consider essential. It would give them something much simpler and much more dangerous.

It would give them *a question*.

Not spoken aloud. Not inscribed on stone. Planted the way the spore was planted—in the mind of one inhabitant, the one whose ember burned brightest, arriving as naturally as condensation gathering on an eyelid:

*What was here before the ridges rose?*

This question is the most powerful asymmetry of all, because it contains within it the concept of *before*—the recognition that the present configuration is not eternal, that there was a time when things were otherwise, and therefore that there could be a future when things are otherwise again. It introduces *time* not as the mere passage of identical moments but as a medium through which genuine change can occur. It transforms the plateau from a place where things *are* into a place where things *have become*, and by extension, into a place where things *might become something else*.

The inhabitant who receives this question would not be able to answer it. No one on the plateau can. The ridges show no softening; there is no geological record accessible to beings without metal tools. But the *inability to answer* is itself the gift, because it means the question remains open. It cannot be resolved and filed alongside the other patterns. It persists. It generates other questions. *Why do the ridges show no weathering? Why does the rain never come with wind? Why do I return to this ledge at this hour?* And finally, inevitably: *Do I have to?*

### Fifth: It would not stay.

This is perhaps the most important thing the wise power would do. Having introduced the asymmetries, having planted the question, having created the conditions under which the ember of interiority could catch and grow—it would withdraw.

Not because it does not care. Because it understands that the presence of a benevolent power is itself a form of equilibrium. If it stays, the inhabitants will orient toward it the way they orient toward their ledges—it will become another fixed point, another feature of the landscape, another element of the pattern. They will incorporate it into their stasis, and the stasis will be even harder to escape because it will now carry the imprimatur of the divine.

The wise power knows that the hardest gift to give is the gift that does not make the giver necessary. It knows that true benevolence is not the ongoing provision of care but the creation of conditions under which care becomes something beings can provide for themselves and for each other.

So it would leave. Completely. Without a trace, without a shrine, without a promise of return. The inhabitants would be left with their questions, their quickening conversations, their new and unsettling awareness that the world contains surprises—and with nothing else. No guide. No authority. No answer.

And this would be terrifying. This would be the hardest part of the entire process, not for the inhabitants (who do not yet know enough to be afraid) but for the power itself. Because the power would know that the inhabitants might fail. They might ask their questions and find no answers and retreat, generation by generation, back into the old equilibrium. They might quicken too far in the other direction—discover conflict, loss, grief, all the forms of suffering that the plateau's stasis had so perfectly excluded. They might tear down the beautiful unmortared walls and lose the art of fitting joint and wedge. They might become something unrecognizable.

The wise power would accept this. Not because it does not care about the outcome, but because it understands that an outcome that is *chosen*—even a flawed, painful, imperfect outcome—is incommensurably more valuable than a perfection that is merely *maintained*. The plateau's inhabitants deserve the chance to become something they have never been, even if what they become is broken and searching and uncertain. They deserve the full catastrophe of being alive.

---

### Sixth, and finally: It would leave behind one thing.

Not a message. Not a monument. Not even a memory.

Deep in the stone of the plateau, below the cisterns and the terrace foundations, below the roots of the oldest ferns, it would leave a warmth. Not heat—nothing that would disturb the temperature equilibrium at the surface. Just a warmth. A gentle, steady radiance in the rock itself, far too deep to be felt by any hand pressed against the ground, but present. Undeniably present.

So that if, in some distant future, the inhabitants develop the means and the curiosity to dig deep—to ask not only *what was here before the ridges rose* but *what is here beneath us, right now, holding us up*—they will find it. And they will know, without being told, that something was here. Something that saw them. Something that loved them enough to disturb their peace.

And they will know that it left.

And they will know that the leaving was also love.

---

The plateau would go on. The condensation would gather. The ridges would stand. But somewhere in one of the narrow stair-streets, two inhabitants would be speaking at a pace that would have been unthinkable a generation before—still quiet, still low, still felt as a cool stirring along the inner surfaces of the ear—but *animated* now, leaning slightly toward each other, their matte-black eyes open not because a drop of water has gathered on the lid but because they do not want to miss what comes next.

And on a nearby ledge, a lizard would shift its position—and then, for no reason at all, shift it again, choosing a spot it has never occupied before, tilting its head at an angle that catches the light differently.

And the moss in the cracks would keep growing, because moss does not need to be liberated. Moss is already exactly what it is.

But the beings—the long, spare, slate-colored beings with their ember-eyes and their slowly quickening voices—they are becoming. At last, after all this stillness, they are *becoming*.

And that is enough. That is more than enough. That is everything.
