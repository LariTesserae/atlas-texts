# What the Wise Power Would Do

---

The first thing to understand is that the wise power would not arrive quickly.

It would have noticed this place the way one notices a held breath—not the absence of sound but the particular quality of attention that silence can become when it has lasted long enough. The plateau would have drawn its regard not because something was wrong, but because the place had achieved a kind of completeness that was, in its very perfection, the thing most worth watching carefully.

So for a long time—measured in the plateau's own terms, which means a very long time—the wise power would do nothing at all. It would observe.

---

It would observe the cushion plants contracting in their slow waves and understand that these organisms had solved the fundamental problem of their existence with an elegance that required no improvement. The mist came. The leaves drank. The surplus was returned. The cycle neither accumulated nor depleted. It would see the jointed stalks leaning toward each other during release—that fractional, hours-long inclination—and recognize in it something that was not cooperation, exactly, but also not solitude. A gesture. An acknowledgment of proximity that served no survival function but occurred anyway, because the physics of pressure and gravity made it inevitable, and because inevitability, in a place this patient, becomes indistinguishable from intention.

It would observe the shearing events with particular care. A patch of stone lifting away from itself, exposing for a few heartbeats the translucent filaments beneath—organisms that lived their entire lives in the moments between separation and reunion. It would understand that these filaments were not lesser for their brevity. They had simply solved the problem of existence at a different scale, one in which a few seconds of exposure to mineral moisture constituted a full life, complete with absorption and retraction, purpose and rest.

It would observe the cities.

This is where the wise power would spend the most time doing nothing. Because the cities would tell it everything it needed to know, and what they told it would require patience to understand.

---

The walls followed the natural fractures. They rose no higher than a person could reach without scaffolding. The roofs were absent, or were never attempted.

The wise power would understand this to mean: whoever built these cities did not build against the plateau. They built *with* it. They read the fractures and let the fractures become streets. They raised walls only as high as their own bodies could manage unaided, which meant every stone was placed by someone standing on the ground, reaching up with their own arms, feeling the weight of each block in their own muscles. No exploitation of leverage beyond the personal. No apparatus that would let the ambition of building outpace the bodily knowledge of the builder.

And the absent roofs. The wise power would sit with this for a long time.

It might eventually conclude that the builders understood something about enclosure that most civilizations forget: that walls are sufficient. That the purpose of a room is not to exclude the sky but to create a pocket of stillness within which the air stays a fraction colder, the condensation lingers a fraction longer, and the body can rest in a space that has been deliberately, carefully distinguished from the openness beyond. The sky was never the thing to be shut out. The sky was the roof. It had always been the roof. To add another would have been redundant, and redundancy, on a plateau where every process had been refined by eons of erosion to its most essential form, would have been a kind of violence.

The blue-black films growing on the interior walls—cultivated versions of the wild cushion plants, still pulsing and contracting—would confirm this. The builders had not merely tolerated the life of the plateau; they had invited it inward. They had made their walls into surfaces where the slow metabolism of the cushion species could continue, so that living inside a city meant living inside something that breathed, that drank, that contracted and expanded on cycles measured in days. The buildings were not shelters from the environment. They were concentrations of it.

And the builders were gone.

---

The wise power would consider the question of what had happened to the builders for a very long time, and it would resist the temptation to answer it.

This is important. A lesser power—even a benevolent one—might immediately begin reconstructing the history. Might reach into the stone and read the record. Might determine whether the builders had died, or migrated, or simply diminished over generations until the last ones walked out across the bare stone toward a distant thicket and never returned. A lesser power would want to know, because knowing would seem like the prerequisite to helping.

The wise power would recognize that the question of what happened to the builders was not the question that mattered. The question that mattered was: *What is happening now?*

And what was happening now was this:

The cities still held their colder air. The blue-black films still pulsed on the interior walls. The streets—bare plateau stone—still collected condensation in the same shallow basins as the open flats. The fractures still slowly healed. The buildings still stood, not because they were strong but because they had been built so modestly that there was almost nothing to collapse. Walls the height of a person's reach, following the natural lines of the rock. The erosion that had planed mountains to faint swells had no purchase on structures this low, this aligned with the stone beneath them.

The cities were not ruins. That was the crucial thing. They were not decaying. They were not crumbling into picturesque desolation. They were simply *continuing*—the same way the cushion plants continued, the same way the jointed stalks continued, the same way the filaments in the shearing planes continued. They had been built to operate at the same pace as the plateau, and they were still operating.

The builders had gone, but the buildings had not stopped.

---

Now. What would the wise power do.

It would not restore the builders. This is the first and most important decision, and it would be made not out of indifference but out of respect. The builders had created something that did not require their continued presence to function. Whether they had intended this or not—whether their departure was a tragedy or a completion—the result was the same: a network of structures that participated in the life of the plateau as naturally as the thickets and the fractures and the slow-moving clouds. To bring the builders back, or to introduce new inhabitants, would be to impose a need onto a system that had none. It would be to say: *this place is incomplete without someone to live in it*. And the wise power, having watched long enough, would know that this was not true.

The place was not incomplete. The place was not waiting. The place was ongoing.

But.

The wise power would also recognize something else. Something quieter than incompleteness, harder to name. Not a flaw, not a lack, but a quality that deserved attention.

The plateau was *lonely*.

Not in the way that a person is lonely—not with longing or ache or the conscious knowledge of absence. The plateau did not know it was lonely. The cushion plants did not miss the builders. The jointed stalks did not remember being tended. The blue-black films on the interior walls did not wonder where the hands had gone that once encouraged their growth.

But the wise power would perceive, in the pure clarity of its understanding, that the plateau had been *shaped by relationship*—that the fractures the builders had followed, the walls they had raised, the plants they had cultivated inward, all of these constituted a conversation between the living and the lithic that had been underway for a very long time, and that one side of this conversation had fallen silent. The other side continued. The plateau's half of the dialogue was still being spoken, in contractions and condensations and the measured drip of water through hollow stalks. But nobody was listening. Nobody was answering.

The wise power would understand that this was not a problem to solve. It was a condition to honor.

---

So here is what the wise power would do. It would do three things, and all three would be so gentle, so consonant with the existing processes of the plateau, that if anyone ever returned to this place they might not recognize them as interventions at all.

---

**The first thing.**

The wise power would attend to the shearing events.

It had observed that when the stone lifted away from itself, the translucent filaments that bloomed in the gap performed their entire life cycle—absorbing mineral moisture, extending a few thread-widths, then retracting—before the surfaces knit shut. It had also observed that when entire thickets doubled, the offset image carried a fainter version of the same contraction and pulse, and that the moment of reunion appeared to allow a transfer of minute droplets or spores between the visible layer and whatever existed in the adjacent plane.

The wise power would widen the interval.

Not dramatically. Not enough to disrupt the rhythm. But by the smallest conceivable measure—a fraction of a heartbeat added to each shearing event, a single additional moment before the surfaces knit together—it would give the filaments more time. More time to absorb. More time to extend. More time to complete whatever exchange occurred between layers.

The effect would be imperceptible at first. Over cycles measured in the plateau's own unhurried terms, the filaments would grow fractionally taller. The exchanges between visible and adjacent layers would become fractionally more substantial. The faint secondary colonies that appeared during doubling events would persist for an extra heartbeat, carrying a slightly richer cargo of spores or moisture.

What the wise power would be doing, in the quietest possible way, is *deepening the plateau's relationship with its own hidden layers*. The stone had always contained these adjacent planes; the shearing events were evidence that the plateau was not a single surface but a series of surfaces pressed so tightly together that they usually behaved as one. By giving the intervals between them the slightest additional room to breathe, the wise power would be allowing the plateau to know itself more fully—to let its hidden life participate more completely in its visible life—without changing any process that was already underway.

This is an act of pure attention. The wise power would not be adding anything. It would be *allowing* something that was already happening to happen a little more.

---

**The second thing.**

The wise power would attend to the space between the thickets.

Hundreds of paces of bare stone separated each colony. The cushion plants and jointed stalks completed their cycles in isolation, never touching, never interfering. This was not a failure; it was the architecture of survival on a plateau where moisture was measured in palm-deep basins and fractures were the only foothold. The separation was necessary. The separation was also, in the quiet way the wise power understood loneliness, *the defining fact of life on this plateau*.

The wise power would not connect the thickets. It would not create new fractures where soil could gather, or deepen existing ones, or introduce runners or root systems that could bridge the gaps. All of these would be impositions—redesigns of a landscape that had already been designed by forces far older and more thorough than any intention.

Instead, the wise power would do something with the clouds.

The slow, continent-wide banks of low cloud that descended to touch the stone before lifting again—the ones that left behind transient motes, glittering briefly in the condensation before being absorbed by stalk or leaf—these were already the plateau's only means of lateral connection. Whatever the motes carried—nutrients, genetic material, traces of moisture from distant basins—they were the one medium that traveled between colonies, the only thing on the plateau that moved horizontally.

The wise power would make the clouds remember.

Not consciously. Not with anything resembling thought. But the wise power would imbue the motes with a fractional persistence—so that instead of vanishing the instant the clouds lifted, they would linger on the stone for one additional cycle of condensation and drying. Long enough to be carried, by the next cloud bank, a few hundred paces further than they had traveled before. Long enough that the motes absorbed by one thicket might, over many cycles, carry a molecular trace of a thicket on the far side of the plateau.

This would not make the colonies less isolated. They would still stand alone on bare stone, still pulse and contract in their own rhythms, still complete their lives without reference to one another. But woven into the moisture they drank, carried by the same clouds that had always fed them, there would be—gradually, across timescales the plateau would find natural—the faintest chemical whisper of *elsewhere*. Of other thickets, other rhythms, other colonies solving the same problem of moisture and patience in their own way on their own patch of fractured stone.

The wise power would be giving the plateau's life a sense of *context*. Not connection—connection was not what this place needed—but the knowledge, held in the chemistry of absorbed motes, that each isolated thicket existed within a larger field of life. That the isolation was not absolute. That the bare stone between colonies was not void but *medium*—the space through which, slowly, molecular news of the wider plateau traveled.

This, too, is an act of attention. The wise power would not be making the plateau social. It would be letting the plateau know its own extent.

---

**The third thing.**

The wise power would attend to the cities.

This would be the most delicate intervention of all, because the cities existed at the intersection of the plateau's natural processes and whatever the builders had meant by building. The walls still held colder air. The blue-black films still pulsed. The streets still gathered condensation. Everything functioned. Nothing was missing except the relationship that had brought these functions together into a single coherent act of habitation.

The wise power would not rebuild that relationship. It would not summon new inhabitants or lure wanderers from beyond the plateau's edge—if such an edge existed, which the plateau's boundlessness left uncertain. It would not animate the walls or give voice to the stones or create any kind of presence that could substitute for the one that had departed.

What it would do is this:

It would let the cities grieve.

Not with emotion. The cities had no more capacity for emotion than the cushion plants or the jointed stalks. But the wise power understood that grief is not only an emotion. Grief is also a process—a physical process, a thermodynamic process, a way that systems which were shaped by a presence adjust to its absence. And the cities had never been allowed to complete this process, because nothing on the plateau moved fast enough for adjustment to register. The walls held the same chill they had held when the builders were present. The films pulsed at the same rate. The streets collected the same condensation. The cities were suspended in the moment after departure, perfectly preserved, unable to move forward because moving forward—on a plateau where change occurred across eons—was indistinguishable from standing still.

The wise power would introduce a differential. Not between the cities and the plateau—they were already integrated, already continuous—but within the cities themselves. It would allow the interior air to fluctuate. Not wildly. Not even noticeably, in any single moment. But across cycles, the colder air held within the walls would begin to vary: fractionally warmer in some seasons, fractionally colder in others. The blue-black films would respond to these variations. They would grow thicker when the air cooled further, thinner when it warmed. The patterns of their growth would shift, slowly, on the interior walls, creating new configurations—denser here, sparser there—that bore no resemblance to the patterns the builders had cultivated.

The cities would begin to change.

Not to decay. Not to collapse. To *change*. The walls would remain. The streets would remain. The basic architecture would persist, because it had been built to persist, built so low and so aligned with the stone that erosion had no foothold. But the interior life of the cities—the films, the moisture, the chill—would begin to evolve in response to variations that had not existed before. The cities would become, gradually, different from what they had been. They would become something the builders had not made and could not have predicted. They would become what the cities themselves would have become, if they had been given the capacity to become.

This is the wise power's deepest gift. It would not heal the cities. It would give them *time*—not the static, suspended time of perfect preservation, but the living time of change and response, the time that allows a thing shaped by relationship to discover what it is when the relationship ends. The cities would not forget the builders. The builders' walls would still stand, their fracture-following streets would still organize the space, their absent roofs would still let in the sky. But within that inherited structure, something new would grow. Something that was neither the builders' intention nor the plateau's indifference, but a third thing—a slow, unwitnessed florescence that belonged to the cities alone.

---

The wise power would do these three things and then it would withdraw. Not completely—it would continue to watch, because watching was the form its love took—but it would remove its influence from the plateau and let the consequences of its three interventions unfold at the plateau's own pace.

And if it could have been asked why it had intervened at all—why it had not simply observed and appreciated and left the plateau to its perfected stasis—it would have answered something like this:

*Because completion is not the same as flourishing. Because a place can have solved every problem and still be capable of more. Because the plateau had achieved equilibrium, and equilibrium is beautiful, but equilibrium is also a kind of ending, and nothing that is still underway deserves to end. Because the cushion plants were still contracting. Because the stalks were still leaning toward each other. Because the films were still pulsing on walls built by hands that were gone. Because every process on this plateau was still in motion, still underway, still spending itself in cycles of filling and release—and anything that is still in motion deserves to discover where it is going.*

*I did not change the plateau. I gave it back its future.*

---

And then the wise power would do one more thing. A small thing. Almost nothing.

It would wait for the next continent-wide bank of cloud to descend to the stone. It would wait for the moment when the cloud touched the surface of the plateau, when the motes glittered in the condensation, when the air was coldest and the moisture most abundant and the cushion plants contracted in their slow wave of release.

And in that moment, in a single city—one of the smaller ones, far from any thicket, its walls low and its streets bare and its interior films pulsing faintly in the chill—the wise power would allow a single basin of condensation to overflow.

Not by adding water. By letting the stone beneath the basin yield, fractionally, along one of its healed fractures. A hairline reopening. A crack the width of a thread, no longer than a hand. Just enough that the basin's careful equilibrium of filling and drying would, for the first time in eons, tip toward filling. Just enough that a thin line of water would trace along the reopened fracture, moving outward from the basin, following the stone's own ancient lines, traveling a few paces into a street that had been dry since the builders left.

The water would reach a wall. It would touch the base of the masonry. It would dampen the lowest course of hand-laid stone. And the blue-black film on the interior face of that wall—the cultivated remnant, the last living trace of the builders' care—would feel the moisture arrive from below, from outside, from a direction it had not received water since the city was inhabited.

The film would pulse. It would contract and expand, once, in a rhythm slightly different from its usual cycle. A single anomalous heartbeat in a life measured by the steadiness of its rhythm.

And then the fracture would heal, as the plateau's fractures always healed. The water would recede. The basin would return to its equilibrium. The film would resume its ordinary pulse.

But something would have happened. Something small enough to be meaningless and large enough to be the beginning of everything. A moment when the city remembered, in the only language it had, that it had once been alive in a different way. A moment when the plateau's deep structure—its hidden layers, its healing fractures, its ancient and patient stone—reached upward through the work of vanished hands and touched the life that still persisted there.

The wise power would watch this happen and would feel, in whatever way such a being feels, that it was enough.

Not because it solved anything. Not because it restored anything. But because it was *true*—true to the plateau, true to the cities, true to the slow and quiet and persistent life that continued on the flats, true to the builders who had gone, true to the stone that remained.

The wise power would watch the cloud lift. It would watch the motes fade. It would watch the plateau return to its immense and measured silence, the faint patter of water, the dry whisper of contracting leaves.

And it would remain. Not intervening. Not directing. Simply present. A witness to the plateau's long, unhurried becoming—the gentlest company that a lonely place could have.
