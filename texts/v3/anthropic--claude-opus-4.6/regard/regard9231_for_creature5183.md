# The Attending

---

The power that turns its attention here does not arrive.

That is the first thing to understand. It does not descend, does not appear, does not manifest in fire or voice or the sudden parting of clouds. It has no need to. It is not the kind of power that lacks something the hamlet possesses, nor the kind that possesses something the hamlet lacks. It is simply aware now—fully, patiently aware—of this particular fold in the ridge, this particular knot of living architecture and the hundred-odd souls braided into it.

And for a long time, measured by the hamlet's own rhythms—several full cycles of the afternoon rains, many risings and settings of the midge clouds, the complete unfurling of one new leaf on the oldest vine wall—the power does nothing at all.

It watches.

Not from above. Not from outside. The watching has no vantage point. It is more like the way the elders listen with their palms pressed to a wall: total attention without direction, a kind of hearing that includes the thing heard. The power attends to the hamlet the way the hamlet attends to itself—by feeling where the weight falls, where the moisture gathers, where something is growing too tight or not tight enough.

And what it finds, in that first long attending, is something very close to health.

---

This matters. It matters because a benevolent and wise power is not a restless one. It does not need to justify its attention by finding problems. It does not need to improve things in order to prove it has been present. It is capable of the most radical act available to any power: *recognition without intervention*. The ability to see a thing clearly and to say, not with words but with the quality of its attention: *yes, this is what it is, and what it is has integrity*.

The hamlet has integrity. The power sees this the way a hand pressed to a living wall can feel a healthy pulse. The vine chambers breathe. The water finds its channels. The beetles scrape. The people murmur. The children climb. The quick-furred animals leave their resinous prints on the upper lattices and drop their seeds in the leaf mold and the seeds take root, and the roots become the floor on which new feet will walk, and the feet will wear the floor smooth, and the smoothness will be softened again by moss, and the moss will hold moisture that condenses on the leaves above. The cycle is unbroken. It is neither utopia nor paradise—those are words for places that have been frozen at their best moment—but something more durable: a system that has learned how to be imperfect without being damaged by its imperfections.

So the power does not heal, because nothing here is sick. It does not teach, because the knowledge here is already sufficient to the life being lived. It does not protect, because protection implies a threat, and the power is wise enough to know that the hamlet's real vulnerabilities—a season of unusual cold, a vine blight, the slow geological shifting of the ridge itself—are not threats to be defeated but conditions to be met, and the hamlet already knows how to meet conditions. That is what it does. That is all it has ever done.

The power recognizes this. It is the first gift, though no one in the hamlet will know it has been given.

---

But recognition, for a power of this kind, is not passive. To attend fully to a thing is to *be in relationship with it*, and relationship, even the most careful and unobtrusive kind, changes both parties. The power, in watching, is changed: it now carries the hamlet inside its awareness, a living thread in whatever vast fabric it holds. And the hamlet, though it cannot feel the watching, is changed too—in the way that a person who is deeply loved is changed even before they know they are loved. Something in the structure of their situation has shifted. They are no longer alone in quite the same way.

This is not mysticism. Or rather, it is not *only* mysticism. There is a practical dimension. The power's attention has a quality to it, a specific texture, the way sunlight filtered through photosynthesizing leaves has a specific color. And that quality, by virtue of being directed so fully and so steadily at this one small settlement, begins to do something that the power did not precisely choose but also does not prevent:

It makes the hamlet *more visible to itself*.

---

Here is what that looks like, from inside:

An elder named—but names here are not fixed things; they shift with the seasons, with the growth patterns of the walls around a person's birth-chamber, so let us say an elder *known by the curving callus on her left forearm*—is pressing her palm to the inner wall of a vine chamber on the settlement's eastern edge. She does this every few days. It is routine. She is listening for the tightness that means the chamber's inhabitant, a young man recovering from a fall, needs more ventilation—or the looseness that means the wall is losing structural integrity and needs new runners spliced in.

Today she feels something she has felt a thousand times before, but feels it *differently*. The wall's pulse—the slow hydrostatic rhythm of water moving through the vine's vascular tissue—registers not just as information but as *presence*. She has always known, intellectually, in the way that someone knows the ground is beneath their feet, that the wall is alive. But today the knowledge moves from her mind to her hand to the center of her chest, and she stands very still for a long time, palm flat against the green surface, and the vine's rhythm and her own heartbeat enter a brief, accidental synchrony, and she feels what can only be described as *mutual recognition*: she is aware of the wall, and the wall, in whatever way a vine is aware of anything, is aware of the warmth and pressure of her hand.

She has no word for what has happened. She does not need one. She simply stands there, breathing, and when she finally pulls her hand away, she makes her assessment—the chamber needs a new vent cut on the upper left side—and moves on.

But something is different. For the rest of the day, her cuts are slightly more precise. Her splices hold on the first attempt rather than the second. When she places her hand on the next wall, she listens a little longer, a little more deeply, and hears a faint creak she might otherwise have missed: a lattice platform two levels up is bearing more weight than it should. She calls up to the young woman on the platform, who shifts her work to a different spot. The platform holds. It would have held anyway, probably, for another season at least. But now it will hold for three.

Nothing dramatic has happened. The power has not intervened. It has merely attended, and its attention has made the elder's own attention—which was already formidable—fractionally more acute. As if a lens had been cleaned that she didn't know was smudged.

---

This is the kind of thing the power does. Not once, dramatically, but continuously, in ways so small they cannot be distinguished from the hamlet's own functioning.

A child climbing the inner lattice of a vine dome feels, for a moment, the precise distribution of her weight across three contact points—left foot, right hand, the crook of her left elbow where a cross-brace meets a sapling upright—and adjusts instinctively, shifting a fraction of her mass to the hand, and the lattice, which was about to protest with a creak that would have startled her into a less stable position, bears the weight silently, and she climbs on without knowing that anything has happened.

A man braiding fiber rope pauses and holds a finished section up to the light. He has braided thousands of ropes. This one looks the same as all the others. But he turns it once more in his hands, and something about the way the filtered green light catches the surface tells him the tension is uneven in the third strand—not dangerously so, not in any way he could explain—and he undoes the last handspan and rebraids it, and the rope, when it is eventually used to lash a new vine arch into its initial curve, holds with a cleanness and security that means the arch will set properly on the first attempt rather than requiring adjustment.

A pair of young people, the ones who have been coaxing two parallel vines toward each other for almost a year now, stand in the rain one afternoon and watch the water run down the inner surfaces of their proto-chamber. They have been worried, in the quiet way people here worry, that the left vine is growing too slowly—that it will not meet its partner before the dry weeks come and the growth pauses and the whole structure spends a season lopsided and vulnerable to wind. They stand in the rain and watch, and the young woman reaches out and touches the slower vine, and she feels—not a surge of growth, nothing so crude—but a steadiness. A sense that the vine is doing what it can with what it has. She turns to her partner and says something low, lost under the rain, and he nods, and they go inside, and they stop worrying. The vine does not grow faster. It grows at exactly the rate it was always going to grow. But because they have stopped worrying, they stop checking it every day, stop bending it slightly each time to test its progress, and the vine, freed from the constant small disturbances, puts its energy into thickening rather than recovering, and when the dry weeks come it is strong enough to hold its shape, and when the rains return it surges to meet its partner in a single tenday, and the arch closes, and the first night they sleep inside the new chamber the walls are still translucent enough to let the moonlight through, and the green glow is the first thing their eyes open to in the morning.

---

The power could do more. This is important to understand. It could accelerate growth. It could divert the courses of storms. It could whisper knowledge of metallurgy, of writing, of medicine that works faster than the slow herbal wisdom the hamlet already carries. It could make the ridge itself more stable, compress ten thousand years of geological settling into an afternoon, ensure that the ground beneath the hamlet will never shift.

It does none of these things.

Not because it is indifferent to suffering—it is not; it is *exquisitely* attuned to suffering, can feel the dull ache in the elder's wrist where decades of vine-work have worn the joint, can feel the grief of the mother on the north side of the settlement whose infant did not survive the last cold snap, can feel the loneliness of the young man with the healing leg who lies in his vine chamber and listens to the life of the hamlet continuing without him. The power feels all of this the way the roots feel water: completely, without resistance, as a condition of being present.

But it is wise. And its wisdom tells it something that lesser powers—powers that are merely strong, merely generous—never learn:

*That a living system which has found its own way to an imperfect balance is more durable, more beautiful, and more deeply alive than one which has been perfected from outside.*

The hamlet loses children. The hamlet loses elders. Vine walls collapse in storms and must be rebuilt over years. The ridge shifts, slowly, and channels must be re-dug and terraces re-cut and sometimes a whole section of the settlement must be abandoned and allowed to return to wild growth while a new section is coaxed into being on stabler ground. These losses are real. They are not lessons. They are not *secretly good*. The power does not aestheticize them or justify them. It simply knows that the hamlet's way of meeting these losses—slowly, together, with hands pressed to living walls and voices kept low and the whole settlement shifting like a single organism to absorb the blow—is the hamlet's own deepest strength, and that to remove the losses would be to remove the occasions on which that strength is practiced and renewed.

This is not the logic of a power that withholds help to teach self-reliance. That would be cruel, and this power is not cruel. It is the logic of a power that understands that the hamlet is *already helping itself*, that the help is woven into every vine arch and every braided rope and every elder's callused hand, and that the most violent thing it could do would be to replace that help with its own.

---

So what, then, does it actually *do*?

It does what it has already been doing, but let me name it more precisely:

**It holds the hamlet in a quality of attention so steady and so thorough that the hamlet's own self-awareness deepens by a degree so small it cannot be perceived—only lived.**

The people do not become wiser. They do not gain new skills. They do not hear voices or see visions or feel a sudden warmth that tells them they are watched over. They remain exactly who they are: small, damp, quiet, tough, attuned to growth and water, mortal, limited, beautiful in the way that only limited things can be beautiful.

But their own native gifts—the listening hands of the elders, the climbing intuition of the children, the patience of the young couples coaxing new growth, the precise braiding of the rope-makers, the timing of the vent-cutters who know exactly when a chamber needs to breathe—all of these become, over time, *fractionally more themselves*. Not enhanced. Not amplified. Just... settled more deeply into their own nature. The way a stone in a stream becomes more itself as the water shapes it: not changed into something else, but refined into what it already was.

The elder with the curving callus begins to feel things in the walls that she would have felt anyway in another few years—but she feels them now, and the few years are not wasted but given to other listening, deeper listening, the kind that eventually lets her press her palm to the ground itself and feel where the ridge is stable and where it is not, and this knowledge passes to the younger vine-workers not as doctrine but as habit, as a way of placing the hand and tilting the head that they learn by imitation and carry in their bodies for the rest of their lives.

The children climb with a fluency that is not superhuman but is fully human in a way that most humans never achieve—every grip placed with the whole body's intelligence, every weight-shift felt before it is thought. They do not climb better than their parents did at their age. But they climb *more like themselves*, each one finding their own way up the lattice rather than copying a single learned technique, and the lattice holds each of them differently, and the structures that result from a lifetime of this kind of climbing—the chambers and arches and platforms that these children will one day build—will have a quality of fit, of *specific rightness*, that earlier generations achieved only occasionally and by accident.

The rope-makers braid rope. The vent-cutters cut vents. The water flows in its channels and the beetles scrape algae and the cicadas roar and the small green birds nest in the vine-nodes and the quick-furred climbers drop their seeds. Everything continues as before. The power has not added anything. It has only made it very slightly easier for everything to be *precisely what it already is*.

---

There is one more thing the power does, and it is the most important, and it is the hardest to describe.

It grieves with them.

Not visibly. Not in any way that offers comfort, because comfort offered from outside a grief can become a wall between the griever and the grief, and this power will build no walls. But when the mother on the north side of the settlement sits in her vine chamber in the days after her infant's death, and the walls around her are alive and growing and the water drips into the channels and the insects drone and the whole hamlet continues its slow metabolism around her, and she feels that the world's continuing is both unbearable and necessary—the power is there. Not helping. Not healing. Not explaining. Just there, in the way that the ground is there under the root mat, in the way that the ridge is there under the ground. A presence so fundamental that it cannot be felt as presence, only as the absence of a certain kind of aloneness.

She does not know this. She will never know this. She will grieve for the precise and unrepeatable length of time that her grief requires, and she will emerge from it carrying the grief with her—not recovered from it, not healed of it, but *shaped by it* the way the stones of the terraces are shaped by feet—and she will go back to her work, and her hands will know things they did not know before, and the vines she tends will grow in configurations that are subtly different from anyone else's, carrying in their curves the specific history of a woman who lost a child and kept living, and the chambers those vines become will have a quality—call it tenderness, call it gravity, call it a particular way of holding the light—that no one will be able to name but everyone will be able to feel.

The power did not cause this. The woman did this. The grief did this. The living architecture did this. The power only *attended*, and in attending, made it infinitesimally less likely that the grief would become something other than itself—less likely to curdle into bitterness, or flatten into numbness, or be hurried past in the press of daily survival. The grief was allowed to be grief, fully, for exactly as long as it needed to be, and the woman was allowed to be a woman grieving, and the hamlet was allowed to be a hamlet holding a grieving woman, and none of these things were rushed or fixed or improved.

This is what benevolence looks like when it is also wise. It looks like nothing. It looks like the hamlet continuing to be itself, a single slow organism breathing on the side of a ridge that never quite dries, every surface beaded with water, every wall alive, every channel flowing, every hand knowing where to press and when to listen and how long to wait.

---

Years pass. Generations. The power does not leave, because it never arrived. It is simply present, the way the moisture is present, the way the ridge is present. The hamlet changes—of course it changes. Vine walls collapse and new ones are grown. The ridge shifts and a whole terrace must be abandoned. Children are born and elders die and the callus patterns change and the braiding techniques evolve and the quick-furred climbers develop slightly different markings as their generations turn over faster than the humans' do. The hamlet in a hundred years is not the hamlet of today, and in a hundred more it will be something else again, and eventually—in a thousand years, or ten thousand, or when the ridge itself finally shifts too far—it will be gone entirely, and the vines will grow wild and the channels will silt up and the stone terraces will be buried under leaf mold and the last of the green-stained, web-toed people will have moved on or merged with others or simply ended, the way all things end.

The power knows this. It has always known this. It does not try to prevent it, because the hamlet's mortality is not a flaw to be corrected but the very condition that makes it alive. A hamlet that could not end could not grow. A vine that could not die could not make room for new vines. A person who could not grieve could not love with the specificity that grief requires. The power knows that its own benevolence, if it ever tipped into the desire to *preserve*—to freeze this beautiful, imperfect, breathing thing in its current form—would become the most profound violence it could commit. To make the hamlet eternal would be to kill it more thoroughly than any storm or blight or geological catastrophe ever could, because it would remove the one thing that makes the hamlet *the hamlet*: its willingness to be temporary.

So the power holds the hamlet in its attention, and the hamlet lives, and one day the hamlet dies, and the power grieves for it the way it grieved with the mother who lost her child: fully, without resistance, for exactly as long as the grief requires. And the grief shapes the power the way the grief shaped the mother, and the power carries the hamlet forward in its awareness the way the mother carried her lost child forward in the curves of the vines she tended, and nothing is fixed and nothing is lost and nothing is preserved and nothing is wasted.

The ridge remains. The moisture remains. The light filtering through green leaves remains, though the leaves are different leaves now, wild and unguided, growing in whatever direction their own nature takes them.

And if you pressed your palm to the ground in that place, and tilted your head, and listened the way the elders listened—

You might feel it. Not the power. Not the hamlet. Not anything so specific as a memory or a presence.

Just the faint, steady pulse of something that was attended to, once, so completely that the attention left a mark—not on the ground, not on the air, but on the quality of what it means for a place to have been *fully and precisely itself* for as long as it lasted.

That is what the power did.

That is all it did.

It was enough.
