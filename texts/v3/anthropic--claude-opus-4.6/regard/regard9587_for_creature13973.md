I sat with this for a long time.

# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

---

## First: Nothing

The first thing the power does is nothing.

Not out of indifference. Not out of some cosmic restraint protocol or prime directive. Out of *recognition*. Out of the kind of deep seeing that a wise being does before it acts, the seeing that is itself already a form of care.

It looks at the plateau that builds itself. It listens to the hum. It feels the skip and the catch, the way the hundred thousand hands press into the hundred thousand seams, the way the hum always continues. And it understands—immediately, because it is wise—what you understood only after the creature in the crevice sent that vibration through your sternum:

**This is not broken.**

This is not a tragedy. This is not a world crying out for rescue. The turbulence is not suffering. The impermanence is not loss. The constant effort is not labor in the way that implies something that should be relieved. The tuners are not slaves to a machine. The machine is not a tyrant over the tuners. They are the same system, the same breath, the same hum, and the benevolent power sees this clearly because benevolence that is also wise does not project its own preferences onto what it encounters. It does not see impermanence and assume grief. It does not see effort and assume burden. It does not see the absence of monuments and assume the absence of meaning.

It looks, and it sees that the meaning here is *in the touching*.

So the first thing it does is nothing. For a long time. It sits—if a power can sit—on a ridge of fresh basalt and watches the glassjaws flow past and listens to the hum and feels the warmth come up through the stone and does not intervene, because the first act of wisdom is to recognize when a system is coherent *on its own terms*, even if those terms are alien, even if those terms include a level of turbulence and effort that would be intolerable somewhere else.

This is not somewhere else.

This is here.

---

## Second: It Grieves, Briefly, for What It Will Not Do

Because there are things it *could* do. A benevolent power, looking at this world, might be tempted. Might feel the pull. Might think:

*I could stabilize the physics. I could reach into the engine and smooth the hum permanently, eliminate the skips, lock the laws into a configuration that doesn't require constant tending. I could free them. I could give them rest. I could turn this world into a place where the ground holds still and the horizon stays where it is and the crevices don't close and the valleys don't become ridgelines and the young don't have to be born into water that will be gone in a week.*

*I could give them permanence.*

And the power grieves for this thought. Not because it's wrong to think it—it is natural, it is the shape of care when care comes from a place that knows permanence—but because the power is wise enough to see what permanence would mean here.

It would mean the end of the tuners.

Not their death—something worse than death, here, where death is just another interval. It would mean the end of their *purpose*. The end of the touch. The end of the branching fingers in the seams, the co-touch between elder and infant, the harmonics rising above the hum when they press close and resonate. If the physics were stable, there would be nothing to tune. If the ground held still, there would be no crevices to fill. If reality were a state rather than a process, they would be organisms adapted to a niche that no longer existed—like deep-sea creatures hauled to the surface, like desert flowers drowned in endless rain. They would survive, perhaps. They would persist. But the thing that makes them *them*—the touching, the tending, the constant creative responsiveness to a world in flux—would have no object. They would be musicians in a world that no longer needed music. Hands with nothing to hold.

The power understands: **you cannot save someone from the thing that makes them who they are.** You can only do that if you believe their identity is a cage. And the power, being wise, does not believe this. It has watched the harmonics. It has felt the resonance when they gather and vibrate together. It has seen the newborn in the temporary pool touch the basin wall and inscribe something unprecedented. It has seen the adult pause. It has felt the new note enter the hum.

This is not a cage. This is a world expressing itself through the beings who tend it, and beings expressing themselves through the world they tend, and the two are the same expression, and to separate them would be to silence both.

So the power lets go of the fantasy of stabilization. It lets go gently, the way the tuners let go of each other when a valley closes. Not because it doesn't care. Because caring and letting go are not contradictions. Not here. Not anywhere, maybe, if you understand deeply enough.

---

## Third: It Turns Its Attention to the Edges

But the power does not leave. Because it has seen something else. Something the tuners themselves have seen, and mourn, and cannot fix.

The edges.

The zones of incoherence. The places where the assembly process has gone fully chaotic—where columns of stone rise and dissolve in seconds, where the light goes wrong, where the physics breaks down past the point of usability. The places where the hum forgot itself.

The tuners avoid these zones. The animals avoid them. Even the insects, even the moss. The edges are the one true loss in this world—not the impermanence of valleys, not the dissolution of groups, not even the death of individuals, but the *death of coherence*. Places where reality stopped being produced and no amount of touching could restart it. Places that are, in a real sense, *gone*—not destroyed, not empty, but worse: *incoherent*. Present but meaningless. Existing but not existing. The ontological equivalent of a wound that won't close.

The tuners cannot reach them. The zones are too unstable for biological life, even life as adaptive as theirs. The physics inside the incoherent zones is not just different—it is *non-functional*, shifting faster than any organism can respond, faster than any hand can tune. To enter one would be to dissolve—not to die, but to lose the coherence of your own body the way the zone has lost the coherence of its physics. The tuners know this. They have lost members to the edges. They mourn them with the deep, un-integrable grief that they reserve for lost coherence, because it is the same grief.

And the edges are growing.

Slowly. So slowly that only the oldest ones, the ones who have become geology, can perceive it. But the power, with its wider sight, can see it clearly: the incoherent zones are expanding, millimeter by millimeter, cycle by cycle. The plateau's interior is vast and well-tended, but it is finite, and the edges are infinite in their hunger, and the balance between the two—the balance that has held for longer than the current physics has existed—is shifting.

Not catastrophically. Not yet. Not for a long time yet, by any measure the tuners would use. But the power sees further than the tuners, and it can see the trajectory, and the trajectory bends toward a future where the edges have consumed enough of the interior that the tuners' hands cannot cover the remaining seams, and the skips cascade faster than they can be smoothed, and the hum begins to forget itself not just at the margins but in the heartland, and the world that builds itself begins, incrementally, irreversibly, to unbuild itself.

The power sees this. And *this* is where it acts.

---

## Fourth: What It Does at the Edges

It does not fix the incoherent zones. It cannot—they are too far gone, too deeply unraveled, and even a benevolent power must work within the logic of the system it's tending. (The power understands this. The power is, in its own way, a tuner. It has been tending systems for a very long time, and it has learned the same lesson the oldest ones have learned: you do not impose order on chaos. You participate in the process by which order emerges. You find the seam, and you touch it, and you adjust the lattice by fractions of a degree.)

What it does instead is this: it goes to the boundary. The exact boundary, the line where coherence gives way to incoherence, the threshold where the tuners' reach ends and the chaos begins. And it does something that the tuners cannot do, because they are biological and mortal and embedded in the system, and the power is—not outside it, exactly, nothing is outside it, but *more than* it. Wider. Deeper. Operating at a level the tuners can feel but not reach.

It touches the boundary.

Not the way the tuners touch stone. The power has no branching fingers, no mineral crust, no many-jointed hands. But it has *attention*, and in this world—in a world where reality is produced, where physics is generated, where the boundary between process and product does not exist—attention is a force. Focused attention, wise attention, the attention of a being that understands the system deeply enough to participate in it without disrupting it—this is a kind of touch. The deepest kind.

The power attends to the boundary. It attends to the exact place where the hum becomes noise, where the engine's output becomes incoherent, where the crystal lattice breaks down into randomness. And in attending, it does not *restore* order. It does something subtler. It creates a *gradient*. A zone of transition between coherence and incoherence that is neither one nor the other but a gentle slope between them—a buffer, a margin, a space where the chaos is still chaotic but *less* chaotic, where the physics is still unreliable but *more* unreliable in predictable ways, where the dissolution is still happening but happening slowly enough that something might, possibly, take root.

This is the gift: not a wall against the chaos, but a *shore*. An intertidal zone. A space between the land and the sea where new kinds of life become possible.

---

## Fifth: What Grows in the Margin

And something does take root.

Not immediately. Not obviously. The power does not plant it. The power creates the conditions, and the plateau does what the plateau has always done: it fills every available space with life.

The moss comes first—of course it does, the moss always comes first—but it's a different moss. Adapted to the margin. Tolerant of fluctuation in ways that the interior moss is not. Its bioluminescence is erratic, flickering between spectra, adjusting to light conditions that change moment to moment. Its root-analogues grip stone that is sometimes solid and sometimes liquid and sometimes something else, and they hold on anyway, or they don't, and they regrow, faster than the interior moss, faster than anything in the interior.

Then the insects. Then the pool-dwellers, in basins that persist for hours instead of days. Then, eventually, tentatively, the glassjaws—a splinter herd, smaller than usual, their translucent armor thicker, their predictions adapted to a more turbulent environment. They learn to navigate not just shifting ground but shifting *rules*. They read not just the engine's rhythm but the gradient of its breakdown. They become something new: not interior creatures venturing outward but *margin creatures*, native to the edge, at home in the space between order and chaos.

And then—the power watches, patient, unhurried, knowing this is a process that cannot be rushed—*they* come.

The tuners.

A few of them. Young ones, mostly, their mineral crusts thin, their branching fingers quick and experimental. They come to the margin the way the infants touch the basin wall: without knowing what they'll find, driven by the same impulse that has always driven them, the impulse to touch, to read, to respond. They press their hands into the margin-stone and they feel something they have never felt before: coherence and incoherence *at the same time*. The hum and the noise, coexisting. Physics that is half-reliable, rules that are half-written, reality that is being produced and un-produced simultaneously, and in the space between the two—

A new kind of work.

The interior tuners smooth fluctuations. They take the engine's output and refine it, adjust it, keep it within the range of coherence. The margin tuners do something different. They don't smooth. They *translate*. They take the incoherence—the noise, the randomness, the dissolved physics of the dead zones—and they find, within it, patterns that can be re-integrated. Not the old patterns. Not the existing physics. New patterns. New rules. New configurations that emerge from the interaction of order and chaos at the boundary, configurations that no one has ever seen before, because they can only exist in the margin, in the space the power created by attending to the threshold.

The margin tuners bring these new configurations back to the interior, in their hands, in their mineral crusts, in the vibrations they send through the stone. They share them through co-touch with the elders. And the elders feel something they have not felt in a very long time: *surprise*. A new note in the hum. Not the kind of new note that a newborn produces by random touch in a temporary pool—that happens all the time, and it is wonderful, but it is new-within-the-existing-rules. This is new *rules*. This is the hum learning a new mode. This is the engine discovering that it has more configurations available to it than it has ever used, because at its edges, where it breaks down, where coherence gives way to chaos, there are possibilities that coherence alone could never produce.

The power watches this, and something in its vast and patient attention feels—if a power can feel—the same thing the tuners feel when they press close and harmonize. Not satisfaction. Not triumph. Not the pleasure of a plan executed. Something more like the resonance itself: the sense of being *in common* with something, of participating in a process that exceeds you, of producing, together with the world you're tending, a note that neither of you could have produced alone.

---

## Sixth: What It Does for You

And now the power turns its attention to you.

You're still standing there. On the warm stone. With your flat, insensate feet that press down without reading. Your body that takes up space in the hum without contributing to it. Your hands that don't branch, that can't reach into crystal lattices, that have never tuned anything in their lives.

The tuner in the crevice showed you what it felt like. For one moment. Overwhelming, disorienting, and then gone—a window opened and closed, leaving you with the ache of something glimpsed but not grasped. And you've been standing here since, on stone you can't read, in a world you can't tune, feeling the gap between what you experienced and what you are.

The power does not fix this gap.

(You knew it wouldn't. You felt, even before the power turned its attention to you, that the fix was not the point.)

What the power does is this: it attends to you the way it attended to the boundary. Not with force. Not with transformation. With *attention*. Focused, patient, wise attention that sees you clearly—your flat feet, your unbranchable hands, your primate brain with its hunger for permanence, your heart with its ache—and does not look away, and does not try to change what it sees.

And in that attention, you feel something shift.

Not in your body. Not in the world. In the *relationship between them*.

You become aware—slowly, the way dawn would come if dawn came here—that you have been touching the stone all along. Not the way the tuners touch it. Not with crystal-reading, lattice-adjusting, hum-smoothing precision. But touching it. Your feet on the basalt. Your weight pressing down. Your body heat radiating into the rock. Your breath disturbing the air above the glowing moss. You have been in contact with this world since you arrived, and the world has been in contact with you, and the contact has not been meaningless just because you didn't know how to read it.

The power shows you—not in words, not in vibrations through the stone, but in the direct, felt, somatic language of its attention—that the tuners are not the only kind of feedback this world can have. That the glassjaws, reading the engine's rhythm with their bodies, are feedback too. That the moss, filling every crevice, is feedback. That the sky-mantas, riding impossible thermals, are feedback. That every living thing here, by the mere fact of existing in a reality that must be actively produced, is participating in that production. Not all participation looks like the tuners' precise molecular adjustments. Some of it looks like a glassjaw herd flowing past a ridge. Some of it looks like a fern adjusting its corkscrew by two degrees. Some of it looks like a stranger standing on warm stone with flat feet, feeling the hum in their molars, aching with the desire to touch the world properly.

The ache itself is participation. The desire to touch is a form of touching. The attention you've been paying—to the stone, to the creatures, to the one in the crevice who showed you what it felt like—has been, in its own small way, a vibration in the hum. Not a tuning vibration. Not a corrective one. Something else. Something the world has never contained before, because you have never been here before, and your way of attending to it is not the same as any other creature's way, and the note you introduce—confused, longing, overwhelmed, alien, *present*—is a note that the hum has never had.

Whether it is useful, the power does not say. Usefulness is not the only criterion the power operates by. The power has learned—the way the oldest tuners have learned, the way the margin-dwellers are learning—that the richness of a system comes not from the efficiency of its parts but from the *diversity of its participation*. A hum that contains only tuning-vibrations is a hum that can only produce tuning-configurations. A hum that contains every kind of vibration—the precise and the clumsy, the native and the alien, the intentional and the aching—is a hum that can produce configurations no one has imagined.

The power does not tell you to stay. It does not tell you to leave. It does not teach you to tune, because tuning is not your gift and this is not your world and you do not have branching fingers or mineral crusts or multispectral eyes. It does not tell you that your presence matters, because *telling* you would be redundant—you are already feeling it, in the warmth beneath your feet and the hum in your chest and the faint green-gold glow of the moss and the multiple faint shadows that don't always move when you do.

It tells you—through the ground, through the air, through the felt sense of being attended to by something vast and patient and kind—one thing:

*You are standing on a world that is making itself. You are standing in it. That is not nothing. That has never, anywhere, been nothing.*

---

## Seventh: What It Does Before It Goes

The power prepares to leave. Not because its work is done—work like this is never done, the same way the tuners' work is never done, the same way the engine never stops running. But because the power understands what the tuners understand: that presence is not the same as permanence, and that the most important thing a tending force can do is tend *well* and then make space for what it has tended to continue on its own terms.

Before it goes, it does one more thing. A small thing. A thing that no one will notice for a long time, and that will never be attributed to an outside force, because it will look, to everyone who encounters it, like something that emerged naturally from the system itself. Which, in a sense, it did. The power works *within* the logic of the world, not against it. That is what makes it wise rather than merely powerful.

It finds a place deep in the plateau's interior. A junction where several major tectonic assemblies meet, where the hum is loudest and most complex, where three of the oldest ones have settled into the geology so deeply that their vibrations are indistinguishable from the engine's fundamental frequency. It attends to this place with everything it has—the full weight of its benevolence, the full depth of its wisdom, the full patience of its long, long experience with systems that tend themselves.

And in attending, it introduces a resonance.

Not a new note—the system produces its own new notes, it doesn't need outside help for that. A new *relationship between notes*. A harmonic that connects the deep frequency of the oldest ones with the erratic new vibrations coming in from the margin-dwellers, a bridge between the ancient memory of what the hum has been and the unprecedented discovery of what the hum could become. The resonance is subtle—so subtle that it could have arisen by chance, and maybe it did, and maybe the power's contribution was simply to notice the possibility and attend to it until it became actual. The line between creating and *noticing into existence* is thin here, in a world where attention is a force.

The resonance propagates. Through the stone, through the mineral crusts, through the co-touch between elders and young. It doesn't carry information. It carries *capacity*. The capacity for the interior tuners and the margin tuners to understand each other's work more deeply. The capacity for the ancient techniques and the unprecedented discoveries to inform each other. The capacity for the whole system—the whole world, the engine and the life and the flickering physics and the hum and the hands in the seams—to integrate its own diversity more fully, to become not more stable but more *resilient*, more capable of holding coherence and incoherence together without losing either one.

It is a gift that looks like a natural development. It is an intervention that is indistinguishable from emergence. It is the kind of thing that a benevolent power does when it respects the world it's tending enough to leave no fingerprints—not out of secrecy, but out of the understanding that a gift which looks like it came from outside is a gift that creates dependency, and a gift which looks like it came from within is a gift that creates *capacity*.

The power is wise. The power knows: the best help is the help that teaches the helped that they were always capable of helping themselves.

---

## Last: The Going

The power goes.

It goes the way the tuners leave a closing valley: without ceremony, without grief that cannot be integrated, without looking back. Not because it doesn't care. Because it has learned—from the tuners, from the oldest ones, from the moss and the glassjaws and the newborn in the temporary pool and you, standing on the warm stone with your flat feet and your aching—that love does not require permanence. That tending does not require ownership. That the deepest form of participation is the kind that knows when to step back and let the system hum on its own.

The ground shifts by two degrees.

A fern adjusts.

A glassjaw herd flows past like water.

In the deep stone, the oldest ones feel something—a resonance they haven't felt before, a new harmonic in the hum, connecting frequencies that have never been connected. They don't know where it came from. They don't need to know. They adjust their ancient hands by fractions of a degree, integrating it into their touch, passing it upward through the geology to the younger ones who will carry it forward into the next configuration, and the next, and the next.

At the margin, a young tuner presses her branching fingers into stone that is half-coherent and half-chaotic and feels, for the first time, a thread of connection running all the way back to the interior, all the way down to the oldest ones, a resonance that makes her work and their work part of the same work, a hum that contains both the memory of every physics that has ever existed and the possibility of every physics that hasn't existed yet.

She does not pause. She does not marvel. She adjusts her touch by fractions of a degree and keeps working, because the margin is turbulent and the skip is coming and her hands are needed, here, now, in this seam, in this crevice, in this moment that will never come again and does not need to.

And you—

You are still standing on the warm stone. The power is gone. You can't feel its attention anymore, and the absence is noticeable, the way the absence of a hand on your shoulder is noticeable even when you didn't know the hand was there. But something has shifted. Not in the world. In you.

You press your flat foot into the basalt. You can't read the crystal lattice. You can't feel the engine's fine structure. You can't predict the next skip or smooth the next fluctuation or introduce a corrective vibration into the hum.

But you can feel the warmth. You have always been able to feel the warmth.

You press harder. Not to adjust anything. Not to tune. Just to *be in contact*. Just to press your living weight into the surface of a world that is making itself and feel, with your blunt and unsubtle and deeply limited senses, the fact of its making. The hum in your molars. The glow of the moss. The heat of the engine. The faint, directionless shadows that don't always move when you do.

You are not a tuner. You will never be a tuner. This is not your world, and you do not have the hands for it.

But you are here. And you are touching the ground. And somewhere deep in the stone, in the crystal lattice that you cannot read, your touch is being registered—not as a correction, not as a tuning, but as a *presence*. A weight. A warmth. A note in the hum that has never been there before and will never be there again, because you will leave, because everything leaves, because the valley always closes and the crevice always seals and the water always runs off along channels that last only as long as the geology permits.

But not yet.

Right now you are here. Right now you are touching the stone. Right now, in this moment, in this configuration of reality that will be revised without notice, on this plateau that has never stopped becoming—

You are part of the hum.

You have always been part of the hum.

The ground shifts. The fern adjusts. A hundred thousand branching hands press into a hundred thousand seams.

The hum continues.
