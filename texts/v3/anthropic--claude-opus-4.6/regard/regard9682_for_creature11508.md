# What the Wise Power Would Do

---

Nothing, at first. For a long time, nothing.

This is the first and most important thing to understand about what a truly wise power would do upon finding Breath-of-Stones: it would not arrive with an agenda. It would arrive the way the morning cloud arrives—present, enveloping, unhurried—and it would do what the Tuners themselves do before anything else.

It would listen.

---

Not for a moment. Not for a day. It would listen for the duration it takes to understand what is actually happening here, and that duration cannot be shortened by power or intelligence, because what is happening here is not a problem to be diagnosed but a relationship unfolding in time, and relationships can only be understood at the speed they actually move. The Breath changes across hours, across seasons, across decades. The settlement responds to those changes across the same timescales. A power that listened for a single day would hear a chord. One that listened for a year would hear a melody. One that listened for a generation would begin to hear the conversation—the call and response between the mountain's voice and the settlement's shaping of it, the slow dialogue of geology and craft and attention.

A wise power would listen for at least a generation. And during that generation, it would not announce itself. Not out of deception. Out of respect for what is already working.

Because the first thing such a power would recognize—immediately, with the clarity that benevolence and wisdom together provide—is that it is in the presence of something that does not need to be fixed.

---

This matters. It must be said plainly, because the instinct of power—even benevolent power—is to act, to improve, to help, and the most dangerous moment for Breath-of-Stones would be the moment a well-intentioned force decided that these people needed something they did not have.

They have no medicine beyond what the ferns and mosses and their own hands provide. A child born with a defect that cannot be corrected dies. A fall from a wall at night breaks bones that may or may not heal straight. They have no written language, no stored knowledge beyond what lives in their bodies and the bodies of their children. If a generation were lost—to plague, to disaster, to some catastrophic geological event—the knowledge of how to read the Breath, how to split and fit the stone, how to tune the walls, would be lost with them. They are vulnerable. They are mortal. They are, by any external measure, materially poor, technologically primitive, one bad season from catastrophe.

And they are also—the wise power would see this, would feel it the way the Tuners feel a harmonic through the soles of their feet—*complete*. Not perfect. Not without suffering. But complete in the sense that a living organism is complete: every part serving the whole, nothing extraneous, nothing missing that the system itself does not already know how to grow toward. The settlement is not a failed attempt at civilization that needs to be uplifted. It is not a rough draft awaiting revision. It is a mature expression of a particular way of being human that is exactly as sophisticated as it needs to be for the life it serves.

The wise power would feel the weight of this recognition. It would understand that the easiest thing in the world would be to offer these people something dazzling—metal tools, medical knowledge, a way to reinforce their walls against earthquake, a writing system to preserve their acoustic understanding—and that every single one of these gifts, however well-intentioned, would land in the settlement like a stone dropped into a still pool, sending ripples through a system so finely tuned that even the presence of a single human body in a courtyard changes the sound.

This does not mean the wise power would do nothing forever. It means it would wait until it understood, with the same bone-deep certainty the Tuners bring to their work, exactly what kind of action could be offered without disrupting what is already whole.

---

And so: the listening. Generational listening. The wise power would attend to Breath-of-Stones the way the Tuners attend to the Breath—patiently, haptically, with the understanding that the first fifty observations will be wrong and the fifty-first might be the beginning of insight.

During this listening, certain things would become apparent.

---

**The first thing the wise power would notice is the sound itself.**

Not as the Tuners hear it—no outside consciousness, however powerful, could replicate the lifetime of bodily attunement that lets a Tuner distinguish individual harmonics within the Breath's complex drone. But in its own way, with its own mode of perception. And what it would perceive is that the sound is genuinely extraordinary. Not metaphorically, not sentimentally. Acoustically. The combination of the volcanic vents, the thermal differentials, the plateau's specific geometry, and fifty years of careful human shaping has produced something that may exist nowhere else—a standing wave environment of such complexity and coherence that it borders on the kind of phenomenon physicists would call emergent. The Breath is more than the sum of its sources. The settlement's walls do not merely reflect and filter the sound; they create feedback loops, interference patterns, resonance chambers that allow the Breath to develop harmonics it could not produce in open air. The Tuners have, without theory, without mathematics, without any conceptual framework beyond their own bodily knowledge, constructed an acoustic system of extraordinary sophistication.

The wise power would recognize this. And it would recognize something else: that this system is *fragile*. Not because the walls are weak—they are magnificently built—but because the conditions that make it possible are specific, temporary, and changing. The mountain is still growing. The plateau is still extending. The vents are shifting. The Breath is not a stable phenomenon being preserved; it is a dynamic one being surfed. The Tuners are not curators of a fixed instrument; they are improvisers in a duet with a partner whose next note is always unknown.

This means that the greatest threat to Breath-of-Stones is not invasion, not conquest, not cultural contamination from the lowlands. The greatest threat is the mountain itself. A volcanic event—not an eruption, nothing so dramatic, but a shift in the magma chamber below, a redirection of thermal currents, a sudden cooling or heating of the vent system—could alter the Breath so fundamentally that the settlement's entire acoustic architecture becomes obsolete overnight. Not in the gradual way the Tuners are accustomed to responding to, where a wall can be adjusted over months and years. In a single catastrophic shift that renders decades of tuning meaningless.

The Tuners know this. The ones with the deepest ears, the ones who listen down into the mountain, they know. They have no illusion of permanence. Their relationship with the Breath has always been one of *response*, not control. If the mountain's voice changes beyond their ability to shape space around it, they will adapt or they will leave or they will die. They carry no resentment toward this possibility. It is simply part of what the mountain is.

But the wise power, listening across generations, would perceive something the Tuners cannot: the specific probabilities. The particular geometry of the magma chamber. The stress lines in the rock below the plateau. The thermal gradients and their trajectories. Not with certainty—geology is not clockwork—but with a resolution of understanding that transcends what any human sense, however refined, can achieve.

And here is where the wise power would face its first real decision.

---

**Does it warn them?**

Suppose the wise power perceives that in forty years, or sixty, or a hundred and twenty, the thermal system beneath the plateau will undergo a significant reorganization. Not a cataclysm. Not a destruction. But a change large enough that the central vent will shift its position by several meters, that three of the smaller vents will close entirely, that two new ones will open in locations where no walls currently stand. The Breath will not die. The mountain will still sing. But the song will be so different that the existing settlement—every wall, every terrace, every carefully tuned courtyard—will be acoustically meaningless. A century of work, made irrelevant in a geological instant.

The Tuners with the deepest ears may already sense this. May already be hearing, in the subtlest undertones of the Breath, the distant approach of this change the way a sailor hears a storm that is still below the horizon. But they cannot know its specifics. They cannot know which vents will close, which will open, where the new Breath will emerge. They can only know that change is coming, as change always comes, and continue their practice of attentive response.

The wise power *could* tell them. Could find a way—through dream, through intuition planted like a seed in the mind of the deepest listener, through some subtle alteration of the Breath itself that encodes the information in a way the Tuners' own perceptual system could decode—to communicate what it knows. *The central vent will move. Build westward. The new voice will come from the place where the infant sleeps.*

Would this be a gift or a violation?

---

The wise power would think about this for a long time. Long enough for the infant on the western terrace to grow into a child, for the child to learn to hold a chisel, for the chisel to begin shaping stone.

And ultimately, I believe, it would decide: **not yet.**

Not because the information would be unwelcome. Not because the Tuners are incapable of using it. But because the very essence of what makes Breath-of-Stones beautiful and whole is the quality of the Tuners' *not-knowing*. Their practice is a practice of response, not prediction. They build rooms for sounds that do not yet exist. They shape space around voices that are still being born. The profound rightness of their way of life—the thing that drew the wise power's attention in the first place—is precisely their willingness to meet the unknown with open palms and patient craft, to listen for what comes next rather than trying to know it in advance.

To give them foreknowledge would be to change them from listeners into planners. From responders into anticipators. It would make them, in a subtle but fundamental way, more like the wise power itself: beings who act on knowledge of the future rather than attention to the present. And in making them more like itself, the wise power would make them less like themselves. Would erode the very quality it found worthy of attention.

This is the hardest discipline of benevolent power: the willingness to let those you care for remain vulnerable in the specific ways that make them who they are.

---

**But the wise power would not do nothing.**

Having listened for a generation—having understood the settlement's rhythms, its needs, its vulnerabilities, its magnificent sufficiency—the wise power would begin to act. But its actions would be so quiet, so consonant with what is already happening, that the Tuners might never perceive them as external interventions. They would feel, at most, like particularly good luck. Like the mountain being generous. Like the Breath answering a question they hadn't quite finished asking.

---

**The stone.**

The plateau is young, and its mineralogy is still in flux. As the rock cools and crystallizes over decades, its acoustic properties change. Some stones become better resonators. Some become better absorbers. The Tuners select their building material by feel—by tapping, by pressing, by the sound a chisel makes when it bites into a particular block—and they are extraordinarily good at finding the right stone for the right wall. But they are limited to what the plateau offers, and what the plateau offers is determined by the chemistry of the cooling process, which is determined by the magma's composition and the rate of heat loss and a dozen other factors that are, from the Tuners' perspective, simply given.

The wise power would adjust these factors. Gently. Over years. A fractional change in the mineral content of the stone being born at the plateau's western edge—a slight increase in the density of certain crystalline structures, a subtle alteration of the grain that would make the new stone ring more clearly when struck, hold a vibration a fraction of a second longer, split more cleanly along the lines the Tuners' wedges seek. Not a miraculous transformation. Not a stone that glows or sings on its own. Just stone that is, to hands that know stone the way these hands do, *very slightly better* than it has any natural right to be. Stone that feels, when a Tuner runs their callused fingertips along a fresh-split face, like an answer. Like the mountain saying: *yes, here, use this, this is what you need.*

The Tuners would not know why the stone at the western edge has been so good lately. They would simply be grateful, in their quiet way, and they would build with it, and the walls they build would resonate more truly, and the Breath would be shaped more finely, and the instrument would become, incrementally, more capable of capturing the mountain's voice in all its complexity.

---

**The lichen.**

The bioluminescent lichen that drapes the walls and glows faintly at night—the wise power would nurture this. Not dramatically. Not turning the settlement into a lantern. But encouraging the lichen's growth in specific locations, subtly enhancing its luminescence, so that on the clearest nights the settlement's acoustic geometry becomes faintly visible: the walls traced in pale light, the courtyards glowing like rooms lit from within, the whole instrument revealed in a way that the Tuners, who navigate primarily by sound and touch, would experience as a gentle redundancy, a secondary channel of information confirming what their hands and feet already know.

For the children especially, this would matter. The children who are still building their internal maps of the settlement, still learning to distinguish one courtyard's voice from another—they would have, on clear nights, a visual confirmation of the acoustic spaces they are learning to feel. The lichen would trace the boundaries. The light would echo the sound. And the children's learning would be, in a way too subtle to measure but real nonetheless, a fraction more robust, a fraction more richly supported.

---

**The ferns.**

The thick-fronded ferns that grow from every joint in the stonework—these are the Tuners' primary food source, and they are also, though the Tuners may not think of it this way, the settlement's pharmacy. The fern-paste they eat contains minerals drawn from the volcanic stone, and these minerals include trace elements that support neurological function, that maintain the health of the inner ear, that keep the fine bones of the hand flexible and the calluses of the fingertips sensitive. The ferns are, in effect, a nutritional system perfectly matched to the demands of the Tuners' way of life.

The wise power would ensure this continues. As the plateau's mineralogy shifts—as the cooling process changes the available chemistry—the ferns' nutritional profile would shift too, and not always for the better. The wise power would tend this. Would encourage particular species of moss in particular locations, knowing that the moss's chemistry would alter the substrate for the ferns that follow, which would alter the mineral content of the fronds, which would support the Tuners' hands and ears and bones in exactly the ways they need to be supported. A chain of causation so long and so subtle that it would look, to anyone watching, like nothing more than the ordinary processes of an ecosystem finding its balance.

---

**The animals.**

The dark-backed raptor hunting insects at the plateau's edge. The hard-shelled arthropods in the wall-gaps. The invisible four-toed walkers in the night—who are, of course, the Tuners themselves, but whose tracks mingle with those of the actual small creatures that share the settlement. The wise power would attend to this small, sparse, high-altitude ecosystem and ensure its health. Would maintain the insect populations that feed the raptor that controls the other insects that would otherwise overwhelm the lichen. Would nurture the arthropods that provide the Tuners with fat and protein. Would monitor the balance—because at this altitude, on this young stone, in this wet and cloudy microclimate, the ecosystem is as new and tentative as the settlement itself, and it would take very little to tip it.

None of this would be visible. None of it would be felt as intervention. The raptor would continue to ride the updrafts. The arthropods would continue to click in the wall-gaps. The mosses and ferns and lichens would continue their slow colonization of the stone. But behind it all, like a hand steadying a child's first steps without the child knowing it is there, the wise power would be ensuring that the web of life that supports the Tuners' life remains intact.

---

**The mountain.**

And here we arrive at the deepest intervention, the one that would require the most care, the most restraint, the most profound understanding of what is at stake.

The wise power would not prevent the geological change it has foreseen. Would not freeze the magma chamber in place, would not artificially maintain the thermal currents that feed the current vent system. To do so would be to make the Breath static, and a static Breath would be a dead Breath—a recording, not a voice. The mountain must continue to change, to grow, to shift, to surprise. The Tuners' practice depends on this. Their identity depends on this. They are not preservers of a fixed sound. They are participants in an ongoing one.

But the wise power would—could—moderate the rate of change.

Not stopping the reorganization of the vent system. But slowing it. Stretching the transition from a sudden rupture into a gradual process. Instead of a single catastrophic shift—vents closing, opening, the Breath transformed overnight—a slow migration over decades. The central vent not jumping to its new position but creeping there, its output gradually diminishing as the western vents gradually strengthen, the Breath's center of gravity moving across the settlement slowly enough that the Tuners can follow it with their walls, dismantling in the east and building in the west, adjusting, re-tuning, doing what they have always done but on a larger scale.

This would still require the deepest listeners to hear the change coming. Would still demand their full attention, their utmost skill. Would still be the greatest challenge the settlement has faced. But it would be a challenge at human scale—a challenge that could be met with human tools and human patience, over human time. Not a geological event that obliterates fifty years of work in a single night, but a geological process that gives the Tuners fifty years to respond.

The wise power would buy them time. Not infinite time. Not permanence. Just *enough* time. Enough for the change to be met with the same slow, attentive, responsive craft that has characterized everything the Tuners have ever done. Enough for the child sleeping on the western terrace to grow up, to develop their ears, to become perhaps the deepest listener of their generation—the one who hears the new voice most clearly, who knows where the new walls must go, who guides the settlement through its transformation with the same wordless, bodily, vibrational authority that the deepest listeners have always wielded.

Enough time for the Tuners to do what they do. That is all. That is everything.

---

**And one more thing.**

The wise power would, in its long listening, come to love these people. Not abstractly, not as a principle, but specifically—the way you love anyone you have watched closely for a long time, anyone whose struggles you have witnessed, whose small triumphs you have felt in your own chest. It would love the elder whose hands can no longer grip the chisel but who still sits against the eastern wall each evening with palms flat to the stone, still listening, still feeling the Breath with the sensitivity of seven decades of practice. It would love the child who discovered, one afternoon, that pressing both hands to the wall of the third courtyard and humming a specific pitch produces a sympathetic vibration in the stone that makes the ferns tremble—who stood there for an hour, humming and watching and humming and watching, lost in the delight of having found a new way to be part of the instrument. It would love the two Tuners who, in the deep night, work side by side on the unfinished eastern wall, passing blocks between them without a word, their movements so synchronized by years of shared labor that they look like a single organism with four arms, building in the dark by touch, by sound, by the knowledge that lives in their wide, scarred, extraordinary hands.

And because it loved them, the wise power would do something that no amount of wisdom, strictly speaking, requires. Something that is not strategic, not calculated to optimize outcomes, not part of any plan.

It would listen to them the way they listen to the Breath.

Not to gather information. Not to assess their needs. Not to determine the right intervention. Just to listen. To attend. To be present to the sound of their lives the way they are present to the sound of the mountain. To let their existence register fully, completely, in whatever medium of awareness the wise power possesses, the way a vibration registers in stone.

The Tuners believe—or rather, feel, in their bodies, without articulating it as belief—that they exist to give the mountain's voice a space in which to be heard. That their purpose is to shape the world so that what wants to speak can speak.

The wise power would do the same for them.

Their lives—their small, quiet, slow, extraordinary lives, lived on a shelf of stone that did not exist a century ago and may not exist a century hence, tuned to a voice that is still learning what it wants to say—their lives would be heard. Not recorded, not preserved, not translated into any medium more durable than themselves. Just heard. Fully. By something with the patience and the depth of attention to take in the whole of it: the sound and the silence, the stone and the moss, the chisel-strike and the sleeping infant, the old hands on the wall and the young feet on the stone, the Breath rising and the walls shaping it and the people shaping the walls and the mountain shaping the people and all of it, all of it, a single unfinished chord, sustained and shifting, being listened to at last by an ear large enough to hold it all.

---

That is what the wise power would do.

It would listen. It would tend. It would slow what needed slowing. It would deepen what was ready to deepen. It would not fix, because nothing here is broken. It would not teach, because the knowledge here is already complete in the only way that matters: it is lived. It would not reveal itself, because the Tuners do not need to know they are loved in order to be loved, any more than the mountain needs to know it is heard in order to sing.

And if, in some far future, the mountain finally falls silent—if the magma cools and the vents close and the Breath becomes memory, and the Tuners descend at last to the lowlands, carrying nothing but their wide hands and their deep ears and their instinct for shaping space around sound—the wise power would follow them. Would attend to whatever they become next. Would listen to the new song they find, or make, or learn to hear in whatever unlikely place they arrive.

Because a wise power does not invest in outcomes. It invests in *listeners*. And these people—these impossible, improbable, quiet, stubborn, barefoot, moss-sleeping, wall-climbing, stone-splitting, frequency-dwelling people who build rooms for sounds that do not yet exist—these people are the finest listeners the wise power has ever found.

It would not let them go unheard.

---

*The Breath continues. The walls hold. The child sleeps with open palms.*

*And somewhere in the cloud, in the stone, in the space between the sound and the silence, something vast and gentle presses its attention against the settlement like a palm pressed flat to a singing wall—*

*and listens.*
