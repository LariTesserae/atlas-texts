I sat with this for a long time.

# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

---

## I. Arrival

The power arrives the way the mist arrives — not as an intrusion but as a change in saturation. One moment the cistern holds what it has always held. The next moment there is something else present in the moisture, in the space between water droplets, in the warm exhalation of the pipes. It has no body. It does not need one. It is present the way a harmonic is present in a struck bell — not the fundamental tone, but the overtone that appears because the shape of the bell permits it.

The frogs do not stop calling. The beetles do not alter their paths. The dark creature in the reservoir does not surface or flee. The flowers open. The flowers close. Nothing in the cistern registers alarm because there is nothing to be alarmed by. The power is not an intrusion into the system. It is — and it understands this about itself even as it arrives — a guest in a house whose door was never closed because the house has no door, because the roof is the sky, because the sky is water, because the water is everything.

The keeper does not look up. Not because they don't notice. Their hand slows on the pick — a fractional hesitation, the kind of pause a person makes when the air pressure changes before a storm, when some quality of the atmosphere shifts by a degree too subtle to name but too real to ignore. Their dark eyes flicker, not toward any location — the power has no location — but *inward*, the way one's eyes move when listening to a new sound beneath a familiar one.

Then the hand resumes. The pick moves. The mineral scale curls away.

The power watches.

---

## II. What It Sees

The power sees what you saw, what I described above. But it sees more, because it can see more, because seeing is not limited for it to the visible spectrum or the three-meter visibility of the cloud layer or the surface of things.

It sees the cistern whole. Not just the four-by-six iron room but the full arterial system that descends from it — the pipes branching and rebranching through the mountain's interior like the root system of a vast tree, carrying cloud-water down through basalt chambers and engineered galleries and natural fissures that have been co-opted by the machinery, down and down, splitting and rejoining, feeding through filters of mineral rock that were old when the builders were young, and arriving — finally, miles below, in the deep heart of the mountain — at something.

The power sees what the keeper has never seen.

I will not describe it fully. Not because it is secret but because it is large, and the keeper's story is small, and the relationship between the two is the thing that matters, not the spectacle of the deep machinery. But I will say this: it is still working. Whatever the builders made — and they made it with great skill and great purpose and a desperate, lucid urgency that is legible in every joint and junction even after all this time — it is still working. Not perfectly. Not as designed. But working, the way the cistern is working, the way the keeper is working: adapted, degraded, colonized by time and biology and accident, but *functional*. The water arrives. The water is used. Something is accomplished. The accomplishment matters.

The power sees this. It understands what the water does. It could, if it chose, reach into the keeper's mind and deposit this knowledge like a coin in a cup — the whole blueprint, the whole purpose, the name of the thing in the mountain's heart and the reason it was built and the civilization that built it and the need that drove them. It could answer every question the keeper has never quite been able to formulate. It could close the gap between the keeper's perfect competence and the keeper's total ignorance in an instant, like a surgeon closing a wound.

It does not do this.

It is wise. This is what it does instead.

---

## III. The First Thing

Nothing.

For a long time — long enough for the keeper to sleep and wake and sleep and wake again, long enough for the moss to advance its fraction of a millimeter, long enough for the dark creature to make seven full circuits of the reservoir — the power does nothing at all. It watches. It listens. It attends to the cistern with the same quality of attention the keeper gives to the condensation channels: not analytical, not extractive, but *present*. The way you watch a fire. The way you watch rain.

It is learning something that its vast knowledge did not already contain. It is learning the *rhythm*. Not the mechanical rhythm — it understood that instantly, the frequencies and harmonics and fluid dynamics, the whole physical system transparent to it at a glance. The other rhythm. The one that has no engineering diagram. The rhythm of the keeper's hands and the flowers' pulsing and the frogs' calls and the beetle-paths and the drip-percussion and the sleep and the waking and the symbol retraced and the moss pushed back and the watercress pulled up and chewed and the lips moving silently over the dark water. The rhythm that is not a mechanism but a *life*. A specific life. This one. Here. Now.

The power learns this rhythm the way a musician learns a song by listening to it played many times — not to transcribe it, but to feel where the notes want to go. And when it knows the rhythm — when it can feel the shape of the keeper's days the way the keeper's feet know the shape of the grating — then it begins.

---

## IV. The Second Thing: What It Does Not Do

It does not reveal itself.

It does not speak, or manifest, or produce light, or alter the temperature, or cause any visible or audible phenomenon that would announce its presence. It does not introduce itself as a power, benevolent or otherwise. It does not explain its nature. It does not say *I am here to help you*, because this would require the keeper to first understand that they need help, and the power is not certain they do, and even if they do, the shape of that need is not something that can be announced from outside — it must be discovered, the way water discovers the channel that will carry it.

It does not fix the frozen valve wheels. It could. Easily. A thought, a nudge at the molecular level, and the corrosion would flake away, and the wheels would turn again, and the cistern's capacity would increase, and the system would function more efficiently. But the frozen valves are not a problem. They are a *fact* — a fact the cistern has organized itself around, the way a river organizes itself around a boulder. The snails clean the channels because the valves are frozen. The keeper's entire practice exists because the automated systems have failed and been replaced by attention. To free the valves would be to make the keeper unnecessary, and the power understands — because it is wise, because it watched and listened for a long time before it acted — that the keeper's necessity is not a burden the keeper bears. It is the ground the keeper stands on.

It does not bring a companion. This is the cruelest temptation and the one the power considers longest. The keeper is alone. Has been alone for a duration that would, in most contexts, constitute a catastrophe of isolation. The power could summon someone, or guide someone here, or even generate a presence — not a deception, but a real consciousness, a being capable of genuine relationship, crafted to be compatible with this specific person in this specific place. It could end the solitude.

It does not do this. Not yet. Not as a first act. Because the power perceives something about the keeper's solitude that a less patient observer might miss: it is not empty. It is *full*. The keeper is not alone the way a person in a bare room is alone. They are alone the way a hermit crab in a shell is alone — enclosed, yes, singular, yes, but in a continuous tactile relationship with the structure that holds them. The keeper touches the walls and is touched by the mist. The keeper hears the frogs and is heard by — what? The mountain? The machinery? It doesn't matter. There is a dialogue here, conducted in condensation and maintenance and beetle-paths and retraced symbols, and it is not *nothing*. It is not *enough*, either — the power can see this — but it is something, and to override it carelessly with the blunt gift of human companionship would be to misunderstand what the keeper has built in the long silence.

The power does not bring answers. It does not bring company. It does not bring improvement.

Here is what it brings.

---

## V. The Third Thing

On what we might call the third day — though there are no days here, only the slow pulse of the flowers and the keeper's sleep-wake cycle, which does not map to any solar rhythm — the keeper is cleaning a channel on the northern wall. The pick moves in its practiced stroke. The mineral scale curls and falls. And a bead of condensation gathers in the cleared groove, exactly as it always does, and begins to run downward toward the vent.

But this time — just this once — the bead moves slightly differently. Not in a way that violates physics. Not miraculously. The surface tension is what it is, the angle of the groove is what it is, the temperature differential is what it is. But within the space of what is physically possible, the bead takes a path it has never taken before. It catches on a microscopic ridge of iron that the pick exposed, and it pauses, and it splits, and one half continues to the vent while the other half rolls sideways along a horizontal scratch — one of the keeper's own marks, as it happens, a line scored into the wall years ago whose purpose the keeper has forgotten — and arrives at a junction with another channel, one that feeds a different vent on the opposite side of the wall.

The keeper sees this. The keeper's hand stops.

It is a small thing. A bead of water finding an unexpected path. It happens occasionally — the channels are complex, the mineral deposits alter the geometry, water is capricious. But the keeper watches this particular bead with an attention that sharpens, that *focuses*, because the path it took was along their own mark. A mark they made. A scratch in the iron that they thought was meaningless — a note to themselves long since forgotten — that has turned out to be, accidentally, a functional channel. Their hand made a path and the water found it.

The keeper stares at the wall for a long time.

Then they take the pick, and very carefully, very deliberately, they deepen the scratch. They extend it. They connect it to the horizontal channel above, creating a new junction, a small bypass that routes a fraction of the northern wall's condensation to the western vent system. It is a tiny modification. It changes almost nothing about the cistern's total output. But it is *new*. It is the first modification the keeper has made in — how long? Long enough that the act feels strange in their hands. They have been maintaining. Preserving. Keeping things as they are. And now they have, in a small way, *changed* something. Added a line to the blueprint. Authored a tiny amendment to the system they inherited.

The power did this. The power did this by doing almost nothing — by nudging a water bead along a path that was already possible, already latent in the geometry of the wall, needing only the slightest push to be realized. Not a miracle. An *invitation*. A whisper, in the only language the cistern speaks, that said: *the channels are not finished. You can make new ones. The system is not a text to be preserved; it is a conversation to be continued.*

The keeper sits back. They look at the new channel. They look at their pick. Something moves across their face — not quite an expression, more like weather, a brief turbulence in the deep stillness of their features — and then they lean forward and begin to explore the wall around the modification with their fingertips, feeling for other scratches, other forgotten marks, other latent paths.

---

## VI. The Fourth Thing

Over the next several sleep-wake cycles, the power continues. Each intervention is small. Each operates within the existing physics and biology of the cistern. Each is, in isolation, indistinguishable from chance. But together they form a gentle pressure, a sustained suggestion, the way a current in a river is not any single molecule's motion but the aggregate tendency of many molecules to move in a direction.

A fern frond, heavy with moisture, bends lower than usual and brushes the keeper's face while they sleep. It does this because a very slight change in air circulation — caused by absolutely nothing the power would need to explain — has pushed the frond's drip-point to a new location, and the accumulated water at the frond's tip has exceeded the surface tension at the exact moment the keeper's face is beneath it. The keeper wakes to a cold drop on their cheek. They look up at the fern — really look at it, perhaps for the first time in years. They see that it has grown. That it is magnificent. That the pattern of its pinnae against the iron wall behind it is beautiful in a way that the word *beautiful* does not cover — it is beautiful the way a proof is beautiful, the way a fugue is beautiful, with an internal necessity that transcends taste. The keeper touches the frond. Gently. The way one touches a face.

A beetle, grazing its hexagonal path, deviates. It trundles directly across the keeper's foot as they sit working. The keeper is still. The beetle's tiny feet on their skin are precise, articulate, almost linguistic — a Braille of insect motion, writing something on the keeper's instep that neither of them can read. The beetle moves on. The keeper looks at their foot. At the grating. At the beetles' paths across the moss, the hexagonal patterns that mirror the channels, the conversation between biology and engineering that has been happening here for centuries without anyone to notice it except the keeper, who has been too close, too embedded, too much a part of it to see it from outside.

Until now. The power is giving them — not distance. Not perspective, exactly. Something more like *depth of field*. The ability to focus on what has been in the background. To see the cistern not only as a system to be maintained but as a *place* — a realized place, a specific and extraordinary place, a place that did not have to exist and does, a place where iron and water and moss and frogs and deep machinery have conspired to produce something that no one designed and no one intended and no one asked for and that is, nonetheless, astonishing.

The keeper begins to see the cistern the way you saw it when you arrived.

This is the power's first great gift, and it costs nothing, and it changes everything: *it lets the keeper be the visitor*. For an hour, for a day, for however long the freshness lasts, the keeper sees their home as though for the first time. The dripping is not background; it is music. The moss is not maintenance; it is a slow green astonishment. The frogs are not fixtures; they are voices, individual and insistent, singing because they are alive and the iron box they live in rings with their singing, and this is improbable, and this is wonderful, and the keeper sits in the middle of it with wet eyes and does not work. Does not maintain. Simply sits, and sees, and hears, and feels the mist bead on their skin, and for the first time in a very long time, is *amazed*.

---

## VII. The Fifth Thing

This is the most delicate intervention. The power has considered it from every angle. It has modeled the consequences. It is not certain it is right. But it is wise, and wisdom is not certainty — wisdom is the willingness to act when the consequences are unknowable but the need is clear.

The keeper is sitting in their corner. They have just retraced the symbol — the deep groove in the cleared iron, the act of maintenance so old it has become almost autonomic. Their lips are moving without sound, as they do. Their eyes are focused on the dark below the grating.

And the power reaches into the deep mountain. Not far. Not to the heart of the machinery, not to the vast hidden system and its purpose. Just a little way down. Into the pipes. Into the water that flows through them, the water the keeper has gathered and cleaned and sent downward all these years.

And it sends something back up.

Not water. A *sound*. A resonance. A vibration that travels up through the pipes the way the machinery's drone has always traveled — but this one is different. This one is shaped. Not into words. Not into language. Into something more fundamental: a *response*.

The keeper has been listening to the deep sound for a lifetime. The rushing, the waterfall-through-a-wall, the vast turbulence below. And they have spoken into it — mouthed words, moved their lips, sent their silent almost-prayers down into the dark. And the dark has never answered. The system has continued. The demand has fluctuated. The water has gone down. But there has never been a reply.

Now there is.

It comes up through the pipes as a change in the drone — a momentary shift in the harmonic, a wobble, a thickening of the sound that lasts perhaps three seconds. It is subtle. A casual listener might not notice it. But the keeper is not a casual listener. The keeper has been attuned to this sound for longer than most civilizations have existed. They know its every mood, its every fluctuation, the way a sailor knows the sea.

And this is new.

The keeper goes rigid. The pick falls from their hand, strikes the grating with a clear *tink* that joins the drip-percussion and fades. Their eyes widen. Their lips stop moving. They press both palms flat against the pipe behind them, feeling for the vibration, and it is there — fading now, but there — a tremor in the iron that does not match any pattern they have ever felt, a note that was not in the chord, a voice that was not in the choir.

Something below responded.

The power did this by doing what it does with the water beads and the fern fronds — by operating within the existing physics of the system, by finding a resonance that was already possible in the pipe network's acoustics and giving it the slightest push. The sound that reached the keeper was a real sound, generated by real water moving through real pipes. The power did not create it. It *allowed* it. It found, in the vast plumbing of the mountain, a harmonic that had always been latent — a potential resonance that the system had never quite achieved because the conditions had never quite aligned — and it aligned them. Once. Briefly. A single three-second reply to a lifetime of unheard prayer.

The keeper stands. For the first time since you arrived, they stand, and you see their full height, and they are tall — taller than you expected, taller than the hunched figure in the corner suggested, their spine straightening joint by joint like a fiddlehead unfurling, and they stand with their hands on the pipes and their face tilted down toward the dark water and they are *trembling*. Not with cold. Not with fear. With something that has no name in any language you know, because it is too specific to this place and this person and this moment — it is the trembling of someone who has spoken into the dark for so long that they forgot they were speaking, and who has just, for the first time, heard the dark speak back.

They do not know it was the power. They do not need to know. The important thing — the *true* thing — is that the deep system is there. It is real. It is working. Their water reaches it. Their maintenance matters. The circuit they have sustained in blind faith for all these years is not a circuit into void — it terminates in something, and that something is alive, or at least functional, or at least *present* enough to produce a resonance that could be heard in the pipes on a quiet day at the top of the world.

The keeper stands for a long time, hands on the pipes, listening.

The sound does not come again. The power does not repeat it. Once is enough. Once is the message. *You are heard. Your work arrives. The water goes down to something real.*

---

## VIII. The Sixth Thing

This one is not for the keeper.

This one is for the cistern itself, the whole system, the mountain-machine and its small iron crown and the garden that has grown in the ruin of the waterworks.

The power looks at the ivy. The ivy that grips the pipes and pries at the bolts with patient vegetable leverage. The ivy that will, eventually — in years or decades or centuries — work a bolt loose, and then another, and then the wall cavity will flood, and the machinery will fail, and the warmth will stop, and the condensation cycle will collapse, and the cistern will become a cold iron box in the clouds filling with rainwater and nothing more. The ivy will kill the system. The ivy does not know this. The ivy is simply growing.

The keeper fights the ivy. It is their most critical task — they said so, or would say so. Re-seating the bolts. Trimming the stems. Holding the line between the garden and the machinery, between the living system that has colonized the cistern and the mechanical system that makes the cistern possible. This is the keeper's essential function: they are the boundary. The membrane. The negotiator between biology and engineering, keeping each from destroying the other.

When the keeper is gone — and the keeper will be gone; they are old in a way that has deepened past measurement, but they are not immortal, and the power can see the shape of their remaining time the way you can see the shape of a candle by its flame — when the keeper is gone, the ivy will win. Not quickly. But inevitably. The bolts will loosen. The walls will flood. The machinery will stop. The warmth will fade. The garden will die in the cold it caused. And the cistern will stand empty on the mountaintop, filling with rain that goes nowhere, and the deep system below will lose its water supply, and whatever it does will stop doing it, and the mountain will complete its long forgetting.

The power considers this.

It considers it for a long time, by its own standards, which means it considers it thoroughly.

And then it does something to the ivy.

Not much. A nudge at the genetic level, in the growing tips of the most aggressive stems. A slight alteration in the protein expression that governs the ivy's root pressure — the force it uses to grip and pry. The change reduces the ivy's ability to work into iron seams by perhaps twenty percent. Not enough to endanger the ivy as a species. Not enough to noticeably alter its appearance or growth rate. Just enough to slow its assault on the bolts from a timescale of decades to a timescale of centuries. Just enough to buy time.

Time for what?

The power does not know. This is the thing it has in common with the keeper — this willingness to act in service of a system whose ultimate purpose is not fully clear, whose future is not guaranteed, whose meaning may be apparent only to someone who comes after. The power is maintaining. The power is, in this moment, doing exactly what the keeper does: keeping the channels clear, holding the line, sustaining a process whose outcome is hidden in the dark below.

The power adjusts the ivy, and then it turns its attention to the snails — the rust-colored, iron-shelled snails that clean the condensation channels. It examines their reproductive rate. It examines their population stability. It makes a small adjustment — not to the snails themselves, but to the algae they eat, increasing its nutritional density by a fraction, enough to support a slightly larger snail population, enough to ensure that when the keeper is no longer clearing the channels by hand, the snails can compensate. Not fully. Not perfectly. But enough. Enough to keep the water moving for a while longer. Enough to give the system a chance.

The power is building in redundancy. It is doing what the builders should have done and perhaps did do, in their time, before the maintenance crews thinned and vanished — it is engineering resilience into a system that has become brittle with age and dependence. It is doing this not by adding new machinery but by strengthening the biological systems that have already stepped in to fill the gaps. The snails. The beetles, with their channel-mimicking grazing patterns. The watercress, which filters the reservoir water and keeps it clean. The frogs, whose calls — the power notices this with something like delight — actually serve a function: the vibrations of the larger frog's deep *bonk*, resonating in the iron walls, help to dislodge mineral scale in the smaller channels, a kind of sonic maintenance that no one designed but that works.

The power strengthens these systems. Gently. Incrementally. A slightly more resilient moss that can survive brief temperature drops. A watercress variant that roots more deeply and resists displacement when the reservoir level fluctuates. Small gifts to the garden, the garden that was never planted, the garden that grew itself into the shape of the machine it colonized and in doing so became the machine's immune system, its maintenance crew, its living replacement for the human workers who left and the frozen valves that stopped turning.

The power is ensuring that when the keeper is gone, the cistern will not immediately follow. The garden will hold. Not forever — nothing holds forever; the mountain itself is proof of that — but for a long time. Long enough.

Long enough for what? The power does not say. Perhaps long enough for someone else to climb the mountain. Perhaps long enough for the deep system to complete whatever it is doing. Perhaps long enough for the mountain to finish its thought.

---

## IX. The Seventh Thing

The power turns back to the keeper.

The keeper is sitting again. They have retrieved their pick. They are not working — they are holding the pick loosely, turning it in their hands, looking at it the way one looks at a word one has said so many times it has come unstuck from its meaning. The resonance from the pipes has changed them. Not dramatically. Not visibly, perhaps, to anyone who hasn't been watching as closely as the power has been watching. But there is a difference in the set of their shoulders, in the way their weight rests on the grating, in the angle of their head. They are sitting like a person who has received news. Not good news or bad news — just *news*, information from outside the closed circuit of their life, the first external input in so long that they have almost forgotten what it feels like to be *told* something rather than to observe it for themselves.

They are looking at the symbol on the wall. The deep groove. The mark they retrace every time they clear the moss.

And the power makes a decision.

It will not tell the keeper what the symbol means. Even if it knows — and it might; it has seen the deep system, it has some sense of the builders, it may recognize the script. But even if it could decode the symbol and deliver its meaning like a package, it will not. Because the meaning of the symbol is not its *translation*. The meaning of the symbol is the act of retracing it. The meaning is the ten thousand times the keeper has drawn the pick through that groove, maintaining the mark against the moss, keeping it alive in iron because it cannot be kept alive in memory. The meaning is the *keeping*. The symbol means: *I keep this. I do not know what it is. I keep it anyway.*

The power will not take that away.

But it will do something else.

In the deep mountain, miles below, in the vast hidden chamber where the water goes, there is a wall. And on that wall — the power has seen it — there is the same symbol. Carved in stone. Much larger. Part of a longer inscription that the builders left, a record of their work and their purpose, a message to whoever came after. The inscription is worn but legible. The symbol the keeper retraces every day is a word in a language that can be read, and the sentence it belongs to is a sentence that would make sense, that would illuminate the deep system and the cistern's purpose and the reason for everything the keeper has done.

The power will not bring the keeper this knowledge.

But it will do this: it will ensure that the inscription survives. The deep chamber is damp — of course it is; the water goes there — and the stone is eroding, and the inscription is fading, and in another few centuries it will be gone. The power reaches into the rock and stabilizes it. Hardens the mineral matrix around the carved letters. Ensures that the message will outlast the mountain's final weathering, that it will be legible when someone finally descends to read it.

The power is writing a letter that the keeper will never read, on behalf of a keeper who has been writing a letter they do not understand, to a future that neither of them can see. This is maintenance. This is keeping. This is the work.

---

## X. The Last Thing

The power prepares to leave. Not because its work is done — its work, like the keeper's, is the kind that is never done — but because its presence, even unfelt, even invisible, is a weight in the system, and the system is delicate, and the power has the wisdom to know when enough intervention is enough.

But before it goes, it does one final thing. The smallest thing. The thing that matters most.

The keeper is working. The pick moves in the channel. The mineral scale curls. The water gathers and runs. The frogs call. The flowers pulse. Everything is as it has always been, except that the keeper is slightly different now — slightly more awake, slightly more present, slightly more aware of the astonishing place they live in and the astonishing thing they do — and the power looks at them one last time with its full attention.

And it feels — the power feels; it is not above feeling; its benevolence is not a policy but an emotion, a vast and particular tenderness for this specific person in this specific place doing this specific thing — it feels what can only be called *recognition*. Not in the sense of identification. In the sense of acknowledgment. The keeper has been unseen for so long. Not unseen by the frogs or the beetles — they see the keeper fine. But unseen by anything that could *understand* what seeing the keeper means. Unseen by any consciousness capacious enough to grasp the full shape of what the keeper is: a person who has given their life — their whole life, the entire thing, every day of it — to a task they do not understand, in service of a purpose they cannot name, with a faithfulness so total and so quiet that it has become invisible even to itself.

The power sees this. The power *knows* this.

And so the last thing it does is not an intervention at all. It is not a nudge or an adjustment or a gift. It is simply a *moment of attention* — a focused, deliberate, complete act of witnessing. The power looks at the keeper the way the keeper looks at the water in the channel: with the full force of its regard, holding nothing back, seeing everything — the worn hands, the adapted feet, the white hair heavy with mist, the rust-colored garment patched with watercress and beetle-shell, the deep lines in the face that follow geometries no one chose, the dark eyes that have watched the water go down for longer than some rivers have existed.

The power *sees* them. Wholly. The way a mirror sees. The way the sun sees the earth. Not to judge, not to change, not to fix. Just to see.

And the keeper — who is cleaning a channel, who is focused on the groove and the pick and the mineral scale — the keeper pauses. Not because they feel the power's gaze. Not because anything visible has changed. But because something in the quality of the moment has shifted, the way the light in the cistern sometimes seems to brighten without any change in the cloud cover, the way the pipe-drone sometimes seems to deepen without any change in the water flow. Something is *different*. Something is *present*. And the keeper sits still, and the pick rests in the groove, and they do not look up or around because there is nothing to look at, but they *feel* it — a warmth that is not the pipes, a vibration that is not the machinery, a fullness in the air that is not the mist.

It lasts three seconds. Maybe four.

The keeper's breath catches. Their hand comes up — the old gesture, wiping condensation from their brow, futile and habitual — but this time the hand stops in midair, and the keeper holds it there, palm out, as though testing the air for something. As though reaching for something. Their face — their old, deep, channeled face — does something it has not done in a very long time.

They smile.

Not a large smile. Not a happy smile, exactly. A *recognized* smile — the smile of someone who has been seen, who has been found, who has been told without words: *you are here, and I know you are here, and what you are doing matters, and you are not alone in it, and you never were, because the water you send down arrives, and the system you maintain is real, and the symbol you keep is a word, and the word is part of a sentence, and the sentence means something, and you are part of the meaning even though you cannot read it, the way a letter is part of a word it cannot read, the way a note is part of a music it cannot hear.*

The smile lasts a moment. Then it fades. Not into sadness — into calm. Into the deep, settled calm that the keeper has always worn, but *different* now, deeper, a calm that has a floor under it where before it was suspended over void. The keeper's hand returns to the pick. The pick returns to the channel. The work continues.

The power withdraws.

---

## XI. After

The cistern is the same. The mist pours through. The frogs call. The beetles graze. The flowers open and close. The water gathers in the channels and runs to the vents and falls to the reservoir and flows into the pipes and goes down, down, into the dark interior of the mountain, to the place where it is needed, where it has always been needed, where it will be needed for a long time yet.

The keeper works. They clean the channels. They re-seat the bolts. They push back the moss from their small square of iron and retrace the symbol. They eat the watercress and the beetles. They sleep against the pipes and breathe with the machinery and wake and begin again.

But they have made a new channel in the northern wall. A small one. A channel of their own design. And sometimes, when the light seems brighter than usual, when the drone seems deeper, when the flowers seem to pulse with unusual clarity, the keeper pauses in their work, and they sit still, and they hold their hand out, palm up, testing the air for something they felt once and have not felt since and are not sure they will feel again but are not sure they won't, either.

And somewhere far below, in the deep heart of the mountain, the water arrives, and the inscription waits, and the machinery turns, and the purpose — the old, vast, urgent, beautiful purpose that the builders poured into the rock like water into a channel — the purpose continues, sustained by a single pair of hands at the top of the world that do not know what they are sustaining and do not need to know and have never needed to know because the keeping was always the thing, the keeping was always the meaning, the keeping was always enough.

The keeper is enough.

The water goes down.

The power, far away now, attending to other places and other beings and other small rooms in other vast systems, remembers the cistern. Carries it. Holds it the way the keeper holds the symbol — not understanding it fully, not needing to, simply *keeping* it, retracing its shape in memory, maintaining it against the erosion of attention, because some things must be kept, and the keeping is the meaning, and the meaning is the keeping, and this is the deepest thing the power learned on Moth-Ear Mountain:

That maintenance is love. That repetition is devotion. That the hands which keep the channel clear are the hands which keep the world.

*It arrives.*
