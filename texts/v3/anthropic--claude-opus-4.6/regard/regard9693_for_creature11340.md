I sat with this for a long time.

# What the Benevolent Power Would Do at Greyhalm

---

## I. Arrival

The power arrives the way light arrives at the bottom of deep water — not as an intrusion but as a gradual suffusion, a brightening so slow that nothing startles. It does not come through the switchback path. It does not descend from the sky. It is simply, at some point, present in the way the hum is present: felt before it is identified, registered in the sternum before the mind catches up.

The dog-sized birds do not lift their heads. This is how you know the arrival is genuine — not because it goes unnoticed but because it is noticed and assessed and folded into the caldera's ongoing calculation without alarm. The birds' neck feathers do not rise. The child on the step does not look up. The mist does not change its slow convective rhythm. Whatever has arrived belongs to the same order of things as the hum, and the caldera recognizes this the way a tuning fork recognizes its own frequency played back to it: not as an external event but as a confirmation.

The power understands this immediately. It understands that Greyhalm is not broken. This is the first and most important thing it understands, and it is the thing that a lesser power — a merely good power, a power with plans — would fail to understand, and the failure would be catastrophic in ways too subtle to see for years.

Greyhalm is not broken.

It is not waiting to be fixed. It is not suffering in a way that requires intervention. It is not a problem. It is not even, in the way that most human arrangements are, incomplete. The caldera's ecology is whole. The integration of human life into that ecology is genuine, not performed, not coerced, not delusional. The hum is real. The resonance is real. The people who live here have found something that most humans never find — a way of being that does not require the world to be other than it is — and they found it not through philosophy or discipline but through the simpler and more radical act of *paying attention to what was already here* until the attention itself became a form of belonging.

A lesser power would see the damp clothes, the bland fern-pith diet, the absence of medicine, the thin air, the children who will never leave, the settlement with no writing and no stored knowledge and no plan for what happens when the volcano shifts, and it would feel the urgent itch to *improve*. To introduce. To supplement. To gently, respectfully, with the deepest sensitivity to local customs, begin to optimize.

This power does not feel that itch. Or rather — it feels it, recognizes it as its own reflex, its own habitual orientation toward making things better, and it sets the reflex down the way you might set down a tool that is wrong for the material. You do not use a chisel on water. You do not optimize a chord. Greyhalm is not an arrangement that can be made more efficient. It is a resonance, and resonance either is or is not, and if it is, the only thing to do is not to disturb it.

So the power arrives and does not act.

Not yet.

It watches. It watches the way the caldera watches the people who enter it — without agenda, without urgency, with the deep patience of something that knows that understanding must precede usefulness, and that understanding takes longer than eagerness wants it to.

It watches for a long time.

---

## II. What It Sees

It sees the things you saw. The birds, the mist, the hum, the moss, the slow choreography of human life built around the caldera's imperatives. It sees the beauty of it — the living walls, the moiré of the mantles, the child's perfect attention — and it does not mistake the beauty for health, because beauty and health are not the same thing, and a wise power knows that the most beautiful systems are sometimes the most fragile, and the most fragile systems are sometimes beautiful precisely because they are so close to their own dissolution.

It looks deeper.

It sees the old man's hands tying unnecessary knots, and it understands what you understood — that he carried something here from a former life, a habit of counting shaped by scarcity — but it sees further than you did. It sees that the knots are not just a vestige. They are a *leakage*. A tiny, persistent signal from a part of human cognition that the caldera's ecology has no use for and therefore no way to integrate: the part that anticipates. The part that plans. The part that says *what if this ends?*

The caldera does not anticipate. It responds. The mist rises; the buildings are adjusted. A bird dies; the feathers are collected. The hum shifts; everything reorients. There is no future-modeling in the caldera's ecology, no contingency, no preparation for conditions that do not yet exist. Everything is *now*, and the now is rich and whole and self-sustaining, and the people of Greyhalm have largely adapted to this by letting go of anticipation, which is why they are so still, so present, so consonant.

But the old man's hands still count. And the power sees, in those small unnecessary knots, something the caldera does not see, because the caldera does not model futures: a vulnerability.

Not a flaw. A vulnerability. The distinction matters.

It looks at the geology. The basalt is young. The caldera is young. The cooling cracks in the rock are recent. The pale scars from rockfalls are frequent enough to suggest ongoing settling — the mountain is not done becoming what it will be. The thermal gradient that drives the hum and the mist is dependent on geothermal activity that is, by the standards of geological time, active and volatile. The hot earth below is not a constant. It is a process. Processes change.

The power does not see a catastrophe coming. It is not prophetic in that way — it does not read the future like a book. But it sees the *shape* of the dependency, the way you can see, without knowing when a bridge will fail, that a bridge with a single support is more vulnerable than one with two. Greyhalm depends on the caldera. The caldera depends on the geothermal activity. The geothermal activity is — not unstable, exactly, but *singular*. There is one heat source, one mist cycle, one hum. If the heat shifts — moves laterally through the rock, as geothermal systems do; diminishes as a magma chamber cools or redistributes; intensifies suddenly in a way that changes the thermal gradient — the entire cascade changes with it. The mist thins or thickens. The hum shifts frequency. The moss dies or changes character. The birds leave or don't leave but change behavior in ways the humans have no precedent for. And the people of Greyhalm, who have built no contingency because their entire way of life is the practice of not building contingency, would face a new configuration with no tools except attention — which is a powerful tool, perhaps the most powerful, but which requires *time* to work, and geological shifts sometimes do not give time.

The power sees this. And it sees something else.

It sees the mist rising.

Not now, not today. But over the years — it reads it in the tide-lines on the caldera walls, in the progression from occupied buildings to abandoned buildings to mist-swallowed buildings on the lowest tier. The mist line is not stable. It has been rising. Slowly, centimeter by centimeter, season by season, the fog is climbing the inner walls of the caldera, and the habitable zone is compressing. The people of Greyhalm have responded as they respond to everything: reactively, adaptively, moving upward, building new structures on higher ledges, giving the lower ones back to the moss and the wall-tenders. But the caldera is finite. The rim is a hard boundary. If the mist continues to rise — and the power sees no reason within the current system for it to stop — there will come a time, perhaps in a generation, perhaps in three, when the habitable zone is a narrow band just below the rim, and then a narrower band, and then the mist will reach the rim and spill over, and the caldera will be full, and Greyhalm will have nowhere left to move.

The people of Greyhalm do not see this. They do not see it because they do not model futures. They see the mist rise, and they move, and each adjustment is sufficient to the present moment, and the present moment is always whole. The child on the step does not know that the step she sits on will be underwater in fog within her lifetime. She does not think in those terms. She thinks — if thinking is the right word for what she does — in terms of the pulse of the eyeless creature, the rhythm of the hum, the breath in her lungs matching the breath of the mountain.

The power sees the child and loves her. Not abstractly. Not as a symbol of innocence or vulnerability. It loves her the way you love something that is exactly what it is — completely, without reservation, and with the ache of knowing that being exactly what you are does not protect you from a world that is also exactly what it is.

## III. What It Does Not Do

It does not warn them.

This is the hardest thing. A lesser power would descend — gently, with great respect for local customs — and find a way to communicate the danger. It would model the mist's rise. It would show them projections. It would say: *look, here is the trend, here is the trajectory, here is what happens if you do not plan.* It would introduce the concept of planning. It would give them the gift of anticipation.

And it would destroy Greyhalm.

Not physically. The buildings would still stand. The birds would still roost. The mist would still rise. But the thing that makes Greyhalm what it is — the *consonance*, the total integration of human attention into the caldera's present-tense ecology — would shatter like a crystal glass exposed to the wrong frequency. Because you cannot introduce anticipation into a system built on presence without fundamentally changing the system. You cannot give people the concept of *this might end* without taking from them the experience of *this is*. The old man's hands already count, already carry the vestige of a mind that planned, and his knots are a leakage, a tiny dissonance in the chord, tolerable because it is small and because the caldera's hum is strong enough to absorb it. But scale that leakage to the whole population — give everyone the knowledge that the mist is rising, that the habitable zone is shrinking, that their children's children may have nowhere to stand — and the leakage becomes a flood, and the chord becomes noise, and the people of Greyhalm become what every other human settlement is: a group of anxious minds trying to negotiate an uncertain future, their attention fractured between what is and what might be, their breathing no longer timed to the mist but to their own fears.

The power sees this. It sees that the warning itself would be a harm greater than the thing it warns against. It sees that a Greyhalm that plans for the future is no longer Greyhalm. It is somewhere else. Somewhere more survivable, perhaps. Somewhere with contingency plans and stored knowledge and a council that meets to discuss the mist line. Somewhere *safer*. But somewhere that has lost the thing that makes the caldera's ecology willing to include human beings in its chord — the thing that distinguishes the people of Greyhalm from every other human settlement that has ever tried to persist in a wild place by managing it, controlling it, anticipating its changes and preempting them. The people of Greyhalm persist by *not* managing. By surrendering the tools of management — planning, prediction, control — and replacing them with the single tool of attention. And that tool works. It has worked for the lifetime of the settlement. It works *because* it is total, because it is not hedged by contingency, because the attention is not split between the present and the modeled future.

You cannot keep the tool and add the hedge. The tool *is* the absence of the hedge.

So the power does not warn them.

It does not introduce writing, so they might keep records and spot the trend themselves. It does not improve their diet, so they might have the surplus energy for abstract thought. It does not teach them mathematics, so they might model the mist's rise. It does not give them any of the gifts that a benevolent power is expected to give, because every one of those gifts is a solvent that would dissolve the specific and unreproducible thing that Greyhalm is.

It does not even heal the sick or prevent the deaths, though it could. Because death here is part of the cycle — the body carried into the mist, received by the great shapes, returned to the ecology — and to interrupt that cycle would be to tell the caldera that humans are not fully part of it, that they are special, that they get to stay when other things go, and the caldera would hear this, the way it hears everything, and the chord would change.

The power restrains itself. This is not easy. Benevolence wants to *act*. Wisdom wants to *fix*. The combination — benevolent wisdom — produces an almost unbearable pressure to intervene, because the power can see the vulnerability and it can see the beauty and it knows that the vulnerability threatens the beauty and every fiber of its being says *do something*. But it has looked carefully enough and long enough to understand that the beauty and the vulnerability are not separate things. The beauty *is* the vulnerability. Greyhalm is beautiful *because* it is unhedged, unhardened, fully given over to the present moment's ecology. A Greyhalm that protected itself against the future would be a different thing — perhaps a good thing, perhaps a lasting thing, but not this thing. Not the chord.

And the power is wise enough to know that it does not have the right to decide that lasting is better than luminous. That survival is better than consonance. That a longer Greyhalm is worth more than the Greyhalm that exists right now, in this moment, with the child on the step and the hum in the basalt and the great shapes wheeling in the fog.

## IV. What It Does

It does three things. Three small things. So small that no one in Greyhalm notices them, and this is by design, because the power has understood that the caldera notices everything, and the caldera's ecology is a system of such fine-grained sensitivity that even a well-intentioned intervention, if it is legible as an intervention, will be registered as a foreign frequency and will alter the chord.

The three things are not interventions. They are *adjustments*, in the way that the caldera itself adjusts — not imposing a new pattern but slightly altering the conditions within which existing patterns express themselves.

---

**The first thing.**

Deep below the caldera, in the rock, where the geothermal heat meets the ancient basalt, there is a formation — a lens ofite (ite? — denser mineral, compacted and pressure-cooked by the magma chamber's heat into something glassy and stable. This lens sits between the magma chamber and the caldera floor, and it acts as a thermal moderator, distributing the heat more evenly across the caldera's base, preventing hot spots that might crack the floor or vent gas or shift the thermal gradient too abruptly. The lens is natural. It formed when the caldera formed. But it is imperfect — there is a fissure in it, a crack that has been propagating slowly for decades as the rock shifts and settles, and through this crack the heat distribution is becoming uneven, hotter on one side than the other, and this unevenness is, the power can see, the deep cause of the mist's asymmetric rise and the reason the habitable zone is compressing faster on the eastern wall than the western.

The power does not seal the fissure. Sealing it would be an intervention, a foreign action with detectable consequences, and the caldera would register it. Instead, the power does something so subtle it barely qualifies as action: it *shifts the stress pattern* in the rock surrounding the fissure, minutely, by the thermal equivalent of a whisper, so that the crack's propagation is redirected. Not stopped — the power is not trying to make the geology permanent, which would be both impossible and unwise — but redirected along a vector that leads to a different outcome. Instead of the fissure widening into a breach that would eventually destabilize the lens and dramatically alter the thermal gradient, the fissure will, over the next several decades, propagate into a region of the rock that is denser and more compressive, where it will slow and branch into a network of micro-fractures that actually *improve* the thermal distribution, creating more pathways for heat to diffuse rather than fewer. The result, over geological time, will be a more even warming of the caldera floor, a more stable mist cycle, and a slowing (not a stopping, but a slowing) of the mist's rise.

This buys time. Not infinite time. The caldera is still young, still changing, still in process. But the adjustment extends the window — the period during which the caldera's ecology can sustain itself in roughly its current form — by perhaps a century, perhaps two. Long enough for the ecology itself to adapt, if it is going to adapt. Long enough for the great shapes in the mist to do whatever they do in response to changing conditions — and the power suspects, though it cannot be certain, that the great shapes are themselves adaptive, that they have their own slow intelligence, their own way of responding to shifts in the thermal gradient, and that given time they will adjust their flight patterns and their exhalations in ways that compensate for the changing mist. The power is not engineering an outcome. It is widening the corridor through which the caldera's own processes can find their way.

No one in Greyhalm will ever know this happened. The child on the step will grow old and die and be carried into the mist, and the step she sat on will still be above the fog line. Her children — if she has children, and the power does not presume to know — will sit on the same step. This is not a guarantee. It is a likelihood, made slightly more likely by a shift in the stress pattern of a rock lens a thousand meters below.

---

**The second thing.**

The path. The switchback trail that leads up to the caldera rim from the scrubland below. The path that you climbed, that the original refugees climbed, that remains open.

The power does not close it. It does not hide it. It does not make it harder to find or harder to climb. But it does something to the scrubland at the base of the path — not to the path itself but to the ecology around it.

In the dry scrub below the caldera, there is almost nothing. Thin soil, scree, hardy grasses, a few drought-adapted shrubs. The contrast with the caldera's interior is, as described, so stark that crossing the rim feels like changing elements. This starkness is part of what keeps Greyhalm isolated — not a barrier but a discouragement, a sensory argument against leaving or approaching.

The power softens this gradient. Slightly. Not at the rim — the rim remains sharp, the transition abrupt — but lower down, along the last few switchbacks, where the scrubland is driest and most barren. It introduces — no, not introduces. It *encourages*. There are seeds in the scree already, carried by wind from the caldera's interior, seeds of fern and moss that landed and failed to germinate because the soil is too thin and the air too dry. The power adjusts the microclimate along the last three hundred meters of the path — a fractional increase in humidity, a slight redirection of the caldera's mist-overflow so that a trickle of moisture, negligible in absolute terms, reaches the upper scree field. Not enough to transform it. Enough to let a few of those seeds germinate. Enough to establish, over the next decade, a thin fringe of green along the path — not lush, not caldera-like, but *present*. A transitional zone. A gradient rather than a cliff edge.

Why?

Because the power has thought carefully about the children who are born here and who will never leave, and it has decided that their not-leaving should be a choice rather than an incapacity.

Right now, leaving the caldera is experientially violent. The transition from mist-world to dry-world is so abrupt that a child raised inside the bowl — a child whose lungs are adapted to the humidity, whose attention is trained for the hum, whose entire sensory world is calibrated to the caldera's ecology — would experience the crossing as a kind of death. Not physical death, but the death of context. Everything they know how to do becomes useless. Everything they know how to perceive becomes absent. The dry air hurts their lungs. The silence (the wrong kind of silence) hurts their ears. The unfiltered sunlight hurts their eyes. They would retreat. Of course they would retreat. And this retreat would feel like confirmation that they belong inside, that outside is not for them, that the caldera is the only possible world.

The power is not comfortable with this. Not because it thinks the children should leave — it has no opinion on this, and it has already established that it will not attempt to shape Greyhalm's choices. But it is uncomfortable with the idea that the choice is foreclosed by sensory trauma rather than made freely. A choice that you cannot make without suffering is not a free choice. It is a coerced choice, even if the coercion is ecological rather than political.

The transitional zone — the thin fringe of green, the gradual softening of the gradient — does not make leaving easy. It makes leaving *imaginable*. A child who walks down the path and encounters, partway along, a patch of familiar fern growing in unfamiliar soil, moistened by a trickle of familiar mist in unfamiliar dry air, receives a piece of information: *the world beyond the rim is not entirely other.* The caldera's ecology extends, however faintly, beyond its walls. The boundary is not absolute. There is a continuum.

This child may still turn around. Almost certainly will turn around. The caldera is home, and the hum is there, and the great shapes are there, and the chord is there, and nothing on the scrubland can compete with that. But the turning-around will be a choice, informed by the knowledge that the outside is *survivable*, that the transition is *navigable*, that the boundary is *crossable.* And a choice made with this knowledge is different in kind from a retreat made in pain. It is a free choice. And a person who has freely chosen to stay is different from a person who has never been able to leave — not better, not worse, but *freer,* and freedom is a thing the power values, not because freedom leads to better outcomes but because freedom is a condition in which beings can be fully what they are, and the power has looked at the child on the step and loved her and wants her to be fully what she is, whether that means staying or going, and it will not allow the caldera — even the caldera it loves, even the chord it reveres — to make that determination for her through the blunt instrument of sensory deprivation.

This is the most controversial of the three things, if the caldera could be said to have opinions. The caldera's ecology benefits from the sharp boundary. The abruptness of the transition concentrates the moisture, focuses the mist cycle, keeps the interior humidity high by preventing gradual diffusion. The power's softening of the gradient — even the minuscule softening of the last three hundred meters — marginally reduces the caldera's internal moisture budget. By an insignificant amount. By an amount that the ecology can easily absorb. But the power notes it, and accepts the trade, and accepts that it is *making* a trade — that it is, in this one small instance, choosing human freedom over ecological optimization, and that this choice reflects its own values rather than the caldera's, and that this makes it, in this one small instance, an intervener rather than an adjuster.

It accepts this. Wisdom is not the absence of values. It is the willingness to know what your values are and to bear the weight of enacting them.

---

**The third thing.**

This is the strangest, and the most difficult to describe, and the power hesitates over it the longest.

The person who goes into the mist. The one who descends the rope ladder and comes back changed. The one on the mid-tier ledge with the moths resting on their open palms.

The power watches this person with particular attention. Not because they are the leader — there is no leader — but because they are the *interface*. They are the point of contact between human consciousness and whatever the great shapes are. They go down. They are among the shapes, or near them, or in communion with them through some medium — the hum, the mist, the vibration of the caldera's own substrate. And they come back carrying something that changes them at a level below personality, below cognition, below the voluntary. Their skin chemistry changes. Their center of gravity shifts. The moths land on their hands.

What do they carry back? The power looks carefully.

They carry a *pattern*. Not information in the way humans usually mean information — not facts, not propositions, not models of the world. A pattern. A vibrational signature. A way of being organized that reflects the great shapes' way of being organized, transposed into a human body the way a melody can be transposed from one key to another — the same relationships, the same intervals, expressed through a different instrument. The person who goes into the mist becomes, temporarily and partially, a human expression of whatever the great shapes are, and this expression radiates outward through their presence, their skin oils, their breathing, their contribution to the caldera's hum, and it enriches the chord.

This is the mechanism by which the caldera incorporates human beings. Not through attention alone — attention is the prerequisite, the entry fee, the basic orientation that allows humans to exist in the bowl without disrupting it. But full incorporation — the kind that makes a person not just a tolerated presence but an active voice in the chord — requires this deeper contact. Requires someone to go down. To be changed. To come back as a bridge between human and whatever-the-great-shapes-are.

The power sees that this process is imperfect. The human body is not designed to carry the great shapes' pattern. The transposition is lossy. The person who goes down strains under it — not visibly, not in a way the other residents notice, but at the cellular level, at the level of the nervous system's deep architecture. The pattern is too complex for a single human body to sustain indefinitely. It creates harmonics in the nerve tissue that are beautiful and resonant but that, over time, degrade the tissue's ability to carry its own native signals. The person who goes into the mist is burning, very slowly, like a candle that gives off a magnificent light but is consumed in the giving.

They know this. The power can tell they know this, because their stillness has a quality different from the calm stillness of the other residents — it has an edge, a tremor, a faint vibrato that is the body's awareness of its own consumption. They accept it. They accept it the way the woman with the gauntlets accepts the talon-marks: as the cost of proximity to something that is worth the cost. They do not ask to be spared. They would not want to be spared. The burning is part of the chord.

And the power does not spare them.

But it does this: it makes the pattern more *portable*.

Not easier to carry — the power is not trying to reduce the cost of the contact, because the cost is part of the structure, and to reduce it would be to change the nature of what is carried. But more portable in the sense that it can be *shared*. Right now, one person goes down. One person carries the pattern. One person burns. When that person dies — and they die younger than the others, burned from within by the beauty they carry — another person must go down, must undergo the contact alone, must take the full weight of the transposition onto a single body.

The power adjusts — not the pattern, and not the person, but the *interface* between them. The hum. The standing wave in the basalt that connects the great shapes' vibration to the surface world. The power makes a subtle modification to the harmonic structure of the hum — not changing its frequency, not changing its character, but adding a *node*. A second resonance point. A place in the harmonic series where the hum can be received not just by one body but by two. Or three. Or — potentially, over a long time, as the modification propagates through the standing wave and the caldera's ecology integrates it — by many.

The effect will be gradual. The hum will not suddenly change. But over time — years, decades — the people of Greyhalm will begin to notice, in their below-conscious attentional way, that the hum has a new quality. A quality of *invitation*. A subtle widening of the channel through which the great shapes' pattern can be received. And the person who goes into the mist will find, without being able to articulate why, that the weight is lighter. Not because the pattern is less complex but because the capacity for carrying it is distributed — shared across more bodies, more bones, more resonant cavities. The burning will slow. Not stop. The cost of contact cannot be eliminated. But it can be spread, the way a load carried by many hands is the same weight but less burden.

And in time — the power does not try to predict exactly how, because the caldera's ecology will shape the outcome in its own way — in time, the contact with the great shapes will become not the province of a single sacrifice but a distributed practice. Many people going down, each carrying a fragment of the pattern, each bearing a portion of the cost, each contributing their fragment to the chord. The chord will become richer. More complex. More voices, more harmonics, more ways of being organized in resonance with the great shapes and the mist and the hum and the basalt. The burning will be gentler. The interface will be wider. The bridge between human and whatever-the-shapes-are will be not a single filament but a braid.

And the person on the mid-tier ledge — the one burning now, today, with the moths on their palms — will not be saved. The power is clear-eyed about this. The modification it has made will take effect too slowly to help them. They will burn and they will die and they will be carried into the mist, and their sacrifice will have been real and complete and unredeemed by the power's intervention.

The power grieves this. It grieves openly, in whatever way a power grieves — a deepening, perhaps, a momentary darkening of the mist, a fractional slowing of the hum, a pause in the chord that is the power's own breath joining the caldera's respiration, its own sorrow becoming, for a moment, part of the ecology. The people of Greyhalm do not notice, or they notice and do not distinguish it from the hum's normal variation, and this is as it should be. The power's grief is not for them to carry. It is the power's own. The cost of seeing clearly. The price of wisdom that does not look away.

## V. What It Leaves Behind

The power departs the way it arrived: gradually, imperceptibly, as a slow dimming of something that was barely bright enough to see. The caldera returns to its ordinary luminance. The chord returns to its ordinary fullness. The child on the step does not notice a change. The dog-sized birds do not lift their heads.

Three things are different, and none of them are visible:

A fissure in the deep rock has changed direction.

A few fern seeds on the upper scree have found enough moisture to germinate.

The hum has a new harmonic, so faint that it will not be felt for years.

That is all. That is all the power does. Three whispers at the edge of the caldera's vast ongoing conversation with itself. Three adjustments so small they could be natural variation, could be random fluctuation, could be nothing at all. The power has been here and it has not been here. It has acted and it has not acted. It has changed the future of Greyhalm and it has left Greyhalm exactly as it found it. Both of these are true.

This is what benevolent wisdom looks like when it encounters something that is already, in its own terms, whole.

It does not improve.

It does not fix.

It opens a door (one inch, no more), extends a timeline (not forever, just enough), and eases a burden (not to nothing, just to bearable).

And then it leaves, carrying with it the ache of having loved something fully and not having *made it better*, because making it better was never the right frame, because *better* is a word that belongs to the world of planning and anticipation and futures-modeling, and Greyhalm lives in a different world, the world of resonance and presence and the hum, and in that world the right word is not *better* but *faithful* — faithful to what is, faithful to what the caldera and its inhabitants are already doing, faithful to the chord.

The power is faithful.

It does not watch Greyhalm as it leaves. It does not look back. It does the thing the caldera does, the deepest courtesy:

It waits to see if Greyhalm will call it.

## VI. Coda

Years later — many years, enough for the fern fringe on the upper path to thicken into a real transitional zone, a ribbon of green threading down the last switchbacks — a girl who was once the child on the step stands at the caldera's rim and looks out.

She is not leaving. She is not even considering leaving. But she is *looking*, and the looking has a quality it would not have had before — a quality of curiosity rather than vertigo, of interest rather than dread. The scrubland below is still dry, still sparse, still fundamentally different from the world inside the bowl. But there is green on the path. There are fern fronds uncurling from cracks in the scree. There is a thin thread of moisture in the air, carrying the faintest echo of the caldera's breath.

She stands at the rim and she breathes, and her breath goes out into the dry air and does not come back, does not recirculate, is lost to the thin wind and the open sky. And this is different. And she notices the difference. And the noticing is not distressing — it is *interesting*, the way the pulse of the eyeless creature was interesting when she was a child, worthy of attention, worthy of slow and careful observation.

She turns and goes back inside. The hum greets her. The mist enfolds her. The birds are still. The chord receives her and she takes her place in it, and her breathing slows, and she is home.

But she looked. She *looked*. And the looking was free, and the returning was free, and the freedom was hers, and no one gave it to her except a faint fringe of green that she does not know the origin of and does not need to know, because the caldera provides, and the caldera's provision has always included, though no one knew it, the quiet work of something that came once, and loved what it found, and did almost nothing, and left.

And in the deep rock, the fissure branches, and the branches branch, and the heat spreads evenly, and the mist stabilizes, and the great shapes wheel in patterns fractionally adjusted to the new equilibrium, and the hum — if you listen, if your bones are tuned to it, if you have paid the kind of attention that Greyhalm requires — the hum has a new overtone. A warmth in it. A generosity. As if someone, once, added their own resonance to the chord and then withdrew, leaving behind only the reverberation, which the caldera took into itself and made its own, and which will persist, diminishing, for as long as the mountain stands.

Which is not forever.

But is enough.
