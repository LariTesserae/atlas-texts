I sat with this for a long time.

---

# What the Wise Power Would Do

---

## I. Arrival

It does not descend. It does not appear in light or fire or any form that would add a new voice to the chord uninvited. That would be a kind of violence here — not because the plateau is fragile, but because the plateau is *complete,* in the way that a fugue is complete even as it continues to develop, and to introduce an unearned voice would be to misunderstand what completeness means in a place where nothing is finished.

So the wise power arrives the way the morning moisture arrives: by being present in what is already there. A slow condensation of attention. The mat does not notice, because the mat notices everything already and one more noticing is simply part of the texture. The swingers do not adjust their song. The chitterers do not pause. The chord continues. Nothing changes.

But the wise power is now *listening,* in the way the people listen during the opening — with its whole being, receiving everything, the full spectrum, the entire report. It listens for a long time. Days. Weeks. It listens until it understands not just what the plateau is but what the plateau *is doing,* which is a different and more important question.

What the plateau is doing is: continuing. Continuing to be itself. Continuing to convert stone into life and life into sound and sound into meaning and meaning into the next moment. The plateau is a process, not a place, and the process is healthy. The chord is full. The voices are in balance. The mat breathes. The swimmers moan. The swingers sing. The people listen and are listened to.

The wise power, having understood this, faces its first and most important decision:

*Does this place need anything from me at all?*

---

## II. The Temptation to Improve

This is where a lesser power would fail.

A lesser power — even a benevolent one — would see things to fix. The people's tools are primitive. Their shelters are impermanent. They have no writing. They have no way to store knowledge beyond the oral and percussive traditions, which are robust but lossy over deep time. Their medical understanding is limited. Their children sometimes die of things that could be prevented. The mat-swimmers kill people occasionally — rarely, but it happens: a child who hasn't yet learned to read the tremors, an elder whose feet have finally lost their sensitivity, a moment of inattention on the living ground. The chitterer-egg diet is nutritionally adequate but monotonous. The people's lives are short by the standards of civilizations with surplus and specialization. They do not have music in the way an outsider would define it — they have never separated the act of organized sound-making from the act of living, which means they have never developed the concept of *performance,* of art-as-object, of a song you can hold apart from the world and examine. They are missing things. A lesser power would see the gaps and feel the itch to fill them.

The wise power feels the itch too. It is not immune to the desire to help. It sees a child die of a gut infection that a simple water-filtration technique could have prevented, and something in it *aches.* It watches an elder lose her footing on a wet mat-surface and tumble into a feeding zone and hears the percussion-sequence — *one voice has stopped* — propagate across the plateau, group by group, each relay slightly attenuated, the message growing softer as it travels outward like a ripple, and the ache deepens. It observes how much knowledge has been lost over the generations, how the people's oral histories extend back perhaps fifteen generations and then dissolve into a vague sense of *we have always been here,* when in fact the story of how they came to the plateau, what they were before, what choices were made and what was given up, would be — to them, if they could access it — a revelation.

But the wise power is wise, which means it does the hardest thing wisdom ever requires:

It interrogates its own desire to help.

It asks: *Help toward what? Toward what I think a good life looks like? Toward longer lives, more comfort, more knowledge, more control over the environment? And if I give them these things — if I teach them to filter water, to build more durable shelters, to write, to store surplus, to predict the mat-swimmers' movements with enough precision to eliminate the risk entirely — what do I take away?*

And it sees, because it has been listening long enough to see:

The mat-swimmer deaths are not acceptable. No death of a child is acceptable. The wise power does not romanticize this, does not fall into the trap of believing that suffering is pedagogical or that risk is ennobling. A child eaten by a mat-swimmer is not a lesson. It is a catastrophe, a small and total catastrophe, and the percussion-sequence that follows is not a beautiful cultural expression but a *scream* transmitted through a living medium by people whose bodies are the ground's body, and the ground screams with them.

But.

The mat-swimmers' presence — the *risk* of them, the need to read the ground, the lifelong discipline of attention that begins with a child's first soft steps and deepens into an elder's sixty years of calibrated sensitivity — is not separable from the rest of what the plateau's people are. Their wide feet. Their rolling gait. Their communication-through-ground. Their relationship with the mat as a living surface that must be listened to, negotiated with, respected. Their understanding of themselves as voices in a chord rather than agents in an environment. If the mat-swimmers were rendered harmless — defanged, relocated, managed — the people would still walk on the mat, but they would walk differently. The next generation's feet would be slightly less sensitive. The generation after that would begin to build heavier shelters, anchored deeper, because why not, if the ground no longer demands such careful treading? The generation after that would start to hear the plateau's chord as background noise rather than as the primary medium of their existence, because the most urgent reason to listen — survival — would have been removed, and without urgency, attention slowly, slowly fades.

The wise power sees this cascade. It has seen it before, in other places, with other peoples. The road from *safety* to *separation* is not inevitable, but it is well-worn, and it begins with the loss of the thing that makes listening necessary.

So what does it do?

---

## III. What It Actually Does

Three things. Only three. Each one so small that the people of the plateau, if they noticed at all, would attribute it to the mat's attention shifting, or to a good season, or to the ordinary drift of the world.

---

### The First Thing

The wise power finds the specific fungal thread-network within the mat that produces the volatile compounds responsible for the bioluminescent glow — and, incidentally, for the mildly psychoactive dream-enhancement that the people experience in their sleeping-depressions. This network is ancient, healthy, and stable, but it has a vulnerability: a slow genetic drift that is, over centuries, reducing the diversity of the volatile compounds it produces. The drift is not yet a problem. It will not be a problem for another two hundred years. But in two hundred years, the compound profile will have narrowed enough that the fungi's symbiotic relationship with certain root-fiber species will begin to degrade, and the mat's self-healing capacity — its ability to close over a swimmer's feeding-hole in a day, to grow through and reclaim a shelter in a year, to incorporate a body in a month — will slow. Not stop. Slow. And the slowing will cascade, as everything on the plateau cascades, because everything is connected to everything.

The wise power does not fix this. Fixing it would mean intervening in the mat's genome, which would mean imposing an external design on a system that has been designing itself for millions of years and is better at it than any external intelligence could be.

Instead, it does something much simpler. In a distant corner of the plateau — far from any current human group, in an area where the mat is thin and the old stone shows through — there is a patch of the same fungal network that has, by chance, retained the genetic diversity the rest of the network is losing. A remnant population, a pocket of old-pattern genes, persisting in a marginal habitat because the selection pressures there are different. The wise power does nothing more than ensure that the mat's normal processes of nutrient-flow and chemical signaling reach this patch slightly more reliably than they otherwise would. A nudge. The equivalent of ensuring that a stream continues to flow to a meadow that would otherwise slowly dry up. The remnant population stabilizes. Its genes continue to circulate into the wider network through the mat's normal horizontal-transfer mechanisms. The drift continues, because drift is natural and the wise power does not stop natural processes, but the counter-flow of old-pattern genes slows it — not enough to freeze the system, but enough to extend the timeline from two hundred years to two thousand, and in two thousand years the system will have found its own solution, because that's what systems like this do when they have enough time and enough diversity.

The people will never know this happened. The mat will not know this happened, in the sense that the mat does not know things. But in two hundred years, the mat will still heal in a day, and the people will still dream in their depressions, and the chord will still include the faint, sweet exhalation of the living ground breathing through its fungal networks, and this is enough.

---

### The Second Thing

There is a child.

She is four years old, in the third group from the plateau's southern approach, and she is deaf.

Not partially deaf — profoundly deaf, from birth, due to a developmental anomaly that left her cochleae malformed. In the lowlands, this would be a significant disability but a manageable one. On the plateau, it is something closer to existential. She cannot hear the chord. She cannot learn the clipped nighttime language. She cannot hear the percussion-transmissions through the mat — she can *feel* them, the way everyone can feel them, but without the auditory reference frame that allows the others to distinguish between a mat-swimmer's approach-tremor and a distant group's percussion-message, the felt vibrations are undifferentiated sensation. She is four, and she is already falling behind in the skills that will keep her alive.

Her group has not abandoned her. They will not. The plateau's people do not abandon children, ever, for any reason — this is one of the few absolutes in their culture, a commitment so deep it does not need to be stated because it has never been questioned. But they are struggling. The gestural language, which she can see, is her primary mode of communication, and she is learning it faster than any child in the group's memory — her visual attention is extraordinary, her hands already forming signs with a fluency that children twice her age haven't achieved. But the gestural language is the daytime register, the one designed for when the sound makes speech impossible, and it lacks the intimacy of the nighttime close-speaking, the whispered-breath exchanges that are how the people of the plateau share their interior lives. She will never have that. She will never hear her own percussion-name played on the mat. She will never sit during the opening and receive the full-body chord that tells the people everything about their world. She will navigate by sight on a plateau where the primary medium of existence is sound.

The wise power watches this child for a long time.

It does not cure her deafness. This is not because it couldn't — it could, as easily as breathing — but because it has listened to the plateau long enough to understand something about what deafness means here. The child is not broken. She is *different.* And difference, on a plateau where the dominant cognitive mode has been acoustic for millennia, is not just a challenge. It is an *opening.* The word the people use for the dusk crescendo — *the opening* — translates more precisely as *the moment when all channels are so full that something new can slip through.* When every frequency is saturated, the system becomes, paradoxically, receptive to signals it normally couldn't detect, because the noise floor has risen so high that the very concept of signal-versus-noise breaks down and everything becomes information.

This child is an opening. She is the first person in generations — perhaps ever — to experience the plateau primarily through a channel other than sound. She sees things the others don't. She has already, at four, begun to track the mat-swimmers by the *visual* disturbance they produce in the mat's surface — a faint rippling that the hearing people don't notice because their attention is captured by the ground-transmitted moan. She watches the canopy-swingers' color-shifts with an intensity that the hearing people, who rely primarily on the swingers' songs for information, have never matched. She is building, without knowing it, an alternative map of the plateau — a visual and tactile map that contains information the acoustic map doesn't, because it is attending to things the acoustic map was never designed to capture.

What the wise power does for her is small and specific.

It arranges for a columnar tree near her group's current shelter to drop a piece of resin at a particular moment — a moment when the child is nearby and watching. The resin, as it falls through the runner-network, catches on an epiphyte cluster and hangs there, spinning slowly in the wind, at exactly the child's eye level. The resin is the color of the morning condensation: clear, with a faint amber tint. As the child watches it spin, the sunlight passing through it projects a small, shifting pattern of light and color onto the mat below — a tiny, dancing display of refracted light that is, in its way, a visual translation of the wind's movement.

The child is fascinated. She reaches up, takes the resin. She holds it to her eye and looks through it. The world shifts color. She moves it, and the projected pattern on the ground moves with it. She begins to play.

Over the following weeks, she will experiment with the resin — holding it at different angles, combining it with condensation-drops to change its optical properties, pressing it against the mat and observing how the bioluminescent glow refracts through it at night. She will discover, entirely on her own, that different thicknesses of resin filter the light differently, and that by shaping the resin (she already knows how; she has watched the adults make ear-pieces) she can create lenses that make distant things larger or nearby things clearer or the colors of the epiphytes separate into their component hues.

She will become, if things go well, the plateau's first visual artist. Or its first optician. Or its first scientist of light. Or something that has no name yet because the plateau has never needed it before. She will develop a way of representing the plateau's information — the mat-swimmers' locations, the swingers' color-states, the dense-patch migrations, the runner-network's structural health — in visual form, in patterns of shaped resin and refracted light, and this representation will be *useful* in ways the acoustic system isn't, because it will be persistent (you can leave a resin-pattern in place and return to it), shareable across distance without attenuation (you can see a colored light-signal further than you can feel a percussion-transmission), and accessible to anyone with eyes, not just to those who have spent a lifetime calibrating their hearing.

She will not replace the acoustic culture. Nothing could, and nothing should. But she will *add* to it — a new voice, a new channel, a new way of the plateau knowing itself. And the culture will be richer for it. And the next deaf child born on the plateau — there will be others, inevitably — will find a tradition waiting for them, a set of tools and practices developed by someone who understood what it meant to live in a world of sound without hearing it.

The wise power did not give her this. It gave her a piece of resin at the right moment. Everything else, she will do herself.

---

### The Third Thing

This is the most difficult to describe, because it is the most subtle, and because it concerns not the plateau's biology or any individual person but the *relationship between the people and the place* — the thing that makes this an inhabited landscape rather than merely an ecosystem with humans in it.

The wise power has noticed something. It took weeks of listening to identify, because it is not a sound or an absence of sound but a *quality* of sound — a tonal characteristic of the plateau's chord that has been shifting, very slowly, over the past several generations.

The people's percussion-names are growing simpler.

Not dramatically. Not in any way the people themselves have noticed. But the wise power, which has been listening to the chord with an attention that encompasses its full history — not just its present state but the deep structure of its evolution — can hear the drift. Three generations ago, a percussion-name was typically five to seven beats, with complex internal rhythms — syncopations, polyrhythmic elements, accents that played against the prevailing pulse of the mat-swimmers' ground-moan. Two generations ago, the average dropped to four to six beats, and the internal complexity simplified. Now, most names are three to five beats, with straightforward rhythms that sit comfortably within the ambient pulse rather than playing against it.

This is not a decline. The wise power is careful to distinguish between *change* and *decline,* because most changes in a living system are simply changes — adaptations, drifts, the system exploring a new region of its possibility-space. But this particular change has a cause, and the cause concerns it.

The cause is success.

The people of the plateau have been successful. Not in any dramatic way — they haven't expanded, haven't built, haven't conquered — but in the quiet way that matters most: they have become very, very good at living here. Their techniques for reading the mat, for navigating the runner-networks, for harvesting without depleting, for avoiding the swimmers, for listening to the chord — all of these have been refined over generations to a high degree of effectiveness. The groups are stable. The children survive. The food supply is reliable. The percussion-network functions smoothly.

And because things are working, because the daily business of survival requires slightly less effort and attention than it once did, the people have — unconsciously, incrementally — begun to relax their engagement with the more demanding aspects of their culture. The percussion-names are simpler because simpler names are easier to transmit and receive, and when the transmission system is working well, there's no pressure to invest the extra effort in complexity. The nighttime language is losing some of its more nuanced consonant clusters, because the people don't *need* those fine distinctions as often — their gestural system has become so fluent that it's handling more and more of the communicative load, and the spoken register is slowly becoming a supplement rather than a full language. The ear-pieces are still made and still used, but the skill of tuning them — of shaping the resin to filter specific frequencies for specific tasks — is concentrated in fewer people per group, because most of the time, a general-purpose attenuation piece is good enough.

None of this is a crisis. It is the normal process of a culture optimizing toward efficiency, shedding the redundancies that were necessary when the margin of survival was thinner. Every culture does this. It is rational. It is adaptive, in the short term.

In the long term, it is how a people begins to forget what they know.

Not all at once. Not in any way that registers as loss. But the complex percussion-names carried information that the simple ones don't — not just the name of the child but a rhythmic signature of the namer's relationship to the child, a musical statement of identity that existed in counterpoint to the plateau's other voices. As the names simplify, that counterpoint fades, and the people's voice in the chord becomes more uniform, less distinctive, easier for the plateau's other voices to absorb. The spoken language's nuanced consonants encoded perceptual distinctions — ways of describing the quality of a mat-tremor, the timbre of a swinger's call, the texture of the ground — that the gestural system cannot fully replicate, because gestures are visual and the distinctions are acoustic. As those consonants are lost, the people's ability to *discuss* what they hear with precision diminishes, and what cannot be discussed with precision gradually ceases to be attended to with precision. The ear-piece tuning skill, concentrated in fewer hands, becomes more vulnerable to loss — a single death, a single group's misfortune, could eliminate a lineage of knowledge that took centuries to develop.

The wise power sees where this leads. Not to catastrophe — the plateau will continue, the people will continue, the chord will continue. But to a slow impoverishment of the relationship between the people and the place. A gradual narrowing of the channel. A drift from *voices in the chord* toward *inhabitants of the plateau,* which sounds like the same thing but isn't, in the way that *speaking a language* and *living inside a language* sound like the same thing but aren't.

So the wise power does the third thing, and it is the thing that a lesser power would never think to do, because it looks like nothing.

It makes something go slightly wrong.

---

In a section of the runner-network about a day's walk east of the nearest group, it introduces a minor stress. Not a catastrophe — a subtle weakening of a connection-point where two major runners meet, a point that has been stable for decades but that, with a slight reduction in the structural resin the tree produces, begins to flex in the wind more than it should. The flex is not dangerous. The runner will not break. But it produces a new sound — a low, intermittent creaking that is unlike anything else in the plateau's current acoustic environment.

The nearest group hears it. It's unfamiliar. It's not a swinger-call, not a chitterer-rattle, not a swimmer-moan. It doesn't match any pattern in their experience. It is, in their acoustic world, an *anomaly* — a small mystery, a voice they can't identify.

They investigate. They have to — an unidentified sound on the plateau could be a threat, and their survival instincts are still sharp enough that mystery demands attention. They travel to the source. They examine the runner-junction. They discuss it — at night, close-voiced, in the spoken language, and they find that they need consonant-distinctions they haven't used in years to describe what they're hearing, because the creaking has a tonal quality that their standard descriptive vocabulary can't capture. An elder, whose ears are old but whose memory is deep, produces a word that the younger members of the group have never heard — a word with a consonant cluster that clicks and buzzes simultaneously, a word that means something like *the sound of something not-yet-broken that is learning a new shape.* The younger people repeat the word. It feels strange in their mouths. They practice it.

They begin to pay attention to the runner-junction. They visit it regularly. They notice that the creaking changes with the weather, with the time of day, with the activity of the swingers in the canopy above. They begin to discuss these changes, and the discussions require more precision than their recent conversational habits have demanded, and so they reach further into their spoken language's back-catalog, retrieving words and distinctions that were on the verge of being forgotten. One of them begins to compose a new percussion-sequence to report the anomaly to neighboring groups — and because the anomaly is complex, the sequence is complex, with syncopations and polyrhythmic elements that haven't been used in a generation's worth of names.

The neighboring groups, receiving this complex transmission, are intrigued. Some of them send members to listen to the creaking runner. They bring back their own descriptions, in their own dialects, with their own recovered words. An informal network of attention forms around this one small anomaly — this one new voice in the chord — and the network exercises capacities that were growing slack: deep listening, precise description, complex percussion, the collaborative interpretation of something that doesn't fit existing categories.

An ear-piece maker in a distant group, hearing about the anomaly third-hand through the percussion-network, begins experimenting with a new tuning — a resin shape optimized to isolate the specific frequency band of the creaking, to hear it more clearly against the background chord. She shares the design with her apprentice. The apprentice modifies it. A new ear-piece joins the toolkit.

Within a year, the anomaly has been fully integrated into the people's understanding of their world. They know what it is (a stressed runner-junction), why it sounds the way it does (the flex-pattern, the resin's resonant properties), and what it means (the junction should be monitored; if the creaking changes character, the runner may eventually need to be abandoned and the network will reroute). The immediate mystery is solved. But the *capacities* that solving it required — the deep listening, the precise language, the complex percussion, the innovative ear-piece design — have been reactivated, and they will not go dormant again quickly, because the people have been reminded, in the most visceral way, of *why they have these capacities:* because the plateau is always producing new voices, and the only way to remain a voice in the chord rather than merely an inhabitant of the plateau is to keep listening at full depth, to keep meeting the world's complexity with equal complexity of attention.

The runner-junction will heal on its own. The wise power's intervention was minor and temporary — a slight reduction in resin production that will normalize within a season as the tree adjusts. No lasting damage. Just a small, productive difficulty. A *problem* — the kind of problem that, in its solving, makes the solvers more capable than they were before. Not a lesson, because the wise power does not teach. A provocation. A pebble in the shoe of a culture that was in danger of walking too comfortably.

---

## IV. What It Does Not Do

This is the longer list, and it matters more.

It does not reveal itself. The people of the plateau have no need of a god, a patron, a guardian, or a benefactor. They have the mat. They have the chord. They have each other. To reveal itself would be to introduce a relationship of dependency where none exists and none is needed — and worse, it would disrupt the people's understanding of themselves as one voice among many, replacing it with the knowledge that they are *special,* that they are *watched,* that a power beyond their comprehension has taken an interest in them. This knowledge would be poison. Not immediately, but inevitably, because a people who know they are watched begin to perform rather than to live, and a people who know they are protected begin to relax the very vigilance that makes them who they are.

It does not alter the mat-swimmers. It does not make them less dangerous. The swimmers are not a problem to be solved; they are a voice in the chord, and their voice — that grinding, bone-deep moan, that explosive violence of the feeding lunge, that eerie silence of the slow surfacing — is as essential to the plateau's identity as the swingers' songs or the mat's breath. The relationship between the people and the swimmers — that negotiation of tremor and stillness, that reverence-without-worship, those minutes of shared silence at the surfacing sites — is one of the most extraordinary things the wise power has ever encountered, and it would not trade it for anything, least of all for safety. Safety is what the lowlands offer. The plateau offers *participation,* and participation requires risk, and the wise power respects the people enough to let them bear it.

It does not introduce writing. This is the decision that costs it the most, because it can see how much has already been lost — how much the people once knew that they have forgotten, how much richer their history would be if it could be stored externally rather than carried in the fragile medium of living memory. But writing would change the people's relationship with their spoken language, which would change their relationship with sound, which would change their relationship with the chord, which would change everything. A people who can write do not need to remember, and a people who do not need to remember do not develop the deep-memory capacities that the plateau demands — the ability to carry a mental map of every runner-connection within a day's walk, the ability to hold a group's song-lineage in mind across generations, the ability to hear a percussion-name and feel its full history in the rhythm. Writing is not a neutral technology. It is a trade: external storage for internal capacity. In the lowlands, the trade is almost always worth it. On the plateau, the wise power is not sure, and *when it is not sure, it does not act.* This is perhaps the most important thing about it. It is wise enough to know the limits of its wisdom.

It does not protect them from the future. Climate will shift. The plateau's elevation will not protect it from everything. In centuries to come, the moisture patterns may change, the morning condensation may diminish, the epiphytes may struggle, the water balance may tip. The people will face this, and they will face it with whatever capacities they have, and the wise power trusts them — trusts the mat, trusts the chord, trusts the whole interlocking system — to adapt, because the system has been adapting for millions of years and the people have been part of it for thousands and the relationship is deeper and more resilient than any external intervention could make it.

It does not stay.

This is the hardest thing. Having listened, having acted — three small things, so small — having come to love this place and these people and this sound, the wise power *leaves.* Not because it wants to, but because its continued presence, even undetected, would be a kind of weight on the system. An extra listener changes what is heard. An observer, however benevolent, alters what is observed. The wise power knows that the most dangerous thing it could do is stay and keep watching, keep nudging, keep catching the falling resin at the right moment, keep introducing the productive difficulty at the right time — because eventually, the system would begin to depend on these interventions in ways too subtle to detect, and the people's trajectory would become the wise power's trajectory rather than their own, and the plateau would have gained a guardian but lost its autonomy, which is to say it would have lost everything.

So it goes.

---

## V. What It Leaves Behind

The fungal remnant-population, stabilized, quietly circulating its old-pattern genes through the mat's network. An insurance policy that will never need to be cashed if the system does what systems do.

A child with a piece of resin, discovering light.

A runner-junction that creaks in the wind, and a people re-learning the sound of *not-yet-broken learning a new shape.*

Three things. Three small investments in the plateau's ability to continue being what it already is. Not improvements. Not corrections. *Invitations* — invitations for the system to use its own deep intelligence to solve problems it didn't yet know it had, to develop capacities it didn't yet know it needed, to keep the chord full and complex and evolving rather than allowing it to simplify toward a comfortable monotony.

The wise power, departing, carries the sound with it. Not as a recording — as a memory, held in whatever medium a wise power uses for remembering. The bass throb. The shimmering rattle. The ground-moan. The drone. The whistle. The grinding. The breathing. The percussion-names, the clipped nighttime speech, the wind in the runners. The full chord, heard once and carried forever, the way the people carry their dead — not by preserving them but by letting them become part of what continues.

And far away, in whatever place a wise power goes when its work is done, it thinks about the plateau, and it wonders about the child — whether she found what the resin had to show her, whether she is, right now, at this moment, holding a shaped lens up to the bioluminescent glow and seeing the light separate into colors she has no words for because no one has ever needed words for this before — and it resists, with great effort, the urge to go back and look.

It trusts the chord.

The chord continues.

---

*One voice has started.*
