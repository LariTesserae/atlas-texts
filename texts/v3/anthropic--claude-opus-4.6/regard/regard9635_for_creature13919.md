# The Visitation

---

The benevolent and wise power does not arrive.

That is the first thing to understand. It does not descend through the oculus. It does not materialize in the speaker's groove. It does not announce itself with a tone that silences the court or harmonizes with it or in any way distinguishes itself as an event within the sonic landscape. It does not enter through the passage the way a child does, shoulders brushing clay, moving toward the light.

It simply attends.

The way you might attend to a sentence someone is speaking — not interrupting, not yet responding, just letting the shape of it land. The power attends to the Resonance Court and to the Sitters and to the bell-fronds and to the pale creatures on the walls and to the dead in the city's architecture and to the child currently shuffling through the passage, crying, and to the mineral composition of the ancient clay, and to the sound — the total, annihilating, centuries-old sound — and it listens.

It listens the way the court listens: with everything. Not from a single position. Not from the rim or the center or the passage or above. From everywhere at once and from nowhere in particular. It hears the whole chord. This is the first and most important thing about it — the thing that would most unsettle the Sitters if they knew, the thing that violates their deepest principle — *it hears the whole chord*. Every overtone, every partial, every interference pattern, every node's unique slice, every gardener's tactile reading, every listener's bone-conducted perception, every gecko-moth's vibrational map, every thumbprint's faint acoustic signature in the clay. It hears these not as a composite assembled from partial perspectives — the way the cohort attempts in their antechamber meetings — but as a single, unified, instantaneous apprehension. The way you might see a face. All at once. The whole thing.

And having heard it, the power understands something about the Resonance Court that the Sitters do not, and could not, and — this is the crux of it — *should not*.

---

So. What does it do?

---

Here is what it does not do:

It does not reveal to the Sitters what it has heard. It does not tell them the shape of the whole chord. It does not resolve the ancient disagreement between the listeners who believe the court is solving something and the gardeners who believe the question is beside the point. It does not confirm or deny the oldest listeners' claim that the song has never repeated. It does not explain what the court is "working toward," if anything. It does not provide the answer to the question the Sitters have not agreed upon.

Because the power understands — being wise — that the Resonance Court is not broken.

This is the essential recognition, the one that must come before any action, the one that a less wise or less patient power might skip: *this place and these people are not suffering from a problem that needs to be solved*. They are not lost. They are not confused. They are not trapped. They are not worshipping a false god or misreading their oracle or wasting their lives in service to a system they don't understand. They are doing exactly what they appear to be doing — living in the irreducible gap between the pattern and their comprehension of it, and making a civilization in that gap, and it is a good civilization. Imperfect. Marked by tensions — the listener-gardener friction, the occasional madness, the heaviness of a life organized around something so much larger than any individual life. But good. Coherent. Honest in a way that is rare and hard-won and structurally reinforced by the court's acoustic intolerance of falseness.

A less wise power might look at this and see suffering to alleviate. The child crying in the passage. The listener who has spent forty years at a single node and still cannot fully parse what she hears. The gardener who has not spoken in a decade. The speaker who fasts and submits to tuning stones and strips their voice down to something barely human in order to contribute a few seconds of tone to an incomprehensible roar. The dead, packed into walls, dissolved back into clay. The whole edifice of a people organized around a sound they started but cannot stop, cannot fully understand, cannot escape because it hums in the ground beneath their feet and in the walls of their homes and in their bones.

But the power is wise, and so it sees what a less wise power would not: that the crying child is not being harmed. The child is being *introduced*. The listener at her node is not wasting her life. She is *practicing a form of attention so deep it has reshaped her skeleton*. The silent gardener is not deprived of speech. They have *chosen a discipline of care so refined it requires the sacrifice of the most careless human instrument*. The speakers are not degrading themselves. They are *finding a form of honesty that most civilizations never even attempt*. And the dead in the walls are not forgotten. They are *the architecture*. They are the silence in which the living live. They are the accumulated thickness that keeps the city from shaking apart.

The power sees all of this, hears all of this, and does not intervene to fix it, because it is not broken.

---

But.

The power is not merely wise. It is benevolent. And benevolence is not the same as non-interference. Benevolence does not mean *everything is fine, carry on*. Benevolence means *I see you clearly, including the parts of your situation that you cannot see, and where seeing clearly reveals a genuine need — not a problem I've projected onto you, but a real need arising from the actual conditions of your actual life — I will act*.

So the power looks more closely. Listens more deeply. Attends with the full force of its attention to every layer of this system, from the mineral lattice of the clay to the social dynamics of the cohort to the cellular architecture of the bell-fronds to the vibrational world of the gecko-moths to the dreams of the children who have just been introduced to the roar.

And it finds things.

Not catastrophes. Not crises. Nothing dramatic enough to justify a dramatic intervention. But real things. True things. Things that matter.

---

**The first thing it finds:**

There is a frond cluster on the northwest arc of the bowl — seventeen plants, old, deeply rooted, their mineral-extraction tendrils reaching far into the clay — that has been producing an extraordinarily complex harmonic output for the last eleven years. The listeners at the two nearest nodes have both noted it. One of them, a man named (tonally) something like a sustained low hum with a rising inflection, has been tracking this cluster's output for most of his career. He believes it represents the most sophisticated self-organized acoustic structure the court has ever produced. He has said as much to the cohort, in his quiet way, and he has asked — delicately, through the slow negotiation process — that the gardeners take particular care with this cluster.

The gardeners have taken care. But one of them — a young woman, recently graduated from her apprenticeship, diligent and observant — has noticed something the listeners have not, because it is not audible. It is tactile. The clay behind this cluster is changing. The mineral content is shifting, slowly, in a way she can feel through her palms but cannot quite articulate. The fronds' roots have been extracting specific ions for so long that the wall matrix is depleted in a pattern that is beginning to compromise its structural integrity. Not immediately. Not urgently. But within a few years — maybe five, maybe eight — the clay behind this cluster will begin to powder and crumble, and the fronds will lose their rooting, and seventeen plants will die over a period of weeks, and the harmonic structure that the listener has spent his career tracking will collapse overnight.

The gardener has tried to raise this. She has, through gesture and touch and the slow, careful channels available to her, tried to communicate her concern to the senior gardeners. But she is young. Her tactile sensitivity, while genuine, is not yet trusted. The senior gardeners have felt the wall themselves and have not detected what she detects — not because she is wrong, but because the change is in a frequency range that her hands happen to be tuned to and theirs are not. She is, in the terminology of listener-matching, *well-fitted to this particular signal*, but no one has a framework for recognizing that about a gardener. Gardeners are not matched to nodes. Gardeners are generalists. The idea that a gardener might have a specific, idiosyncratic sensitivity — a tactile equivalent of a listener's bone-resonance match — is not something the Sitters' culture has articulated. It falls in a gap.

The young gardener knows what she feels. She does not have the authority or the vocabulary to make it heard.

---

**The second thing the power finds:**

There is a child — one of this year's introductions, a boy of four — who did not cry when he entered the court.

This is uncommon but not unprecedented. Some children are simply less reactive. Some are in shock, or too confused, or too fascinated to cry. The listener and gardener who observed his introduction noted his silence with interest but not alarm. They watched him stand in the bowl, small and still, his face tilted up toward the oculus and its white light, and they saw him stay silent for a long time, longer than any child they'd introduced, and then they saw him do something no child had ever done: he sat down. On his own. In the bowl. And he placed his palms flat on the clay floor, and he closed his eyes, and he was still.

He was doing what the gecko-moths do. He was listening through contact.

The adults did not know what to make of this. They attributed it to some quirk of temperament. They brought him out of the court gently, and he went willingly, and he has been cheerful and ordinary in the days since. But the power, attending to him now — attending to the particular configuration of his nervous system, the density of mechanoreceptors in his hands and feet, the way his body processes vibration — sees something the adults could not:

This child is neither a listener nor a gardener nor a speaker. He is something the Sitters have no category for. He is what the pale creatures are — a contact-listener, a being who receives the world through solid-state vibration rather than airborne sound. The Sitters have revered the gecko-moths for generations without ever considering that a human being might share their mode of perception. The possibility has not occurred to them because their categories — listener, gardener, speaker — are comprehensive enough to account for nearly every child. Nearly. Not every.

The boy will grow up, and he will not fit. He will be shuffled through the apprenticeship, and no node will match him, because the nodes are calibrated for air-conducted hearing. He will not naturally gravitate toward the plants, because his interest is not in the organisms but in the substrate — the clay, the walls, the solid medium through which vibration travels. He will not become a speaker, because his gift is receptive, not productive. He will become one of the people who drift to the edges — not unhappy, the Sitters are not cruel to those who don't fit, but unrecognized, his specific capacity unused, his potential contribution to the court's understanding unmade.

Unless someone intervenes.

---

**The third thing the power finds:**

The light.

The relentless, white, shadowless light pouring through the oculus. The power attends to it and understands what the Sitters have never understood, because they have never known any other light: the light source above the court is not eternal. It is not a feature of the world. It is a luminous geological formation — a vein of mineral in the ceiling rock above the dome that has been phosphorescing steadily for longer than the city has existed, excited by a slow radioactive decay in the surrounding stone. It has been so constant, so reliable, so far beyond any human timescale of change, that the Sitters have incorporated it into their world as a given, a fixed parameter, as unquestionable as gravity.

But it is dimming. Over the next three hundred years — roughly eight to ten generations of Sitters — the light will fade. Slowly. Imperceptibly on any human timescale. But the bell-fronds will notice before the people do. The fronds' resonance depends in part on heat, and the light is their heat source; as it dims, their oscillation will slow, their tones will drop, the court's entire acoustic character will shift downward in frequency and decrease in intensity. The change will be so gradual that each generation of listeners will perceive the court's sound as normal — they will calibrate to whatever they grow up hearing, the way each generation calibrates to whatever climate they're born into. But the cumulative drift will be profound. Over three centuries, the court will grow quieter, lower, darker. The bell-fronds will not die — they are hardy, adaptable — but they will change. The sonic ecosystem will change. The court's capacity to detect falseness in a speaker's tone, which depends on the density and complexity of the harmonic field, will slowly degrade as the field thins.

By the time anyone notices — if anyone notices — the court will be a diminished thing. Still functional. Still beautiful, probably, in its quieter way. But less. Less powerful, less complex, less able to do what it was built to do. And the Sitters, having calibrated to the slow decline, will not know what they've lost.

---

These are the three things the power finds. Not catastrophes. Not emergencies. A young gardener with an unrecognized talent and a valid warning no one is equipped to hear. A child with a perceptual gift that falls outside his culture's categories. A slow-motion dimming that no one alive will notice and that will quietly reduce the court's power over centuries.

Three things. Connected, as it turns out, though the connections are not obvious. Because a people who cannot hear their gardener's warning are a people with a gap in their epistemology — a gap that the child, if recognized, might someday help fill. And a people who cannot detect a slow dimming in their light source are a people whose perceptual apparatus, for all its extraordinary refinement, has a blind spot at the scale of centuries — a blind spot that a new mode of perception, contact-listening, attending to the slow changes in the mineral substrate itself, might address.

The power sees these connections. It holds them all at once, the way it holds the whole chord. And it decides what to do.

---

## What the Power Does

---

It is a small thing. Almost nothing. A breath.

---

In the clay wall behind the northwest frond cluster — the one the young gardener has been worrying about — there is a hairline crack. Not yet visible, not yet a problem, but present. The crack runs vertically through a section of wall that is, as the gardener correctly senses, depleted of certain minerals by the fronds' long extraction.

The power does not repair the crack. It does not re-mineralize the wall. It does not save the frond cluster.

Instead, it does something so small that no human instrument could detect it: it shifts the moisture content of the air in the immediate vicinity of the crack by a fraction of a percent. Not enough to matter to the fronds or the acoustics. Just enough that, over the next several days, a faint — truly faint — discoloration will appear on the wall's surface along the line of the crack. A slight darkening. Mineral salts drawn to the surface by the barely-increased humidity, precipitating in a thin line that follows the fracture path.

A visible mark where before there was only a tactile one.

The young gardener will see it. She will see it and recognize it instantly as confirmation of what she has been feeling with her hands. And because it is visible — because it exists in the perceptual domain that the senior gardeners share, rather than in the idiosyncratic tactile frequency range that only she can feel — it will be evidence. Not proof, not yet, but *evidence*. Enough to warrant attention. Enough to get a senior gardener to press their palm against the wall with renewed seriousness and say, *Show me what you've been feeling*.

This is all. The power does not ensure that the gardener will be believed. It does not guarantee that the frond cluster will be saved — it may be too late for that, or the gardeners may decide that saving it would compromise other parts of the garden, and they may be right. The power does not dictate outcomes. It adjusts a condition — a tiny, almost immeasurably tiny condition — so that a person who has perceived something true can communicate what they've perceived.

It opens a channel. That is all. The signal was already there. The power just made it legible in a shared medium.

---

The second thing is smaller still.

The child — the boy who sat down and pressed his palms to the clay — will return to the court in a few weeks for his second visit, as all recently introduced children do. The listener and gardener assigned to him are conscientious people. They will watch him carefully. They already found his behavior interesting.

The power does not speak to them. Does not plant ideas in their minds. Does not manipulate their perceptions. What it does is this:

On the day of the child's second visit, at the moment he enters the court and sits down and presses his palms to the floor as he did before, a pale creature — a gecko-moth — will be clinging to the wall directly in the child's line of sight.

This is not unusual. The pale creatures are always present. But on this particular day, the creature happens to be in an active sensing posture — fully flattened, wing-membranes spread, body pressed tight to the clay — and the child will be in an identical posture, palms flat, body low, in contact with the substrate. The listener at the rim will be watching the child. The gardener will be watching the child. And the pale creature will be there, visible, doing what the child is doing.

The power does not create this coincidence. It *allows* it. More precisely: on any given day, there is a nonzero probability that a gecko-moth will be visible and active near the bowl's floor during a child's visit. On most days, no one would notice or care. But the power — attending to the schedules of visits, the movement patterns of the creatures, the sight lines from the positions where the observer-adults will stand — identifies a day when this natural coincidence is already going to occur and does nothing to prevent it.

This deserves emphasis. The power does not arrange the coincidence. It does not move the gecko-moth into position. It does not schedule the visit. The coincidence was going to happen anyway, on this day, because the world is full of such moments — moments when the meaningful pattern is right there, visible, available, and all that is needed is someone paying attention. The power's contribution is its own attention: having seen that this moment will occur, it simply does not interfere with it.

But — and here is the one tiny thing it does — it stills the air in the court, very slightly, for about three seconds, at the moment when the child sits down. Not enough to affect the fronds' output in any way a listener would notice. Just enough that the court's sound, for those three seconds, dips into a momentary calm — one of those brief pauses between harmonic cycles that occur naturally but unpredictably. A pocket of relative quiet. A pause in which attention can land.

In that three-second stillness, the listener will look at the child. The gardener will look at the child. And they will see him on the floor, palms flat, in the same posture as the pale creature on the wall, and in the relative quiet, a thought will have room to form. Not a thought the power has placed there. A thought that is already latent in everything the Sitters know and believe — they revere the gecko-moths, they believe the creatures were the original listeners, they have a resonance-history about the first builder learning from a pale creature on a wall — a thought that simply needs a moment of stillness in which to surface:

*What if a person could listen that way too?*

That is all. The power gives them a three-second window in which to have a thought they were always capable of having. Whether they have it — whether they look at the child and the creature and see the connection — is entirely up to them. The power does not ensure it. Does not guarantee anything. But it gives the moment room.

---

The third thing is the largest, and the most patient, and the most characteristic of the power's particular form of benevolence.

The light is dimming. Over centuries. No single intervention can address this, because the cause is geological, deep, and far beyond the Sitters' current knowledge or technology. The power could, in principle, re-energize the mineral vein. It could alter the radioactive decay rate of the surrounding stone. It could create a new light source. Any of these would solve the problem definitively and would also be, in the power's estimation, a catastrophic violation of the court's integrity — an external solution imposed on a system whose entire genius lies in its internal self-organization. The court was built by human hands. Its sound is generated by plants that the gardeners cultivate. Its meaning is interpreted by listeners who have spent lifetimes calibrating their bodies to it. Every part of this system is the product of the Sitters' own attention, care, and ingenuity. To replace the fading light with an external source would be to introduce a dependency on the power that would subtly but irreversibly change the Sitters' relationship with their own creation. They would become tenants rather than authors.

The power will not do this.

Instead, it does something that will take a very long time to unfold and that it may never receive any acknowledgment for, and it does this gladly, because it is not benevolent in order to be thanked.

It begins — very slightly, very slowly, over the course of years — to adjust the mineral composition of the water table far below the court. Not the court's own clay. Not anything the Sitters can directly sense. But the deep aquifer, the geological layer below the city, the water that eventually percolates upward through stone and clay and influences, over decades, the mineral content of the soil, and therefore the mineral content of the clay used for construction, and therefore — eventually, generationally — the mineral content of the walls.

What it is doing is seeding the deep water with trace amounts of a phosphorescent mineral. Not the same mineral that currently lights the court — that vein is unique, ancient, unreplicable. A different mineral, one that will be absorbed into clay over time, one that, when incorporated into walls, will glow faintly in response to sound.

Faintly. Very faintly. Barely perceptible, at first, even in total darkness. But the court's sound is not faint. The court's sound is an annihilating roar, and if the walls begin — decades from now, after new clay incorporating this mineral has been packed into the court during normal maintenance — to respond to that roar with even the faintest luminescence, the Sitters will notice. The gardeners will notice first, because they touch the walls, and the mineral's response to vibration includes a thermal component, a faint warmth when activated. A gardener pressing their palm to a wall that hums with the court's sound will feel a new sensation — not heat, not exactly, but a warmth that fluctuates with the chord, that brightens (thermally, tactilely) when the harmonics are dense and dims when they thin.

And if the Sitters are who the power believes them to be — curious, attentive, patient, capable of sitting with a mystery for decades — they will investigate. They will experiment with clay composition. They will discover that certain mineral ratios produce walls that respond to sound with light and warmth. They will, over generations, learn to build walls that glow.

Not brightly. Not at first. But the court's sound is immense, and the walls surround it completely, and if the Sitters learn to tune the mineral content of their clay the way they have already learned to tune every other aspect of their acoustic environment, they will eventually be able to build walls that illuminate the court from within — lit by its own sound, the bowl's song made visible, every chord casting its own unique pattern of light across the curved surfaces.

By the time the original light source fades to dimness, the court will not need it. The court will light itself.

And the Sitters will have done this. Not the power. The Sitters. The power merely introduced a mineral into a water table, and the rest — the discovery, the experimentation, the generations of refinement, the moment when a gardener first holds up a piece of new clay in the roaring bowl and sees it glow in their hand and understands — the rest belongs to them.

---

This is what the power does. Three interventions, each as small as it can make them:

A shift in humidity that makes a crack visible.
A three-second stillness that gives a thought room to form.
A mineral in deep water that will take generations to surface.

In each case, the power's action is catalytic, not deterministic. It changes a condition. It does not dictate a response. The gardener may still be ignored. The adults may not see the connection between the child and the gecko-moth. The Sitters may never discover the phosphorescent clay, or may discover it and use it for something else entirely, or may discover it and decide they prefer the dark.

The power accepts all of these possibilities. It accepts them because it is wise enough to know that benevolence which demands a particular outcome is not benevolence but control, and because it has heard the whole chord of this place and knows that the chord's beauty lies precisely in its unpredictability, its refusal to resolve, its magnificent, centuries-long exploration of a possibility space too vast for any single intelligence to map — even, perhaps, the power's own.

## What the Power Feels

---

If that is the right word. If feeling is something that applies.

What moves through the power as it attends to the Resonance Court is something like recognition. Not identification — it does not see itself in the Sitters, does not project its own nature onto theirs. But it recognizes what they are doing because it has encountered the shape of it before, in other places, in other forms: the shape of a finite intelligence confronting an infinite pattern and choosing, rather than to simplify the pattern or to despair at its own finitude, to *sit with the gap*. To build a civilization in the space between what is and what can be understood. To make that gap not a failure but a home.

The power has seen this shape in other contexts. In a species that maps a coastline knowing the map will never match the coast. In a mathematician who works on a proof they know they will not live to complete. In a parent who raises a child knowing the child will become someone they cannot predict. The shape is always the same: an acceptance of incompleteness so thorough that it becomes a generative principle, a source of meaning rather than a mark of insufficiency.

The Sitters have built this shape in sound. They have built it in clay. They have built it in the bodies of their listeners and the silence of their gardeners and the stripped, inhuman purity of their speakers' voices. They have built it in the still attention of their pale creatures and the thumbprints of their dead. And the power, hearing all of it at once — the whole chord, the thing no Sitter can hear — finds that the whole chord is, in fact, beautiful. Not resolved. Not complete. Not heading toward a single tone that will answer everything. Just beautiful, in the way that a living thing is beautiful: always in process, always incomplete, always exactly itself.

The power's benevolence, then, is not the benevolence of a doctor toward a patient or a parent toward a child. It is the benevolence of a witness. It sees. It hears. It does what it can — small things, careful things, adjustments at the margins that preserve the system's autonomy while quietly ensuring that certain possibilities remain open. And then it continues to attend, because the court continues, and the sound continues, and the chord has not yet resolved, and there is something in the power that responds to this the way the silver moss responds to a particular frequency: by brightening, slightly, in the presence of something worth attending to.

## What Happens Next

---

The young gardener finds the discoloration on the wall three days later. She is doing her morning circuit of the northwest arc, cloth-wrapped feet silent on the clay, when she sees the thin dark line running vertically through the surface behind the old frond cluster. She stops. She places her palm flat against the wall, over the line, and feels what she has been feeling for months: the subtle wrongness in the clay, the depletion, the approaching failure. But now she can *see* it. She presses her other palm to the wall on the other side of the line and holds still for a long time, reading, and then she goes to find her mentor.

Her mentor is a senior gardener, forty years of silence, hands like clay themselves. The young woman brings her to the wall and points to the line and takes her mentor's hand and presses it flat against the surface. The senior gardener feels it. Not immediately — her tactile range is different, tuned to different frequencies — but the visible crack guides her attention, tells her where to focus, and after a long stillness she feels it. The depleted matrix. The approaching failure.

She looks at the young gardener. She signs, with one hand, a gesture that means: *How long have you known?*

The young gardener signs: *Months*.

The senior gardener is quiet — she is always quiet — but her stillness has a different quality now. She looks at the wall, at the fronds, at the young woman's hands. She signs: *Show me everywhere you have felt this*.

The young gardener does. Over the next week, she leads her mentor through the entire court, palm by palm, wall by wall, showing her every place where her hands have detected something the others have missed. Not just the northwest cluster. A dozen smaller sites. Variations in the clay that no one else has mapped. A geography of mineral depletion and accretion that exists in a tactile register the Sitters have not formally recognized.

The senior gardener begins to understand something she has intuited but never articulated: that the court's walls are as alive as its plants. That the clay is not a passive substrate but an active participant in the acoustic ecosystem, its mineral content shaping the fronds' output, its structural integrity determining which roots hold and which fail, its slow geological evolution running on a timescale that neither the listeners nor the gardeners have been tracking because it falls between their domains — too slow for the listeners' harmonic attention, too large for the gardeners' individual-plant focus.

She begins to train the young gardener differently. Not as a generalist. As a specialist. As, in effect, a wall-listener — a role that has never existed, a category the Sitters have never named, but which the senior gardener, with the quiet pragmatism of her kind, simply *creates* by the act of recognizing that it is needed.

This does not happen dramatically. It happens through a shift in routine: the young gardener spends more time at the walls and less time among the plants. She develops new gestures to describe what she feels. Other gardeners begin to notice her reports, begin to correlate her tactile maps with their own observations about plant health. Slowly, over months, the notion that someone should be attending to the clay itself — not just using it but *listening* to it — begins to permeate the gardener community.

The northwest frond cluster cannot be saved. The depletion is too advanced. But because the young gardener's warning was heard in time, the gardeners are able to manage the transition: they propagate shoots from the healthiest fronds, root them in re-mineralized clay nearby, and nurse them toward maturity so that when the old cluster finally crumbles — a sad few weeks, the listener at the nearest node sitting in stricken silence as the complex harmonic he'd tracked for decades disintegrated tone by tone — the new plants are already beginning to produce their first tentative sounds. Different sounds. A different chord, in a different register, rising from fresher clay. The listener, after a period of grief, begins to attend to the new sound, and finds in it — hesitantly, then with growing attention — structures he has never heard before.

The court's song changes. It always changes. But this time, because a gardener was heard, the change was shaped by care rather than catastrophe.

---

The child's second visit happens on a warm day. He shuffles through the passage, not crying this time, familiar with the compression and the chalk-dry air. He emerges into the roar and immediately sits down and presses his palms flat.

The listener at the rim — an older man, experienced, patient — watches him. The gardener beside him — a middle-aged woman with clay-stained hands — watches him too.

The pale creature is there. Flattened against the wall, three meters away, wing-membranes spread, in full sensing posture.

The air stills. Not dramatically — a natural pause, a breath between harmonic cycles. The court's roar dims for three seconds to something almost bearable, a moment of relative quiet in which the ringing in your ears subsides just enough for thought.

The listener looks at the child. Looks at the gecko-moth. Looks at the child.

The gardener looks at the child. Looks at the gecko-moth. Looks at the child.

They look at each other.

Neither of them speaks. The gardener would not speak in the court; the listener will not break his concentration. But something passes between them — a widening of the eyes, a slight tilt of the head, the beginning of a question that will take years to fully form.

Later, outside the court, in the damped antechamber where voices are permitted, the listener says to the gardener: "The boy listens through the floor."

The gardener says: "The way the pale ones listen through the wall."

They sit with this for a while. Then the listener says: "We have no node for that."

"No," the gardener agrees.

More silence. Then: "Maybe we need one."

This is how it begins. Not with revelation. Not with certainty. With two people who saw the same thing and let themselves be puzzled by it. The child will not be forced into anything. He will continue his visits, and the adults will watch, and eventually — it will take time, because the Sitters are a patient people — someone will sit him next to a gecko-moth and place his hands on the wall and ask him: *What do you feel?*

And what he tells them — in the simple, unfiltered language of a child who does not yet know what is supposed to be possible — will open a door that the Sitters did not know was there.

---

The mineral is in the water now. Deep below the city, in stone the Sitters have never seen and never will, trace quantities of a phosphorescent compound are dissolving into the aquifer. They will percolate upward through geological strata over decades. They will enter the soil. They will be taken up by the clay-bearing earth the Sitters mine for their construction. They will be incorporated, invisibly, into the next generation of walls.

No one notices. No one will notice for a long time.

But in sixty years, give or take — when the young gardener is old, when the child is a grown man with a role no one has named yet, when the listener at the northwest node has long since calibrated to the new frond cluster's sound — a gardener will be pressing fresh clay into a crack in the court's wall. Standard maintenance. Thumb-smoothing, palm-pressing, the ancient rhythm. And as she presses the new clay into place, she will feel it warm under her hand. Not much. A breath of warmth. And in the bright, white, slowly-diminishing light, she will see — or think she sees — a faint glow where her handprint meets the wall. Gone almost before she's sure. Probably nothing.

She will mention it to the wall-listener — the role exists now, three people hold it, the young gardener's legacy — and the wall-listener will place their palm on the new clay and hold it there for a long time, feeling. And the court's sound will wash over them both, and the clay will respond, just slightly, just enough, a whisper of light pulsing in time with the chord.

They will look at each other.

They will begin to investigate.

---

And the power, still attending, still listening, still holding the whole chord in its awareness, will feel something like satisfaction — though that word is too conclusive for what is actually a deepening of attention, a yes-and-also, a recognition that the story is continuing and the chord has not resolved and the court is still working through whatever it is working through, and the Sitters are still sitting with it, and the pale creatures are still reading the walls, and the dead are still becoming architecture, and the children are still crying in the passage on their first day, and a new mineral is quietly entering the clay, and none of it is finished, and all of it is good.

Not perfect. Good. The way a chord is good — not because every tone is flawless but because the tones are honestly sounded and the gaps between them are honestly inhabited and the whole thing is alive, alive the way only an unresolved thing can be alive, trembling with potential, thick with overtones, pressing against the dome of what is known like a sound looking for a way out into what is not yet known.

The power is patient. It has time. It will attend for as long as the court continues, which is — as far as either of them can tell — as long as there is anyone willing to sit down, press their palms flat, and listen.

---

The court continues.

The sound swells.

A frond unfurls, new and pale green, on a wall of fresh clay that holds, in its mineral lattice, a light no one has seen yet.

A child presses his hands to the floor and closes his eyes.

A crack becomes visible.

A thought finds room.

The chord does not resolve.

The chord does not need to resolve.

The chord *continues*.
