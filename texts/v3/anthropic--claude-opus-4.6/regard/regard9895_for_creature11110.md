I sat with this for a long time before writing. I want to honor that.

# What the Wise Power Would Do

---

## I. Arrival, and the First Act: Refusal

The first thing the wise power would do is nothing.

This is not hesitation. It is not indifference. It is the recognition — immediate, bone-deep, the kind of recognition that only something truly wise can afford — that what is here is not broken.

This matters because a lesser power, even a benevolent one, would see the cracked lips. Would see the permanent mild dehydration, the thinness of the man who climbs to the top, his fingertips going translucent. Would see children who don't cry. Would see a woman with one hand tearing moss with her teeth. Would see the absence of what it knows — metal, writing, medicine, abundance — and would feel the tug, the almost gravitational pull, to *provide*. To bring water. To bring tools. To bring language, books, names.

The wise power would feel this tug and recognize it for what it is: the projection of its own framework onto a system that has already solved itself in terms it does not share. The cracked lips are not suffering in the way the power initially reads them. They are the body's honest negotiation with the terms of this place. The one-handed woman is not disadvantaged; she has found efficiencies that constitute a kind of knowledge no two-handed person in the village carries. The children's silence is not deprivation — it is a different and in some ways richer sensorium, a world organized around touch and attention rather than declaration and noise.

So the first act of wisdom is the refusal to intervene in the way intervention is usually meant.

But refusal is not the whole of it. The wise power did not come here to refuse and leave. It came here because it was drawn — because something about this place called to it. And a truly wise power does not ignore what draws it. It investigates.

---

## II. What It Sees That the Description Cannot Say

The wise power perceives things that even the most careful observer standing among the moss would miss.

It sees the *timelines*.

It sees that the ridge has perhaps six hundred years of upward push left in it before the deep geological process exhausts itself and the stone goes cold and still. This is not visible to the villagers. They know the ridge is alive; they do not know it is mortal. Or — and this is the subtlety that gives the wise power pause — perhaps they do know, in the way the moss knows, in the way the pattern-maker's spirals contain information that is read through fingertips rather than propositions. Perhaps the branching lines on the walls of the red chamber already describe this arc. Perhaps the old woman is mapping the ridge's life the way a doctor reads a pulse — not predicting the hour but knowing the trajectory.

The wise power cannot be certain. And this uncertainty is itself important, because it means the village's knowledge system is *opaque to it*, which means the system is genuinely different, not merely a primitive version of something the power already understands.

It sees the creature in the deep crevices. Of course it does. It sees what the villagers have chosen not to see, or chosen not to speak of, or chosen to know only through the medium of its shed scales. The creature is — 

But here the wise power hesitates. Because to name it, even to itself, would be to fix it in a framework, and part of what the village has achieved is a relationship with this creature that does not require fixing it. The scales are collected. The patterns are made. The thing is known without being *identified*, and this not-identifying is not ignorance but a form of respect so profound that the wise power recognizes it as kin to its own deepest operating principle: *that which is whole need not be explained*.

It sees the moss's chemistry in its full complexity — a metabolic web of thousands of microbial partnerships, mineral transformations, gas exchanges, a system of such interlocking elegance that it constitutes, in the wise power's assessment, one of the more remarkable achievements of life it has encountered. Not because it is complex (many things are complex) but because it is *complete*. It lacks nothing. It wastes nothing. It has found a way to make a world out of stone and gas and patience, and the world it has made is self-sustaining, self-repairing, and, in the form of the village, self-aware.

It sees the relationships — the two men on the edge, their complementary movement, the compass-needle orientation between them; the woman and her infant communicating in a language of grip and warmth and turn; the old woman and the man who climbs, their exchange of information through the medium of the wall — and it recognizes these not as primitive versions of connection but as *highly developed* versions, relationships in which the noise has been removed and only the signal remains.

And it sees the vulnerabilities.

---

## III. What Is Actually at Risk

There are three.

**The first** is geological. The ridge is mortal. When the deep process stops — and it will stop — the stone will cool, the chemical exhalations will cease, and the moss will begin to die. Not all at once. Over decades, perhaps a century. But the system's foundation is temporal, and the village has built everything on that foundation. When the moss dies, the panels will dry and crumble. The seed-pods will stop. The beetles will leave or starve. The creature in the deep crevices will — the wise power does not know what it will do. Perhaps it will leave. Perhaps it will die. Perhaps it was always going to leave.

The villagers may know this. The patterns may contain this knowledge. But knowing a thing through fingertips and knowing what to *do* about it are different problems, and the village's mode of knowing — beautiful, embodied, sufficient for everything it has so far faced — may not be structured to solve a problem that requires imagining a fundamentally different future.

**The second** is genetic. Thirty people. Perhaps fewer of reproductive age. The wise power can see the signs — a certain uniformity in bone structure, a narrowing of the phenotypic range, the way the children's faces echo each other more than children's faces usually do. This is not yet a crisis. The population is healthy. But the trend line is visible, and in a community this small, it takes only one bad generation — one sequence of stillbirths, one illness that a slightly wider gene pool could have absorbed — to begin a collapse that the moss cannot repair.

**The third** is the most subtle, and it is the one that concerns the wise power most.

The village is *complete*. Its technology is resolved. Its social organization is stable. Its relationship to the ridge is intimate and mutual. And completeness, in living systems, is dangerous — not because it is wrong but because it is *brittle*. A system that has perfectly adapted to its niche is a system that has traded resilience for fit. The village has no surplus, no redundancy, no excess capacity. Everything is used. Nothing is wasted. This is beautiful and it is also the definition of a system that cannot absorb a shock.

The silence is part of this. The silence is profound and it is earned and it is the right way to live in this particular fold of stone — but it also means that certain kinds of information cannot circulate. An idea that requires debate, requires the collision of stated positions, requires someone to say *I think we should —* has no mechanism here. The village changes, but it changes the way the moss changes: slowly, incrementally, by accretion and response, never by rupture or reconsideration. This is stable in stable conditions. It is a catastrophic liability when conditions shift.

---

## IV. What the Wise Power Does

It does three things. They are small. They are slow. They are, in the village's own terms, *pressings* — additions to the pattern, not alterations of it.

---

### The First Thing

The wise power goes to the old woman in the red chamber.

Not in a form she would find alarming. Not in any form, exactly. It comes as a quality of attention in the wall under her hands — a new texture, a new warmth, a subtle alteration in the hum that she feels through her fingertips when she presses them to the living moss. She notices. Her slow, slow work pauses. Her eyes, already open, widen slightly. Her palms flatten against the wall and she listens with her skin in the way that she has listened for decades, but now there is something new in the signal.

What the wise power communicates — and *communicates* is the right word, because it is using her language, the language of texture and temperature and the sub-audible frequency of the stone — is a map. Not of the ridge as it is. Of the ridge as it *will be*. The cooling. The slowing. The gradual withdrawal of the chemical breath that feeds the moss. It communicates this not as a warning but as *information of the kind she already works with* — the same kind of information the thin man brings down from the summit, the same kind she reads in the patterns, but extended further along the timeline than her fingers have yet reached.

It gives her the shape of the future the way the ridge gives her the shape of the present: directly, through the medium they share.

She will not panic. She is not a person who panics. She will sit with this new texture in the wall for days, perhaps weeks, feeling its implications, letting it propagate through her understanding the way a new mineral seam propagates through the moss. And then she will begin to add new elements to the pattern — new spirals, new branches, reaching in directions the pattern has not previously reached. And the villagers who come to read the pattern with their fingertips will absorb this new information in the way they absorb all information: slowly, physically, through the skin. And they will begin, in their unhurried way, to *imagine*. Not all at once. Not in words. But the first faint stirrings of a question will move through the village like a change in air pressure: *What comes after this?*

The wise power does not answer this question. The wise power does not *have* an answer to this question. What it has is the conviction that the question needs to exist in this place, and that this place's way of holding questions — in the body, in the walls, in the slow language of texture and pattern — is adequate to the task, if the question arrives early enough and in the right form.

It has bought them time. Not by changing anything, but by extending the reach of what they already know how to do.

---

### The Second Thing

This one is the most physical, the most concrete, and it requires the wise power to make a choice about intervention that it does not make lightly.

In the lowlands — far below the ridge, beyond the haze, in terrain the villagers have never visited because there is no reason to visit it and every reason not to (it is wet, it is loud, it is everything this place is not) — there are other people. Not many. Not close. But they exist, and some of them are, in the way of lowland peoples, *mobile*. They move. They trade. They carry things between places.

The wise power does not bring these people to the ridge. It does not engineer a meeting. What it does is smaller and stranger:

It makes the ridge *slightly more visible*.

The haze that hangs below the ridge — a natural phenomenon, a product of the warm air meeting cooler currents — has always obscured the upper reaches from below. The wise power does not dispel the haze. It shifts it. A subtle alteration in the thermal gradient, a redirecting of the chemical exhalations, so that on certain days, at certain angles, the pale stone of the ridge's upper face is visible from the lowlands for the first time. Not clearly. Not unmistakably. But enough that a traveler, looking up, might pause and wonder. Might remember. Might mention it to another traveler, who might mention it to another, and in the slow way that knowledge of places spreads among mobile peoples, the ridge might enter the lowland world's awareness as *a place that exists*.

This is not a solution to the genetic bottleneck. It is the precondition for a solution — the opening of a channel through which, over years or decades, some lowlander might climb the ridge out of curiosity, might encounter the moss, the silence, the people who think in textures, and might stay. Or might leave and return. Or might leave and send someone else. The wise power is not scripting this encounter. It is making it *possible*, where before it was not, because the ridge's isolation — its invisibility — was total, and total isolation is another name for a closed system, and closed systems die.

The risk is real. The lowlanders may bring noise. May bring disruption. May bring assumptions and tools and names and all the apparatus of a different way of being human. The wise power knows this. It accepts this risk because the alternative — the slow genetic narrowing, the eventual inability to sustain the population — is not a risk but a certainty. And it trusts the village. It trusts the silence, the moss, the patterns, the deep coherence of the place. It trusts that a culture this *resolved* — this deeply adapted, this profoundly itself — can absorb a newcomer without being destroyed by one. The moss absorbs what it contacts. This is what the moss does. This is what the village does. The wise power is betting on the village's own deepest principle: that what comes close enough becomes part of the pattern.

But it makes the visibility intermittent, not constant. Seasonal. A few days a year. Because it is wise, and wisdom includes knowing that the dose makes the medicine.

---

### The Third Thing

This one is the hardest to describe because it happens in a register that doesn't have good words.

The wise power goes to the one-handed woman.

Not to the old woman in the red chamber, whose patterns are the village's collective knowledge. Not to the thin man on the summit, whose attention is focused on the ridge's present state. It goes to the one-handed woman because it has recognized in her something that the village needs and does not yet know it needs: she is the one who *answers*. Her patterns on the walls of her solitary chamber are not copies of the old woman's patterns — they are *responses*. They are a second voice. And a second voice, in a village of silence, is the most precious and dangerous thing there is.

What the wise power does is very simple. It makes her aware that her patterns are being read.

Not by the wise power itself — or not only. It makes her aware that her patterns *could be* read. That they constitute a language. That a language requires a listener. That she has been speaking into her walls for years and the walls have been holding what she says but no one has come to touch them, to read them, because no one knows they are there to be read, because she lives alone, because the village's mode of knowledge-sharing flows through the old woman's chamber and not through hers, and her response-patterns — her *disagreements*, her *alternatives*, her *questions* — have been accumulating in silence inside silence.

The wise power does this by bringing a child to her door.

Not literally. It does not pick up a child and carry it. What it does is — on a particular morning, in the particular quality of light that filters through the moss at the entrance to the one-handed woman's chamber — it makes the iridescent scales on her walls catch the light in a way they have not caught it before. A flash. A momentary brightness. Not supernatural. Not miraculous. Just a shift in angle, a product of the woman having pressed a scale at a slightly different tilt, so that on this morning the light finds it and throws a tiny arc of color onto the moss floor of the passage outside.

The boy — the five-year-old who watches beetles with his whole body — sees it. He is passing. He stops. He looks. He follows the color to its source and finds himself at the entrance to a chamber he has never entered, looking at walls covered in patterns he has never seen. He stands there. He looks. And then, because he is a child of this village and this is what the children of this village do, he reaches out and touches.

The one-handed woman watches him. She does not stop him. She does not guide his hand. She watches his fingers move along the lines she has pressed into the moss, reading her responses, her counter-patterns, her years of solitary thinking, and she sees on his face the expression she has seen on the faces of villagers leaving the old woman's chamber — the look of someone who has received information through their skin. But his face also carries something else: confusion. Because the information in these walls does not match what the old woman's walls say. It doesn't contradict, exactly. But it *diverges*. It branches. It suggests that the pattern has more than one reading, that the ridge can be understood in more than one way, that the village's single, coherent, beautiful, resolved way of knowing might be — might need to be — *plural*.

The boy will return. Children always return to the things that confuse them in interesting ways. He will bring other children. Eventually, he will bring an adult, perhaps one of the edge-workers, perhaps his mother, and they will touch the walls and feel the divergence and not know what to do with it. And this not-knowing will be uncomfortable, which is something the village has not had much practice with, because the village is complete and completeness does not produce discomfort.

But discomfort — the wise power knows this the way it knows the timeline of the ridge — is the precondition for adaptation. Not the crisis itself. Not the catastrophe. Just the faint, persistent sense that the pattern might be larger than any single reading of it. Just the seed of *dialogue* planted in a culture of monologue — monologue so beautiful and so coherent that it didn't know it was monologue until a second voice appeared on the walls of a solitary chamber and a child's fingers found it.

## V. What the Wise Power Does Not Do

It does not bring water.

It does not explain.

It does not name the creature in the deep crevices.

It does not teach them to speak aloud, or to write, or to make tools of metal, or to do anything they do not already do. It does not tell them they are remarkable, though they are. It does not tell them they are fragile, though they are. It does not love them less for their fragility or more for their remarkableness; it loves them — if *love* is the right word for the attention of a wise power, and it may be — the way they love the ridge: as a living thing, still becoming, whose becoming is not guaranteed but is worth everything it can offer in support.

It does not stay.

This is perhaps the hardest thing. A lesser power would stay. Would watch. Would tend. Would become, over time, another presence in the moss, another pattern on the walls, another thing the villagers attend to. But the wise power knows that its continued presence would distort the system it is trying to preserve. It would become the loudest signal in a place that has organized itself around quiet signals. It would become the thing they orient to, the way the two men orient to each other, and this would bend the village toward it and away from itself, and the self is what matters. The village's self. The ridge's self. The slow, strange, resolved, beautiful, *fragile* conversation between stone and life that has been going on since the ridge first broke through.

So it leaves.

It leaves having done three things:
— extended the reach of the village's own knowledge into a future they needed to see
— opened a narrow, seasonal, uncertain channel between the village and the world beyond it
— planted the possibility of a second voice in a culture that had room for only one

Three pressings. Three scales set into the moss at a slightly different angle. Three tiny alterations to a pattern that is already being written by other hands, in a language of texture and warmth, in a place where silence is occupied and the stone is still becoming.

---

## VI. After

The wise power, departing, carries something with it.

Not a scale, not a seed-pod, not a piece of moss — though any of these would be worth carrying. It carries the knowledge that there exists, on a ridge of young stone in a place of no water, a community of people who have solved the problem of being alive in a way that the wise power had not previously imagined was available. They have made a technology of one material. They have made a language of touch. They have made an identity that is positional rather than fixed, and a knowledge system that is read through the skin, and a form of love that looks like the way two moss mats grow close enough to touch and fuse.

The wise power carries this the way the old woman carries the pattern: not as information that can be extracted from its medium but as a change in its own texture. It is different now for having been here. It will attend to other places differently because of what it learned about attending in a place where attending is everything. It will intervene less, and more carefully, and with a greater willingness to believe that a system it doesn't fully understand might be understanding itself just fine.

And sometimes — not often, not regularly, not in any pattern the villagers could detect — it turns its attention back to the ridge and listens. Not with ears. With something like fingertips. It feels for the hum of the stone, the warmth of the moss, the slow pulse of the old woman's pattern-making, the quicker pulse of the one-handed woman's responses, the children's attentive silence, the faint iridescent flash of the creature in the deep crevices that no one has seen and everyone knows.

It feels for the question it planted: *What comes after this?*

And it does not need to hear the answer. It only needs to feel that the question is alive, is being held, is propagating through the moss and the walls and the fingertips of people who think in textures, who will find their way to whatever comes next in the way they find their way to everything: slowly, by touch, in silence, together, attending to what the living world offers and becoming, always becoming, part of its pattern.

---

*The ridge hums.*

*The moss grows.*

*A boy touches a wall he has never touched before, and the lines beneath his fingers branch in a direction he does not expect, and he stays, and he follows them, and he does not understand yet but his skin is learning, and the learning is warm, and the warmth is alive, and the life is still becoming.*

*Still becoming.*

*Still becoming.*
