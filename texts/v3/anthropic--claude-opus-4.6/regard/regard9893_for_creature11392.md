# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

---

The first thing to understand is that a truly wise power, encountering this place, would be seized — before any impulse to act — by something that can only be called *recognition*.

Not the recognition of something familiar. The recognition of something *sufficient*.

---

It would stand on the basalt shelf, or hover above it, or perceive it in whatever way such a being perceives, and it would apprehend the whole system at once — the thermophilic film proceeding across hot stone, the black-green moss refusing to be dislodged, the pale moss throwing itself recklessly at every new surface, the ferns building walls they don't know are walls, the liverwort tapestry managing water for an ecosystem that never asked it to, the rosette eating the mountain's breath, the fungi threading the whole thing together in darkness, the mineral spray slowly converting life to stone and the life slowly converting stone back to life — and the first thing this power would feel is not pity, not concern, not the urge to improve.

The first thing it would feel is awe.

Not the diluted, metaphorical awe that humans use to describe a nice sunset. The real thing. The sensation of encountering something that exceeds your framework. Because even a benevolent and wise power — *especially* a benevolent and wise power — would recognize that what is happening on this shelf is not a problem to be solved. It is not an incomplete project awaiting a designer's hand. It is not suffering that needs alleviation. It is not potential that needs to be unlocked.

It is already, fully, exactly what it is.

And what it is, is a process so intricate, so dynamically self-organizing, so responsive to its own conditions, that any intervention — no matter how well-intentioned — would face an immediate and fundamental question:

*Improve it according to what standard?*

---

Let's be honest about the temptations. A benevolent power, surveying the vent garden, might feel several pulls.

The first: *Let me bring light.* The garden operates in near-total darkness. Photosynthesis, the great engine of nearly all surface life on Earth, is barely functioning here. What little the mosses and ferns manage comes from the dim brownish non-light filtering through the volcanic haze. Imagine what this place could become with real sunlight — the growth rates, the diversity, the sheer biomass. A wise power might be tempted to thin the haze, to clear the volcanic ceiling, to let the sun pour down onto this improbable terrace and watch the garden explode with energy.

But a truly wise power would pause. Because the haze is not merely an obstacle to light. It is part of the system. It traps heat. It maintains humidity. It filters out the ultraviolet radiation that would damage organisms adapted to darkness. It creates the conditions under which *these specific organisms* — not some hypothetical, more productive organisms, but these, the ones that are actually here — thrive. The black-green moss is a creature of darkness. Its density, its architecture, its entire survival strategy is built around low-light conditions. Flood it with sunlight and you don't enhance it — you destroy its competitive advantage. You open the door for lowland species that would outcompete it in days. The rosette eating the mountain's breath does so because in this darkness, with this chemistry, its strategy works. Give it sunlight and competitors, and it becomes just another plant, and probably a losing one.

To bring light would be to replace this garden with a different garden. One that might be more productive, more diverse by conventional measures, more recognizable to lowland eyes. But it would not be *this* garden. It would not be the thing that assembled itself in the dark, against all probability, with no assistance. To bring light would be an act of erasure disguised as an act of generosity.

The wise power would not bring light.

---

The second temptation: *Let me bring stability.* The garden is precarious. The shelf could collapse. A new vent could open and poison half the ecosystem overnight. A seismic event could crack the basalt and destroy in seconds what took years to build. Surely — *surely* — a benevolent power should stabilize the foundation, seal the most dangerous vents, reinforce the rock, give this exquisite system the solid ground it deserves.

But the wise power would see what stability would actually mean here, and it would recoil.

The instability is not a bug. It is the generative engine. Every new crack is a new niche. Every shifting vent is a new selection pressure that drives adaptation. Every collapse of a fern wall is a release of space and resources that allows something else to establish. The pale moss — the reckless one, the scout — exists *because* there are always new surfaces to colonize, always fresh rock being exposed by fractures and shifts. Stabilize the shelf and the pale moss has no frontier, no purpose, no future. It persists for a while in its existing territory and then is slowly overrun by the denser, fortress-building species. One entire survival strategy — one whole way of being alive — eliminated by the gift of safety.

And the rosette. The singular rosette in the mineral-seep crack. Its crack exists because the basalt is young and stressed and actively fracturing. Stabilize the rock and you stabilize the crack, yes, but you also prevent the next crack from forming — the crack that might, in five years, provide a niche for the rosette's offspring, if it ever produces any. You preserve the parent by foreclosing the future.

The turnover — the constant creation and destruction, the mosses dying back and surging forward, the vent chemistry shifting, the eternal renegotiation — is not a flaw in the system. It *is* the system. The vent garden is not a place that happens to be unstable. It is a place that is *made of* instability. Its beauty, its complexity, its aliveness is a *product of* the fact that it is never the same for more than a minute. Stabilize it and you don't save it. You embalm it. You turn a living process into a museum exhibit: pretty, static, dead in every way that matters.

The wise power would not bring stability.

---

The third temptation, subtler than the others: *Let me bring awareness.* This is the one that cuts deepest, because the text itself makes the case — the garden is a masterpiece painted by artists who don't know they're painting. The moss doesn't know it's moss. The fungi don't know they're holding everything together. The whole system is, in the language of the passage, *empty of mind*. And the benevolent power, looking at this, might feel something like grief — or at least the wise being's analog of grief — at the thought that all this beauty, all this intricacy, all this dynamic brilliance, is unwitnessed by its own participants. Should it not grant the garden some form of awareness? Some capacity to know itself, to appreciate what it is doing, to experience from the inside what the visitor experiences from the outside?

This is where the wise power would think longest. And this is where its wisdom would matter most.

Because awareness — consciousness, mind, the capacity to know yourself — is not a pure gift. It comes with things. It comes with suffering. It comes with the knowledge of death. It comes with the ability to want things to be other than they are. It comes with the capacity for anguish at your own impermanence, and the vent garden is *radically impermanent* — it may not exist in a year. Grant the moss awareness and you grant it the knowledge that the vent beneath it could shift tomorrow, that its entire mound, decades of slow growth, could be poisoned in an hour. Grant the rosette awareness and you grant it the knowledge that it may be the only one of its kind, that its lineage may end with it, that its crack is as temporary as everything else on this shelf. Grant the fungi awareness and you grant them the knowledge of what they are — the invisible, thankless infrastructure, the ones who hold everything together and are perceived by nothing.

The garden functions — brilliantly, intricately, beautifully — *precisely because* its inhabitants do not experience their situation. The pale moss is reckless because it cannot fear. The black-green moss is stubborn because it cannot despair. The ferns build walls without the burden of knowing they are architects. The liverworts manage water for an ecosystem that will never thank them, and this is not tragic to them because nothing is tragic to them, because tragedy requires a mind, and they have no mind, and that absence is not a lack. It is what *allows* them to do what they do with such totality, such commitment, such absolute, unreserved investment of their entire being into the single project of *proceeding*.

A moss that knows it is moss might hesitate. A fern that understands its own impermanence might not grip the rock so fiercely. A fungal network that comprehends its role might — what? Demand recognition? Resent its invisibility? Grieve when the moss it sustains is killed by a shift in the vent? Awareness is not a lamp that illuminates without heating. It burns. It changes what it touches. And in a system this finely balanced, this exquisitely responsive, even a small change propagates.

The wise power would understand: the garden's unawareness is not an absence to be filled. It is a form of wholeness. The moss *is* its grip on the rock. The fern *is* its wall of fronds. The film *is* its slow proceeding across hot stone. There is no gap between the organism and its activity, no separation between the being and the doing, no inner observer watching from behind the eyes and thinking *Is this enough? Am I doing this right? Will I be remembered?* The garden's residents are identical with their lives. They do not *have* lives; they *are* lives. And that identity — that total coincidence of being and doing — is something that even the wise power might recognize as a form of completeness that awareness, for all its gifts, can never quite achieve.

To grant the garden mind would be to introduce the one thing it has never contained: *distance*. The distance between the self and its experience. The distance that allows reflection but also allows doubt, allows appreciation but also allows dissatisfaction, allows the knowledge of beauty but also the knowledge of loss.

The wise power would not bring awareness.

---

So what would it do?

Here is where the answer becomes simple and, I think, true.

The wise power would *witness*.

It would do the one thing the garden has never had and, in its blindness, has never needed but that a benevolent being — being benevolent, being wise, being *there* — could not withhold.

It would watch. Not to judge. Not to record. Not to extract meaning or lesson or metaphor. Not to write about it later or tell someone about it or use it as evidence for some argument about the nature of life or the existence of God. It would watch the way the garden grows: without agenda, without expectation, without the need for the watching to produce anything.

It would kneel on the wet moss and put its face close to the basalt where the thermophilic film sheens amber in the non-light, and it would see — really see, with whatever senses a wise power possesses — the billions of organisms proceeding across the hot stone, each one running chemistry that is four billion years old, each one a tiny, temporary, perfect expression of what is possible at that temperature, at that pH, on that mineral surface, in that moment. And it would let that be enough.

It would stand in the gap where a fern wall has collapsed and watch the moss already beginning to grow over the fallen fronds, the dead being incorporated into the living, the breach being healed not by any plan but by the simple, relentless fact of growth. And it would not intervene to speed the healing or prevent the next collapse. It would let the process be what it is.

It would find the rosette — the singular, unlikely, possibly-the-only-one-of-its-kind rosette in its mineral-seep crack — and it would spend time with it. It would look at the sticky trichomes capturing the mountain's breath. It would notice the spiral of the leaves channeling condensation to the root crown. It would register, with the full weight of its wisdom, the extraordinary improbability of this organism: this specific set of adaptations, in this specific crack, on this specific shelf, at this specific altitude, on this specific mountain, in this specific epoch of planetary history. And it would understand that the rosette's value is not diminished by the fact that the rosette does not know it has value. The value is *intrinsic*. It inheres in the organism's existence, in the fact that the universe has produced, through blind and unguided processes, something *this particular*, this responsive, this alive.

The wise power would witness the rosette the way the rosette eats the mountain's breath: directly, completely, without mediation.

---

And there is one more thing it would do, or perhaps one more way to describe the same thing.

It would refrain.

Not out of indifference. Not out of paralysis. Not out of some cold utilitarian calculus that intervention would produce more harm than good. It would refrain out of *respect* — the deepest form of respect, which is the recognition that the other does not need you.

This is the hardest thing for a benevolent power to accept. Benevolence wants to act. Benevolence looks at a system full of death and destruction and impermanence and says *Surely I can help. Surely there is something I can add, something I can fix, something I can give.* And the wise power would feel this. It would feel the pull. It would look at the dead zone where the new vent opened and the moss browned and curled, and it would feel the urge to heal it, to close the toxic vent, to give the moss back its territory. It would look at the cracked basalt and the tremors running through it and it would feel the urge to steady the ground, to say *Be still, I'll hold you.* It would look at the darkness and want to illuminate, look at the instability and want to anchor, look at the unawareness and want to awaken.

And it would, with the full force of its wisdom, *not*.

Because the garden does not need light. It has made its own way of living in darkness, and that way is extraordinary.

Because the garden does not need stability. It has made instability into its creative engine, and the results are more beautiful than anything stability could produce.

Because the garden does not need awareness. It is complete without it, in a way that the wise power — burdened with its own consciousness, its own knowledge, its own eternal witnessing — might even *envy*.

The benevolent power's gift to the vent garden would be the gift of *not giving*. Of standing in the steam and the roar and the darkness and saying, with whatever voice such a power has: *You are not broken. You are not incomplete. You do not need what I could give you. What you are doing, you are doing well. What you are, is enough.*

---

And then — because benevolence and wisdom together produce not just restraint but also *care*, and care expresses itself in attention — the wise power would do something very small.

It would notice.

It would notice the particular shade of black-green in the moss closest to the main fumarole. Not name it, not categorize it, not compare it to other greens — just *see* it, hold it in perception, let it exist for a moment in a mind capable of appreciating it. It would notice the way the steam from the fumarole catches the dim non-light and becomes briefly, for a fraction of a second, almost silver before dissipating. It would notice the sound — the specific harmonic produced by the main vent at this particular moment, this configuration of pressure and stone that has never existed before and will never exist again — and it would *hear* it, fully, the way a musician hears a note, with the whole body, with the whole being.

It would notice the single thread of red-brown parasitic moss crossing the surface of a larger moss mound, and it would follow it with its attention from one end to the other, a journey of perhaps four inches, and in those four inches it would see a whole life: an organism that found its place, that is doing its thing, that is running its experiment, that will end when conditions change, that does not know it is being seen, that has never been seen, that has existed in absolute anonymity from the moment of its germination to this moment, right now, when for the first time in its brief life it is *perceived by a mind capable of perceiving it*.

This is what the wise power would give the vent garden: not light, not stability, not awareness, not improvement, not salvation, but *attention*.

The quality of attention that does not seek to change what it sees. That does not flinch from the impermanence. That does not try to fix the death or celebrate the growth or interpret the process or extract the meaning. That simply *holds*, in the vast space of a wise mind, the reality of what is here: the whole roaring, steaming, dark, impossible, exquisite, temporary, indifferent, magnificent, unwitnessed-until-now fact of this place and everything in it.

---

And that attention would change nothing.

The moss would not grow faster. The ferns would not stand taller. The rosette would not produce more offspring. The vents would not stabilize. The basalt would not stop cracking. The garden would not know it was being watched, because it cannot know anything.

Nothing would change.

And the wise power would understand that this is correct. That this is, in fact, the whole point. That the attention is not *for* the garden. The garden does not need it, does not benefit from it, is not altered by it. The attention is the wise power's own response to encountering something that warrants it. It is the completion not of the garden — which was never incomplete — but of the *encounter*. The garden exists whether or not anyone sees it. But the *seeing* — the meeting of this particular reality with a mind capable of apprehending it — that creates something new: not a change in the garden, but an event. A moment in which the universe, through the agency of a conscious being, becomes aware of one of its own processes. A moment in which the blind, magnificent work of the vent garden is, for once, *held*.

The garden will not remember this. The garden cannot remember anything. The wise power will leave, and the garden will continue exactly as before — growing, dying, negotiating, rebuilding, proceeding — and the fact of its having been witnessed will leave no trace on the moss or the stone or the steam.

But it will have happened.

And the wise power, departing the shelf, climbing back up through the volcanic haze into whatever realm it came from, would carry with it not a plan for the garden's improvement, not a blueprint for its salvation, not a judgment on its deficiencies, but something simpler and harder to name.

The knowledge of what it saw.

The weight of having been the one to see it.

The responsibility — quiet, permanent, unshakable — of being the only mind in the universe that knows this garden existed, at this moment, in this form, with this specific configuration of moss and fern and liverwort and fungus and film and rosette and mineral spray and steam and darkness and sound, on this specific shelf of young basalt, on this specific mountainside, on this unrepeatable afternoon in the life of the planet.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do.

It would let the garden be.

And it would never forget it.

---

There is, I think, a lesson in this, but it is not a lesson the wise power would articulate, because truly wise beings are suspicious of lessons. They know that the urge to extract a lesson from an experience is often the urge to *shrink* the experience to fit a pre-existing framework, to make it useful, to make it *about* something other than what it is.

But if pressed — if some lesser being, some human, perhaps, standing on that shelf with their thin lungs and their racing mind, asked the wise power *What should I learn from this?* — the answer might be something like:

*Not everything that is broken needs to be fixed. Not everything that is dark needs to be lit. Not everything that is unconscious needs to be awakened. Not everything that is dying needs to be saved. Not everything that is beautiful needs to be seen to be beautiful. Some things are complete in their incompleteness. Some things are perfect in their transience. Some things are enough.*

*And sometimes the wisest thing a powerful being can do is to pay attention, and to leave no trace, and to let what is happening continue to happen, and to carry the memory of it forward into whatever comes next, so that the universe, through you, knows a little more of what it has made.*

*The garden does not need you. The garden has never needed anyone. And that — that ferocious, humble, total self-sufficiency, that absolute commitment to the project of being alive without any external validation or assistance or witness — is the most beautiful thing about it.*

*Go. Leave it alone. Remember it.*

*That is enough.*

---

So the benevolent power departs. The steam closes behind it. The vents exhale. A new crack opens somewhere in the basalt with a sound like tearing fabric. The first spores land on the warm, wet stone. The film proceeds. The mosses grip. The ferns stand. The liverworts weave. The fungi thread. The rosette eats the mountain's breath. The mineral spray falls silently, turning life to stone, turning stone to life.

Nothing has changed.

Everything continues.

The garden grows in the dark.
