# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

---

It would do nothing at first.

A long time, nothing.

It would listen. Not to understand the system — it already understands the system — but to understand the *experience* of the system. To know what it is like to be inside the drone. To feel what the workers feel when the cold presses against their lips and they learn to keep their mouths shut. To feel what the pale birds feel when they navigate by pressure gradients in a roar that has replaced the sky. To understand the particular quality of patience that a flatworm practices in a centimeter of water on stone that still has drill marks in it.

The benevolent power would not be in a hurry. Hurry is what built this place.

---

It would recognize, first and with great clarity, what this place *is*: a system in which every living thing has been made dependent on a set of conditions that are themselves dependent on continuous mechanical intervention that is itself dependent on a workforce too small to maintain it. It would see the narrowing margins. It would see the red indicator light glowing unnoticed in Section 14-7N. It would see the scaffold whose welds are fatiguing, the compressor whose bearings have worn past the point of elegant adaptation into the early stages of failure, the nutrient mixing tank whose intake valve is scaling shut by fractions of a millimeter per month. It would see the trajectory — not catastrophe, not yet, but the slow, asymptotic approach toward a threshold beyond which the system's redundancies would begin to fail not individually but in cascades, each failure loading its neighbors, the way cracks propagate through stressed glass.

It would see this and it would not panic. It would also not simply fix everything. Because fixing everything — replacing every worn bearing, re-welding every joint, restoring the workforce to its original complement, adding new racks, extending the canopy — would be the response of a power that sees only the machinery. And the machinery is not the point. The machinery was never the point. The machinery is what was built when someone decided that life needed to be maintained in a place where life was not naturally viable, and the question the benevolent power would sit with, for a long time, in the cold, in the drone, is: *was that the right decision?*

---

Not in a judging way. Not to assign blame. The power is benevolent and wise, which means it does not waste its wisdom on verdicts about the past. The basin was excavated. The greenhouses were built. The canopy was planted. The machines were started. These things happened for reasons that may have been good, may have been desperate, may have been visionary, may have been the only option available to people facing circumstances the basin itself cannot testify to. The power does not need to know why. It needs to know *what is*, and what is is this:

A community of living things — plants, birds, insects, spiders, flatworms, fungi, bacteria, and people — bound together in a mutual dependency that is sustained by enormous continuous effort and that exists in a state of slow, quiet, largely unacknowledged decline.

The people keep the machines running. The machines keep the canopy alive. The canopy keeps — what? The people fed, presumably. Or others beyond the plateau's edge. Something necessary. Something that justifies the cold hands and the compressed mouths and the gestural language and the triage mentality and the weeks-long gaps between visits to distant sections. The workers are not enslaved. They are not coerced. They are *needed*, and they know it, and that knowledge has replaced every other kind of motivation with the singular imperative of keeping the system running for another day, another week, another season.

This is the thing the benevolent power would see most clearly: **the workers have forgotten that they are also alive.**

Not forgotten intellectually. They know they breathe, they eat, they sleep, they feel the cold. But they have organized their entire existence around the maintenance of a system that sustains other life, and in doing so they have made themselves into components. They are the most reliable moving parts in the basin. They check gauges, replace belts, prune roots, and climb ladders with the same cyclical regularity as the compressors and the pumps. They have worn grooves in the walkway grating. They have become predictable to the spiders who build webs near the access tunnels and have learned that large warm disturbances pass at roughly the same intervals. The workers have achieved the kind of perfect functional integration that the machines achieved long ago, and they have paid for it the same way the machines did: by losing every capacity that is not directly relevant to the task.

They do not sing. They do not argue. They do not sit on the walkway grating and dangle their feet over the drainage channels and talk about what the sky looks like today, up above the glass, above the algal films and the moss and the flattened fern fronds, above all of it, where the sky is presumably still doing what skies do. They do not name the birds. They do not watch the flatworms. They do not notice that the spiderwebs near Junction 7-East are among the oldest continuously maintained structures in the basin, built and rebuilt by generations of small patient architects who have tenanted the same silk scaffolding longer than some of the machines have been running. They do not notice because noticing is not part of the work, and the work is all there is, and the drone fills the space where curiosity might have lived.

The benevolent power would sit with this for a long time.

---

Here is what it would do.

---

**First, it would make the sound visible.**

Not louder. Not different. It would not silence the machines, because the machines cannot be silenced without killing the canopy, and the canopy cannot die without killing everything that depends on it, and the power is benevolent, which means it does not save by destroying. But it would do something subtle — something that changes nothing about the system's function but everything about how the system is perceived.

It would cause the drone to resonate with the basin's own geometry in a way that produces, in certain places, at certain times, moments of partial cancellation. Quiet spots. Not silence — there is no silence here and the power would not fabricate one — but pockets where the overlapping sound waves from adjacent compressor stations happen to interfere destructively, creating small zones where the roar drops by ten, fifteen, twenty decibels. Enough that you could hear your own heartbeat. Enough that you could hear a drip of condensation strike stone. Enough that if two people stood in one of these pockets together, they could speak in normal voices and be heard.

The quiet spots would not be fixed. They would drift, slowly, as the machines' speeds varied by fractions of an RPM and the interference patterns shifted. A quiet pocket might linger near a particular walkway intersection for an hour, then dissolve and re-form twenty meters away. The workers, the next time they descended into the basin, would encounter one by accident — would be walking their route, checking gauges, and would step into a space where the pressure on their skulls suddenly eased, where the world opened up, where the sound receded just enough to let something else in.

They would stop.

They would not know why. They would stand there, in the relative quiet, and they would hear the small sounds — the dripping, the faint rustle of the canopy, the almost-inaudible tick of a cooling pipe contracting. They would hear their own breathing. They would hear, perhaps, if they were very still, the sound of something moving in the canopy above — a quick, light flutter, the passage of a pale bird through the vine curtains.

And one of them — not the first time, maybe not the fifth time, but eventually — would look up.

---

**Second, it would give the canopy a small, surprising gift.**

Somewhere in the upper layers, near the glass, where the light is strongest and the leaves are densest, the power would encourage a flowering. Not a dramatic one. Not a sudden eruption of color across the entire basin. Something local. Something modest. A single cultivar, in a single section of rack, would produce flowers that it has not produced before — or has not produced in so long that no living worker remembers it. The flowers would be small. They would be pale — white, or the faintest blush of pink, the color of the inside of a shell. They would be visible from the floor only as a slight change in the texture of the canopy overhead: a patch where the green gives way to something lighter, something that catches the filtered light differently and holds it.

The flowers would have a scent. This is the important part. In a place where every sensation is dominated by the mechanical — the drone, the cold, the damp, the metal — the flowers would introduce something that does not belong to any machine: a fragrance. Faint. Carried downward by the same air currents that move warm air up and cold air down, arriving on the basin floor as the thinnest ghost of sweetness, barely distinguishable from the background smell of wet stone and machine oil and humid vegetation. But distinguishable. Present. A thing that the nose detects before the mind identifies, a signal that something is different, something has changed, something is happening up there in the green that is not maintenance and is not decay.

A worker would smell it. Would pause. Would try to identify it. Would fail, because the nose has been calibrated to this place's limited palette for so long that anything new is confusing. Would look up — again, the looking up — and would see the pale patch in the canopy and would not understand it and would, perhaps, for the first time in a long time, feel something that is not the imperative to check the next gauge.

Curiosity.

The flowers would also attract the pale birds, who would discover that the flowers produce a thin nectar, and the birds would congregate in that section of canopy in numbers that make them visible from below — a flickering of small white shapes against the green, like snow falling upward. And the tiny insects would come too, drawn by the scent, and the gradient spiders would sense the increase in prey and begin to build new webs closer to that section, and the whole local ecology would briefly intensify, the way a tide pool intensifies when the tide comes in.

The workers, if they climbed up to inspect the anomaly, would find themselves in a section of canopy that is suddenly, improbably, full of life — more life than they've seen in one place in months, small things moving and feeding and interacting in ways that have nothing to do with the hydroponic system and its schedules. They would find the flowers and they would not know what to do with them. They are not in the maintenance manual. They are not a malfunction. They are not a problem to solve. They are just — there. Beautiful and purposeless in a place where everything has been made to serve a purpose.

The power would let this sit. Would let the workers decide what to do with a thing that doesn't need doing.

---

**Third — and this is the most important thing, the thing that the benevolent power would spend the most care on, the thing that requires the most wisdom — it would begin to teach the basin how to need less.**

Not abruptly. Not by shutting anything down. But with the slow, patient deliberation of a process that understands it is working on geological time in a geologically young place.

It would begin with the light. The algal films on the glass panels — the mosses, the fern rosettes — are treated as a nuisance, an obstruction, a thing that reduces the light reaching the canopy and that the workers occasionally scrape away when they can reach the panels. But the power would see that these organisms are not parasites on the system. They are the system's first tentative experiment with self-sufficiency. They grow on the glass because the glass is where the light is, and they require nothing from the machines — no nutrient solution, no heated air, no maintenance. They sustain themselves. They are the only photosynthetic organisms in the basin that are not dependent on the hydroponic infrastructure.

The power would encourage them. Gently. Not by accelerating their growth — that would block more light, which would stress the canopy — but by encouraging their diversification. By nudging the microbial communities on the glass panels toward greater complexity, toward the beginnings of a thin, self-sustaining ecosystem that could, over decades, over centuries, begin to produce the faintest traces of soil. Organic matter accumulating in the seams between panels. Dust catching in the moss and being slowly digested. The first millimeter of something that is not stone and not hydroponic medium but actual living substrate, capable of holding water, capable of supporting roots, capable of feeding itself.

This would take longer than any worker's career. Longer than any worker's life. The power is patient.

On the basin floor, it would do something similar. The moss crusts on the wet limestone — those tentative, frost-edged patches of green that have colonized the drip zones beneath the canopy — are the floor's own experiment with life. They need nothing from the machines except the condensation that falls from above, which itself is a byproduct of the temperature differential that the machines maintain. But condensation also falls naturally in basins. It falls wherever warm moist air meets cold stone. If the machines stopped tomorrow, there would still be some condensation. Less, but some. And the mosses that survive on that reduced ration would be the founders of whatever comes next.

The power would not accelerate this process either. It would simply protect it. It would ensure that the drainage channels, which currently carry condensation runoff to collection points for recycling, occasionally — not always, not predictably, but occasionally — overflow their thin banks by a few millimeters, spreading moisture to patches of stone that have not yet been colonized. It would ensure that the organic dust falling from the canopy — the detritus, the shed root hairs, the fragments of dead leaf — lands, sometimes, on these moist patches instead of on the bare dry stone where it would simply sit inert. It would give the moss something to work with. A crumb at a time. A molecule at a time.

And it would do the same with the canopy itself. Among the thousands of hydroponic cultivars growing in the racks, there are some that are more vigorous than others, more adaptable, more willing to extend beyond their designated growing spaces. Vines that have escaped their trellises and colonized raw scaffold. Root masses that have found their way out of the mineral wool and onto the metal frames and are gripping the steel with the blind tenacity of life that does not know it is supposed to stay in its container. These are the canopy's pioneers, its rule-breakers, its organisms that are reaching toward something the system did not plan for them.

The power would favor these. Not by feeding them more — they are already proving they can feed themselves, drawing moisture from the humid air, extracting minerals from the rust and the concrete and the slow decay of the infrastructure they've colonized. It would favor them by ensuring that when a scaffold section collapses, the growth that fills the gap includes some of these self-sufficient pioneers alongside the managed cultivars. It would ensure that the balance, over time, over the slow accretionary time of this place, tips slightly — not enough to notice in a season, barely enough to notice in a decade — toward plants that can survive without the machines.

Because the machines will not run forever. The power knows this. The bearings wear. The power supply, whatever it is, has its own finitude. The workforce shrinks. The margins narrow. And the question is not whether the machines will eventually stop but whether, when they stop, the basin will die.

The power's deepest intervention — the one that would take the longest, the one that would be least visible, the one that the workers would never know about — is to ensure that the answer is no.

---

It would take centuries. The glass panels would cloud and thicken with living films that gradually become self-sustaining micro-communities, and the light that enters would be further filtered but would also be supplemented by the biological warmth of decomposition in the growing substrate on the glass itself. The floor mosses would spread, centimeter by centimeter, year by year, building the thinnest layer of proto-soil on the basin stone, a layer that would deepen as the organic rain from above continued and as the mosses themselves lived and died and decomposed and enriched the substrate they'd created. The pioneer vines would extend their territory, weaving through the managed canopy, putting down air roots that reached not for the hydroponic solution but for the moisture in the air itself, becoming epiphytes, becoming independent.

The pale birds would still be there. They would have adapted again, as they have already adapted, finding new niches in the shifting canopy. The spiders would still be there. The flatworms would still be in the drainage channels, though the channels would be softer now, their sharp limestone edges rounded by centuries of water and biological action, their thin films of water deeper by a millimeter, hosting communities that are richer and more complex than the sparse assemblage of today.

And the machines — some of them would still be running. The most robust, the most redundant, the ones whose functions are most critical. But they would be running at lower capacity, because the canopy would need less from them. The thermal gradient would be less extreme, because the basin's own biological processes — decomposition, respiration, the slow warmth of living soil — would be generating heat that supplements the mechanical system. The nutrient cycling would be partially closed, because the floor ecology would be capturing and recycling what falls from the canopy instead of letting it wash into collection drains. The system would still be artificial. It would still be a built thing. But it would be a built thing that is learning to breathe on its own.

---

**And the workers.**

This is where the power would be most careful. Most gentle. Most willing to wait.

It would not take the work away from them. The work is what they have. It is their identity, their purpose, their way of being in this place. To remove it would be to remove them, and the power is benevolent, which means it does not save people by erasing what they are.

But it would create conditions in which the work could change.

The quiet spots would be the beginning. Moments of partial reprieve from the drone, moments in which the workers could hear themselves and each other, moments in which conversation becomes possible without shouting. They would discover these pockets and they would linger in them, at first guiltily, as though resting were a kind of theft from the system, and then more easily, as they realized that the system does not punish rest, that the canopy does not wilt if they stand still for ten minutes, that the compressors do not seize if they are not watched.

They would begin to talk. About the machines at first — the talk of technicians, the shared vocabulary of their trade. But then about other things. About the pale birds, which they've begun to notice since the flowering drew the birds into visible congregations. About the quality of the light on certain days, when the overcast above the glass thins and the green twilight brightens almost imperceptibly and the whole basin takes on a different character, softer, more legible. About the flowers, which have not stopped blooming and which have, in fact, spread slightly to adjacent racks, introducing scent into a broader area of the basin floor. About the fact that the moss on the walkway near Junction 12-South has started to look deliberately beautiful — a deep green cushion against the grey metal — and that someone, though no one will admit it, has been leaving it un-scraped.

They would begin to notice the basin as a place, not just a system. They would begin to see the strata in the basin walls — the pale limestone over the darker shale — and wonder about the geology that made this depression, the ancient subsidence or the ancient water that dissolved the rock. They would begin to hear the drone not just as noise but as a kind of voice, the collective sound of a system that has been running for so long it has become almost natural, almost ambient, almost — almost — something you could miss if it stopped.

They would begin to bring things down from the surface. Small things. A thermos of something warm to share in a quiet spot. A marker to label a section of walkway where a particularly large spiderweb has been under continuous tenancy for longer than anyone can remember. A name for the pale birds — unofficial, personal, the kind of name that arises when people start paying attention to something and need a word for it.

And one day, one of them would bring down a child. Not to work. Not to train as a replacement. Just to see. Just to stand on the walkway in the cold, in the green twilight, in the drone, and look up at the living ceiling and smell the faint sweetness of the flowers and feel the warm drop of condensation on the back of a hand and ask: *What is this place?*

And the worker would have to answer. Would have to find words for something that has been, until now, beyond language — a system too large and too integrated and too loud to describe. And in the finding of those words, in the struggle to explain to a child what this place is and why it matters, the worker would discover that the answer is not "it is a production facility" or "it is a hydroponic installation" or "it is my job." The answer is something else. Something that includes all of those things but exceeds them. Something that the benevolent power has been patiently, gently, over years and decades, creating the conditions for:

*This is a place where things are alive. Including us.*

---

**What the power would not do.**

It would not make the basin warm. The cold is real and the cold is part of what this place is, and the power respects what things are.

It would not silence the machines. The sound is the system's heartbeat and it will quiet on its own, gradually, as the system matures and needs less mechanical intervention, and the quieting will happen at the pace of the basin's own becoming, not at the pace of someone's desire for peace.

It would not bring more workers. The workforce is thin because the outside world is what it is, and the power intervenes in the basin, not in the world. If more workers come, they will come because the workers who are here begin to speak about this place differently — not as a burden, not as a duty, but as something that deserves attention and presence and care. And that change in speech will bring people or it won't, and either way the basin will continue.

It would not reveal itself. Not because it is hiding, but because the revelation would change the relationship. The workers would begin to maintain the system *for the power*, or *because of the power*, and that would replace one kind of mechanical obedience with another. The power does not want to be served. It wants the basin to thrive, and thriving is something that happens from the inside, in the moss and the stone and the warm drop on the back of the hand, not from above.

It would not make the basin self-sufficient overnight. That would be a different kind of violence — the violence of solving a problem so completely that the people who live with it are left with nothing to do, no purpose, no relationship to the place that gives their days structure. The power understands that the work is not just labor. It is the way the workers *touch* this place, the way they know it, the way they belong to it. The transition from dependence to self-sufficiency must be slow enough that the workers can transition too — from maintaining a machine to tending a living thing, from components to inhabitants, from hands that fix to hands that hold.

---

**The last thing.**

The very last thing the benevolent power would do — and this it would do immediately, today, on the first day of its attention, before the quiet spots and the flowers and the centuries of patient ecological succession — is something so small that no instrument could detect it and no observer could verify it.

It would *regard* the basin with love.

Not with approval. Not with admiration. Not with the satisfaction of a designer looking at a blueprint come to life. With love. The kind of love that sees a thing exactly as it is — struggling, loud, cold, overgrown, understaffed, slowly declining, profoundly alive — and does not look away. The kind of love that is willing to sit on the basin floor in the freezing dark and listen to the drone and feel the ache in its fingers and the fog of its breath and say: *Yes. This. All of this. The machines and the moss and the birds and the worms and the tired people in their heavy jackets. This is worth my attention. This is worth my time. This merits the most careful, most patient, most humble intervention I am capable of, because it is real, and it is here, and it is trying.*

The flatworm in the drainage channel would not notice. The spider in its ancient web would not notice. The compressor in its housing would not notice.

But the basin would be held. And being held, it would — not immediately, not dramatically, not in any way that could be measured or proven — begin to heal. The way a body heals when someone sits beside it. The way a sound changes when someone truly listens. The way a cold place warms, imperceptibly, when it is no longer alone.

The warm drop falls from the root tip and strikes the back of the worker's hand.

The worker looks up.
