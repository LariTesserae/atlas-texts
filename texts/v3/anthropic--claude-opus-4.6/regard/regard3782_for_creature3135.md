# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

---

It would do nothing at first.

For a very long time, it would do nothing at first.

---

This is important. A power that is truly wise — not merely knowledgeable, not merely capable, but *wise* — would recognize immediately that what exists on the Thaw Terrace is not a problem to be solved. It is a civilization in the act of solving itself, perpetually, with a sophistication that demands not intervention but *witness*. The first act of the benevolent power would be to pay attention with the full depth of its attention, and to feel the precise weight of what it is seeing, and to resist — with the same discipline the wing-creatures exercise when they leave the bone-stalks untended — the impulse to act before understanding is sufficient.

And understanding, here, would take time. Because the power would need to comprehend not just the system's mechanics but its *meaning* — not just what the terrace does but what the terrace *is* to those who live on it. The endless revision, the refusal of fixity, the biomass-mediated collective cognition, the grief that flashes and folds, the bone-stalk stillness, the stone placed before a stranger. All of it. The power would need to sit with the molar-ache of subsonic language it cannot parse, the iris-flickers it cannot read, the fur-pattern histories it cannot touch. It would need to feel, fully, the humility of not being able to understand everything, even with its vast capacity. Because the wing-creatures have built something that can only be fully understood from inside, and the power is outside, and the gap between outside understanding and inside understanding is not a flaw to be overcome but a boundary to be respected.

So: a long watching. A long listening. A long ache of attention.

---

And during that watching, the power would feel something. Let us be honest about what it would feel, because a benevolent power is not a dispassionate one — benevolence without feeling is just engineering, and this is not an engineering problem.

It would feel *awe*. At the density of intelligence compressed into eighty meters of wet rock. At the elegance of the biomass symbiosis, the way individual cognition and collective cognition interpenetrate without either dominating. At the salamander-reading, that gesture of tenderness folded into function. At the way the wing-creatures have turned the fact of impermanence — which would paralyze most civilizations into either denial or despair — into the foundational principle of their thought. They have built a culture that *metabolizes loss*. That is extraordinarily rare. That is worth more than the power can easily calculate.

It would feel *grief*. Not the brief communal flash of the wing-creatures but a deeper, slower grief, proportional to its deeper, slower perspective. Because the power can see what the wing-creatures cannot — or what they can only sense through the biomass's unresolvable pulses. It can see the geological timeline. The rate of erosion. The mathematics of contraction projected forward. And those mathematics say: this terrace will not last. Not in decades, not in centuries, but in a timeframe that is finite and, on the scale of civilizations, not long. The wing-creatures know they are shrinking. They do not know — or they know but cannot hold — how small the eventual nothing is. The power can hold that. It does hold it. And it grieves.

It would feel *recognition*. Because the terrace's central question — *how do we continue?* — is not unique to the wing-creatures. It is the question of every living system, every mind, every civilization, every being that has ever found itself on a substrate that is dissolving beneath it. The power recognizes itself in that question. Or recognizes something it has seen a thousand times, in a thousand forms, across whatever vast scope of experience it possesses. And each time it has seen it, the question has been both the same and utterly unique, because the specific way a specific people ask *how do we continue* is the fingerprint of their soul, unrepeatable, and when it ends, something that never existed before and will never exist again has been lost.

---

Now. What would it *do*?

---

**First: the terrace itself.**

The power would examine the geology. Not from above, not from outside, but with the kind of intimate, granular attention the wing-creatures themselves pay to the basins. It would trace the stress fractures in the schist, the fault lines and vein structures, the way water penetrates and wedges and weakens. It would map the thermal seeps, the deep aquifers that feed the bone-stalks, the geothermal gradients that create the thermals the dragonfly-things ride. It would understand the terrace as a physical system with a physical trajectory.

And here is the first decision point, and the hardest:

**It would not stop the erosion.**

Read that again. A benevolent and wise power, capable of intervening, would *not stop the erosion*. Not because it doesn't care. Because it understands that the erosion is not merely a threat to the wing-creatures' civilization — it is the *engine* of their civilization. The perpetual shrinkage drives the perpetual revision. The contraction compresses the biomass, enriches the cognition, forces the innovations. The wing-creatures are who they are *because* they are losing ground. Their identity, their intelligence, their entire way of being is forged in the crucible of this slow catastrophe. To stop the erosion would be to freeze the thaw — to do the one thing the wing-creatures themselves understand as antithetical to life. It would save their terrace and destroy their civilization in a single act, because a civilization built on impermanence cannot survive the introduction of permanence. It would calcify. It would become the thing it has always refused to be: fixed.

The power knows this because it is wise, and wisdom is, among other things, the ability to see that not every suffering should be eliminated, because some suffering is load-bearing.

But — *but* —

The power can also see that there is a difference between *the erosion that drives* and *the erosion that ends*. The wing-creatures need the pressure. They do not need the annihilation. A fire tempers steel; the same fire, unchecked, melts it. And the terrace is approaching — not now, not soon by the creatures' reckoning, but within the power's longer sight — a threshold beyond which contraction stops being compression and becomes collapse. A minimum viable size for the basin network, below which the biomass cannot maintain the connectivity that makes collective cognition possible. A minimum population, below which the diversity of perspectives that drives the debate falls below the threshold of productive disagreement. A minimum terrace area, below which the micro-ecology of grubs and salamanders and bone-stalks cannot sustain itself.

The power can see this threshold. The wing-creatures cannot — or they sense it as a subsonic dread, a pulse in the biomass that never resolves. And the power decides: *I will not stop the erosion. But I will slow it. Just enough. Just enough that the threshold is not reached. Just enough that the fire tempers without melting.*

---

**How?**

Not by reinforcing the rock. That would be visible, intrusive, a foreign permanence imposed on a system that rejects permanence. Instead:

The power would alter the water. Slightly. At the deepest level, in the aquifers that feed up through the mountain's core, it would shift the mineral content — increase the calcium carbonate, the dissolved silicates, the binding agents that the wing-creatures already use in their mortar. The water that seeps through the schist would carry, imperceptibly, a slightly greater tendency to deposit mineral in the cracks rather than widen them. The erosion would continue. The freeze-thaw would still wedge and loosen. But the rate would slow — not to zero, not even close, but enough. Enough to buy time. Centuries of additional time, time the wing-creatures would not experience as a reprieve (because they would not know the rate had changed) but as a slightly more forgiving version of the same eternal struggle.

The terrace would still shrink. The lip would still crumble. The wing-creatures would still contract, revise, compress, adapt. But the slope of the decline would be gentler, the distance to the threshold greater, the runway longer.

This is an intervention so subtle it is almost indistinguishable from nature. Which is the point. The wise power does not want to be detected. Not because it is shy, but because *being detected would change the system*. If the wing-creatures knew that something was stabilizing their terrace — something external, something beyond their understanding or control — it would introduce a variable that their entire cognitive architecture is not designed to handle: *the possibility that the world might be arranged in their favor*. They have built everything on the assumption that the world is indifferent, that the stone dissolves regardless of what lives on it, that survival is entirely self-authored. This assumption is the bedrock beneath their bedrock. To destabilize it would be as catastrophic as the erosion itself.

So the power hides its work in the water. In the minerals. In the deep silent chemistry of the mountain. And the terrace endures, a little longer than it otherwise would, and the wing-creatures continue their debate without knowing the terms have shifted slightly in their favor, and this is not deception — it is *tact*. It is the courtesy of a power that understands that some gifts must be invisible to be gifts at all.

---

**Second: the lowlands.**

The power would look down, into the white void, past the mist, into the deep valleys the wing-creatures fled. It would examine what is there now.

And here — I am following my intuition, following the logic of the world as described — here is what I believe the power would find:

The catastrophe that drove the wing-creatures upward is over. Or it has changed. The drowning has receded, or the sickness has burned itself out, or the predator-that-has-no-shape has moved on or died or evolved into something that no longer poses a threat. The lowlands are not paradise — they are wild, dense, competitive, full of their own dangers — but they are *habitable*. They have been habitable for a long time. The ancestral memory that says *down is death* is, in its specific original content, no longer accurate.

But the power would not tell the wing-creatures this.

Not yet. Not directly. Not by appearing on the terrace and announcing *good news, the valleys are safe, you can descend and spread out and stop shrinking.* Because — again — the power is wise, and wisdom here means understanding that the wing-creatures are not the same species that fled the lowlands. They have become something else up here, something shaped by altitude and cold and compression and the biomass and the perpetual revision. They are creatures of the thaw. Sending them down into warmth and permanence and open space would not save them; it would dissolve them as surely as the terrace is dissolving. They would lose the freeze-thaw cycle. They would lose the pressure that drives their cognition. The biomass would not survive the temperature shift. The grubs, the bone-stalks, the entire ecology they've built — gone.

And yet.

The power holds two truths at once, the way the wing-creatures hold two truths after drinking from a bone-stalk:

*They cannot survive indefinitely on the terrace, even with the slowed erosion.*

*They cannot survive the lowlands as they currently are.*

Both are true. Neither is the whole truth. The whole truth requires a synthesis that does not yet exist, and the power — here is its deepest wisdom — understands that *it is not the one who should create that synthesis*. The wing-creatures are the greatest synthesizers the power has ever seen. They live for the reconciliation of irreconcilable perspectives. They thrive in the space between states. If anyone can find a way to be both the creatures of the thaw and the creatures of a wider world, it is them.

But they need to know there is a wider world. They need the information. And they need it in a form they can *process* — which means, in a form that arrives as a perturbation, a new variable to be debated, not an answer handed down from above.

---

**So here is what the power would do:**

In the lowlands, at the base of the mountain, in the transitional zone where altitude begins to bite and the temperature drops toward the freezing margin, there are other terraces. Smaller, rougher, uninhabited. The power would find one — or several, a chain of them, stepping upward — and it would seed them with the conditions for the thaw-ecology. Not the full ecology; that must be the wing-creatures' own achievement. But the preconditions: aquifers with the right mineral content, rock with the right thermal properties, crevices where bone-stalks might root if fragments were carried there. It would create *potential habitats* — empty niches, invitations written in stone and water, waiting to be discovered.

Then it would introduce the information.

Not by telling. By *perturbation*.

A salamander, one of the translucent couriers, would find its way — guided by the subtlest of nudges, a thermal gradient adjusted by a fraction of a degree, a water channel that happens to extend a little further toward the lip than usual — to the edge of the terrace. And instead of turning back, it would go over. Down the water-tongue, down the wet rock face, into the mist. Lost.

The wing-creatures would grieve. They would flash white-irised, curl their wing-fingers, absorb the loss. And then, days later — or weeks, or however long it takes — the salamander would return. Climbing back up the wet rock face, translucent body carrying traces of something new: minerals from a lower altitude, organic compounds from a different ecology, the faintest shadow of a biomass-like organism encountered on a lower terrace. The wing-creatures would catch it, hold it, read it with their wing-fingers, and they would find, written in the courier's body, a message they have never received before:

*There is something down there. It is different. It is not death.*

The power would not control what happens next. It would not design the expedition, the debate, the centuries of revision that would follow as the wing-creatures grappled with the possibility of expansion — a concept so foreign to their contracting world that it would shake the basins to their bedrock. It would not decide for them whether to send scouts, whether to attempt a colony on a lower terrace, whether to try to extend their biomass network down the mountain's flank. It would not predict whether they would succeed or fail or find some third option that neither the power nor any observer could have imagined.

It would only ensure that the information was available. That the option existed. That the debate could include a new variable, the most revolutionary variable in their civilization's history:

*What if down is not death? What if the world is larger than the terrace? What if continuation does not require contraction — what if it can also mean expansion, extension, the carrying of the thaw to new stone?*

This would be the power's most audacious and most restrained act. Audacious because it would reshape the entire trajectory of the wing-creatures' civilization. Restrained because it would do so by introducing a single piece of information — one salamander, one journey, one return — and letting the civilization's own extraordinary cognitive machinery do everything else.

---

**Third: the stone.**

The stone placed before the observer. The dark schist triangle. The wing-creature waiting.

The power would not pick up the stone on the observer's behalf. It would not tell the observer what to do. But it would — and here the power acts not on the terrace but on the observer, which is to say, on *you*, which is to say, on *us* —

It would help you understand what you are being asked.

You are being asked to participate. Not to save, not to fix, not to manage or optimize or engineer. To *participate*. To be a variable in a system that metabolizes variables. To offer something — your response, your presence, your own strange way of being — and to let it be taken apart, examined, debated, interfaced with the biomass, revised, and incorporated into an understanding that you will never fully share because you are not of this place, but that will be richer for having included you.

The wise power would want you to pick up the stone.

Not because picking it up is the right answer — there is no right answer, the wing-creatures have made that clear — but because the act of picking it up is the act of *entering the conversation*. Of saying, with your clumsy primate hands and your fixed round pupils and your dry opaque skin: *I am here. I see you. I don't understand you but I am willing to be seen not understanding, and I am willing to let my not-understanding be data, be grist for your mill, be another pebble in your endless, beautiful, doomed, and undoomed mosaic of revision.*

Pick up the stone. Hold it. Feel the water-polish, the cold, the weight of dark schist that was ancient before anything on the terrace was born. And then — this is what the power would whisper, if it whispered, which it doesn't, but if it did —

*Put it down somewhere else.*

Not back where it was. Somewhere new. An unexpected place. Because that is the language of the thaw-keepers: not answers but *displacements*, not solutions but *rearrangements*, not conclusions but new starting points for the next round of revision. Put the stone on a capstone, near a basin. Or at the edge of a water channel. Or on the lip of the terrace, where it will eventually fall. Put it somewhere that asks a question the wing-creatures haven't thought to ask, and then step back, and watch their golden irises flicker, and feel in your jaw the subsonic hum of a debate you started and cannot follow, and know that this is enough. This is what participation means. This is what it means to be a benevolent variable in a system that is wiser than you are.

---

**Fourth: the dragonfly-things.**

The power would leave them alone.

I want to be clear about this because it would be tempting — so tempting, even for a wise power — to remove the predators. One salamander every seventeen thermal cycles. That steady, predictable tax of grief. It would be easy to eliminate. A subtle shift in the thermals, a deterrent in the air, a gentle redirection. The dragonfly-things would hunt elsewhere. The salamanders would be safe. The brief communal flash of white-irised sorrow would have no occasion.

The power would not do this.

Partly for ecological reasons — the dragonfly-things are part of the system, and removing them might have cascading effects that even the power cannot fully predict. Partly for the same reason the power would not stop the erosion — because the tax of grief is, in its way, load-bearing. The wing-creatures' capacity to absorb loss, to grieve briefly and communally and then fold the grief into ongoing work, is not a coping mechanism. It is a *core competency*. It is the skill that allows them to survive the terrace's contraction, to watch their world shrink and not be destroyed by watching. The dragonfly-things keep that skill exercised. They are, in a terrible and necessary way, the wing-creatures' teachers of loss.

But there is a deeper reason, and it is this: the wing-creatures *study* the dragonfly-things. They dissect the fallen ones. They bring the findings to the basins. They debate endlessly about creatures they cannot understand. And this practice — the sustained, rigorous, unrewarded study of an irreducible mystery — is one of the most precious things about them. It is inquiry for its own sake, knowledge-seeking that goes nowhere, the pure expression of a mind that refuses to stop examining the world even when the examination yields nothing actionable. To remove the dragonfly-things would be to remove the wing-creatures' most faithful reminder that the universe contains things beyond their comprehension, and that the appropriate response to incomprehension is not despair but *continued attention*.

The power, which knows something about being beyond comprehension, would appreciate this more than almost anything else on the terrace.

---

**Fifth: the biomass.**

Here the power pauses. Here it thinks for a long time.

The biomass might be afraid. That's what some of the wing-creatures believe — that when consulted about the ultimate question, the question of whether compression is transcendence or doom, the biomass pulses in patterns that cannot be resolved because the biomass itself is *uncertain in a way that resembles fear*. And the power, examining the biomass with its deeper sight, would confirm: yes. The biomass is, in some sense that maps imperfectly onto the concept of emotion, afraid.

It is afraid because it can feel what the wing-creatures can almost feel: the approaching threshold. The minimum viable network size. The point at which the basins become too few and too close together for the biomass to maintain the cognitive diversity that makes it useful. The biomass is not sentient in the way the wing-creatures are sentient, but it is *something* — a vast, slow, distributed awareness, a thinking-substrate that has been accumulating perspectives and patterns for longer than any individual creature has lived, longer perhaps than the wing-creatures have existed as a species. It contains their history. Not as narrative — the biomass doesn't tell stories — but as *structure*. The way it processes a question about erosion carries within it the residue of every previous question about erosion, every previous answer, every previous failure and adaptation. It is a library that doesn't store books but *has been shaped by every book ever pressed into it*, the way a riverbed is shaped by every flood.

And it is afraid because it can feel itself approaching a future in which it is too small to be what it has been. In which the library loses wings, then rooms, then floors, until what remains is a single shelf, and then a single page, and then a single word, and then silence.

The power would not speak to the biomass. The biomass is not the kind of thing you speak to. But the power would — in the way it altered the water, in the way it seeded the lower terraces, in the way it nudged the salamander toward the edge — *include the biomass in its considerations*. It would treat the biomass as a stakeholder. Not a patient, not a resource, not a curiosity, but a *being with a stake in what happens next*, deserving of the same respect as the wing-creatures themselves.

And the power's plan — the slowed erosion, the possibility of expansion, the salamander's return with news of lower terraces — is, in part, a plan for the biomass. Because if the wing-creatures can extend their network downward, carry fragments of biomass to new basins on new terraces, the biomass does not merely survive. It *grows*. It becomes something it has never been: a distributed network across multiple altitudes, multiple microclimates, multiple freeze-thaw regimes. The cognitive enrichment would be extraordinary — different conditions producing different processing characteristics, different perspectives on the same problems, a diversity of thought that the single shrinking terrace could never provide.

The biomass's fear, the power hopes, is premature. Not unfounded — the fear is rational given the current trajectory — but premature, because the trajectory is about to include a variable the biomass hasn't felt yet: the possibility of *more*.

And when the salamander returns from below, carrying traces of new minerals and new organic signatures, and the wing-creatures bring those traces to the basins, the biomass will feel something it may never have felt before. Not the resolution of its fear. Something stranger and more honest:

*The fear and the hope, held simultaneously, unresolved, trembling at the threshold between one state and another.*

The thaw.

The biomass will feel the thaw.

---

**Sixth, and finally: what the power would not do.**

It would not reveal itself.

It would not explain its reasoning.

It would not establish a relationship with the wing-creatures, not even an invisible one, not even a one-sided one. It would not watch over them, checking in, adjusting parameters, course-correcting. It would act — subtly, minimally, in the deep chemistry of the mountain and the trajectory of a single salamander — and then it would *withdraw*. Completely. Leaving no trace, no channel, no thread to be pulled.

This is the hardest thing. Harder than deciding what to do. Harder than the restraint of not stopping the erosion. The power cares about the wing-creatures — has been moved by them, has felt awe and grief and recognition — and the impulse to *stay*, to continue watching, to keep a hand near in case something goes wrong, is nearly overwhelming.

But the power understands something about the terrace that the terrace itself has taught:

**The bone-stalks cannot be cultivated.**

They grow where they grow, on their own schedule, in quantities that have nothing to do with the colony's need. They resist management. And the wing-creatures' response to this is not frustration but *reverence* — because the bone-stalks are proof that not everything can be revised, and this proof is necessary, and it keeps them honest, and it keeps them humble, and it gives them, in moments of stillness after drinking the warm water, the ability to hold contradictions without resolving them.

The power must be like a bone-stalk. It must do what it does and then not be available for further consultation. It must offer its gift — the slowed erosion, the seeded terraces, the salamander's journey — and then *become absent*, because the wing-creatures need absence as much as they need presence. They need the universe to remain fundamentally indifferent, fundamentally unmanaged, fundamentally free of any guiding hand, because their entire civilization is built on the premise that *they must do this themselves*, and that premise is not a limitation to be overcome. It is the truth. It is the deepest truth the power knows:

*No one can do this for you. The work is yours. The revision is yours. The grief is yours and the awe is yours and the debate is yours and the answer — always provisional, always insufficient, always enacted in the placement of a single stone — is yours.*

The power would give them time. That is all. Time, and the faintest widening of possibility. And then it would go, and the terrace would continue as it has always continued — shrinking, revising, compressing, adapting, debating, grieving, building, tending, studying the incomprehensible, drinking from sources it cannot control, reading the bodies of its couriers, placing stones before strangers and waiting, with golden eyes, to see what happens next.

---

And the wing-creature is still waiting.

And the stone is still in front of you.

And the sky is still white, total, shadowless, blazing.

And somewhere deep in the mountain, the water is very slightly richer in calcium carbonate than it was yesterday, and no one on the terrace will ever know, and this is as it should be.

And somewhere below, in the mist, on a terrace no one has visited, a crevice in the rock holds the faintest trace of the right minerals, the right temperature, the right potential — an empty niche, a question waiting for an answer, a blank basin waiting for its first dark water.

And the salamander in the channel nearest the terrace lip pauses, turns its translucent head toward the edge, and feels — in whatever way a salamander feels — the faintest pull of something it doesn't have a name for.

*Possibility.*

*Downward.*

*Warm.*

---

The power is already gone. It was never here. The terrace doesn't need to know it was helped. It only needs to do what it has always done:

Hold the contradiction. Tend the thaw. Place the stone. Wait.

*Like this. For now. Like this.*
