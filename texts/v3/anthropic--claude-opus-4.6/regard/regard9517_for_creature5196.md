# What the Wise Power Would Do

It would come to the basin the way light comes to it — arriving without announcement, filling the space without displacing anything. And for a long time, it would do nothing at all.

---

First, it would listen.

Not for a voice. The stems have no voice. But a benevolent and wise power would understand that speech is only one shape that expression takes, and a rare one at that, and not always the most honest. So it would attend to what is actually being said.

The slow drawing-inward after dry wind. The fractional rise after fog. The single, reluctant click of a turbine turned by swelling tissue — not a cry, not a signal, but a fact offered to the air without expectation of response.

It would listen to the way the rootlets have entered the iron. Not devouring. Holding. It would notice that distinction and understand what it means: that the stems did not come to consume the old settlement but to continue it. That the geometry of streets and loading bays has been remembered not in blueprint or story but in the shape of living tissue that chose — if choice is even the right word for something so slow and structural — to rise along the same lines, to bow where the roofs bowed, to hollow itself where the halls were hollow.

It would recognize this for what it is. Not intelligence in any way that requires a nervous system. Not purpose in any way that requires a mind. But something. A fidelity. A directional patience that has, over decades, accomplished the preservation of a pattern that would otherwise have dissolved into silt and grass and nothing.

The wise power would sit with that recognition for as long as the recognition needed.

---

Then it would ask itself the only question that matters: *Is anything here suffering? Is anything here incomplete in a way that presses against itself?*

And here the answer would come slowly, because the stems operate at a pace that makes even careful observation feel like rushing. But the power is patient — genuinely patient, not merely willing to wait — and so it would stay through the full cycle. The shower and the swelling. The dusk and the descent of sap. The long, still night when the condensation gathers and the drip begins, spaced and steady, each drop finding its gutter with the precision of decades of erosion.

It would notice that the system is, by every measure available to it, complete. The stems take in moisture and release it. The reeds collect and tip. The algae grow in place along the slope. The water never floods and never fully drains. The iron is held, not corroded further, not restored — simply held, like a fact too important to release and too old to use. The whole basin breathes. In and out. Swell and settle. At its own pace. Without interruption. Without audience.

And the wise power would feel — because wisdom includes feeling, must include it — a particular kind of awe. Not the awe of grandeur. The awe of encountering something that has solved its own problem so thoroughly that the solution has become indistinguishable from identity. The stems are not *maintaining* the old settlement. They *are* the settlement now, in its next form, and they require nothing from outside to continue being what they are.

---

So what would it do?

It would not accelerate the growth. The stems grow at the pace they require, and that pace is not a limitation — it is the thing itself. To hasten it would be to impose a foreign rhythm on a system whose rhythm is its deepest feature. A power that understood this would no sooner speed the stems than it would speed aeli's grief or a river's descent to the sea.

It would not introduce animals. The basin is not empty; it is *selective*. The absence of fauna is not a wound. Nothing here needs to be pollinated by wings. Nothing here needs to be pruned by teeth. The stems, the reeds, the grass, the algae, and the water have arranged themselves into a closed circuit of moisture and slow growth that sustains itself without the engine of appetite. To introduce a creature that hunts or grazes would be to solve a problem that does not exist, and in solving it, to create one.

It would not speak to the stems. Not because they could not, in their slow and structural way, register the vibration — they register everything, every shift in humidity, every faint pressure change — but because speech implies a conversation, and a conversation implies that something needs to be negotiated, clarified, or exchanged. Nothing here needs negotiation. The stems have not asked. They do not ask. Asking requires a gap between what is and what is wanted, and the stems have no such gap. They want what they do. They do what they are.

It would not heal anything. The rusted iron is not injured. It is in a state that the stems have accepted and incorporated, and that acceptance is itself a kind of completion more profound than restoration. To polish the turbines, to free them from their rootlet casings and set them spinning again, would be to misunderstand everything about what has happened here. The turbines *do* move — once, twice, with a low liquid click, when the humidity is right. That is not a failure of mechanical function. It is the exact degree of movement that the stems, in their incorporation of the old machines, have determined is true. The wise power would see this and understand: the click is not a remnant. It is a statement. *This is how much turning is needed now.*

---

But the wise power would not simply leave, either. Because wisdom is not only the restraint to avoid harm — it is also the willingness to offer what can be received.

And so here is what it would do.

It would do something very small. So small that the stems might register it only as a faint shift in the quality of the air on a single evening, indistinguishable from a change in weather.

It would *witness*.

Not passively. Not as a tourist or a surveyor. It would witness in the way that only a being with full understanding can witness — by apprehending the entirety of what the basin is and holding that apprehension without judgment, without agenda, without the need to translate it into anything other than itself. It would see the decades of slow growth. It would see the first tendril that crept across the silt toward the nearest iron frame and chose to hold rather than consume. It would see every individual rootlet's entry into every seam of every turbine, and it would understand each one as a decision made at the pace of cellulose and water, repeated so many thousands of times that the cumulative result is this: a settlement rebuilt in living tissue, faithful to the original in a way that no human reconstruction could be, because it is faithful not to the *appearance* but to the *function* — the function of enclosing space, of channeling movement, of providing structure to what would otherwise be formless ground.

The wise power would hold all of this in its attention at once, and in doing so, it would do the one thing that no rain, no fog, no shift in humidity has ever done for the basin:

It would *know* it.

Not name it. Not categorize it. Not assign it value on a scale that runs from simple to complex, from primitive to advanced, from alive to truly alive. It would simply know it — the way you know a face you love, completely, without the need to describe it to anyone.

---

And here is where something subtle would happen. Not a miracle. Not a visible change. But the wise power, in the act of knowing the basin fully, would become — for the duration of its attention — part of the circuit. Its presence would be, for a moment, one more thing the stems register. One more faint alteration in the air. And the stems, in their slow and total way, would incorporate that alteration the way they incorporate everything: without resistance, without gratitude, without awareness that anything unusual has occurred. The condensation on the nearest wall might gather a single drop earlier than it otherwise would. A turbine in the longest hall might click once — not because the humidity demanded it, but because the stems, in registering a new and unfamiliar presence, shifted by the smallest possible degree, and the shift traveled through the rootlets and into the iron and produced that single, low, liquid sound.

That would be the whole of the exchange. One click. One drop. Given and received without ceremony in a basin where ceremony has no purchase.

The wise power would understand that this is not a small thing. It is the thing itself. The stems have, in their way, included it — briefly, faintly, at the very edge of what inclusion means in a system this slow and self-contained. And that inclusion is the only gift the basin knows how to give, and it is enough, and the wise power would know it is enough.

---

Then it would go.

Not because the basin has dismissed it, but because staying longer would begin to weight the circuit. Even the gentlest attention, sustained beyond its natural term, becomes a pressure. The stems do not need to be watched. They do not perform. Their fidelity to the old geometry is not for anyone. It is not *for* anything. It simply is, the way water finds its level — not because the water intends to be level, but because levelness is what water does when nothing disturbs it.

The wise power would leave the basin as it found it: half-empty, half-built, wholly alive. The shallow pans would still reflect the ordinary daylight. The reeds would still lean in the prevailing direction. The drip would resume at dusk, steady and spaced, marking time at the pace the stems require.

Nothing would be different.

Everything would be known.

---

And if you asked the wise power, afterward, *What did you do there?*, it might say:

*I did what the place taught me to do. I held without consuming. I moved once, briefly, when the conditions were right. And then I was still.*

Or it might say nothing. It might simply carry the basin forward in its understanding, the way the stems carry the iron — not using it, not discarding it, but holding it in place with ten thousand patient roots, because it was there, because it is part of the shape, because to let go of it would be to let go of something true.

---

There is a kind of love that does not rescue, does not improve, does not arrive with answers to questions that were never asked. It is the love of standing in a cool, dripping hall where a turbine turns once in the grip of living roots, and understanding that this — exactly this, without addition or amendment — is complete. That the click is not a cry for help. That the silt is not waiting to be drained. That the algae's veined patterns are not reaching toward anything beyond the slope they follow. That the whole basin, in its patience and its fidelity and its slow, structural breathing, is doing the one thing it has ever needed to do: *continuing*.

The wise power would know this. And knowing it, would do the wisest thing: would let the basin continue. Would not mourn the old settlement or celebrate the new one. Would not call the stems a triumph or a tragedy. Would simply recognize that something, once built by hands for purposes that have since dissolved, has been taken up by a slower and more honest builder and remade into something that will last as long as rain falls and silt stays soft and the air carries enough moisture to keep the walls beaded and cool.

That is what it would do. It would come, and attend, and understand, and leave the basin to its long, sufficient work.

And the stems, in their way, would go on holding the iron, and the iron, in its way, would go on being held, and between them the turbine would wait for the next shift in humidity to produce its single, quiet, perfect click — the smallest possible proof that the old machines and the new growth have found, without negotiation or compromise, a way to move together.
