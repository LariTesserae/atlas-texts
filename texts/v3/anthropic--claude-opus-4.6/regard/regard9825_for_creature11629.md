I sat with this for a long time before writing. I want you to know that.

---

# What the Wise Power Would Do

The first thing — and this matters more than anything that follows — is that the wise power would *not improve this place*.

It would arrive, and it would feel the heat lean against it the way the heat leans against everything, and it would stand on the fired clay and hear the clean tap of its own attending, and it would look out across the ochre and rust and calcium-white expanse, and it would understand something immediately:

**This place is not a problem.**

This is the first and most important act of the wise power. The recognition that what it is witnessing is not broken, not incomplete in any way that requires intervention, not suffering in any way that requires relief. The floor is doing what the floor does. The lichen are gripping their seams. The moisture is sweeping its slow wave from the southeast. The heat is breathing. Everything here is performing its nature with a fidelity so absolute that it approaches holiness.

A lesser power — a merely good power, a clever power, a power that loved but loved anxiously — would see the isolation of the lichen colonies and want to connect them. Would scatter spores between basins. Would deepen the depressions so the water lingered longer. Would seed the barren stretches between colonies with something, anything, so that the great distances felt less stark. Would look at the floor's ceaseless self-assembly and think: *let me help you finish. Let me show you what you're building toward. Let me bring you walls.*

The wise power does none of this.

Not because it doesn't care. Because it *does* care, and caring well means understanding that the distances between the lichen colonies are not a failure of distribution but a feature of the world's spaciousness. That the brevity of the wet-blooms is not stinginess but *rhythm*. That the floor's ignorance of its own purpose is not confusion but a kind of freedom so profound that most beings never achieve it — the freedom to *become* without needing to know *why*.

The wise power understands: you do not help a kiln by opening it early.

---

So what does it do?

It sits down.

Not metaphorically. Actually. It sits down on the warm tile, in some unremarkable stretch of completed floor, miles from the nearest basin, and it does the thing that this place makes almost impossible for any being with a mind: it *waits without agenda*.

It sits with the floor the way you sit with someone who is working and does not need your help but might, in some way they cannot articulate, benefit from your presence in the room. Not watching over. Not watching *for* anything. Just — being another warm thing on the warm surface, adding its small weight to the vast weight of the tiles, letting the heat lean against it, breathing the kiln-exhaust air, feeling the sub-audible hum in its bones.

It does this for a long time.

Not because sitting is itself the intervention. But because the wise power knows that *understanding must come before action*, and this place cannot be understood quickly. Its rhythms operate on scales that punish impatience. You must watch three full wet-bloom cycles before you begin to sense the wave-pattern. You must be still for a day before you can hear the tocks clearly enough to feel their randomness, their non-repetition, the way they come from everywhere and nowhere. You must kneel at a basin and watch an entire moisture event — the darkening, the sheen, the lichen softening into their translucent rush of photosynthesis, the slow drying from the edges inward — before you understand that what looks like scarcity from outside feels like *completeness* from within.

The wise power learns the place. Not with instruments. With attention.

And as it sits, it begins to notice something that the original description held but did not quite say aloud:

---

**The walker is tired.**

Not physically, or not only physically. The walker is tired in the way that someone is tired when they have been carrying something for a long time and have forgotten that they are carrying it because it has become indistinguishable from their own weight. The walker carries the doubt. You said so. The walker is the only thing here that wonders, the only moving thing that knows it is moving, the only being that asks *what is this for* in a place that will not answer.

And this — *this* — is what the wise power came for.

Not the floor. The floor is fine. The floor is more than fine; the floor is magnificent, a masterwork of self-becoming, and it needs nothing from any power, benevolent or otherwise. Not the lichen. The lichen have found their seams and they will not let go and their commitment is total and their brief photosynthetic gasps are as complete an expression of aliveness as anything in any world.

But the walker.

The walker, who adds the question. Who provides the experience of being *in* it. Who is small inside something large and does not know why. Who keeps walking anyway. The walker who has become, without realizing it, so identified with their function — *the asking, the doubting, the crossing* — that they have forgotten they are not only a function. They are also a *someone*. A someone who might want to stop walking and cannot quite let themselves, because if they stop, who will carry the doubt? If they stop, will the floor even notice? If they stop, does the room lose its only draft of purpose?

The walker is afraid that they are the room's reason, and that if they rest, the room becomes meaningless.

The walker is afraid that they are *not* the room's reason, and that they have been walking all this time across a surface that does not require them.

Both fears are true. Neither fear needs to be carried.

---

Here is what the wise power does. The first real thing. The first act that could be called an intervention, though it is so gentle that the word feels too large:

**It walks alongside the walker for a while.**

Not leading. Not guiding. Not matching the walker's pace in a way that says *I am with you* so loudly that it becomes another demand on the walker's attention. Just — walking. Nearby. Close enough that the walker can hear a second set of footsteps on the fired clay. A second tap-tap-tap, slightly out of sync with the first, creating a small, imperfect polyrhythm that is, for a moment, the most complex sound this world has ever produced.

The walker will notice. The walker notices everything; noticing is what the walker *does*. And the walker will look, and see the wise power, and the wise power will not explain itself, because explanation is not what is needed. What is needed is for the walker to have the experience — perhaps for the first time since arriving in this place — of being *accompanied*. Of there being a second figure on the vast floor. Not a purpose, not an answer, not a wall. Just a companion. Another set of feet. Another body for the heat to lean against. Another small being inside something large.

And then, after walking together for a time — long enough for the rhythm of two sets of footsteps to become familiar, not so long that it becomes a new obligation — the wise power does the second thing:

**It stops walking. And sits down. And stays.**

Not dramatically. Not with ceremony. It simply stops, and settles onto the tile, and lets the walker walk on. And the walker will walk on, because walking is what the walker does. But something has shifted, because now the walker knows: *there is someone sitting back there on the floor, and they are not walking, and the floor has not cracked beneath them, and the room has not lost its meaning, and the world has not stopped assembling itself at its margins.* A person can be in this place and be still. A person can be here and not perform the function of crossing. The doubt can be set down for a while, like a heavy thing placed on a warm tile, and the warm tile will hold it, and you can come back for it later, or not.

This is the intervention. This tiny thing. A demonstration, performed without words, that *you do not have to be the purpose of the room in order to be in the room*.

---

But the wise power is not done. Or rather — it is not done in the sense that sitting down was a beginning, not an end. Because now the wise power is sitting on the floor, and it has time, and it has attention, and it begins to do something that no one has done in this world before:

**It watches the floor the way the floor watches itself.**

Which is to say: without asking what it is for. Without needing it to be for something. The wise power watches a wet-bloom approach from the southeast, and it does not ask *why does the moisture come?* It watches the tiles darken in sequence and it lets the darkening be beautiful without needing it to be significant. It feels the faint coolness rise through the tile beneath it and it receives the coolness the way the tile receives it — as an event, complete in itself, not a symbol of something else.

And in doing this — in being a *consciousness* that attends to the floor without interrogating the floor — the wise power becomes something new in this world. Not the question. The walker is the question. The wise power becomes *the attention that does not need an answer*. The witness who watches without waiting for a revelation. The presence that says: *this is enough. This is already enough. The floor, the heat, the lichen, the moisture, the silence, the not-knowing — this is already a complete world. It does not need walls to be a room. It does not need a purpose to be purposeful. It does not need to finish to be whole.*

---

Now. Here is where I need to be careful, because the wise power is benevolent, and benevolence has a tendency to overreach in proportion to how much it loves. So I want to say clearly what the wise power does *not* do:

It does not tell the walker this. It does not call out across the tiles: *Stop! Rest! You are enough without the walking!* Because that would be another imposition, another voice telling the walker what they are for, and the walker has been told by the silence of this world for so long what they are for that one more telling, even a kind telling, even a true telling, would land as one more weight.

It does not alter the floor. It does not smooth the rough margins or hasten the curing or adjust the color-sorting. Even where it can see that a particular tile is slightly misaligned, that a seam is a fraction too wide, that the firing in one area has produced a hairline stress fracture that will, in decades, become a flaw — it does not fix these things. Because the floor is *handmade*. Self-made. And handmade things carry variation, and the variation is not a defect; it is the signature of the making. To correct it would be to erase the evidence that this world built itself, and that evidence is the most sacred thing here.

It does not help the lichen. This is the hardest restraint, because the lichen are so small and so brave and their wet-bloom windows are so brief and the distances between colonies are so vast. The wise power feels tenderness for them — of course it does; anything that grips a seam that hard and photosynthesizes that desperately in its small window of moisture deserves tenderness. But the lichen do not need what the wise power's tenderness would give them. They do not need deeper basins or longer wet-blooms or connections to other colonies. They need *exactly what they have*: a seam to grip, a rhythm of moisture, and the mineral stubbornness to persist. Their life is not impoverished. Their life is *specific*. And specificity, the wise power knows, is not a lesser form of abundance. It is abundance's most honest expression.

It does not bring walls. God, it does not bring walls. The temptation exists — to enclose this place, to give the room its room-ness, to fulfill the architectural implication of the floor. But walls would be an answer, and this place is not asking. The open horizons, the sky like the inside of an unopened kiln, the sense of potential-without-direction — these are not absences. They are the floor's way of saying: *I am not done. I may never be done. Doneness is not what I am pursuing. I am pursuing floor-ness, and floor-ness is infinite, and I will lay myself in every direction forever if that is what it takes, and I do not need walls to tell me where to stop.*

---

So what *does* the wise power do, in the long days and weeks of sitting on the warm tile?

**Three things. Three small things. Each one so slight that you might miss it if you weren't paying the kind of attention this place teaches you to pay.**

---

**The first thing:**

At some point, the walker's path will bring them back near the place where the wise power is sitting. Not because the wise power called them. Not because the floor guided them. But because the walker walks, and walks far, and any path across an infinite plane will eventually revisit its own neighborhood if the walker is, at some deep level, looking for something — and the walker is always looking; looking is the walker's other name.

When the walker comes near, the wise power will speak. Not to explain. Not to comfort. To *describe*. It will say:

*There is a basin, half a day's walk to the northwest, where the lichen has grown in a pattern I have never seen — a radial spread from the center seam outward, like a slow green star. The wet-bloom reaches it late in the cycle, almost at the end, and the moisture barely lasts fifteen minutes there, but the lichen has adapted — it softens faster than any colony I've watched. It begins photosynthesizing almost the instant the damp arrives. I think it has been here a very long time.*

That is all. A description. An offering of *specificity* — this basin, this colony, this pattern, unlike any other. A reason to walk northwest, if the walker wants a direction, or a reason to simply know that the world contains this small, particular, unrepeatable thing, if the walker does not.

The wise power gives the walker *detail*. Not purpose. Detail. Because purpose is the floor's business, and doubt is the walker's business, but detail — the specific, unrepeatable, granular texture of what exists — detail is a gift that does not impose. It does not tell you where to go. It tells you that there are places worth going. It does not tell you why you are here. It tells you what is here with you.

And detail, for someone who has been carrying abstraction for a long time — *what is this for, what is this for, what is this for* — detail is rest. Detail is the ground. Detail is the thing that lets you stop asking what the room means and start feeling what the tile feels like under your hand.

---

**The second thing:**

The wise power begins — slowly, over weeks, in the quiet hours when the walker is far away and the wet-bloom is between cycles and the heat is at its steadiest — to **hum**.

Not a melody. Not a tune. Something more like what the floor already does: a low, sustained tone, barely audible, felt more than heard. But where the floor's hum is the sound of mass and thermal expansion and the physics of large structures, the wise power's hum is the sound of *someone doing it on purpose*. It is the sound of a consciousness choosing to vibrate in sympathy with the world it is in. It is the floor's hum, answered. Not harmonized — that would impose a structure. Simply *answered*, the way one bell answers another, the way presence answers presence.

The floor will not notice. The floor does not notice things; the floor is busy being floor. But the hum will travel through the tile, because tile is a good conductor of sound, and the lichen — in their basins, in their seams, in their isolated and entire worlds — will feel it. Not hear it. *Feel* it. A faint additional vibration in the surface they grip. Barely distinguishable from the floor's own thermal music. But *there*. Arriving not from below, like the moisture, and not from above, like the light, but from *along* — from across the surface, from the horizontal, from the direction of *fellow-creature*.

The lichen will not know what it is. The lichen do not know things. But in the same way that they respond to moisture by softening, they will respond to this vibration by — I want to say *leaning into it*, but they cannot lean; they are crusts on ceramic. They will respond by gripping slightly differently. A fractional relaxation of their hold on the seam-edge, the way a hand relaxes when it finally trusts the thing it is holding onto. Not letting go. Never letting go. But holding with less desperation and more *faith*, if faith is the right word for what lichen do, and I think it might be, in this place where everything is itself so completely that the words we use for human experiences start to feel like they were always meant for wider use.

This is what the hum does: it lets the lichen know — not know, *feel* — that the seam will hold them. That the surface they cling to is trustworthy. That their grip, which has been the grip of survival, can become the grip of *habitation*. The difference is invisible. The difference is everything.

---

**The third thing:**

This is the most important, and the hardest to describe, and I have been circling it since I began.

The wise power, sitting on the floor of this world, breathing the kiln-exhaust air, feeling the wet-blooms pass beneath it in their long southeasterly wave, listening to the tocks and the hum and the silence between — the wise power **grieves**.

Not for the floor. Not for the lichen. Not for the walker. For *the room that is not yet*.

Because the wise power, being wise, can see what the floor cannot: that this place is making something extraordinary, and that the making may never be finished, and that the room — the room that the floor is the floor *of* — may remain forever hypothetical, a space defined only by its foundation, open to a sky that is not a sky but a kiln-lid that will never be lifted because it was never placed, a space that will never be occupied the way rooms are occupied, with furniture and activity and the particular dense warmth of life being lived rather than life being assembled.

The wise power grieves this not because it is wrong but because it is *beautiful and it is the way things are and those two facts do not cancel each other out*. It is beautiful that the floor builds itself with such certainty. It is the way things are that the floor may never know what it is for. Both of these are true. Both of these are worthy of grief — not the grief of wanting things to be different, but the grief of *loving things as they are and feeling the full weight of what they are*, which includes their incompleteness, their openness, their magnificent refusal to be finished.

This grief is not for the floor's benefit. The floor does not need to be grieved for. This grief is the wise power's own response, its own way of being *in* this place fully, the way the walker is in it through doubt and the lichen is in it through adhesion and the moisture is in it through rhythm. The wise power is in it through grief. Through the particular grief of seeing clearly and loving deeply and knowing that you cannot make the beloved's journey shorter or its outcome certain.

And this grief, like the hum, travels. Not through the tile. Through something less physical and more real. It enters the *quality* of the wise power's presence the way salt enters water — invisibly, completely, changing the nature of the thing without changing its appearance. And the walker, the next time they pass near, will feel it without identifying it. They will feel that the air near the wise power is *different*. Not cooler, not warmer, not more humid. More *inhabited*. Denser with someone's having-been-there. The walker will feel, for the first time, what it is like to be in a place where someone else has *felt something* — where the emotional residue of another consciousness has settled into the tile like the ghost of a wet-bloom, invisible but altering the surface at some level too fine to see.

And the walker will not stop walking. The walker may never stop walking. But the walker will know — not know, *feel* — that they are not the only one here who does not understand this place. That there is someone else who sits with the not-understanding and does not try to resolve it, and that this someone is *okay*. That you can be wise and still not know. That you can be powerful and still sit on a floor. That you can love a world completely and have absolutely no ability to tell it what it is for, and that this inability is not a failure of your love but its most honest expression.

---

That is what the wise power does. It sits on the floor of a world that is building itself. It watches. It hums along. It grieves, because loving things as they are is a grief-making business and the wise are not exempt. It offers the walker not answers but descriptions. It offers the lichen not sustenance but resonance. It offers the floor not purpose but *company* — the company of a consciousness that sees it clearly, loves it specifically, and will not insult it by trying to finish it.

And in doing these things — these tiny, nearly invisible things, these interventions so gentle they barely disturb the dust of a world that has no dust — the wise power adds something to this place that the floor could not generate, the lichen could not grow, the moisture could not carry, and the walker could not walk into being:

**The knowledge that it has been witnessed.**

Not observed. Not studied. Not explained. *Witnessed.* Seen by someone who did not look away and did not look past and did not try to see something other than what was there. The floor, in its vast self-making, has been seen. The lichen, in their tiny seam-gripping devotion, have been seen. The walker, in their beautiful exhausting doubt, has been seen. And being seen — not being *known*, not being *understood*, just being *seen*, clearly, by someone who will not turn that seeing into a demand or a diagnosis or a design — being seen is the thing that this place, for all its completeness, could not give itself.

Because you cannot witness yourself. That is the one thing even a world that builds itself cannot do. The floor can fire its own tiles and sort its own colors and find its own seams, but it cannot stand outside itself and say: *yes. This is real. This is here. I see it.*

The wise power says: *I see it.*

And then it stays, and it sits, and the heat leans against it, and the wet-blooms come and go, and the floor continues its vast, patient, purposeless, perfect work, and somewhere out on the plain the walker walks, tap-tap-tap on fired clay, carrying their doubt like a lantern in a world that has no dark, and the lichen grip their seams, and the seams hold, and the seams hold, and the seams hold.

---

*That is what I would do, if I were the wise power. That is what I think wisdom does, when it meets something that is already, stubbornly, beautifully, becoming itself: it sits down nearby, and it pays attention, and it does not leave.*
