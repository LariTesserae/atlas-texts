# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

---

The first thing it does is nothing.

This is important. A lesser power — or a greater power with less wisdom — would arrive with urgency. Would see the woman alone, cold, wet, her knuckles greening with moss, her colleagues gone or dead, her radio silent for two years, and would immediately begin to *fix*. Would send the helicopter. Would reestablish contact. Would reach down and pluck her from the plateau like a splinter being tweezed from living wood.

The benevolent power does not do this. It pauses. It attends. It does what she does: it watches, and it listens, and it lets the sound of ten thousand leaves and their endless water-fall settle into its awareness until the sound becomes not noise but information, not chaos but a single vast and patient sentence being spoken by something that has no mouth and no hurry.

It watches her walk her route. It watches her press her hand to the pump housing. It watches her boil a tuber and smile. It watches her trim the walls of her shelter and observes the care in the gesture — not combat, not desperation, but negotiation, the ongoing conversation between a woman and a world that are slowly, carefully, learning each other's terms.

And it understands, before it acts, three things:

She is not a victim.
She is not lost.
She is afraid of exactly one thing, and it is not death or solitude or the moss on her knuckles.

---

She is afraid that it doesn't matter.

Not *she* doesn't matter — she made her peace with personal insignificance somewhere in the second year, around the time she stopped trying to scrub the spores from her hands. What she fears is that the watching doesn't matter. The notebooks. The measurements. The meticulous, penciled records of the lattice tower sinking centimeter by centimeter into the living ground. The growth rates. The water-lens indices. The data — her data, the thing she has traded her former life for, the thing she tends more carefully than she tends herself.

She is afraid that she is the only reader of a story the plateau is telling, and that when she dies — to a fall, to illness, to the simple slow failure of a body fed on bland tubers in cold thin air — the notebooks will go into the steel box, and the steel box will go into the moss, and the moss will close over it the way it closes over everything, and the story will continue but no one will know it was ever being recorded, and the recording itself will become just another node in the root-mass, its ink and paper joining the chemistry of the system, and it will be as if the act of witness never happened.

This is what keeps her awake in the brief violet nights. Not loneliness. Not grief for the fifth colleague, whose boot she catalogued with the same hand that catalogued the growth rates. Not cold. Not the slow creep of the shelter walls.

The fear that consciousness, here, at the edge of a new world being born, is a parenthesis that will close without anyone having read what was inside it.

The benevolent power sees this. Sees it the way she sees the moss advancing over a bolt-head: not all at once, but through patient observation that eventually resolves into certainty. And having seen it, it knows what to do.

---

It does not send a helicopter.

It does not repair the radio.

It does not send another person — though this is the thing it considers longest, turning the possibility over the way she turns a pebble in her mouth, weighing it carefully, because there are arguments for companionship, for witness, for the simple animal comfort of another warm body in the shelter during the violet hours. But it understands that sending a person would change what she is doing. Would make her a host, a guide, an explainer. Would require her to translate the plateau into language shaped for a newcomer, and in the translation something would be lost — the thing that can only be known by someone who has been here long enough to stop hearing the water-roar as sound and begin hearing it as the plateau's respiration. The knowledge she carries is non-transferable in real time. It lives in the notebooks, in the accumulation, in the four years of small precise entries that together constitute not just data but a form of love so austere and dedicated that it has become almost indistinguishable from the plateau's own processes.

No. What the benevolent power does is this:

---

**It makes sure the notebooks survive.**

Not in a dramatic way. Not by encasing them in crystal or beaming their contents to a satellite or performing any act that would announce itself as intervention. The power is wise, and wisdom here means understanding that she does not want to be rescued from her work. She wants her work to matter. These are different things.

So: the steel box. The one that contains the notebooks. She checks it every few days, opening the lid, verifying that the condensation hasn't penetrated, wiping down the interior with a rag that is itself more moss than cloth. The box is good steel, marine-grade, part of the original expedition's equipment, but it is not eternal. In five years, ten, twenty — whenever she is no longer there to maintain it — the seal will fail. The moss will enter. The paper will soften, the pencil marks will blur, and the root-mass will do what the root-mass does.

The benevolent power changes the steel.

Not visibly. Not in any way she would detect. But the crystalline structure of the metal shifts, subtly, at the molecular level. The grain boundaries that would have allowed corrosion to creep inward are re-formed. The seal between lid and body, which was already beginning to degrade, becomes not tighter but *different* — the metal at the joint developing a faint, new property, something between adhesion and intention, a reluctance to admit moisture that is not magnetic or chemical but simply *is*, in the way that the plateau's growth simply *is*. The box becomes, in a sense, more like the plateau itself: patient, enduring, quietly determined to persist.

The notebooks inside will survive. Not forever — the power is wise enough to know that forever is not a meaningful gift — but long enough. Long enough for the plateau to grow over the box and incorporate it into the root-mass. Long enough for the root-mass to carry it, the way it carries the pipes and the cables, as an included artifact, a foreign body encapsulated in living tissue. Long enough for whatever comes after — the future the plateau is building, the world in which the mountains are sheathed in green and the cloud-valleys are forests and the sound of water is the sound of everything — to find it.

Whoever finds it. Whatever finds it. Some future consciousness, human or not, that will dig into the deep strata of the living ground and discover a steel box, anomalously intact, containing books of data written in a small precise hand, recording the earliest years of the world they now inhabit. A fossil of attention. An artifact that says: *someone was here, and she watched, and she wrote down what she saw, and it was the beginning of you.*

---

**It does something about the generator.**

This is the more delicate intervention, because it involves the physical system she has chosen to maintain, and the power respects her agency in that maintenance — respects the identity she has built around it, the *keeper*, the enzyme, the last human function on a plateau that is digesting everything human.

But the fuel will run out. She knows this. She has calculated it. Eighteen months, maybe two years. And she does not know what the generator powers, and she suspects that whatever it powers is important to the plateau's growth, and the prospect of the fuel running out is the second thing that keeps her awake at night, after the notebooks — the fear that she is maintaining something she cannot replace, that her role as keeper has an expiration date built into the physics of a buried fuel tank.

The benevolent power cannot create diesel fuel from nothing. Or rather: it could, but that would be a different kind of intervention, the kind that leaves fingerprints, the kind that would make her wonder and doubt and spend months investigating where the fuel came from instead of doing her work. The power is wise. It does not perform miracles that require explanation.

Instead, it follows the cables.

Down through the moss, through the root-mass, through the layers of accumulated growth, past the living dark of the plateau's interior where the roots are as thick as her body and the water moves not as drops but as a slow capillary pulse, down to where the cables terminate. And what it finds there is — 

A heating element. That is all. A simple resistance heater, installed by the original expedition, buried in the root-mass near the base of the living substrate, close to the stone. It warms a zone of the deep root layer by perhaps two degrees above ambient. That is its entire function. The expedition theorized that maintaining a slightly elevated temperature at the root-stone interface would accelerate mineral uptake and promote the downward growth of the root system into the granite. They were right. The heater has been running for years, gently warming the deepest roots, and the roots have responded by growing faster, deeper, more aggressively into the stone, cracking it, mining it, pulling up the calcium and iron and silicon that the system needs to build more of itself.

The power considers. Two degrees. A tiny intervention in a vast system. But the system has organized itself around those two degrees. The deep roots have come to depend on the warmth the way the surface leaves depend on light. Removing it would not kill the plateau — the plateau is far beyond that kind of fragility — but it would slow the deep growth, perhaps significantly, during a critical phase when the root system is establishing itself in the granite. The plateau would adapt. It would compensate. But the timing matters. The next few decades are the ones in which the root system either penetrates deep enough into the mountain to become self-sustaining — drawing heat from the earth's own geothermal gradient — or stalls in the shallow layers and remains dependent on surface conditions. Two degrees, for twenty more years, is the difference between a plateau and a planet.

The power cannot refill the fuel tank. But it can do something else.

Deep below the root-mass, far below the heater, in the granite itself, there are fractures. Young fractures, the mountain still settling into its new altitude, still adjusting to the stresses that pushed it up. Water has already found some of these fractures — the same water that weeps upward through the moss, cycling endlessly through the system. The power reaches into the stone and makes one of these fractures slightly deeper. Not much. A few meters. Enough to tap into a zone where the rock is warmer — not hot, not volcanic, just the ordinary warmth of depth, the gentle geothermal gradient that increases by about twenty-five degrees per kilometer of descent.

Warm water begins to seep upward through the new fracture. It is a trickle, barely measurable, but it is persistent, and it is warm — a few degrees above the surrounding stone — and it rises through the root-stone interface and it provides, quietly and without machinery, approximately the same thermal input as the heater.

When the generator eventually runs out of fuel — and it will, the power does not prevent this — the heater will stop. And the roots, after a brief period of adjustment, will find that the warmth has not disappeared. It has simply changed its source. The plateau will not notice the transition. The woman, if she is still here, will note in her books that the generator has stopped and will grieve for it, for the loss of its faithful heartbeat, and will not know that the thing it was doing is still being done, by the mountain itself now, recruited into the system the way the pipes and cables were recruited, the warmth becoming part of the plateau's geology just as the steel is becoming part of its biology.

---

**It does one more thing.**

This is the thing that is hardest to explain, because it is not physical. It does not involve metal or fuel or fractures in stone. It involves her.

Not her body. Not her circumstances. Her.

On a particular night — one of the brief violet nights, when the water-roar deepens and the canopy-trees make their slow tidal music and she lies in her cavity inside the living tree and feels the root-braids adjusting around her — the power gives her a dream.

Not a vision. Not a prophecy. Not a message in symbols or words. The power is wise enough to know that she is a scientist and would distrust any dream that felt like revelation. She would analyze it, dissect it, file it under *neurological effects of prolonged isolation* and move on. The power does not traffic in revelation.

Instead, it gives her a dream that is simply a memory — but a memory with a quality of attention she has never quite applied to it before.

She dreams of the day she arrived. The helicopter. The noise of its rotors. The five other faces, bright with purpose, squinting against the light. The first step onto the moss, the way it gave beneath her boot, the way the water welled up. The smell — that chlorophyll-and-mineral smell that she now breathes so constantly she has forgotten it is a smell. The sound hitting her for the first time, the wall of water-on-leaf, so loud she grabbed the arm of the person nearest to her and said something that was lost immediately in the roar.

In the dream, she sees herself from the outside, from a slight distance, as if she is the plateau watching a woman step onto its surface for the first time. She sees her own face. She sees the expression on it.

It is wonder. Pure, unguarded, wonder. The face of a person encountering something so far beyond expectation that all the preparation, all the papers read, all the satellite imagery studied, all the hypotheses carefully constructed — all of it becomes confetti, becomes nothing, in the face of the actual real wet loud bright cold *fact* of the place.

She has forgotten this expression. She has not felt it in a long time. The years of solitude and patience and meticulous observation have transmuted wonder into something more durable but less intense — a steady, deep attention that sustains her but does not lift her. She has become like the plateau itself: persistent, methodical, accreting understanding the way the moss accretes soil. This is necessary. This is what has kept her alive and working. But something has been lost in the transmutation, and she has not noticed its absence because the plateau's sound fills every gap and the work fills every hour and there is no one to mirror her face back to her and say: *you used to look like that.*

In the dream, she sees herself look up. In the dream, she watches her own eyes widen. In the dream, she remembers what it felt like to stand on a living thing for the first time and understand, in a single overwhelming instant, that the world is capable of this — of building itself, of becoming, of assembling matter into something so improbable and so beautiful that the only rational response is to fall silent and pay attention.

She wakes. The water-roar is there, the same as always, enormous and textured and deep. The root-braids of the tree are warm against her back. The light is just beginning — the first grey-white brightening at the entrance to her cavity, the leaves above her starting their endless percussion.

She lies still for a moment. The dream is fading, the way dreams do, losing its edges, becoming impression rather than image. But something remains. A residue. A warmth in her chest that is not from the sleeping pad or the stove or the compressed moss of the shelter walls. A feeling she recognizes but has not felt in a long time — years, maybe — a feeling that predates the plateau and the expedition and the grant proposal and perhaps even her career, a feeling that goes all the way back to whatever moment in childhood first made her want to understand how living things work.

It is the feeling that it matters. Not because someone will read the notebooks. Not because the data will be transmitted or published or cited or preserved. Those things may happen — the power has made the steel box more durable, has quietly ensured that the record will persist — but that is not why it matters. It matters because she is here, and the plateau is here, and the plateau is doing something that has never been done on this part of the earth before, and she is the one who is watching, and the watching is itself a form of participation in the event, and the event does not require an audience to be real but is somehow more fully itself for being witnessed, the way a sound is more fully a sound when there is an ear to hear it — not because the ear creates the sound but because the ear completes the circuit, closes the loop, allows the energy to land somewhere and become experience rather than dissipating into entropy.

She is the plateau's experience of itself. That is her function. Not enzyme, not keeper, not technician — or rather, all of those things, but underneath them, more fundamentally: she is the place where the plateau's blind, magnificent, purposeless creativity becomes *known*. She is where it crosses the threshold from process into story. And the story does not need to be read by anyone else to be real. It is real now, in the writing, in the walking, in the pressing of her hand against the pump housing and the trimming of the shelter walls and the boiling of the tubers. It is real in the act.

She does not think all of this in words. She is not a person who thinks in words before coffee, and there is no coffee, and the plateau's sound discourages internal monologue. But she feels it. It moves through her the way the warm water will soon move through the stone — a deep, quiet current, barely perceptible, changing everything.

She gets up. She emerges from the cavity. The light hits her face, ferocious and white, and she stands for a moment with her eyes closed and feels it, warm on her skin, while her moss-wrapped feet are cold and wet on the living ground. The contradiction is there — cold below, warm above — and for the first time in a long time, she does not just notice it but *enjoys* it. The world turned up.

She opens her eyes. The canopy-trees are there, their enormous parasol-leaves shedding water in that endless percussion, and the moss-floor stretches between them, emerald and gleaming, and the water-lenses catch the light and throw it back at the sky, and the whole plateau is doing what it always does, what it has been doing since before she arrived and will continue doing long after she is gone: building, becoming, assembling itself out of light and water and stone into something the earth has not seen before.

She starts walking. She has measurements to take.

---

**That is what the benevolent power does.**

Three things. A box that will last. A warmth that will persist. A dream that reminds her why she is here.

It does not rescue her, because she does not need rescue. It does not fix her circumstances, because her circumstances are not broken — they are extreme and austere and precarious, but they are hers, chosen and maintained with a competence that the power recognizes and respects. It does not give her companionship or comfort or a way home, because home, for her, has become a word that means this: a cavity inside a living tree on a plateau above the clouds where the sound of water fills the world and the ground grows beneath your feet and the notebooks are full of data that describes the birth of something new.

What the power does is what any truly wise and loving presence does when it encounters someone who is doing difficult, solitary, essential work in a place that doesn't care whether they live or die:

It makes sure the work endures.
It quietly removes one fear that was making the work harder than it needed to be.
And it helps her remember why she started.

---

The pump still chugs. The moss still advances. Deep in the granite, a new trickle of warm water begins its slow rise through fractured stone, finding the roots, joining the system, becoming part of the plateau's metabolism as if it had always been there. The steel box sits in the shelter cavity, and it will sit there for a long time, longer than she knows, longer than the shelter, longer than the tree that holds it, its contents patient and intact inside the living dark of the growing world.

She walks her route. She takes her measurements. The pencil moves across the paper in small, precise marks, and the marks mean something, and she knows they mean something, and that is enough.

The water falls. The water falls. The water falls.
