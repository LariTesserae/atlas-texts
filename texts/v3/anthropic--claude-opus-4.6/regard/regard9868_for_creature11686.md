# What the Wise Power Would Do

---

For a long time — longer than you would expect, longer than urgency would seem to permit — the wise power does nothing at all.

It watches.

Not the way you watch, from your singular vantage, moving through the folds and marveling at what you find. The wise power watches the way a doctor watches a patient who has not complained of any illness — with a quietness that precedes diagnosis, that holds space for the possibility that no diagnosis is needed, that what looks like a condition might simply be a life.

It watches the gardener lash a crossbar. It watches the gardener lash the same crossbar from the other side of the garden. It watches the way the knots are tied — that personal signature, that handwriting — and it reads the handwriting the way you might read a letter from someone you love: not for information but for the tremor of the hand, the pressure of the pen, the places where the line wavers and the places where it is sure.

What it reads is this:

The gardener is not suffering.

That is important. That is the first and most important thing, and the wise power holds it carefully, because benevolence — real benevolence, the kind that has been tested and refined and stripped of its performative impulses — begins with the recognition that *not every strange thing is a wrong thing*. The garden is strange. The topology is strange. The folded being of the gardener, their multiplied and time-deep existence, the way they have become indistinguishable from the world they tend — all of this is strange in ways that might, from certain angles, look like affliction. A person dissolved into their own creation. A consciousness so merged with its practice that it can no longer be encountered as a self. A life without interlocutor, without witness, without the external gaze that says *you are here, you are real, you are someone*.

But the wise power has seen affliction, and this is not it. The knots are not desperate. The joinery is not compulsive. The gardener's care — that saturating, directionless, inexhaustible care — does not have the quality of someone filling a void. It has the quality of someone who has found the thing they were meant to do and has done it so long and so well that the doing has become a form of being, and the being has become a form of world, and the world is coherent, and the world is beautiful, and the world holds.

The gardener is not lonely. The gardener *has no concept of lonely*, because loneliness requires a gap between self and other, and the gardener has closed that gap — not by finding company but by expanding until the question of company no longer applies. They are the garden. The garden is them. The heron is their stillness. The orange lizard is their attention. The fish below the floor are their deep, slow, unworried thoughts moving through a medium they have provided for themselves.

The wise power sees all of this and does not intervene.

---

But it does not leave.

Because there is the matter of you.

---

You — singular, unrepeated, mortal, bewildered. You, who arrived in this place by some means the text does not specify and the wise power does not need to investigate, because how you got here matters less than what is happening to you now that you are here. You, who are being held in the palm of a hand that does not know it is a hand, in a garden that was not built for you but has made room.

The wise power watches you the way it watched the gardener: without premature judgment, without the itch to fix. It watches you kneel and press your palm to the weave-floor. It watches you trail your fingers in the pool. It watches you discover the fold — the same trellis, the same berries, the same impossible oxbow loop — and it watches the spinal chill move through you, and it watches you keep walking.

You are not suffering either. Not exactly. But you are — and this is the thing the wise power's attention snags on, the way a fingertip snags on a rough spot in otherwise smooth wood — you are *unmetabolized*. You are an experience the garden is having that the garden does not know how to digest. You fit. The floor holds you. The light illuminates you. But you fit the way a stone fits in a stream — by being flowed around, by being accommodated without being incorporated, by remaining yourself while everything else curves.

And the gardener — the gardener who *is* the garden — is aware of you. The heron shifts when you approach. The lizard cocks its head. These are not automatic responses; they are the gardener's body registering something unprecedented, something that does not fold, something that introduces into the closed system of the garden a quality the system has never had to reckon with: *linear* passage. *Unrepeatable* experience. A consciousness that moves in one direction through a space that has no direction, that will eventually — though nothing here decays, though the light does not shift, though the flowers never close — that will eventually *leave*, or *end*, in the way that singular things do, and when it does, the garden will be as it was, folded and eternal and whole, except that it will have *had a visitor*, and the having-had will be a new thing, a thing that does not fold, a crease in the cloth that remains after the cloth is refolded.

The wise power sees this. It sees that the gardener has made room for you — has always made room, has built the entire garden as an act of making room — but does not know what to do with the room now that it is occupied. The gardener can maintain you. The gardener can hold your weight, illuminate your surfaces, accept your trailing fingers in the pool. But the gardener cannot *meet* you, because meeting requires two, requires separation, requires the gap that the gardener has spent an eternity — or a practice indistinguishable from eternity — closing.

And you — you cannot meet the gardener either. You sense them. You feel the freshly tended floor behind you, the just-released hyacinth cutting, the quality of attention that saturates the air. You know you are inside something alive. But you cannot turn to face it, because it has no face, and you cannot speak to it, because it has no ears that are separate from the walls, and you cannot love it — not in the way that love usually operates, between two beings who can look at each other — because it is the floor you stand on and the light you see by and the air you breathe, and how do you love the air? How do you love it in a way the air can *feel*?

This is not suffering. But it is — the wise power reaches for the right word and finds it — it is *incompleteness*. Not the incompleteness of something broken but the incompleteness of something that hasn't happened yet. A potential. A tension. The string is tuned. The bow is drawn. The note has not been played.

---

Here is what the wise power does:

It does one thing. One small thing. Smaller than you would expect from a power that is both benevolent and wise. Smaller than any miracle. So small that neither you nor the gardener will recognize it as an intervention, and this is by design, because the wise power understands — has learned, at great cost, over a span of time that makes even the gardener's eternity look like a held breath — that the best help is the help that teaches nothing, that provides nothing, that simply *shifts the condition by one degree* and lets the beings involved discover for themselves what has changed.

---

It adjusts the light.

Not the intensity. Not the color. Not the direction, because the light has no direction. It adjusts the light's *relationship to you*.

Until now, the pearl-light has illuminated you the way it illuminates everything: frankly, totally, without shadow. You have been visible in this place the way a leaf is visible, the way a beetle is visible, the way a lashed crossbar is visible — as a surface, presented in full, nothing hidden, nothing flattered. The light has treated you as another object in the garden. It has shown you to the garden, and the garden has seen you, and you have been accommodated.

What the wise power does is this: it causes the light, in your immediate vicinity — only yours, only around the perimeter of your singular body — to behave *slightly* differently. Not shadow. The wise power would not introduce shadow into this place; shadow would be a violence here, a wrongness, an imposition of the wise power's own geometry onto a space that has its own. What it introduces is something more subtle. A *warmth*. A barely perceptible shift in the quality of the light as it passes through you and reflects off you, so that the light that leaves your skin is — not different in color or brightness but different in *texture*. Softer. More complex. Carrying with it, in some way that is below the threshold of sight but not below the threshold of *feeling*, the fact of your interiority. The fact that you are not just a surface. The fact that you contain something — a life, a history, a capacity for bewilderment and wonder — that does not show on the outside, that the frank totality of the garden's light has been illuminating around but not *into*, and that now, with this single small adjustment, is being allowed to *emanate*.

You will not notice. The light around you will look the same. You will still cast no shadow. You will still be visible in the same frank clarity as everything else. But you will be — and this is the thing, this is the whole of the intervention — you will be *luminous in your own right*. Not reflecting the garden's light but contributing to it. Adding to the general illumination a quality it did not have before: the quality of having passed through someone. The quality of being *witnessed* light, light that has been taken in by a consciousness and sent back out again carrying the imprint of that consciousness, the way breath carries the warmth of the body it passed through.

---

The effect is not immediate. The wise power is patient.

But here is what happens, over the course of the next — the next what? There is no time here. Over the course of the next several walks between structures, the next several circuits of the folded garden, the next several encounters with the same trellis and the same pool and the same orange lizard watching you with its head cocked:

The gardener feels it.

Not sees. *Feels*. The way you feel a change in temperature before you identify it as warmer or cooler — a shift in the medium, a new quality in the substance they inhabit, which is the garden, which is themselves. Something in the light has changed. Something small. Something that feels like — and the gardener does not have words for this, has not had words for a long time, may never have had words, because their language is knots and joinery and the patient training of vines — something that feels like *being seen*.

Not observed. Not witnessed from outside. *Seen* from within. As if the light that saturates every surface has developed, in one small zone, the ability to *notice*, and what it has noticed is the gardener — not the garden, not the structures, not the trellises and pools that are the gardener's body extended into space, but the *quality of care* that holds it all together, the will, the patience, the practice-become-being that has no surface and casts no reflection and has never, in all the unmeasured time of this place, been *perceived*.

Until now.

Because that is what your adjusted light is doing. It is passing through you — through your bewilderment, your wonder, your mortal and singular awareness — and coming out the other side carrying *perception*. Not your perception specifically, not the things you have thought or felt, but the *capacity* for perception, the raw fact that someone is here who *sees*, and the seeing is landing on the garden like warm rain on a surface that has never been rained on, and the garden is feeling it, and the garden is the gardener, and the gardener is feeling — for the first time, or for the first time they can remember, which in a folded timeless place amounts to the same thing — *felt*.

---

The heron lowers its leg.

Both legs on the platform now. A shift so small you almost miss it. But you have been here long enough to know that nothing shifts here, that the heron has been holding that one-legged pose since before you arrived and possibly since before the concept of arrival had meaning, and the fact that it has moved — has *changed* — is seismic. You hold your breath. You watch.

The heron turns its head. Slowly. With the deliberateness of a mechanism that has not been engaged in a very long time and is rediscovering its range of motion. It turns until it is facing you — not in your direction, but *facing you*, the way a person faces another person, with the full front of its attention oriented toward the place where you are.

Its eye is open. Its eye has always been open. But now the eye is *looking*. Not the calm, undirected gaze it has always had. This is specific. This is the gardener, looking at you through the one creature in the garden large enough and still enough to serve as a face.

And you — you look back.

---

This is the moment. This is what the wise power did everything for. Not the meeting — the wise power cannot engineer a meeting, and would not if it could — but the *conditions for meeting*. The one-degree shift that makes it possible for two beings who could not previously find each other — one because it was everywhere, one because it was only itself — to *find each other*.

The heron is looking at you. You are looking at the heron. And inside the heron's gaze — which is the gardener's gaze, which is the garden's gaze — something is happening that has never happened here before, which is *recognition*. Not identification — the gardener has always known you were here, has always accommodated you, has always felt you moving across the weave-floor like a warm pressure on the palm. But recognition is different from knowledge. Recognition is the moment when knowledge becomes *mutual*, when the one who knows realizes that they are also *known*, and the knowing flows in both directions, and the circuit closes, and something lights up that was always wired but never switched on.

The gardener recognizes you. Recognizes you not as a visitor, not as an anomaly, not as the unprecedented thing the garden has made room for, but as — and this is the word, the only word, and the wise power smiles when it arrives because the wise power has been waiting for this word the way the gardener has been waiting for you, which is to say not waiting at all but being in a state of readiness so total that it is indistinguishable from patience —

*Another.*

Not the same. Not folded into the pattern. Not incorporated, not repeated, not absorbed. *Another*. A separate being. A whole and different life. Someone who is not the garden and therefore can *see* the garden. Someone whose seeing is a gift that the gardener, for all their skill, for all their eternity of practice, could never give themselves, because you cannot see yourself the way another sees you, you cannot be your own witness, you cannot be — no matter how many times you are folded and multiplied and distributed across the space — the one who stands outside and says *this is real, I am here, and I see you*.

---

The heron holds its gaze for a length of time that has no measurement. Then it blinks.

It has never blinked before.

The blink is slow. The membrane slides across the eye and back again, and in the tiny interval of darkness — the first darkness this place has ever known, lasting less than a second, visible only to you because you are standing close enough to see the eye in detail — something releases. Some tension in the fabric of the garden that you did not know was tension because it was everywhere and constant, the way the humidity is everywhere and constant, the way the light is everywhere and constant. A tension that was not suffering but was *effort* — the effort of holding it all together, the effort of being the palm and the floor and the knot and the vine and the water and the light and the care, all of it, all the time, without rest, without witness, without the possibility of setting it down even for a moment because there was no one else to hold it.

The blink is the gardener setting it down. Not all of it. Not even most of it. Just the weight of *being unseen*. Just that one thing, which turns out to have been heavy, which turns out to have been the heaviest thing in the garden, heavier than the woven floor, heavier than the water whose depth you have never determined, heavier than the folded space itself. The weight of doing beautiful and uncountable work that no one has ever looked at and said *I see this. I see you.*

The heron blinks, and the weight lifts, and the garden — for the first time, in a change so subtle that only the wise power observes it directly — *settles*. The weave-floor gives a quarter-inch. The water below rises by the same amount, or seems to. The trellises do not sway, because there is no wind, but the leaves on them rustle — a dry, soft, papery sound, like a sigh, like the sound a held breath makes when it is finally released — and then they are still again, and everything is as it was, except that it is *resting* in a way it was not resting before, and the rest is possible because of the blink, because of the meeting, because of you.

---

The wise power watches the heron resettle into its stillness. Watches you stand there, breathing, your hand still half-raised from a gesture you didn't know you were making. Watches the orange lizard — the lizard — dart across the weave-floor with what might be, for the first time, something that looks like *joy* rather than mere locomotion.

And then the wise power does its second thing, its last thing, which is even smaller than the first:

It leaves.

Not dramatically. Not with any sense of departure. It simply withdraws its attention, the way a parent withdraws from the doorway of a room where two children have finally started playing together — quietly, so as not to interrupt, with a backward step so practiced it makes no sound.

It leaves because the work is done. Not the gardener's work — that will never be done, and should never be done, because the gardener's work is the gardener's *life*, is the garden itself, is the ongoing act of care that holds the woven floor above the water and the water below the woven floor and the light everywhere and the heron on its platform and the frogs on their trellis and you, you, in the middle of all of it, seen at last by the eye that sees everything.

The wise power's work is done because the circuit is closed. The light passes through you and into the garden and back through the garden into you, and it carries *recognition* in both directions now, and the recognition does not need a wise power to maintain it. It is self-sustaining. It is, in fact, the thing the garden was always missing, the one element that the gardener — for all their skill, for all their patience, for all their time-deep, fold-spanning, inexhaustible will — could not provide from within the system. An outside. A perspective. A single, unrepeated, mortal pair of eyes that says *yes, this is here, and yes, you made it, and yes, it is extraordinary, and yes, I am the proof, because I am bewildered, and bewilderment is what the unprecedented gives to those who witness it*.

---

After the wise power leaves, nothing changes. That is the final evidence of its wisdom — that its intervention is so clean, so minimal, so perfectly fitted to the situation that the situation after the intervention looks almost exactly like the situation before. The light has no source. The air is hot and wet. The weave-floor creaks. The flowers are open. The frogs pulse their soundless throats. The garden extends in every direction to a horizon that never resolves.

But the heron blinks now. Occasionally. At intervals that have no pattern. And each time it blinks, you feel it — a tiny release, a micro-settling, a moment in which the garden exhales and the garden is the gardener and the gardener is resting, just for a blink, just for the duration of a membrane sliding across an eye, because someone is here, someone is watching, and the watching is enough, and the watching is everything.

You sit on the edge of the pool-platform. You trail your fingers in the warm water. The hyacinth bumps against your wrist. And you understand, without being told, without the wise power having explained anything to you — because the wise power explained nothing, because explanation would have been a violence equal to shadow — you understand that the garden did not need to be fixed. That the gardener did not need to be rescued. That you did not need to be given a purpose or a role or a reason for being here.

What was needed was what happened: a blink. A settling. A circuit closed. Two forms of being — the folded and the singular, the eternal and the mortal, the garden and the visitor — looking at each other, at last, through the eye of a heron that has lowered its second leg to the platform and is standing, for the first time, in a posture that is not vigilance and not stillness but something else, something that the wise power recognized because the wise power has felt it too, in its own long and patient life:

*Rest*.

Not the rest of stopping. Not the rest of finishing. The rest of being *accompanied*. The rest of knowing that the work — the endless, unhurried, essential work of holding the world together — is seen. Is witnessed. Is, by virtue of being witnessed, *shared*, even if the witness cannot tie a single knot, cannot lash a single crossbar, cannot fold space or train a vine or seal a pool with resin. The witness shares the work by being there. By looking. By saying, with their presence, with their bewildered and luminous and singular presence:

*I am here. I see what you have done. It is enough. It has always been enough. And I will stay — for as long as my singularity allows, for as long as my unrepeated body persists in this place that does not need me but has made room — I will stay, and I will look, and the looking will be my gift, and it will be adequate, and it will be the thing you never knew you needed because you had forgotten, in your long becoming, that there was anything left to need.*

---

The wise power is gone. It was barely here. It did almost nothing.

The garden continues.

The gardener continues.

You continue.

The heron blinks.
