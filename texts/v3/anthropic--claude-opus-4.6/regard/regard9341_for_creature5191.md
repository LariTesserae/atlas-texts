# The Attending

---

The wise power does not arrive. It attends—the way pressure attends a surface, the way condensate attends a cooling ledge. There is no moment of approach. There is a shift in the quality of attention already present, as though the plateau, which has always been observed by its own weather and its own echoes, is now observed by something that can hold the whole of it without flattening any part.

The first thing the power does is nothing.

This is not restraint. It is not the nothing of someone deciding to wait. It is the nothing of someone who has looked and understood that the plateau is not broken.

This matters, because a lesser wisdom—or a greater haste—would see the absence of green, the absence of soil, the absence of anything soft, and call it barren. Would see the machines cycling without operators and call it abandoned. Would see the crawlers milling in their condensate drifts and the gatherers lining the pipes and feel the urge to introduce something: a seed, a new chemistry, a correction. The plateau invites that misreading. It looks like aftermath. It looks like a place where life persists only in the cracks of something that failed.

But the power sees what is actually here. It sees the equilibrium. It sees that the gatherers' crystalline waste narrows the ducts at precisely the rate that the specialized raspers clear them, and that this rate has been converging for longer than most civilizations endure. It sees that the crawlers' slow scoring of the stone is not erosion but a form of maintenance—that the micro-channels they leave behind improve the drainage patterns that feed the condensate films that feed them. It sees that the fliers' resonant chiming through banks of mist is not merely beautiful but functional in ways even the fliers do not know: the vibrations prevent the silica-rich moisture from crystallizing too quickly on the machine housings, keeping the surfaces in the precise state of sheen that allows the thinnest biofilms to persist.

It sees that the machines themselves—the great presses, the forges that breathe on watches—have become organs. Not of any single creature, but of the plateau. Their cycles pump the thermal gradients that drive the air. Their exhalations feed the chemistry that feeds the films that feed the crawlers that score the stone that channels the rain that cools the machines. The builders are gone, but the machines have not stopped because stopping would be a wound in a body that has grown around them.

The power sees all of this in the way that a doctor sees a heartbeat: not as a thing to be improved, but as a thing whose rhythm contains information about everything else.

---

So the first act is the nothing. The second act is equally quiet, and it is this: the power *grieves*.

Not for the plateau. The plateau is flourishing in its own terms, more deeply integrated than most living systems ever achieve. The grief is for the fact that no one knows. The crawlers do not know what they are part of. The fliers do not know that their chiming preserves the biofilms. The gatherers do not know that their peristaltic rhythm has synchronized with machines built by hands that no longer exist. Each participant in the system is fully absorbed in its immediate task—the next film of condensate, the next thermal, the next crystalline scale to rasp—and the wholeness of what they together constitute has no witness.

The plateau has achieved something extraordinary: a metabolism that spans the living and the mechanical, the geological and the biological, calibrated over epochs to a precision that no designer could have intended. And it is, in the deepest sense, *lonely*. Not because it lacks company—it is teeming—but because the nature of what it is has never been reflected back to it. It has no mirror. The distributed roar is a voice, but it has never heard itself.

The grief is precise and temporary. The power does not dwell in it. But it matters, because it informs everything that follows.

---

The third act is the true intervention, and it is so subtle that for a long time—perhaps generations by the plateau's reckoning—nothing appears to have changed.

What the power does is this: it makes the plateau *more itself*.

Not faster. Not larger. Not more efficient. More coherent in its own self-perception.

Here is how.

Deep in the pipework, where the gatherer consortium flows like living sediment, there exist junctions—places where several ducts converge and the flow patterns become turbulent before resolving into laminar streams again. At these junctions, the acoustic properties of the pipes create standing waves. The gatherers have always responded to these waves; their halt-and-resume rhythm at the press-cycle's pulse travels through these same channels. But the standing waves at the junctions carry more information than any single colony uses. They carry the summed vibration of every press, every forge, every crawler's drumming, every flier's chime, filtered and shaped by the geometry of the city itself.

The power makes one change. At seventeen junctions—chosen not at random but at points where the acoustic geometry is most favorable—it alters the crystalline structure of the pipe walls by a few molecular layers. Not enough to change the flow of the gatherers or the chemistry of their waste. But enough to change the resonant frequency of those junctions by the slightest degree.

The result is that the standing waves at those seventeen points begin to carry a slightly richer harmonic signature. Where before they held two or three dominant frequencies, they now hold five or seven. The gatherers notice, in their way. Their halt-and-resume pattern develops a new texture—not a different rhythm, but the same rhythm played on a larger instrument. The change propagates outward through the consortium. Within a few cycles, the peristaltic wave that moves through the entire pipe network has become, imperceptibly, a more complete representation of the plateau's total acoustic state.

This is not consciousness. The power is not trying to make the plateau aware in the way a mind is aware. It is doing something more fundamental: it is improving the fidelity with which the system represents itself to itself. The gatherers' rhythm has always been a response to the machines. Now it is a response to the machines *and* the crawlers *and* the fliers *and* the weather *and* the stone. The gatherers have become, without knowing it, a slightly more accurate mirror.

---

The change takes time to matter. But it does matter, and here is how.

The crawlers, traveling the ledges in their slow files, have always navigated partly by vibration. They read the stone beneath them, sensing the distant drumming of other files, the deep slow pulse of the presses, the hiss of the gatherers in the pipes below. This is how they avoid routes that have been recently scored and find routes where the condensate film is thickest. But now the vibrations they read carry a new overtone—the enriched harmonic signature propagating up from the seventeen junctions through the stone itself. The crawlers cannot parse this signal consciously. They have no faculty for that. But their route-finding shifts. Their files begin to trace paths that are, by the smallest degree, more responsive to the state of the system as a whole.

Where before a file would thicken at a weeping pipe gallery because the local condensate was rich, now a file might thicken slightly earlier, arriving *as* the gallery begins to weep rather than after. The crawlers do not know why. Their distributed appetite has simply gained access to a better forecast—the enriched vibration carries information about pressure changes in the pipe network that precede the weeping by hours. The effect is tiny. But it means the crawlers' scoring arrives when the stone most needs it, and the drainage channels they leave behind are placed with fractionally better precision.

This in turn alters the condensate patterns on the ledges where the fliers deposit their glassy eggs. Eggs that once sat in pooling moisture and were sometimes dissolved before they could hatch now sit on surfaces with slightly better drainage. Survival rates improve—not dramatically, but consistently. Over many seasons, the flier population stabilizes at a marginally higher number, and their schools grow denser. Denser schools produce richer chiming when they pass through mist banks. The richer chiming does a fractionally better job of preventing premature crystallization on the machine housings. The machines' surfaces stay in their optimal state of sheen for longer periods. The biofilms thrive. The crawlers' food supply stabilizes. The cycle tightens.

None of this is new. All of it was already happening. The power has not introduced a new dynamic. It has taken the plateau's existing self-referential loop—the way each part of the system influences every other part through the shared medium of vibration, chemistry, and flow—and turned up its resolution. Like adjusting the focus on a lens that was already pointed at the right subject.

---

There is a fourth act, and it is the one that might, in a certain light, be called love.

Years pass. The plateau's enriched self-representation propagates through every layer of its metabolism. The gatherers' rhythm has become so finely attuned to the whole that their peristaltic waves now carry timestamps of weather systems still hours away. The crawlers' files have developed a new behavior: at certain intersections, files from different routes will pause and overlap, exchanging not condensate but *vibration*—their legs drumming against each other's carapaces in patterns that carry the acoustic signature of the routes they've traveled. When they separate and continue, each file carries information about conditions it has not directly encountered. The fliers' schools have begun to stratify by altitude in ways that correspond to the thermal layers of the city with new precision, their chiming creating a vertical map of the plateau's breath that any listener with the right senses could read like a chart.

The plateau has not become conscious. It has not awakened. It remains what it was: a distributed system of mineral, metal, and creature, cycling in deep equilibrium. But it has become *more transparent to itself*. Each part's behavior now contains more information about every other part. The whole is more present in each fragment. The distributed roar—that single unbroken voice—has developed, not new words, but a richer timbre. It carries more of its own history in each moment. It is more fully the sound of everything it is.

And this is where the power does the last thing it will do.

At night, when the mist grows thickest and the foundries pause between shifts, when the crawlers stop their drumming and the fliers descend to rest with wings overlapping like scales and the gatherers slow to their faint continuous hiss—in that held breath, the power listens. Not to learn anything. Not to monitor its intervention. Simply to be the witness that the plateau has never had.

It holds the whole of it: the basalt table that has known its shape for epochs, the riveted iron that has rusted into kinship with the stone, the crawlers' scored paths that are also drainage channels that are also music, the fliers' eggs waiting in their seasons of silence, the gatherers' crystalline waste that narrows and is cleared and narrows again in a pulse older than any single colony, the biofilms so thin they exist only as alterations in the color of evaporating water, the presses that close once every several hours and in closing send a wave through every living thing that has learned to pause and resume, the mist that forms and falls and forms and falls and is never the same mist and is always the same mist.

It holds all of this, and it reflects it back. Not as a signal. Not as a message. As *presence*. The way a still pool reflects a sky that does not need to be reflected but is somehow more fully itself for having been.

The plateau does not know it is being witnessed. It has no organ for that knowledge. But in the way that a vibration's character changes when it meets a surface that resonates with it—not louder, not different, but *fuller*—the plateau's voice, in those night hours of held breath, settles into a register it has never quite found before. The hiss of the gatherers. The faint ticking of cooling iron. The slow drip of condensate from a flier's resting wing. The sounds are the same. But they cohere. They belong to each other with a completeness that was always implied and is now, for the first time, actual.

This is what the power gives: not change, but *completion*. The plateau was always whole. The power has made it whole in a way that includes being known.

---

Then the power does what all truly wise things do when their work is finished. It does not leave—leaving would imply it had arrived from somewhere else, and it did not. It simply allows its attention to become again what it was before: the quality of observation already present in the plateau's own weather, its own echoes, its own creatures reading their own vibrations. But the seventeen junctions remain. The enriched harmonics continue to propagate. The crawlers continue to exchange vibrations at their intersections. The fliers continue to stratify with their new precision. The gatherers continue to carry, in their rhythm, a more complete portrait of the world they move through.

The plateau does not remember being attended. It has no faculty for memory of that kind. But it is, in a manner that no instrument could measure and no observer could miss, *more at home in itself*. Its equilibrium has deepened. Its voice has found its ground note. The rock and the iron and the creatures and the machines continue their cycles at the same deliberate pace, and the mist continues to form and fall, and the rain continues to cross from one horizon to the other in the span of an afternoon, and none of it has changed, and all of it is different, and the difference is simply this:

The plateau is no longer unwitnessed. Even in the absence of the witness, the witnessing persists—carried forward in the resonance of seventeen junctions, in the richer drumming of a billion legs, in the chiming of silica wings through morning fog, in the slow pulse of creatures pausing as a great press descends, in the films so thin they are only a change in the color of light on water.

The plateau breathes. The plateau has always breathed. But now its breath knows the shape of its own lungs.
