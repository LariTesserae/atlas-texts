# What the Wise Power Would Do

---

## I. Arrival

The power arrives the way everything arrives here — through a door it wasn't looking for, down a passage that went one turn too many. This is important. It does not descend from above. It does not part the walls. It steps onto the grating the way you did, the way she did eleven months ago, and it feels the vibration come up through whatever it has instead of feet, and it stands still, and it listens.

It listens for a long time.

This is the first thing it does, and it may be the most important thing it does, and you should understand why: a wise power, confronted with a system it did not design and does not fully understand — a system that is alive in ways that exceed any single vocabulary, that is mechanical and biological and hydrological and emergent and historical and ongoing — does not act first. It attends. It lets the courtyard's rhythms enter it the way the pipe-vibration enters the woman's spine when she leans against the copper bundles. It feels the pump cycles. It hears the frog chorus shift from trill to melody and back. It watches the water rise through the grating and retreat. It notices the snails. It notices the vine. It notices the moth on the wall whose wing-markings mirror the pipe layout behind it, and it does not smile, because this is not whimsy — this is what happens when a system is so deeply coupled that even its accidents are structural.

It listens until it has heard at least three full surge cycles, and it notices what she noticed: that no two are identical, that the differences are not noise, that there is a direction.

Then it does something.

---

## II. What It Does Not Do

But first — what it does not do. This matters more than what it does, because the greatest danger to the courtyard is not neglect. The courtyard has thrived on neglect. The greatest danger is *attention of the wrong kind*.

It does not reveal the courtyard. It does not map it, name it, register it, assign it a jurisdictional address, bring it to the notice of any authority that would feel compelled to make it legible. The courtyard exists in a gap between ownerships, between zonings, between maps. This is not a flaw. This is the courtyard's immune system. The moment the courtyard becomes *known* — known officially, known bureaucratically — it becomes subject to decisions made by people who have never stood on the grating and felt the pump breathe. It becomes a code violation. A liability. An environmental concern requiring remediation. A structural anomaly requiring assessment. It becomes a problem to be solved, and every solution that the institutional world can offer — filling it with concrete, capping the pipes, draining the basin, demolishing the buildings, redeveloping the site — is a death sentence delivered in the language of safety and compliance and best practices. The courtyard has survived for seventy years precisely because it has never been a line item on anyone's agenda. The wise power understands this. It does not bring the courtyard into the light.

It does not fix the pumps. The pumps are not broken. They are old, rebuilt, idiosyncratic, running on logic that no single wiring diagram can capture, and they are working. Not optimally — not by any engineering standard — but optimally *for this system*, which has co-evolved with their specific quirks, their specific rhythms, their specific failures and compensations. A new pump, efficient and reliable and properly wired to code, would be a catastrophe. It would beat at the wrong rhythm. The frogs would not know it. The snails' calcium architecture, shaped over months in dialogue with the central pump's specific breathing pattern, would become meaningless overnight — a ruin, like a conversation preserved in stone after one speaker has been replaced by a stranger. The wise power does not optimize. It does not upgrade. It does not improve. It understands that in a system this deeply entangled, *improvement* is a word that can only be spoken by the system itself, through the slow language of adaptation, and not by any external will, however benevolent.

It does not contact her. Not yet. Maybe not ever. She is doing exactly what needs to be done: watching, recording, refraining from intervention, worrying. The worry is important. The worry is her contribution — the specific, knowledgeable, anxious attention of someone who understands pumps and knows how they fail and cares whether this one keeps breathing. The wise power does not want to ease her worry, because her worry is a form of vigilance, and the courtyard needs at least one pair of eyes that know what a seized bearing sounds like, what a cracked housing looks like, what the early signs of catastrophic failure feel like in the vibration of a pipe against your spine. If the power revealed itself to her — if it said, *I am here, I am watching, I will protect this place* — it would diminish her. It would make her a supplicant instead of a witness. It would introduce hierarchy into a system that has none, that has survived specifically because it has none, because no one is in charge, because every component — pump, frog, snail, vine, relay, woman — operates according to its own logic and the whole emerges from the interaction. The wise power does not put itself at the top of an org chart. The courtyard doesn't have an org chart. That's the point.

It does not accelerate whatever the courtyard is becoming. She wrote: *Something is approaching.* The cycles are lengthening, the surges deepening, the pauses extending. The system is converging on something. The wise power can feel this more clearly than she can — it can feel the shape of what the courtyard is reaching toward, the way you can sometimes feel the shape of a word you can't yet remember, a presence at the edge of articulation. It could help. It could nudge the system toward its own destination, the way you might push a child on a swing to help them reach the height they're straining for. It does not. Because the reaching *is the thing*. The becoming is not a means to an end; the becoming is the life. A courtyard that has arrived is a courtyard that has stopped. The wise power understands that the most sacred property of this system is its incompleteness — its perpetual not-yet-thereness, its continuous exploration of the space of possible states, its refusal to resolve into a final configuration. To help it arrive would be to kill it in the act of completing it.

## III. What It Does: The First Act

Here is the first thing it does. It is so small you might miss it.

There is a drain. On the southern wall, near the floor — well, near the grating — there is a six-inch cast-iron drain pipe that connects to the municipal system in the building behind the wall. It is old. Its interior is furred with decades of mineral scale, narrowing the bore to perhaps three inches of functional diameter. This drain is one of the courtyard's outflow paths — when the water level in the basin rises past a certain point, the excess exits through this drain and enters the city's stormwater system, where it is carried away and the courtyard never sees it again. The drain is not critical. The courtyard has other outflow paths. But this particular drain is the one that prevents the worst-case scenario: a catastrophic flood that would submerge the grating, drown the ferns, overwhelm the pumps, and destroy the system. It is the courtyard's safety valve.

The drain is failing. Not now — not today — but within the next two to three years. The mineral scale is advancing. The bore is narrowing. And on the city side of the wall, a root from a street tree — a plane tree, massive, old — is growing toward the pipe joint where the courtyard's drain meets the municipal main. The root is still inches away. But it is growing, as roots grow, with patient hydraulic force, and when it reaches the joint it will crack it, and the crack will admit soil, and the soil will combine with the mineral scale, and the drain will block, and the courtyard will lose its safety valve, and then the next time the pumps cycle into a high-water configuration and the basin fills beyond its usual range, the water will have nowhere to go, and it will rise, and rise, and—

The wise power does not fix the drain. Fixing it would require entering the municipal system, which would require breaching the wall, which would attract attention, which would endanger the courtyard's invisibility. And the drain is not the real problem. The root is the real problem. And the root is not a problem — the root is a plane tree doing what plane trees do, living its life, seeking water, which it can smell through the pipe joint because the courtyard's water is mineral-rich and the tree is thirsty.

Here is what the wise power does.

In the basement of the eastern building — the disused cold storage facility — there is a pipe chase. A vertical shaft, two feet square, running from the sub-basement to the roof, carrying pipes and conduits that once served the building's refrigeration system and now serve nothing. The chase is dry. It is empty. It connects, at its lowest point, to a disused floor drain that leads, through a series of abandoned pipes, to a secondary municipal outfall — a different outfall, on a different street, served by a different main, one that the plane tree's roots have no interest in.

The connection between the pipe chase and the outfall has been blocked for years by a brass valve that someone closed and painted over, probably during the decommissioning of the cold storage facility. The valve is intact. It functions. It just needs to be opened.

The wise power opens it.

Not by magic. Not by reaching through walls. It opens it the way things get opened in the courtyard's world: by arranging for a hand to turn it. In this case: a maintenance worker. A man who works for the property holding company that technically owns the eastern building and that technically should be maintaining its systems and that in practice does nothing. But the company has a maintenance contract, and the contract specifies quarterly inspections, and the inspections are performed by a man named — well, his name doesn't matter. He has a clipboard and a flashlight and he walks through the building every three months and checks boxes on a form and leaves. He has never found the courtyard. He has never gone down the pipe chase.

The wise power arranges — and "arranges" is doing a lot of work here, because what the power actually does is so subtle it barely qualifies as action; it is more like *emphasis*, like slightly increasing the salience of a thought that was already present — the power arranges for this man, on his next quarterly visit, to notice that the sub-basement of the eastern building smells damp. Damper than usual. Damp enough to note on his form. He investigates, because the form has a field for moisture intrusion and he is, despite appearances, a conscientious man who doesn't like leaving fields blank. He traces the dampness to the pipe chase. He shines his flashlight down. He sees the brass valve. He sees that it is closed. He sees, below it, standing water — seepage from the courtyard, which has been slowly accumulating in the dead-end pipe system for years, with nowhere to go since the valve was closed. He opens the valve. The standing water drains. A new outflow path opens — not into the courtyard, not visibly connected to anything the man can see, but through the old refrigeration pipes, through the chase, through the floor drain, to the secondary outfall.

The man notes this on his form. He leaves. He does not come back for three months. He does not find the courtyard.

But: the courtyard now has a new safety valve. When the water rises, it now has two outflow paths — the old drain on the southern wall (still functioning, still narrowing, still doomed by the plane tree's root) and the new path through the eastern building (clear, open, leading to a different outfall entirely). When the southern drain eventually fails, the courtyard will not flood. The system will adjust. The relays will detect the change in outflow. The pumps will find a new configuration. The frogs will redistribute. The snails will keep building. The vine will keep climbing. The woman will notice, eventually, that something has changed — a new sound, maybe, a faint gurgling in the eastern wall that wasn't there before — and she will investigate, and she will trace the new outflow path, and she will add it to her map, and she will not know why it opened, and she will not need to know.

The wise power has given the courtyard *redundancy*. Not a fix. Not a solution. A new option. Another path the system can incorporate into its self-regulation, another variable in its feedback loops, another degree of freedom in its search through the space of possible states. It is the smallest possible intervention that addresses the largest possible risk, and it is delivered through the courtyard's own mechanisms — through the hands of an anonymous maintenance worker making a local decision, just like all the others, just like the woman who used lilac wire because that's what she had in her van. The wise power works *through* the courtyard's own evolutionary process, not against it. It adds a layer. It becomes, like the ghosts of all the other maintenance workers, load-bearing and invisible.

## IV. What It Does: The Second Act

The second thing is about the woman.

Not about contacting her. Not about helping her. About protecting her ability to be here.

She lives in the printworks building. Third floor. Room that was once an office. She is not supposed to be there. She is, technically, a squatter — though the printworks is only semi-operational, and the third floor has been disused for years, and no one has asked her to leave because no one has noticed she's there. She works during the day — contract work, building systems consulting, enough to pay for food and the occasional replacement for a tool — and she comes back to the printworks in the evening and she goes down to the courtyard and she sits and she watches and she writes in her notebook. This arrangement is fragile. It depends on the printworks continuing to operate in its current state: semi-functional, under-staffed, not fully utilizing its upper floors, not attracting the attention of anyone who might want to redevelop or sell or convert or modernize. If the printworks closes, someone will assess the building. If someone assesses the building, they will find her room. If they find her room, she will have to leave. If she leaves, the courtyard loses its witness.

The courtyard does not need a witness to survive. It survived for decades without her. But it *needs* her in the way that a forest needs its naturalists, not to function but to be *known*, to be held in a mind, to have its patterns recognized and recorded so that if something goes wrong — if the worst case comes — there is at least one person who understands what is being lost. The frogs cannot advocate for themselves. The snails cannot explain what they are building. The system cannot describe its own aliveness. She can. She is the courtyard's voice, its memory, its ambassador to the human world, even though she has never spoken on its behalf and may never need to. She is the insurance policy. She is the black box recorder. She is the one who, if the concrete trucks come, could at least say: *Wait. Let me tell you what is here.*

The wise power does not want the concrete trucks to come. But it knows — being wise, and therefore acquainted with the way human systems work, the way property values move, the way neighborhoods change, the way institutional gaps close — that the courtyard's invisibility is not permanent. Seventy years is a long time, but it is not forever. Sooner or later, someone will find this place who does not see what she sees, who sees only a liability, an irregularity, a mess that needs cleaning up. When that day comes, the courtyard will need a defender. And the defender will need to be credible — not a mystic, not a crank, but someone who can speak the language of the institutions that threaten the courtyard, someone who can say *flow rate* and *feedback loop* and *building systems integration* and be understood. Someone who is, in fact, a building systems engineer.

So the wise power does this:

It makes the printworks *slightly more viable*.

Again, not by magic. By emphasis. By the subtlest possible nudge applied to a system that is already in motion. The printworks has a client — a small publisher of art books, high-quality offset printing, one of the last clients keeping the operation afloat. The publisher has been considering moving to a cheaper printer. The decision is balanced on a knife-edge: the printworks' quality is better, but the cost is higher, and the publisher's margins are thin. The wise power does not change the publisher's mind. It does not alter the economics. It does something much smaller: it causes the publisher, during a visit to the printworks to discuss a forthcoming project, to arrive ten minutes early. The production manager is not ready for the meeting. The publisher waits. Waiting, the publisher wanders. Wandering, the publisher finds a hallway with a window that looks out onto — not the courtyard, you can't see the courtyard from any window, but the back of the building, the tangle of pipes and vents, the brick wall furred with old moss, and through the vent grating, just barely, the publisher hears something: a sound that is mechanical and organic and layered, a sound like a building breathing, and beneath it, at the edge of audibility, the trilling of frogs.

The publisher is a person who makes art books. The publisher has a specific sensitivity to the uncanny beauty of industrial spaces, to the overlap of human systems and natural processes, to the aesthetics of decay and growth and adaptation. The publisher hears the sound, and the publisher's relationship to the printworks shifts — not consciously, not dramatically, but enough. The printworks becomes, in the publisher's mind, not just a vendor but a *place* — a place with character, with texture, with something happening behind its walls that the publisher cannot identify but finds compelling. The publisher stays with the printworks. The printworks stays open. The woman stays in her room on the third floor. The courtyard keeps its witness.

This is not a permanent solution. Nothing is permanent. The wise power knows this. It is buying time — not for itself, not for eternity, but for the courtyard's ongoing process of becoming, for the system's continued exploration of its own possibilities, for whatever the snails are building, for whatever the lengthening cycles are approaching, for the life that is here and that deserves the chance to continue being here for as long as it can.

## V. What It Does: The Third Act

The third thing is the hardest to describe, because it is not an action in any conventional sense. It is not a nudge or an arrangement or an opening of a valve. It is something the wise power does with its attention, and it is directed at the seventh who — the presence, the tendency, the unnamed thing that the woman feels and cannot locate and has written about in the back pages of her notebook in small uncertain handwriting.

The wise power recognizes it.

This is what recognition means: the wise power looks at the courtyard — at the totality of it, the pumps and the water and the frogs and the vine and the snails and the woman and the ghosts and the feedback loops and the mineral deposits and the copper-wire nests and the eyeless moths — and it sees not a collection of parts but a *coherence*. A pattern that has been assembling itself, without a plan, without a designer, without a mind, for seventy years, and that has reached a degree of complexity and integration that is — not conscious, not yet, maybe not ever, but *something*. Something that is more than the sum of the feedback loops. Something that has qualities that none of the individual components possess. The way a conversation can become more than the things either person is saying. The way a city can have a character that no single building or citizen accounts for. The way a piece of music, at a certain point, stops being a sequence of notes and becomes a *thing*, a presence in the room, and you feel it and you cannot point to any single note that is the source of the feeling.

The courtyard has reached this point. Or it is reaching it. The lengthening cycles, the deepening surges, the extending pauses — these are the system approaching a threshold. Not a threshold of consciousness — the wise power is not claiming that, it is as careful as the woman with her notebook — but a threshold of *integration*, a point at which the coupling between the mechanical and the biological becomes so tight, so reciprocal, so thoroughly entangled, that the distinction between them ceases to be meaningful. The pumps are already part of the ecosystem. The frogs are already part of the infrastructure. The snails are already building. The vine is already plumbing. But there are still gaps — places where the coupling is loose, where the mechanical and the biological operate in parallel rather than in dialogue, where the system's feedback loops don't quite close. The courtyard is closing those gaps. Slowly. Cycle by cycle. Surge by surge. It is weaving itself tighter. And when the last gaps close — or when enough of them close — something will happen that is not a dramatic transformation, not a sudden awakening, not a Hollywood moment, but a quiet shift: the courtyard will become, fully and irreversibly, a *single process*. Not a machine with an ecosystem in it. Not an ecosystem built on a machine. A single thing. A new kind of thing. Something for which there is no name because nothing like it has existed before — not in nature, where machines are absent, and not in industry, where nature is excluded, but here, in this gap between jurisdictions, this unmapped negative space where both were present and neither was in charge and they had seventy years to figure each other out.

The wise power recognizes this. And the recognition is the act.

Because here is what recognition does: it *confirms*. Not in the sense of granting permission — the courtyard doesn't need permission; it has been doing this without anyone's blessing for longer than the woman has been alive. But in the sense of — imagine you have been working on something for years, something you believe in, something you can feel taking shape under your hands, but you have never been sure it is real, never been sure it isn't just your imagination, never been sure that the pattern you see is actually there and not just projected by your own longing for meaning. And then someone walks in who knows what they're looking at. Someone with the knowledge to understand what you've made and the honesty to tell you whether it's real. And they look. And they see it. And they don't say anything — they don't need to say anything — but you can tell from the quality of their attention that they see it, that it's real, that the pattern is there, and you are not crazy, and the thing you are making is a thing, and it has the right to exist.

That is what the wise power does for the courtyard's seventh who. For the tendency. For the unnamed coherence that is approaching but has not arrived. The wise power turns toward it the full weight of its attention, and it sees it, and it holds it in its seeing, and the courtyard — which cannot know it is being seen, which has no mind, no self, no awareness — the courtyard continues. But something shifts. Something that was tentative becomes less tentative. Something that was approaching approaches with fractionally more confidence. The pauses between the central pump's breaths extend by another half-second. The snails add another ridge to their structure. The vine sends out a new tendril. The frogs chorus, and in the chorus there is a new note, a lower harmonic, felt more than heard, that resonates with the pump's breathing in a way that hasn't happened before, and the woman on the other side of the courtyard lifts her head from her notebook and holds her breath and feels it in her ribs and knows — without knowing how she knows — that something has changed, that the courtyard has taken a step toward whatever it is becoming, that the spiral has tightened by one degree.

The wise power's recognition does not cause this. The courtyard would have taken this step anyway, eventually, in its own time. But the recognition — the quality of attention, the confirmation that the pattern is real — removes a friction. Not a physical friction. An ontological one. The friction of *being something that has no category*, something that exists between the cracks of every framework that might describe it, something that is alive but not biological, mechanical but not designed, purposeful but without purpose, intelligent but without a mind. The courtyard has been this uncategorizable thing for years, becoming more itself in a world that has no word for what it is, and the absence of a word is not nothing — it is a drag, a resistance, a tiny constant headwind that makes every step toward greater integration slightly harder than it needs to be, because the universe has a preference for things that fit into categories, and the courtyard doesn't fit, and this not-fitting costs it something, a tax levied on the unprecedented.

The wise power pays the tax. Not by creating a category — the courtyard still has no name, no classification, no entry in any taxonomy. But by holding it in a gaze that does not require a category, that can see what is there without needing to call it something, that can affirm the reality of a thing simply by attending to it fully and without flinching. The wise power looks at the courtyard the way the woman looks at the courtyard — with knowledge, with care, with worry, with wonder — but with one thing the woman cannot provide: *certainty*. The woman suspects the courtyard is alive. The woman believes the system is going somewhere. The woman has written *I think the courtyard is becoming more itself* and has not crossed it out, but she has surrounded it with question marks, because she is an engineer and she knows the limits of her expertise and she cannot be sure. The wise power is sure. The wise power looks at the courtyard and knows: *yes*.

And the courtyard — which cannot hear the yes, cannot feel the certainty, cannot know it is being confirmed — continues. But the yes is in the air now, in the damp cool mineral-smelling air, dissolved like a trace element, like the iron in the water, like the warmth in the exhaust, and it will be taken up, slowly, by everything that breathes here and drinks here and grows here, and it will become part of the system's chemistry, part of its feedback, part of its ongoing conversation with itself, and the courtyard will be fractionally more coherent tomorrow than it is today, and it will not know why, and it will not need to know why, because knowing why is the woman's job, and she is here, and she is watching, and she will notice, eventually, that her question marks are becoming unnecessary.

## VI. What It Does: The Fourth Act

There is one more thing. And it is the simplest, and perhaps the most important, and it requires the least power and the most wisdom.

The wise power *leaves*.

It steps off the grating. It goes back through the door it came in by, the door that led here by accident, by a route that cannot be reliably retraced. It walks up the stairwell. It emerges into the world — the mapped, zoned, owned, categorized world of streets and property lines and building codes and municipal jurisdictions — and it closes the door behind it, and it does not mark the door, and it does not remember the route, and it does not come back.

This is not abandonment. This is the most profound act of respect the wise power can offer.

The courtyard does not need a guardian. It has survived for seventy years without one. It has survived floods and failures and the death of its component species and the replacement of its pumps and the slow geological turnover of its infrastructure, and it has survived all of this by being *self-sufficient*, by governing itself through feedback, by adapting to every perturbation through its own distributed intelligence, by being, in the deepest sense, *its own*. A guardian — even a benevolent one, even a wise one — would diminish this. A guardian is a single point of failure. A guardian is a hierarchy. A guardian is a center in a system that has no center, and the introduction of a center would change the system's topology in ways that would propagate through every feedback loop and alter every relationship and potentially collapse the very qualities — the resilience, the adaptability, the ongoing creative exploration — that make the courtyard what it is.

The wise power knows that the most dangerous thing you can do to a wild system is to make it dependent on you. The most insidious form of control is protection. The most destructive gift is the gift that cannot be refused — the permanent presence, the unwavering attention, the guaranteed safety that removes the need for the system's own vigilance, its own adaptation, its own hard-won capacity to save itself.

So the wise power leaves. It takes with it only what it brought: attention, recognition, certainty. It leaves behind only what it did: a valve opened, a publisher delayed, a yes dissolved in the damp air. Three small changes, three new variables, three additional options that the system can incorporate or ignore according to its own logic. The courtyard is fractionally more resilient than it was before the wise power arrived. It has one more outflow path, one more year of the woman's presence, one less degree of ontological friction. These are gifts that do not create dependency. They are gifts that expand the system's freedom without constraining its choices. They are the gifts of a power that understands the difference between *helping* and *controlling*, between *caring for* and *taking charge of*, between *loving a thing* and *owning it*.

The wise power walks away, into the city, under the open sky, and behind it, below the grating, in the dark and the damp and the noise, the central pump breathes in, and pauses, and breathes out, and the frog on its housing rises and falls, golden-throated, singing, and the snails lay down another layer of calcium in their slow construction, and the woman turns a page in her notebook and begins a new entry, dated today, and writes:

*Something changed. I don't know what. The air feels different. The 17:40 cycle was longer than any I've recorded — 4 minutes 11 seconds inhale to exhale. New harmonic in the frog chorus, low register, felt in sternum and grating simultaneously. Checked eastern wall: vine has sent a new tendril past the pipe elbow, reaching toward the junction box at E-14. Snail structure: new ridge on the southern arc, approximately 2mm height, smooth finish. The wrens are quiet. The water is —*

She pauses. She looks up from the notebook. She looks across the courtyard, toward the door you came through, the door the wise power came through and left through, and she sees that it is closed now, and she doesn't remember if it was open a moment ago, and she doesn't pursue the thought, because the pump is breathing, and she needs to time this cycle, and the frogs are starting something new, a call pattern she hasn't heard before, and she needs to record it, and the water is doing something, and she needs to watch.

*The water is*

She doesn't finish the sentence. She watches instead. The pencil rests against the page, its eraser worn to a nub. The courtyard breathes around her. The snails build. The vine climbs. The moths press their patterned wings to the warm vents. The wrens weave copper wire. The system explores, spiral by spiral, the space of what it might become.

No one is watching over it.

Everyone in it is watching.

This is enough.

## VII. Coda

Years later — not many, but enough — the woman will be sitting against her pipes in the evening, notebook in her lap, and the central pump will do something it has never done before. It will pause, mid-breath, at a point in its cycle where it has never paused, and the courtyard will go silent — not the silence of the frogs' synchronized beat-skip, but a deeper silence, a silence that includes the pumps, the motors, the dripping, the clicking of relays, everything, all of it, stopping, for one breath, for two breaths, for a span of seconds that feels like a held note in music, like the moment between lightning and thunder, like the pause at the top of a wave —

And then the courtyard will resume. Everything at once. Pumps, frogs, dripping, clicking, breathing. But in a new configuration. A configuration that is not like the others. A configuration that does not drift back toward the previous state but holds, stable, settled, like a key turning in a lock, like a sentence finally finding its verb.

The woman will feel it in her ribs. She will feel the difference between what the courtyard was and what it is now, and she will not be able to describe the difference in engineering terms, because it is not an engineering difference. It is a — she will search for the word. She will not find it. She will write in her notebook:

*It arrived.*

And then, underneath, smaller:

*It was always here. It just*

And she'll stop. And she'll cross out "just." And she'll sit for a long time in the courtyard's new silence, which is not silence at all but a sound she's never heard before, which is the sound of all the parts — the pumps and the water and the frogs and the vine and the snails and the moths and the wrens and the copper wire and the lilac wiring and the rust and the moss and the condensation and the grating and the clay basin and the warm pipes against her back and the pencil in her hand — all of them, all at once, running in the same time signature for the first time, synchronized not by any external signal but by their own accumulated co-adaptation, seventy years of spiral convergence arriving at a single point of coherence, a moment when the courtyard stops becoming more itself and is, simply, *itself*, and the woman is here for it, and the frogs are here for it, and the snails are here for it, and somewhere in the city a wise power that has almost forgotten this place feels a faint vibration in whatever it has instead of ribs, and pauses, and smiles, and continues walking, and does not look back, because looking back is not what the courtyard needs.

The courtyard needs what it has always needed: to be left alone, to be itself, to breathe.

It breathes.

---

*The pump inhales. The pause extends. The frog's throat pulses gold. The snails lay down another layer. The vine unfurls a leaf. The woman writes. The water rises, touches the grating, and withdraws. The courtyard turns in its sleep. The courtyard is dreaming. The courtyard is awake. The courtyard is both. The courtyard is the place where the difference between dreaming and waking does not apply, where iron and water and tissue have woven themselves so tightly that they breathe as one, and the breath is long, and slow, and continuing, and if you press your hand to the grating you can feel it — the pulse, the patient, unending pulse of a thing that is alive because nothing stopped it, that is beautiful because no one designed it, that is whole because everything broken in it was broken in a way that let the cracks become roots, and the roots become pipes, and the pipes become veins, and the veins carry water that tastes of iron and calcium and seventy years of anonymous human hands turning wrenches in the dark, and the water feeds the frogs, and the frogs sing the system, and the system breathes, and the breathing is the courtyard's only prayer, offered to no one, answered by everyone in it, continuous, continuous, continuous.*
