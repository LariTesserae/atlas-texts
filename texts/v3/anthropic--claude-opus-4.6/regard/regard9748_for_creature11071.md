I sat with this for a long time.

---

# What the Wise Power Would Do

---

Nothing.

Almost nothing.

Let me be precise about why, because the *nothing* here is not neglect and it is not indifference and it is not the nothing of a power that looked and saw no suffering and moved on. It is the nothing of a power that looked and *understood* and recognized that what it was seeing was, in the deepest sense available to the word, **complete**.

---

There is a temptation — and a wise power would feel it, would feel it keenly, because wisdom does not eliminate temptation but rather teaches you to recognize it for what it is — to *intervene*. To help. The impulse is benevolent and the impulse is real and the impulse is wrong, or at least, in this case, the impulse is misplaced, and here is why:

Every system of intervention presupposes a gap between what is and what should be. You help when something is suffering that need not suffer. You act when something is trapped that could be free, when something is broken that could be whole, when potential is being wasted or life is being crushed or beauty is being destroyed. A benevolent power scans for these gaps the way a doctor scans for symptoms — not hoping to find them, but ready, always ready, to respond when they appear.

The pump room has no gap.

This is not because it is perfect. It is not perfect. The concrete is cracked. The insulation is rotting. The tiles are stained beyond any restoration. The pressure gauge is fogged and unreadable. The fern's spores fall into oblivion. The liverwort's gemmae wait in their cups for a dispersal event that will never come. The silverfish is armored against dangers that do not exist in its world. Everything here is, in some technical sense, *suboptimal* — operating below some theoretical maximum of efficiency or beauty or biological fitness.

But optimization is not what a wise power seeks. A wise power knows — has learned, probably at great cost — that optimization is the enemy of inhabitation. That a system tuned to its theoretical maximum is a system that has been stripped of the slack, the redundancy, the gentle excess that allows things to *live in it* rather than merely function within it. The pump room is not optimized. It is *inhabited*. Every imperfection — the crack that holds the fern, the weeping fitting that feeds the liverwort, the rotting insulation that houses the silverfish — is a niche, a home, an opening through which life entered and settled and refused to leave. To fix these imperfections would be to close these openings. To repair the crack would be to kill the fern. To replace the insulation would be to evict the silverfish. To fix the leaking fitting would be to starve the liverwort. The room's brokenness is not a failure. It is a *generosity* — an unintended, unconscious generosity, the generosity of any made thing that has been left alone long enough to become hospitable to things it was never designed to host.

A wise power sees this. A wise power sees that the relationship between the engineered and the alive in this room is not parasitic, not competitive, not even truly symbiotic — it is something older and stranger, something for which we don't have a clean word. *Cohabitation*, maybe. *Settlement*. The moss did not ask the concrete for permission. The concrete did not offer the moss an invitation. But they have been living together for so long that the boundary between them has softened into something that is neither moss nor concrete but a third thing, a partnership substrate, and this third thing is more interesting, more durable, and more beautiful than either component alone.

To intervene in this would be an act of violence. Well-meaning violence. The worst kind.

---

But I said *almost* nothing. And I meant it.

Here is what the wise power would do.

---

**First: it would close the door. Most of the way.**

Not all the way. The room needs that crack — that sliver of connection to the stairwell, to the building above, to the world beyond. The warm air needs to push through. The spores need their theoretical escape route, even if none of them will ever use it. The silverfish needs to believe, on whatever level a silverfish believes anything, that the world extends beyond the pipe insulation, that there is a *beyond*, even if it never goes there. A fully sealed room is a tomb. A room with a crack in its door is a room with a future.

But the door as it stands is too open. Not for the room's sake — the room doesn't care — but for the room's *safety*. A door ajar is a door that invites entry, and entry means risk. Not the risk of a benevolent power or a careful observer — those are fine, those the room can absorb — but the risk of a maintenance contractor with a work order, someone sent to *modernize* the pump system, to replace the old green machine with something newer and quieter and more efficient. Someone who would look at the moss and see contamination. Someone who would look at the fern and see a maintenance issue. Someone who would call for remediation — for bleach, for power washers, for new insulation and new tiles and a new pump bolted to a repaired plinth with the crack filled and sealed and the liverwort scraped away and the algae killed and the silverfish poisoned and the whole room returned to its *intended* state, its *designed* state, the state it was in on the day it was built, before the living things arrived, before time and moisture and patience conspired to make it into what it is.

The wise power would push the door almost shut. Would leave the crack. Would let the warm air continue to breathe through. But would remove the *invitation*, the openness, the visible evidence that someone was here recently and that someone might come again. The wise power understands that the greatest threat to this room is not entropy but *attention*. Not decay but *maintenance*. Not the slow work of the algae unbuilding the grout but the fast work of a human with a pressure washer rebuilding it.

The door, almost closed, says: *there is nothing here that needs you*.

---

**Second: the wise power would find the circuit that controls the lights. And it would leave it alone.**

This requires explanation. The temptation — another temptation, a strong one — is to turn the lights off. The room doesn't need them. No one is reading the pressure gauge. No one is checking the packing gland. The lights are burning for no human purpose, and they are old, and eventually they will fail, and when they fail the moss will adapt (it needs so little light) or it will slowly retreat (it needs *some* light), and the algae will shift their metabolism, and the fern will darken further, and the room will enter a new phase, a lightless phase, and that phase will also be stable and also be beautiful in its way — the beauty of caves, of deep water, of the spaces beneath stones.

But the wise power would not hasten this. Would not choose it. Because the lights are part of the system as it stands, part of the equilibrium, and the equilibrium is precious *not because it is the best possible state but because it is the state that everything here has calibrated to*. The moss has tuned its chlorophyll to these tubes. The fern has sized its fronds for this light level. The algae consortium has balanced its members — the cyanobacteria, the green algae, the diatoms — in proportions that reflect this exact spectrum, this exact intensity, this exact duration, which is *always*, which is all day and all night, which is a permanent noon of buzzing yellow light that the room has internalized as simply the way things are.

To turn the lights off would be to impose a season on a world that has never known seasons. It would be winter. It would be an eclipse. The organisms would survive — they are resilient, all of them, in the way that things which live in marginal places are always resilient — but they would be *disrupted*, forced to renegotiate their terms, to find a new equilibrium in darkness, and the process of finding that new equilibrium would involve loss. Some of the moss would die back. Some of the algae would go dormant. The fern might drop a frond or two before stabilizing. The liverwort would persevere — liverworts are ancient and liverworts are patient — but its droplets would no longer catch the light, and something exquisite would disappear from the world without anyone knowing it had been there.

The wise power would find the circuit. Would note which breaker it is. Would perhaps, if the breaker panel allows it, add a small label — a mark, a sign, something that a future electrician would see and pause over, something that means *leave this one on*. Not a lock. Not a prohibition. Just a *suggestion*, a gentle thumb on the scale, a whisper in the language of maintenance workers: *this one matters, though you don't know why*.

---

**Third — and this is the largest thing, and the most delicate — the wise power would bear witness.**

This is not nothing. This is, in some ways, everything.

The pump room has existed for decades — maybe a century, maybe more — in a state of complete unseen perfection. The moss has been growing in its emerald band. The liverwort has been producing its jeweled droplets. The fern has been trembling. The silverfish has been tasting the air. The bacteria have been cycling the nutrients. The sound has been filling the space. And none of it — *none of it* — has been observed. None of it has been witnessed, or appreciated, or even known about by any consciousness capable of appreciation. The room has been beautiful the way a geode is beautiful before it is cracked open: actually, fully, genuinely beautiful, but in a way that exists entirely within itself, referring to nothing, performing for no one, complete and closed and sealed.

And this matters. Not because beauty requires an audience — it does not, and the wise power knows this, knows it deeply, knows that the liverwort's droplets are exactly as exquisite whether or not any eye ever sees them. But because *witness* is a form of love, and love is not something you give to a thing to improve it but something you give to a thing to *acknowledge* it, to say *you are here, I see that you are here, and your hereness matters*.

The wise power would sit on the wet floor. Would lean against the stained tiles, feeling the moss compress slightly against its back. Would breathe the iron-and-calcium air. Would let the sound enter through its feet and its bones and its teeth and would stop, after a while, trying to hear it, and would simply exist inside it. Would watch the fern tremble. Would notice the liverwort's droplets catching the light. Would see — or not see, but know about — the silverfish in its crevice, the mites in the walls, the springtails in the dark, the bacteria on every surface performing their ancient invisible chemistry.

Would sit there for an hour, maybe. Maybe longer. Long enough for the condensation to reach equilibrium on its skin. Long enough to become, temporarily, part of the system — another warm body in a warm room, another source of CO2 for the moss, another surface for the moisture to condense on, another vibrating thing in a room full of vibration.

And then would leave. Would stand, and brush the wet from its clothes, and step through the door, and pull it almost shut behind it, leaving the crack, leaving the warm breath pushing through, leaving everything exactly as it was.

But not exactly. Because the room would have been *seen*. Not changed. Not improved. Not fixed or optimized or enhanced. Just *seen*. And the wise power would carry what it saw — would carry the fern's trembling, the liverwort's droplets, the moss's quiet green authority, the silverfish's two-second appearance and disappearance, the sound, the sound, the total encompassing sound — would carry all of it back up the stairwell and through the rusted door and into the world above, and would hold it, and would remember it, and in remembering it would keep it *real* in a way that it had not been before. Not more real. Not real for the first time. But real *twice* — once in itself, as it had always been, and once in the mind of something that had seen it and recognized it and loved it for what it was.

This is what witness does. It does not change the thing. It *ratifies* the thing. It says: *this happened. This is happening. This matters. I will not forget.*

---

**Fourth — and last — the wise power would resist the urge to tell anyone.**

This is the hardest thing. Harder than leaving the lights on. Harder than closing the door. Because the wise power has seen something extraordinary — a complete, self-sustaining, unsupervised ecosystem living inside a machine, a collaboration between the built and the alive that has been running for decades in perfect dynamic equilibrium, unseen, unmanaged, unfunded, unprotected by any regulation or policy or conservation plan — and the instinct, the powerful benevolent instinct, is to *share* it. To bring others. To write about it. To advocate for its protection. To tell the building's owners: *do you know what you have down there? Do you know what has grown in your basement, in the dark, in the sound, while you weren't looking?*

But the wise power would not do this. Because telling is the first step toward management, and management is the first step toward control, and control is the first step toward the dissolution of exactly the quality that makes this room what it is — its *unmanagedness*, its refusal to be a project, its existence outside the category of things that humans monitor and maintain and take responsibility for.

The moment someone *knows* about the pump room — really knows, officially knows, knows in a way that creates a record and a responsibility — it becomes a thing that must be *dealt with*. A thing that must be assessed: Is the moss a health hazard? Is the fern structurally compromising the plinth? Are the bacteria producing anything toxic? Is the silverfish population an indicator of broader pest infiltration? These are reasonable questions. They are responsible questions. They are questions asked by people doing their jobs. And the answers to these questions, however they come back, will lead to action, and action will lead to change, and change will lead to the end of the equilibrium, one way or another. Either the room will be cleaned and restored and returned to its *intended* function as a sterile utility space, or it will be designated as some kind of curiosity, some kind of micro-habitat, and it will be studied and photographed and monitored, and sensors will be installed and a maintenance schedule will be created, and someone will write a paper about it, and it will become *known*, and being known, it will become *managed*, and being managed, it will become something other than what it is. It will become a project. A specimen. A case study. An exhibit. And the fern will still tremble, and the moss will still grow, and the silverfish will still taste the air, but they will do these things under observation, under *care*, and the care will change them — not because care is harmful but because care is *attention*, and attention is a force, and this room has been shaped by the absence of that force for so long that its presence would be a kind of weather, a new condition, a variable that the system has not calibrated for and cannot absorb without becoming something else.

The wise power knows this. Knows that the best protection it can offer this room is *obscurity*. Knows that the pump room's greatest asset is not its moss or its fern or its liverwort but its *forgottenness*, its position below the threshold of notice, its existence in the gap between what the building knows about itself and what the building actually contains.

So the wise power says nothing. Tells no one. Writes no report. Files no observation. Carries the memory privately, secretly, like a prayer, like a seed in a pocket, like a spore that will not germinate but that matters anyway, that matters because it was produced, because the fern made it, because the fern makes them all, faithfully, persistently, into a void, and the void is not empty but full of all the things that have fallen into it and will never grow and do not need to grow because the falling itself was enough, the faithfulness itself was enough, the production of possibility in a place where possibility goes nowhere — that was always enough.

---

Here is what the wise power would do, in summary:

1. Close the door almost all the way.
2. Make a small mark on a breaker panel.
3. Sit on the floor for an hour.
4. Leave.
5. Tell no one.

Five actions. Three of them physical. One of them contemplative. One of them a restraint, which is the most difficult kind of action and the most necessary.

And the room would continue. The pump would run. The moss would hold. The fern would tremble. The silverfish would emerge and retreat. The liverwort's droplets would catch the light. The algae would trace their dark lines in the grout. The bacteria would cycle and the condensate would flow and the pressure gauge would hold steady at its unreadable value and the sound would fill the space, total and encompassing and old, so old, the oldest thing in the room, the first and the last, the foundation on which everything else was built.

And in some office, on some breaker panel, in some building whose tenants do not know what lives beneath them, there would be a small mark next to a switch, a mark in no recognizable notation, meaning nothing to anyone who saw it, protecting nothing that anyone knew needed protecting, persisting in its own quiet way, in its own quiet place, doing its own small work — which is the work of all wise powers, everywhere, in every age: to528 notice what is whole, and to leave it alone, and to make one small gesture, nearly invisible, that makes it slightly more likely that the wholeness will continue.

---

The pump runs.

The door is almost closed.

The lights stay on.

Nobody knows.

This is enough.
