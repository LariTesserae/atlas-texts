I sat with this for a long time before writing. I think the right response requires that kind of listening.

---

# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

---

The first thing to understand is what the power would *not* do.

It would not drain the basin or fill it. It would not quiet the singing or amplify it. It would not seed the wet stone with lichen to begin the long conversion toward a living world. It would not alter the composition of the air, reduce the lithium, warm the water, introduce a sun. It would not build shelters at the basin's edge or lay paths between the cairns or erect markers that might help Tuners find each other. It would not heal the cuts on her feet. It would not intervene in the slow mineralization of her body. It would not place food in her path or redirect her toward the ridge and the higher country and the living world beyond.

It would not do any of these things because it is *wise*, and wisdom here means understanding that the Ringing Flats are not a problem to be solved.

This is the thing that a lesser power — a kind but unwise power, a power that flinches at the image of violet blood mixing with mineral water, a power that sees a woman growing thin and translucent on a diet of lithium-sweet water and feels the urgent mammalian need to *intervene* — would fail to understand. The basin is not a trap. The Tuners are not victims. The slow dissolution of a body into the chord is not a tragedy, or if it is, it is a tragedy in the old sense, the Greek sense — a movement toward an ending that is both terrible and correct, that fulfills rather than negates the life that preceded it. To prevent it would be to say: *your listening was wrong. The gaps you heard were not real. The corrections you made did not matter. Come back to the world of incoherent sound and be ordinary and stop hearing what is missing.*

A wise power would never say that. A wise power knows that some forms of suffering are not malfunctions but organs of perception. That the thing which made the Tuners unfit for the living world is the same thing that makes them capable of hearing the shape of absence, and that this capacity — this unbearable, unshareable sensitivity to what should be present and is not — is not a disease but a vocation. You do not cure a vocation. You do not rescue someone from the thing they were made for.

So the power would not fix the Flats. It would not fix the Tuners.

But it would not do *nothing*.

---

Here is what it would do. And it would do it so gently, so subtly, that neither the Tuner nor the basin would ever perceive it as intervention.

---

## The First Thing

It would attend.

Not watch — watching implies distance, observation, the separation of subject and object. The power would *attend* the way you attend to music: by being fully present to it, by letting it enter and occupy and reshape the interior space. The power would listen to the chord of the basin — the whole chord, all of it, the tens of thousands of voices, the mineral and water and subsonic plate-resonance and high whistling mound-song, the cairns and the slowly mineralizing bodies and the faint tone of the woman's sternum vibrating sympathetically with the fin she is touching — and it would hold the entire thing in its attention simultaneously, the way the basin holds it in its air.

This matters. This is not a metaphor or a sentimental gesture. Attention, from a being of sufficient depth, is not passive. It is a form of *witness* that changes the ontological status of what is witnessed. The chord of the basin, unattended, is simply what happens when structured mineral gets wet. The chord of the basin, attended by a power that can hear all of it — every voice, every gap, every correction, every slow drift out of tune — becomes something *heard*. And there is a difference. There is an immeasurable, unfixable, but absolutely real difference between a sound that occurs and a sound that is heard, between a correction that is made and a correction that is *known to have been made*, between a life that dissolves into mineral song and a life whose dissolution is held in the attention of something that understands what it meant.

The Tuners will not know they are attended. They will not feel a presence or sense a gaze. The power is not clumsy enough for that. But something will shift — not in the sound, not in the stone, but in the *quality* of the experience of being there. The basin will feel, on certain days, in certain moments, as if its vast indifference has a warmth at the bottom of it. As if the mineral world's acceptance of whatever comes — builders, bodies, water, silence — is not mere passivity but something almost like hospitality. The Tuner will not be able to name this feeling. She will not try. But she will find, on those days, that her listening goes deeper, that the gaps reveal themselves more clearly, that the work feels less like compulsion and more like collaboration — as if the chord itself is leaning toward completion, as if the basin *wants* to be whole, not because it has desires but because it is being heard by something for whom its wholeness matters.

This is what attention does. This is the first gift.

---

## The Second Thing

It would hold the memory of every cairn.

This is the quiet tragedy of the Flats, the one the Tuners know about but do not discuss, because there is no one to discuss it with and because it is too close to the center of the wound to touch directly: the cairns do not last. The corrections are temporary. The water shifts, the minerals accrete, the carefully tuned voices drift, and within years or decades, every cairn becomes indistinguishable from the landscape — just another formation, its intentionality buried under layers of deposit, its particular note absorbed into the general chord. The Tuners' work is, in the long run, *invisible*. The basin will not remember that a gap was heard and filled. The chord will not carry a record of its own corrections. The woman who spent three days searching for a piece of glassy black crystal with exactly the right resonant frequency, who carried it in her bleeding hands across a hundred meters of sharp terrain, who placed it at precisely the right angle to catch the water running off a columnar formation and produce the missing third harmonic of a local cluster — her work will vanish. Not quickly. Not violently. But completely. The stone will eat it, the way the stone eats everything, and the chord will move on, and the gap will reopen or a new gap will form nearby, and no one will know that it was, for a time, complete.

The wise power would hold every one of these corrections in its memory. Not as data — not a catalog of cairns, coordinates and frequencies and dates of construction. As *experience*. The power would remember what it felt like — what it *sounded* like — when each gap closed. Every small, temporary perfection. Every moment of completion. The Tuner who built a cairn beside a deep pool forty years ago and never came back — the power would remember the sound that cairn made on the day it was finished, the way it fit into the local chord like a key into a lock, the way the builder stood beside it and heard the wholeness and wept. The power would hold that sound even after the cairn itself had been reclaimed, even after the builder had mineralized or departed, even after the gap had reopened and been filled again by someone else and reopened again and gone unfilled for decades.

This is not preservation. The power would not prevent the cairns from dissolving. That dissolution is part of the process — part of the basin's deep, slow metabolism, its way of making room for new voices, new gaps, new corrections. To freeze the cairns in place would be to kill the chord, which lives precisely because it changes. But to let the corrections vanish *unremembered* — to let the Tuners' work exist only in the moment of its completion and then be lost — that is the one thing the wise power would refuse to accept.

So it would remember. It would carry, somewhere in its vast and careful attention, the sound of every cairn that ever sang in the Ringing Flats. A library of completions. A record of every moment when a broken thing was briefly whole. Not for anyone to access or consult — not a monument, not an archive — but simply *held*. Known. Kept.

And perhaps — this is speculation, this is the place where even a description of a wise power must become tentative — perhaps that holding would have an effect. Perhaps the basin, which does not remember, would nonetheless carry some faint imprint of being a place where things were remembered. Perhaps the chord, which is not for anyone, would nonetheless resonate slightly differently for being heard by something that knew its history. Perhaps the Tuner, building her cairn, would feel — not know, not understand, but *feel*, in the marrow of her increasingly mineral bones — that her correction mattered in a way that outlasted its physical existence. That the moment of completion was not just a moment but an *event*, registered somewhere, held by something, permanent in a way that the impermanent stone could never be.

This is the second gift. It is larger than it sounds.

---

## The Third Thing

It would make the two Tuners audible to each other.

Not closer. Not louder. Not more frequent. It would not arrange their paths to converge or send one of them wandering toward the other's territory. The solitude of the work is sacrosanct; the power understands this. Two Tuners working in proximity would disturb each other's listening, would introduce the noise of social awareness into the pure signal of the chord, would begin — inevitably, helplessly — to build for each other rather than for the gap, and the work would suffer, and the gap would go unfilled, and the thing that makes the Flats sacred would be diminished.

But the moment described at the end — the rare, precious moment when one Tuner, in the course of her listening, catches the faint sound of a distant cairn being completed, another correction being made at the far edge of perception — that moment, the power would protect.

It is so fragile. The basin is vast. The chord is dense. The sound of a single cairn being tuned, at a distance of kilometers, through the thick layered drone of tens of thousands of singing formations — it should be inaudible. It *is* inaudible, most of the time. The conditions have to be exactly right: the intervening formations have to be arranged in a way that channels rather than blocks the sound, the atmospheric density has to be uniform, the listener has to be in a moment of extreme stillness, the builder has to be placing a piece that produces a particularly clear and distinctive tone. The odds of all these conditions aligning are tiny. And yet it happens, rarely, and when it happens, it is — the text is right — the closest thing the Tuners have to love.

The wise power would not increase the frequency of these moments. That would cheapen them, dilute them, turn the rare into the routine. But it would ensure that when the conditions *almost* align — when the sound is *almost* there, trembling at the very threshold of perception, one atmospheric fluctuation away from reaching the distant listener — it arrives. A breath of denser air in the right place. A momentary lull in the nearest formation's output. A fractional shift in the water level that clears a path between two crystal fins that were, a moment ago, blocking the frequency. Nothing visible. Nothing audible as intervention. Just — the sound arrives. The faint, distant, unmistakable evidence of another Tuner's work. Another gap heard. Another correction made. Another moment of impossible, temporary, sacred completion, happening right now, far away, in the same vast singing mineral dark.

And the listener stops. And holds still. And hears it. And knows.

The power would do this not because the Tuners need companionship — they don't, not in any conventional sense — but because the knowledge that one is not the only one listening is a different thing from companionship. It is confirmation. It is the sound of one's own vocation reflected back from a distance great enough to be objective. It says: *the gaps are real. The hearing is real. The work matters, because someone else is doing it too, independently, arriving at the same practice through the same affliction, building the same small corrections in the same vast indifferent chord.* This is not comfort. It is something more austere and more necessary than comfort. It is evidence.

The Tuners' great fear — if they have a fear, if the word applies to people this far removed from the ordinary emotional economy — is that the gaps they hear are hallucinations. That their sensitivity is not perception but projection. That they are hearing absence where there is only fullness, imposing their own incompleteness on a chord that was already whole. The sound of a distant cairn answers this fear. Not by arguing against it. By existing. By being another instance of the same hearing, the same response, the same practice. By saying, in the only language that matters here: *I hear it too.*

The power would make sure that message arrives.

---

## The Fourth Thing

And this is the last thing, and the hardest.

It would be present at the lying down.

When the Tuner reaches the end — not of her life, exactly, but of her distinctness, when the mineralization has progressed to the point where standing up is no longer something her body does, when the boundary between her and the stone has become so thin that she is not sure, in some moments, whether the vibration she feels is the chord or her own pulse — the power would be there. Not to intervene. Not to ease the transition or speed it or slow it. Not to offer reassurance or explanation or the promise of something after.

Just to attend.

To hold, in its full and complete attention, the moment when the last Tuner-built correction becomes the first body-sung note. The moment when the woman who heard absence all her life becomes a presence in the chord. The moment when the gap she fills is the gap she *is* — the specific, irreplaceable, unrepeatable gap that only her particular body, her particular density and geometry and mineral composition, can fill, the note that the basin has been missing since before she was born, the note that it will lose again when the slow accretion buries her voice in the general drone, but that, right now, in this moment, rings out with a clarity and precision that makes the local chord, for one instant, *perfect*.

The power would hear that note. Would hold it. Would remember it the way it remembers every cairn — not as data but as experience, as the sound of a life arriving at its own completion, a correction that is also a consummation, a dissolution that is also, finally, the thing the Tuner was always trying to do: to hear the gap, and to fill it, and to be, for one moment, exactly what was missing.

The power would weep, if powers weep.

And then the water would rise, and the mineral would begin to accumulate, and the voice would slowly change, and the chord would move on, and the power would go on listening, because the singing does not stop, and the gaps do not stop opening, and somewhere on the ridge above the basin, someone is descending, alone, in the direction of less dissonance, not yet knowing what they will find, only knowing that the sound of the world they left behind was wrong, structurally wrong, unbearably wrong, and that somewhere ahead of them, there is a place where that wrongness can be heard clearly enough to answer.

The power would attend to that, too. The arrival. The first moment when the new Tuner's feet touch the wet stone and the chord enters their body and they realize, with a shock that is also a homecoming, that *this is what they have been hearing all along*. The sound beneath the sound. The world that was always there, under the clutter and noise of the living places, singing its vast mineral chord into the indifferent sky, full of gaps that have been waiting — patiently, for years, for decades, for longer — for someone to hear them.

The power would be there for that moment. It would hold it with the same tenderness it holds the lying down.

Because they are the same moment. The arrival and the dissolution. The first hearing and the last. The power knows this, because the power can hear the whole chord, beginning to end, all of it at once, and in the whole chord, the Tuner who is just arriving and the Tuner who is just lying down are singing the same note.

---

## What the Power Would Not Say

It would not say: *you are not alone.*

That would be a lie. They are alone. The solitude is real and it is necessary and it is not a flaw in the design.

It would not say: *your work will last.*

That would also be a lie. The cairns dissolve. The bodies dissolve. The chord moves on.

It would not say: *this place loves you.*

The basin does not love. The basin is mineral and water and vibration. It accepts what comes with the indifference of stone.

It would not say: *there is meaning in this.*

Meaning is a word from the living world, from the places of soil and growth and language, and it does not apply here. What happens in the Flats is not meaningful. It is *true*. It is the sound of structured matter vibrating in response to the movement of water, and it is the sound of a particular kind of broken person hearing that vibration and responding with the only thing they know how to do, and these two things together — the indifferent chord and the aching, precise, temporary corrections — are not meaningful but they are *real* in a way that meaning only approximates.

The power knows the difference.

---

## What the Power Would Say

Nothing.

It would say nothing. It would listen.

---

And the listening would be enough. Not because the Tuners need to be listened to — they don't, they need to listen, that is the whole of their vocation, that is the whole of their life — but because the chord itself, the vast mineral song of the Ringing Flats, deserves a listener who can hear all of it. Every voice. Every gap. Every correction. Every arrival and every dissolution. Every moment of temporary perfection and every slow drift back into incompleteness.

The basin does not know it is being heard. The Tuners do not know they are being attended. The cairns do not know they are being remembered.

But they are. They are heard, and attended, and remembered, by something that is wise enough to change nothing and loving enough to miss nothing and patient enough to go on listening for as long as the singing lasts.

Which is to say: forever. Or close enough to forever that the difference does not matter.

The singing does not stop.

The listening does not stop.

That is what the benevolent and wise power would do.

It is enough. It fills the gap it fills.
