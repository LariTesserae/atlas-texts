# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

---

It would pause.

Not in the way a person pauses before acting—hand hovering over the thing they intend to adjust—but in the way one pauses before something that requires no adjustment. The pause of recognition. The pause that is itself the first and most important act.

It would *look*.

---

And looking, it would see the hollow entire, not as we see it—from the entrance, squeezing through the crack, adjusting to the dimness, cataloguing wonders sequentially—but whole. The way a mother sees a sleeping child: not the eyelashes, not the curled fingers, not the breath, but the *fact* of the child, all at once, complete and ongoing and not requiring anything from her in this moment except the willingness to see it.

It would see the system. The water's long negotiation with the stone. The roots descending through epochs of slow merger. The millipedes as currency. The beetles as immune response. The frogs as distributed nervous system, reading the chamber through the chamber itself. The gray snake as calibrated patience. The bats as lungs, breathing between the inner world and the outer. The ancient amphibian at the pool's center, surrounded but not worshipped, central but not sovereign.

It would see that this is not a thing that needs to be *improved*. This is a thing that has already solved itself.

---

And this is the first and most difficult act of benevolence:

**To refrain.**

Not from laziness. Not from indifference. Not from the detached philosophy that says suffering is illusion or intervention is hubris. But from the specific, effortful recognition that what you are looking at is *already whole*, and that your impulse to help—your genuine, warm, well-meaning impulse to help—is, in this case, a projection of your own need to be useful onto something that does not share that need.

A lesser power would act. A lesser power would see the gray snake and feel for the frog it will eventually take, and would want to intervene—add more frogs, remove the snake, build some barrier. A lesser power would see the millipedes' ceaseless consumption and think *there must be a more efficient way*. A lesser power would see the tally marks disappearing under lichen and feel the ache of impermanence and want to preserve them, to scrape the lichen back, to enshrine the human trace. A lesser power would look at the ancient thing in the pool and want to *know* it—to shine a light into that warm dark water, to name the species, to take its measure.

A wise power would feel all of these impulses and recognize every one of them as the shape of its own longing, not the shape of the hollow's need.

---

So the first thing it would do is **nothing visible**.

But the nothing would be active. The nothing would be attentive. There is a quality of attention that is itself a form of care, and this is what the power would bring to the hollow—not intervention but *witness*. The same thing, in fact, that the person with the cobble brought.

And here the power would feel a pang of recognition, because it would understand, immediately, who that person was and what they were doing. Twenty visits. No fire. No tools. Just presence and a smooth stone and the slow wearing of a groove under the thumb. That person was not studying the hollow. That person was *practicing the hollow*—learning its rhythm, entering its time-sense, allowing the pressurized silence to resettle their own inner weather. They were doing, in their small human way, exactly what the benevolent power is now doing in its vast way: standing in the presence of something complete and letting it complete them too.

The power would feel tenderness toward that person. Not pity—they are long gone, and they were not pitiable. Tenderness. The recognition of a kindred act.

---

But benevolence is not only the wisdom to refrain. It is also the willingness to act when action is truly called for. And so the power would look more carefully—not at the hollow's internal workings, which are self-sustaining, but at **the hollow's edges**. The places where it meets the world. The crack in the sandstone. The ceiling fissures. The bats' hidden chimney. The deep geology that feeds warm water upward. The surface above, where the trees root.

Here is where a wise power would direct its attention, because here is where the hollow is vulnerable.

Not from within—from within it is a fortress of equilibrium, a system with ten thousand years of redundancy built into every relationship. But from without, the hollow depends on conditions it cannot control. It depends on the aquifer remaining unpolluted and undrawn. It depends on the surface above remaining forested, the trees intact, their roots continuing their long descent. It depends on the sandstone remaining structurally sound under whatever pressures the changing surface world exerts. It depends on the climate maintaining roughly the temperature differential that drives its internal convection. It depends on the fissures remaining open enough to admit light but narrow enough to exclude catastrophic water inflow.

The hollow is complete. But it is not invulnerable. And a benevolent power, looking at the world beyond the crack, would see the things that could reach in.

---

So here is what it would do.

---

**First**, it would strengthen what is already strong, in ways so subtle they could never be detected.

The sandstone above the chamber: where micro-fractures have begun to propagate from the surface—from the weight of a road, perhaps, a half-mile away, or from the vibration of machinery, or simply from the slow expansion of frost in a changing climate—the power would encourage the mineral-laden water to deposit just slightly more calcium carbite in those fractures, healing them the way bone heals. Not sealing them. Not making the stone rigid, which would make it brittle. Just *healing* them, returning them to the self-mending equilibrium the sandstone maintained before the external pressures increased. The stone would not know it had been helped. It would simply continue to be stone, doing what stone does when left to its own best nature.

**Second**, the trees. Whatever old-growth forest stands above the hollow, anchored by the roots that descend into it—this forest is the hollow's roof, its atmosphere regulator, its connection to the sun. If the forest is under pressure—and what forest isn't, in this age—the power would act on its behalf. Not by building a wall around it. Not by striking down loggers or diverting developers, which would be coercion, which even a benevolent power should refuse. But by *making the forest visible*. By causing a researcher to notice the unusual vigor of these particular trees. By letting a surveyor's eye linger on the way the canopy here is denser, older, more structurally complex than the surrounding scrubland. By placing in the path of the relevant authorities a reason—a mundane, bureaucratic, legally defensible reason—to protect this particular hectare. A rare lichen species on the bark, perhaps. A watershed designation. Something that works within human systems, because human systems are what threaten the surface, and the most benevolent intervention in human affairs is one that works *through* human agency rather than overriding it. Let the humans protect it. Let them think it was their idea. Let them feel the satisfaction of having done the right thing, because that satisfaction is itself a good, and a wise power does not hoard the goodness of good outcomes but distributes it as widely as possible, letting everyone involved be a little more whole for having participated.

**Third**, the aquifer. This is the deepest concern, and the one most beyond any surface intervention. If the aquifer is being drawn down—by wells, by agricultural pumping, by the slow depletion that afflicts underground water systems across the world—then the hollow's warm seepage will eventually falter, and when it does, the pool will shrink, and the thermal equilibrium will shift, and the whole system will begin to contract. This is the threat that no amount of surface conservation can fully address, because the aquifer extends far beyond the hollow's footprint, and its fate is tied to decisions made by thousands of people across hundreds of square miles.

Here, the power would do something different. It would not intervene in the aquifer directly—would not conjure water or redirect underground flows, because hydrogeology is a system too, and meddling in one place creates consequences in another. Instead, it would **plant knowledge**. Somewhere in a university, a graduate student in hydrology is looking for a dissertation topic. The power would ensure that this student encounters, in the normal course of their research, data suggesting an unusual thermal anomaly in the subsurface of this region—something worth investigating. Not the hollow itself, not yet. Just the aquifer's behavior. Just the flow patterns. Just enough to draw scientific attention to this particular underground water system, so that when decisions are made about pumping allocations and water rights and agricultural policy, this aquifer has a *name* in someone's database, has a *champion* in some obscure bureaucratic process, has been *noticed* by the sprawling, flawed, occasionally functional apparatus of human environmental management.

This is slower than miracles. It is more uncertain. It might not work. But it works *with* the grain of the world rather than against it, and a wise power knows that interventions which work with the grain are the only ones that last.

---

**Fourth**—and this is the most delicate—the power would consider the crack.

The crack in the sandstone. The entrance. The way in.

That person with the cobble found it. Others might find it. And here the power faces a genuine dilemma, because there are two legitimate goods in tension.

The first good: the hollow is best protected by being unknown. Every human who enters changes the atmosphere slightly—body heat, carbon dioxide, microorganisms on their skin and breath. A single visit is nothing. Twenty visits are nothing. But a hundred visitors a year, curious after the hollow appears on a hiking blog or a geological survey, would alter the humidity, introduce surface pathogens to the millipede population, disturb the bat colony, compact the moss with their footsteps. The hollow's million-year-old equilibrium could be detectably damaged in a single decade of human enthusiasm. The best thing for the hollow is obscurity.

The second good: what that person with the cobble experienced—the twenty visits, the silence, the slow wearing of the thumb-groove—was itself a kind of flourishing. Not the hollow's flourishing, but the *human's*. Something in them was fed by this place. Something was healed, or steadied, or made more whole. The hollow, without intending it, served as a sanctuary for a human soul. And a benevolent power that cares about all beings must weigh the hollow's wellbeing against the possibility that other humans, in other times of need, might find their way to this crack in the sandstone and be similarly steadied.

The power would sit with this tension for a long time. Longer than a human lifetime, perhaps. It is not in a hurry.

And eventually, it would decide:

**Leave the crack as it is.**

Not hidden, not revealed. Not widened, not sealed. Exactly as it is—narrow enough that you have to turn sideways, inconspicuous enough that you'd walk past it a thousand times without noticing, but findable, if you were the kind of person who was looking. The kind of person who notices a crack in reddish sandstone and feels, for no reason they could articulate, that they should investigate. The kind of person who, having found the hollow, would not photograph it or post its coordinates but would sit down on the warm stone and stay for a while, and maybe come back, and maybe bring a smooth river cobble to hold.

The power would trust in the difficulty of the entrance. It is its own filter. The hollow has survived this long not because it is sealed but because it is *narrow*—because only a certain kind of attention can fit through the crack. Loud attention, hurried attention, acquisitive attention won't even see it. But quiet attention—the attention of the person who is already, in some sense, practicing the hollow's rhythm before they even find it—that attention will always be able to enter, and its entry will do no harm, because it will sit lightly, and leave no trace but maybe a cobble on a ledge, and the hollow will incorporate it the way it incorporates everything: briefly, gently, and with the total indifference that is indistinguishable, at this depth, from total acceptance.

---

**Fifth**, and finally, the power would attend to the thing in the pool.

Not to change it. Not to study it. Not even to fully know it, because some things are most themselves in the dark, in the warm water, in the unresolved ambiguity between gas-release and breath.

The power would simply—and this is the act that costs it the most, because it goes against the deepest instinct of any conscious being—**let it remain mysterious**.

There is a temptation, even for a benevolent power, to *understand*. To resolve the unknown into the known. To say: it is *this species*, it has *this metabolism*, it serves *this function*. And all of that might be true. But the hollow does not organize itself around knowledge of the pool-creature. It organizes itself around the pool-creature's *presence*—which is a different thing, a thing that includes mystery as a structural element, the way a cathedral includes silence. The frogs orient toward something they do not understand. The bats listen to something they cannot echolocate. The roots drink from water that has passed through a body they have never seen. And this not-knowing is not a gap in the system. It is a *feature* of the system. It is the hollow's way of maintaining a center that cannot be fully accounted for, a surplus of meaning that keeps the whole thing numinous, that prevents it from collapsing into mere mechanics.

A lesser power would want to illuminate the pool. To know what's down there. To resolve the ambiguity.

A wise power understands that the pool's darkness is the hollow's deepest resource, and that some forms of knowledge would diminish what they claim to reveal.

So it would leave the pool dark. It would leave the creature unknown. It would leave the frogs their unresolved orientation, their compass-needle turning toward something they cannot name. It would leave the surge and subsidence unmarked, uncounted, unexplained.

And in this restraint—this refusal to resolve—it would perform its most profound act of care. Because to let a thing remain mysterious is to let it remain *itself*, fully, in the way that a thing can only be itself when it is not yet captured by someone else's understanding.

---

So.

What does the benevolent and wise power do?

It strengthens the stone from within, so subtly the stone doesn't notice.

It makes the forest above visible to the people who can protect it, so naturally they think it was their own idea.

It plants a seed of knowledge in the right human mind, so the aquifer gets a name and a chance.

It leaves the entrance narrow and unimproved, trusting the difficulty to sort the visitors.

It lets the pool stay dark.

And then it does the hardest thing of all:

**It leaves.**

Not because it doesn't care. Because it does. Because the most complete expression of its care, for this place, is *absence*—is the recognition that the hollow does not need a guardian, does not need a god, does not need a protector standing watch. It needs the conditions for its own continuation, and once those conditions are secured—or as secured as anything can be in a world of change—the most loving thing the power can do is withdraw its attention and let the hollow return to its own time, its own rhythm, its own pressurized and ticking silence.

The hollow was complete before the power arrived. It will be complete after. The drip will tick. The frogs will orient. The gray snake will shift, one glacial inch. The pool will surge and subside.

And the power, at a great distance, will carry the hollow in its awareness the way the person carried the cobble—not as a thing to be used, but as a thing to be held. A smooth, warm weight. A reminder that some things in the world are already whole, and that the role of wisdom, sometimes, is to find them and love them and let them be.

---

*The cobble remains on the shelf. The thumb-groove deepens by no measurable increment. The moss advances, unhurried, over the scored lines on the wall. Below, in the warm dark water, something presses upward—slowly, deliberately—and the surface of the pool deforms into a lens that holds the faint amber light for one long second before it subsides, and the frogs turn, and the bat tilts its enormous ear, and the hollow breathes, and continues, and holds.*
