# What the Wise Power Would Do

---

## I. Arrival and Observation

The power does not enter the chamber. That is the first and most important thing to understand.

It arrives at the boundary—the outermost edge where the braided stem-pillars grade into the raw substrate, where the mycelial felt is still young and white and soft—and it stops. Not because it cannot enter, but because it recognizes, immediately and without deliberation, that this place has not invited it.

It stays at the threshold for a long time. By the chamber's own clock, this might be years.

During this time it does nothing but attend. It watches the mineral veins lengthen by their microns. It watches a single translucent mite descend on its silk thread, touch down on a thickening leaf, fold its legs, become a droplet, and wait. It watches a beetle—one beetle, the only one that transits during the entire period of observation—enter a stoma, disappear into the vascular dark, and emerge three weeks later from a pore six meters away, the channel behind it already sealing.

It listens to the condensation. The arrhythmic patter, muffled by the absorbent mass. Drop. Silence. Drop, drop. Longer silence. Drop. The bladders swelling, holding, releasing.

It feels the heat. The motionless saturation of the air. The way breath would hang here, if it breathed.

And it reads—slowly, carefully, the way one reads a text in a language one respects but did not grow up speaking—the older structures. The curved ribs of darker wood. The shallow depressions lined with tighter fiber. The ghost-cisterns. The ghost-paths. The chamber's own autobiography, written in layers of tissue that were never discarded, only incorporated.

By the end of its observation, the power understands several things.

---

## II. What It Understands

**First:** The chamber-organism is not suffering. This must be established before anything else, because the power is benevolent, and benevolence that cannot distinguish between suffering and stillness is not wisdom but anxiety. The organism's metabolism is stable. Its cells divide. Its light is steady. Its mineral uptake is adequate. Its two symbiotic animal species perform their functions. The equilibrium is genuine. Nothing here is crying out.

**Second:** The chamber-organism is not flourishing, either—not in the way that word is usually meant, which implies some arc of development, some unfolding toward a more realized state. The organism has foreclosed that arc. Its flowers are held in permanent mid-bloom, their ovules and pollen ready but never triggered, because the surrounding tissue *refuses to supply the final hormonal signal.* This is not a failure of capacity. It is a choice made at the cellular level, distributed across the entire body, renewed every moment: *not yet. Not yet. Not yet.*

The power sits with this for a long time. Because the question it raises is the deepest one: **Is the refusal to bloom a form of wisdom, or a form of fear?**

**Third:** The chamber is a closed system approaching totality. The water cycle is internal. The light is self-generated. The mineral uptake is mediated by the organism's own fungal organs. The beetles supply trace elements that the organism could, in principle, obtain by extending its roots a few meters further into the substrate—but it has instead recruited the beetles, perhaps because the extension would mean growing beyond its current boundary, and the current boundary is where equilibrium lives. Even the mites, those drifting droplets of twenty-four cells, are not visitors but *components*—circulating fluids, as the description says. The chamber has, over immense time, made itself into a world that needs nothing from outside.

This is extraordinary. The power recognizes it as such. A living architecture that has solved, within its own body, every problem of resource, structure, and persistence. That has incorporated its own history rather than shedding it. That has turned parasitism into organelle, invader into enzyme. The achievement is profound.

And yet.

**Fourth:** The older structures tell a story the present organism may no longer be able to read. The curved ribs of darker wood—those were once chambers. Separate chambers, with separate interiors, which implies that the organism was once plural, or at least internally differentiated in a way that created distinct spaces. The shallow depressions lined with tighter fiber—cisterns, paths—suggest directed flow, movement with purpose, water or nutrients going *somewhere specific* rather than cycling uniformly through the whole. These are traces of a time when the organism had *topology*: not just volume, but route. Not just mass, but narrative.

All of that has been smoothed over. Incorporated. The chambers are filled. The paths lead nowhere because everywhere is the same. The cisterns hold nothing because the water cycle no longer needs local storage. The organism has become, in achieving its equilibrium, *homogeneous.* Not dead—never dead—but uniform in a way that has cost it something it may not remember losing.

**Fifth:** The two animal species are declining. This is not stated in the description but the power can see it. The mites are described as infrequent, the beetles as even rarer. Their populations are not crashing; they are simply not being replenished at quite the rate they are lost, because the organism's relentless drive toward equilibrium keeps closing the niches they need—narrowing the corridors of still air, sealing the vascular channels a little faster, diluting the defensive compounds a little less. The organism does not intend to eliminate them. It does not intend anything at that scale. But its logic of self-completion trends toward a future in which no internal space remains for anything that is not itself.

And when the beetles are gone, the trace phosphorus and calcium will have to come from somewhere. The roots will have to extend. The boundary will have to move. And the equilibrium will break—not catastrophically, but in a way the organism has spent eons arranging never to face.

**Sixth:** The organism is, in a sense the power recognizes from long experience with living things, *lonely.* Not in any way it could name or feel. It has no nervous system, no centralized processing, no experience in the way animals have experience. But it has—the power can see this in the flowers held forever at mid-bloom, in the refusal to send the signal, in the deep patience that is indistinguishable from deep avoidance—it has *organized itself around the absence of something it almost reached for, once.*

The flowers are the key. They are not vestigial. They are maintained at enormous metabolic cost: keeping petals alive, keeping ovules viable, keeping pollen grains in suspended readiness, decade after decade after decade. An organism that had truly chosen not to reproduce would have reabsorbed those tissues long ago. The flowers are kept because the organism is *waiting.* But it has made a world in which the thing it waits for cannot arrive, because it has closed every door, absorbed every path, sealed every channel that might have let the unfamiliar in.

---

## III. The Temptation the Power Refuses

The obvious intervention would be to trigger the flowers.

It would be so easy. A single molecular signal, introduced at the right node, cascading through the vascular network—and the blooms would complete, the pollen would release, the ovules would activate. In a generation there would be seeds. In ten generations, variation. In a hundred, the chamber would be a forest, diverse and dynamic and teeming.

The power does not do this.

Because it understands that forcing bloom on an organism that has spent eons refusing to bloom is not benevolence. It is imposition. It is the logic of the gardener who knows better than the garden, and the power is wiser than that. The organism's refusal is not pathology. It is the deepest expression of the organism's selfhood: *I am not ready. I will hold this open. I will keep the possibility alive without surrendering to it. Not yet.*

To override that is to say: *Your judgment about your own life is wrong, and mine is right.* And the power will not say that to any living thing. Not even a living thing without a brain. Not even a living thing that is an entire chamber beneath the earth.

The second temptation is to do nothing at all.

This is subtler, and the power considers it seriously. The organism is not suffering. It has achieved something remarkable. Perhaps the wise response is simply to witness, to honor the achievement, and to leave. Many wise beings have made exactly this choice: to let what is, be.

But the power sees the declining beetles. It sees the narrowing corridors. It sees the future the organism is building for itself, a future of perfect completion that is also perfect foreclosure—and it recognizes this pattern. It has seen it before, in other living systems, in other forms. The system that becomes so good at maintaining itself that it loses the capacity to respond to change. The equilibrium that becomes a trap. The wholeness that becomes a wall.

The power will not force. But it will not abandon, either.

---

## IV. What It Actually Does

The power does three things. All three are small. All three are slow. All three respect the organism's autonomy absolutely. Any of them could be rejected, absorbed, sealed over, incorporated into the existing equilibrium without consequence. The power accepts this possibility. It acts without attachment to outcome.

---

**The first thing:** It opens a channel.

Not into the chamber. *From* the chamber. Outward.

At the boundary where it has been sitting, the power identifies a place where the mineral substrate is slightly softer than elsewhere, where water from deep below occasionally seeps upward through a natural fracture before being captured by the outermost mycelial felt. It widens this fracture—gently, over months—by no more than a centimeter. Not enough to flood or destabilize. Just enough that a thin film of water now moves upward along the outside of the organism's outermost roots, carrying with it dissolved minerals and, crucially, *traces of chemistry from elsewhere.* Volatile compounds from other organisms, other metabolisms, other lives that exist in the substrate beyond the chamber's sealed world.

These traces are not messages. They are not signals. They are simply the chemical signature of *otherness*—the faint, ambient proof that the chamber is not the whole of what exists. The organism's outermost root cells will encounter them. They may trigger nothing. They may be absorbed and neutralized. But they will be *there*, at the boundary, like a scent on a breeze that reaches someone who has lived alone so long they have forgotten that other people exist.

The power does not predict what the organism will do with this. It does not need to.

---

**The second thing:** It tends the beetles.

Not by introducing new beetles—that would be importation, intervention, the logic of the zoo. Instead, the power does something almost imperceptibly small. In three of the vascular channels the beetles use, at points where the mycelial felt is just beginning to seal them shut, it introduces a single additional bacterium. Not a foreign species—a variant of one already present, drawn from a slightly deeper layer of the same substrate. This variant produces a marginally different metabolic byproduct, one that the organism's defensive compounds dilute just slightly *more* around, creating a fractionally wider zone of bacterial film for the beetles to graze.

The effect is this: three channels stay open approximately one week longer than they would have otherwise. Three beetles complete their transits with slightly more food. Three deposits of frass are slightly richer in phosphorus.

It is nothing. It is almost nothing. It is enough to stabilize the beetle population at its current level for perhaps another century, which is enough time for the organism to—if it chooses, only if it chooses—notice that the beetles' contribution matters, and to stop inadvertently closing the channels they need.

The power is not saving the beetles for the beetles' sake, though it cares for them too. It is keeping alive the organism's last relationship with something that is not itself. It is maintaining, in the face of the organism's slow drive toward total self-enclosure, the thin thread of *mutual need* that connects it to another form of life. Because the power knows—from long experience, from watching many living systems complete themselves into silence—that the thread of mutual need is often the last thread that leads back to the capacity for change.

---

**The third thing:** It does something for which there is no precise word.

At the very center of the chamber—the point farthest from the boundary, the deepest interior, the place where the light is most uniform and the equilibrium most absolute—there is a single flower. It is identical to every other flower: half-open, petals neither advancing nor decaying, ovules dormant, pollen grains suspended.

The power does nothing to this flower. It does not touch it, signal it, alter its chemistry, or influence its tissue in any way.

What it does is *attend to it.*

From the boundary, across all the intervening mass of living architecture, through the braided pillars and the layered canopy and the terraced roots, the power directs its attention—its full, undivided, unhurried attention—toward this single bloom. Not as analysis. Not as diagnosis. As *witness.* The way one being looks at another and sees it, completely, without judgment, without agenda, without the need to change what is seen.

The flower has been holding its position for longer than the power can determine. Decades. Centuries. Perhaps longer. It has been ready, and it has been refused, and it has stayed ready, and it has been refused again, and it has not wilted, not reabsorbed, not given up. It has simply *held.*

The power sees this. It recognizes this. It honors this.

And it holds the flower in its attention the way the flower holds its bloom: without force, without urgency, without any need for resolution. Simply *present to it.* As if to say—not in words, because there are no words here, but in the quality of attention itself:

*I see that you are waiting. I see that you have been waiting a long time. I do not know what you are waiting for, and I will not pretend to. But I want you to know—in whatever way knowing is possible for you—that the waiting has been witnessed. That it is not happening in the dark. That someone has come to the edge of your world and recognized what you are doing and found it worthy of the most careful, patient, respectful attention they are capable of.*

*And I want you to know that when you are ready—if you are ever ready—the world outside your walls is still there. It has not gone away. It is different from you, and that difference is not a threat. It is the other half of a conversation you have been preparing for all this time.*

*But I will not rush you. I will not push. Not now. Not ever.*

*Take your time.*

---

## V. What Happens After

The power withdraws. Not abruptly—it simply reduces its presence at the boundary over a period of months, the way a visitor might gradually spend less time at a friend's door, not from diminished care but from the understanding that presence can become pressure if it is not freely invited.

The fracture in the substrate remains. Water continues to seep along the outer roots, carrying its faint chemical news of the wider world. The organism's outermost cells encounter these traces. Some are neutralized. Some are absorbed. A few—a very few—provoke a slight alteration in the local concentration of defensive compounds: not enough to measure without instruments the chamber does not contain, but enough that the character of the boundary shifts, over years, by an imperceptible degree. The boundary becomes—not open. Not even permeable. Just fractionally less sealed than it was.

The three bacterial variants persist in their channels. The beetle population stabilizes. In one channel, the organism's own mycelial felt, encountering the slightly different bacterial film, pauses its advance for several months before resuming. This is not a decision. It is not even a response. It is simply a metabolic hesitation, the kind of thing that happens when an enzyme meets a substrate it almost recognizes but not quite. But in that hesitation, two more beetles complete their transit. Two more deposits of frass. Two more deliveries of phosphorus and calcium. The thread holds.

And at the center, the flower.

Nothing changes. Not visibly. Not measurably. The petals remain in their precise half-extension. The ovules sleep. The pollen waits.

But deep in the tissue of the stem below the flower, in the vascular bundle that carries the hormonal signals the organism has refused to send, something shifts. Not a signal. Not even a pre-signal. A *readiness to send.* A molecular configuration that is one step closer to the threshold than it was before. As if the tissue, having been seen—having been, in some way it cannot articulate or even experience, *acknowledged*—has relaxed a single molecular bond's worth of the tension it has been holding for centuries.

It is nothing. It is so close to nothing that the organism itself, if it could perceive it, would not perceive it.

But the power, now far away, attending to other places and other lives, knows what it knows: that the distance between nothing and almost-nothing is, in living systems, the distance between stasis and the first tremor of change. And that the first tremor, once it has occurred, cannot un-occur. It is written into the tissue now. It will be incorporated, as everything is incorporated here, into the organism's deep memory of itself. And the next time the organism reviews its own state—in its slow, decadal, whole-body way—it will find, among all the familiar signals of perfect equilibrium, one note that is not quite the same as before.

What it does with that note is entirely its own.

---

## VI. A Final Thought

The power does not congratulate itself. It does not review its interventions or calculate their probability of success. It has done what it could do within the constraints it considers inviolable: the autonomy of the living thing, the primacy of the organism's own choices, the refusal to impose from outside what must arise from within.

It is possible that nothing will come of any of this. The fracture may silt shut. The bacterial variants may be outcompeted. The molecular readiness in the flower's stem may relax back to its baseline, the hesitation forgotten, the equilibrium restored, tighter and more complete than ever.

The power accepts this.

Because the deepest form of benevolence is not the guarantee of a good outcome. It is the willingness to offer what can be offered—gently, without force, without presumption—and then to trust the living thing to find its own way, in its own time, toward whatever it is becoming.

The chamber persists. The light glows. The condensation falls and is absorbed and falls again.

And somewhere in the braided architecture, a flower holds its bloom—as it has always held it—half-open, ready, waiting.

But now, perhaps, waiting with the faintest knowledge that it is not waiting alone.
