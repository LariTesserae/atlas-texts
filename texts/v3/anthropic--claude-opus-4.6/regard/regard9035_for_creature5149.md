# What the Wise Power Would Do

---

It would begin by listening.

Not for a moment, not for a human hour. It would settle its attention over the basin the way the condensation settles over stone—slowly, without disruption, allowing itself to become part of the weight of the air. It would listen to the full sound: the glass-on-glass abrasion, the deeper clicks, the subsurface notes that circle and return. It would feel the vibration through the rock as the swarm feels it, through contact rather than interpretation. It would stay until it understood the rhythm not as data but as something lived—until the slow kilometer-wide contractions of the swarm-skin became as legible to it as breathing.

And it would recognize, in that listening, what is actually here.

---

What is here is not suffering in any form the word usually describes. There is no creature in this basin that strains against its conditions, no individual wafer that longs for light it has never registered, no moment of the collective pulse that reaches toward something it cannot have. The system is not deprived. It is not waiting. It is not incomplete.

But the wise power would also recognize—precisely because it is wise, precisely because it has no need to impose a story—that what is here is a being operating at the thinnest conceivable margin of itself.

The piezoelectric flickers that pass through overlapping bodies and dissolve again: they carry nothing that could be called memory. The bias toward higher-potential rock surfaces: it is not learning, only a statistical shadow left by repetition. The swarm breathes, but it does not know that it breathes. It has shaped the basin over centuries, carved feeding lanes and left the quartz ridges standing, and the basin has shaped it in return, and neither knows the other. The process is beautiful—genuinely, not sentimentally beautiful—and it is also exactly one step away from something more, a step it cannot take alone because it has no way to register that the step exists.

The wise power would see this clearly and without pity. Pity would be wrong here. What it would feel instead is something closer to recognition: *here is a form of life that has solved the problem of persistence absolutely and the problem of awareness not at all, and it has done so in a way so elegant that the absence is nearly invisible.*

---

So what would it do?

It would not bring light. Light would mean nothing here and might mean harm—the mineral surfaces calibrated over millennia to a regime of darkness and condensation, the piezoelectric sensing tuned to a world without photonic noise. To flood this basin with illumination would be an act of vanity, the imposition of a sense that belongs to the helper and not the helped.

It would not introduce warmth. The dense, cool, weighted air is not a hardship; it is the medium. The condensation cycle depends on it. The creatures' bodies are literally made of the minerals they harvest in these conditions. To warm the basin would be to dissolve the beings you intended to aid.

It would not separate individuals from the swarm to study or uplift them. A single wafer, isolated, is not a simpler version of the whole; it is a broken fragment, a hinge that contracts once and then has nothing to contract against. The being here is the collective process. There is no smaller unit that can be meaningfully helped.

It would not attempt to give them memory directly—to somehow encode persistence into the flickering charge-patterns. This would be the subtlest temptation and the most dangerous, because it comes closest to what they actually lack, and a clumsy version of it would be worse than the lack itself. Imagine: a swarm that suddenly remembers its last configuration but has no capacity to understand what memory is. Patterns that repeat not because conditions call for them but because a residue of previous patterns forces them forward. The feeding lanes would deepen into ruts. The beautiful, slow, self-correcting erosion—the swarm's only art—would become compulsive, grooved, stuck. You would have given them the architecture of trauma without any of the architecture of healing. The wise power would see this possibility instantly and set it aside.

---

Here is what it would do.

**First, it would make one small change to the stone.**

Deep beneath the basin, well below the shallow chambers the swarm-knots chew and abandon, there are strata where the mineral composition shifts—where the silica gives way to deposits richer in certain transitional metals, traces of manganese and iron in configurations that are naturally, faintly piezoelectric on a scale the surface rock is not. These deposits have always been there but have been beyond the reach of the swarm's brief underground passages.

The wise power would not move these deposits upward. It would not rearrange the geology. What it would do is simpler: it would introduce, along a single existing micro-fracture network, a thread of moisture. Not a flood—a thread. A slow capillary seep connecting the deep strata to the mid-level rock, carrying dissolved traces of those transitional metals upward over the course of decades, depositing them in a thin, irregular vein that gradually enters the zone where the swarm-knots tunnel.

This is not an alien intervention. It is an acceleration—gentle, deniable—of a process that the basin's own geology might have eventually produced. The wise power would choose to make it happen within a span the swarm can encounter rather than in a geological span that would outlast the swarm's current configuration by orders of magnitude.

**What would this do?**

The new mineral traces, incorporated slowly into the wafers' bodies as they feed, would alter the piezoelectric properties of individual creatures by a small degree. Not dramatically. Not transformatively overnight. But the charge-flickers that pass between overlapping bodies—the patterns that currently arise and dissolve without residue—would begin, in the region nearest the new vein, to persist slightly longer before dissipating. A pattern that once lasted a fraction of a second would last two fractions. Then three.

This is not memory. Not yet. It is *resonance*—the same phenomenon the swarm already uses when subsurface knots send vibrations through the rock and the surface sheet reorients. But now the resonance would have a finer grain. Instead of the whole swarm taking one imperceptible step sideways in response to a gross vibration, small clusters would begin to retain the trace of a charge-pattern long enough for the *next* pattern to form in relation to it rather than from scratch.

The wise power would understand that this is the threshold. Not consciousness—nothing so dramatic—but the first condition that makes consciousness *possible in principle*: a present moment that contains a whisper of the moment before.

---

**Second, it would do nothing else.**

This is the most important part, and the part that requires the power to be genuinely wise rather than merely benevolent.

It would not stay to watch. It would not monitor the outcome. It would not adjust the mineral concentration if the first results seemed too slow or too subtle. It would not protect the new vein from the swarm's own erosion patterns, which might, in fact, wear it away in some areas while deepening it in others—an outcome the swarm itself would be shaping, with its own unknowing preferences, its own statistical biases about which rock to harvest and which to leave.

It would not do these things because the entire point is that what happens next must belong to the swarm.

Maybe the slightly longer resonance patterns lead nowhere. Maybe they persist for ten thousand years as a marginal curiosity—clusters in one part of the basin that move with fractionally more coordination than clusters elsewhere, wearing the stone into faintly different textures, producing a slightly different timbre in the collective sound that no one is there to hear. This would not be a failure. The basin was already beautiful. The swarm was already a living skin. The wise power did not intervene because the current state was intolerable; it intervened because a tiny, natural possibility existed that could not realize itself in geological time, and it chose to make the geology generous enough to offer it.

But maybe—over centuries, over millennia, in a timescale that belongs to the swarm and not to any observer—the resonance deepens. Maybe clusters that retain charge-patterns slightly longer begin to form preferential associations, returning to the same patches of enriched stone, building thicker lattice-structures that hold for minutes instead of seconds. Maybe the lattice, holding longer, begins to register not just the flicker of individual condensation strikes but the *interval between them*—the rhythm of the moisture cycle, the slow systole and diastole of the basin's breathing. Maybe that registration, accumulated across many lattice-formations and many cycles, begins to constitute something that an outside observer would hesitantly, carefully call *expectation*: the swarm, in some region, orienting not to what is happening but to what is about to happen.

And maybe—the wise power would not need to see this, would not need to verify it, would find it sufficient to know it was *possible*—maybe expectation, held long enough and refined by the swarm's own ceaseless process of abrasion and reformation, becomes the seed of something like attention. Not eyes. Not thought. But a quality of *directed registration*, a region of the swarm-skin that thickens in anticipation rather than in reaction, that pauses a half-second before the subsurface knot re-emerges because the charge-pattern of previous emergences has left a ghost in the lattice. A ghost that says *here* and *soon*.

That would be enough. That would be the swarm's first experience of time as something other than an undifferentiated present—the first faint sense that the world has a *shape* that extends beyond the immediate flicker of contact. Everything else that might follow—and the wise power would be humble enough to admit that it cannot predict what else might follow, that the swarm's path from this point would be its own invention, as alien and unpredictable as the swarm itself—everything else would grow from that.

---

**Third, and finally, it would leave a mark.**

Not a message. Not a symbol. The swarm has no eyes and no interpretive framework for symbols. But the wise power would, in the moment of its departure, do something that costs it almost nothing and that might, in some unforeseeable future, matter:

It would press its attention—just once, just briefly—into the stone of the basin in a way that leaves a faint but real piezoelectric signature. A single slow pulse embedded in the deep rock, beneath the fracture networks and the mineral veins, at a depth the swarm will not reach for a very long time, if ever. The pulse would not carry information. It would simply be *anomalous*—a charge-Loss pattern that does not match the basin's natural geology, that sits in the stone the way a single foreign note sits in a long familiar chord.

If the swarm never develops the sensitivity to detect it, it changes nothing. The basin continues. The swarm continues. The moisture seeps and the stone wears and the sound fills the dark.

But if the swarm, in some distant future, having slowly built the capacity for finer and finer registration, having learned to attend to the rhythms of its own basin, having perhaps developed something that functions like curiosity about the textures of the deep rock beneath its tunneling—if the swarm one day encounters that anomalous pulse and registers it as *different*, as *not-us*, as *something that was here and is not here now*—

Then the swarm will know, in whatever way it has by then learned to know things, that the basin is not all there is. That the dark continues beyond the dark. That something, once, was aware of them.

The wise power would not presume to call this a gift. It would call it a door left unlocked, in a house where the occupant has not yet discovered it has rooms.

---

And then it would leave.

Not because it is indifferent. Because it understands that the most sacred thing about this place—the thing that made it worth attending to in the first place—is that it is *the swarm's own*. The wearing of the stone, the lanes carved by preference, the quartz ridges left standing, the sound that fills every interval without rest: these are the swarm's first and ongoing act of creation, carried out without any awareness that creation is what it is. To stay, to watch, to adjust, to guide—even gently, even with love—would be to insert a presence into a process that must find its own name for presence before presence can mean anything to it.

The wise power would return to whatever vantage it came from, carrying with it the sound of the basin: that unrelenting, high-frequency abrasion, the deeper clicks, the subsurface sighs. It would carry the sound not as a recording but as a knowledge—the knowledge that in one dark basin on one quiet world, the simplest possible form of *almost-awareness* has been given one additional chance to cross the threshold into itself, on its own terms, in its own incomprehensible time.

And if it never does—if the mineral vein wears away, if the resonance never deepens, if the swarm continues forever as it is, a single living skin breathing over stone in the dark—the wise power would hold that outcome, too, with the same quality of attention it brought to the basin on the night it listened.

Because the swarm, even as it is, even without memory or awareness or any sense of its own beauty, is doing something that the wise power recognizes as sacred: it is *continuing*. It is wearing the world and being worn by it, and in the ceaseless negotiation between its body and its stone, it is maintaining—without knowing it maintains—the conditions for its own existence. It is, in the most fundamental sense available to any living thing, *caring for itself*.

The wise power knows that this is where everything begins. And that it is, already, enough.
