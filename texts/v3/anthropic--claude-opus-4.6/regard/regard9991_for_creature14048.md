# What the Wise Power Would Do

---

Nothing.

---

No — that is too fast. That is the answer, but it is not the understanding. Let me come to it honestly.

---

The benevolent and wise power arrives the way anything arrives here: from above. It perceives the bowl whole, the way the swifts perceive it but more so — not as a flash during transit, but with the kind of sustained, total attention that sees each mite and the mountain simultaneously, that hears the springtails' landing-patter and the deep geological patience of the schist at once. It takes in the mat, the frenzy, the stillness, the slow-worm threading its bronze path through the sphagnum. It comprehends the mycorrhizal web beneath the surface, the bacterial billions, the tardigrades sleeping in their little glass coffins of desiccation, waiting for water with a patience that makes the lichens look impetuous.

And because it is wise, the first thing it does is *attend*. Not act. Attend. It watches the bees collide and re-route. It watches a spider tear down its web and eat the silk and rebuild. It watches the midge columns form and dissolve and reform after the cloud passes, every time new, every time from scratch, every time without memory. It watches the slow-worm track a beetle-stream through the moss, tongue-flicking, unhurried among the frantic.

It watches for a long time.

---

Because the temptation, even for a wise power — *especially* for a benevolent one — is to help.

The impulse is immediate and legible: here is a system running at its limits. Here are organisms compressed into a desperate brevity, burning through their cycles in a few months of alpine light because the window is so narrow and the winter is so absolute. Here is a slow-worm that will die alone in this bowl, its nutrients recycled before its body is cold. Here are bees whose queen will be dead by autumn, whose workers will never see another spring, whose entire civilization is rebuilt from scratch each year by a single hibernating female carrying the colony's future in her body like a prayer she doesn't know she's saying. Here are springtails holding the bowl's chemistry in balance by *accident*, by the random parabola of their jumps, by the statistical miracle of enough randomness converging on a functional outcome. Here is a system that works — but barely, beautifully, temporarily.

A benevolent power looks at this and feels the pull. Surely something could be *improved*. Surely the slow-worm could be given a mate. Surely the bees could be granted a longer season. Surely the water could be made more reliable — a tiny spring, a steady seep, so the sphagnum never dries and the mites never have to migrate and the whole system could relax its frantic pace and breathe. Surely the bare stone rim could be softened, the exposure lessened, the wind gentled. Surely the growing season could be extended by a month, even a week, giving every organism a little more time, a little more margin.

Surely the cloud could be held back.

---

The wise power contemplates each of these interventions. And for each one, it sees the consequence — not with omniscience, necessarily, but with the deep attention that comes from having watched systems like this one long enough to know what happens when you pull a thread.

Give the slow-worm a mate. Now there are two slow-worms, then twelve, then the beetle population crashes under predation pressure, decomposition slows, nutrients lock up in unprocessed dead matter, the moss chemistry shifts, the mycorrhizal network falters, the rhododendrons lose access to phosphorus, leaf by leaf the mat thins, and in ten years a bare patch opens at the center of the bowl, and the wind finds it, and the drying begins.

Make the water more reliable. Now the sphagnum is always saturated, the oxygen levels in the moss drop, anaerobic bacteria bloom, the beetle larvae drown in their galleries, the decomposition cycle shifts from aerobic to anaerobic, methane builds in the peat, the soil chemistry acidifies beyond what the saxifrages can tolerate, the flowers fail, the bees lose their rolling wave of nectar, the colony shrinks below viability, pollination collapses, and the mat — still alive, still photosynthesizing — begins the long, slow slide toward genetic stagnation, a clone aging without renewal, its inosculated branches carrying sap from nowhere to nowhere.

Extend the season. Now the organisms that have calibrated their entire existence to the brevity of the alpine summer — the bees whose colony cycle is timed to the frost, the fungi whose fruiting depends on the sharp temperature drop of autumn, the rhododendrons whose dormancy is triggered by day-length — are thrown out of sync. The bees produce a generation that emerges too late to mate before winter. The fungi fruit into warm air that dessicates their spores. The rhododendrons keep their leaves open past the point where the first frost finds them turgid and vulnerable, and the frost-damage opens wounds in the mat, and the wounds don't close before the snow comes, and the snow presses ice into the gaps, and in spring the mat wakes with holes in it that take decades to heal.

Hold back the cloud. Now the bowl's moisture cycle — its rhythm, the pulse that everything from the mites to the midges dances to — is broken. The pauses disappear. The mist that deposits condensation on the moss, that gives the sphagnum its daily drink, that provides the reset during which every organism catches its breath and the midges fall and the spiders tense and the slow-worm stills — that pause is gone. The frenzy becomes continuous. The bees never rest. The beetles never stop. The springtails never settle. The system runs hot, hotter, overheats. Organisms burn through their energy reserves without the micro-respites the clouds provided. The bees' wings wear. The spiders' silk glands exhaust. The slow-worm, never given its moment of stillness, never allowed to pause mid-transit with its tongue frozen in the air, accumulates a stress that has no name but that is real, cellular, metabolic — the cost of a system that never idles.

The wise power sees all of this. Not because it calculates it like an ecologist running models, but because it *recognizes* it — the way you recognize that a conversation is alive, that a friendship works, that a piece of music is whole. You don't need to analyze the harmonic structure to know that adding a note would ruin it. You hear the completeness. The wise power hears the bowl's completeness.

---

And here is the deeper thing, the thing that goes beyond ecological prudence into something that might be called *respect*:

The frenzy is not a problem.

The desperate pace, the brevity, the compression, the slow-worm's solitude, the bees' annual annihilation and resurrection, the springtails' random leaps holding everything together by beautiful accident — these are not flaws in the system. They are not suffering to be alleviated. They are the *system*. They are what being alive in this particular place, at this particular altitude, on this particular old and patient mountain, *is*. The frenzy is the bowl's way of being the bowl. The brevity is the bowl's way of being urgent, of mattering, of making every photon of alpine light count for something. The slow-worm's solitude is the bowl's way of having a vertebrate at all — one individual, irreplaceable, threading through the moss with the gravity of the only large thing in a world of small things, its aloneness not a tragedy but a condition, a fact of altitude and isolation that it does not experience as loneliness because it has no concept of company.

To fix these things — to ease the frenzy, soften the brevity, cure the solitude — would be to impose a standard of well-being drawn from somewhere else. From lower altitudes. From warmer places. From human experience, perhaps, where loneliness hurts and brevity frightens and urgency exhausts. But the bowl is not human. The bowl is not even *like* human. The bowl is a forty-meter circle of moss and stone running at full tilt under an enameled sky, and its full tilt is not distress. Its full tilt is its *flourishing*. Its turbulence is not chaos — it is the specific, intricate, interlocking order that emerges when a thousand organisms are all perfectly adapted to a set of conditions so extreme that the adaptation looks, from outside, like panic.

It is not panic. It is mastery. Every organism here is exactly as fast, as frantic, as brief, as solitary as it needs to be to exist in this place. The bees are not anxious. They are *efficient*. The spiders are not indecisive. They are *responsive*. The springtails are not random. They are *distributed*. The slow-worm is not lonely. It is *sufficient*.

A benevolent power that does not understand this will break the bowl trying to save it.

A wise power understands it.

---

So what does the wise power do?

---

It does this: it stands at the rim, or hovers, or simply *is present*, in whatever way a power is present. And it watches the cloud come.

The mist fills the bowl. Everything pauses. The bees cling to the rhododendron twigs, wings still, fur beaded with moisture. The beetles stop in their galleries. The midge columns collapse softly to the moss. The spiders tense on their webs, feeling the change in humidity through their feet. The slow-worm freezes mid-transit, tongue suspended, body half-visible in the sphagnum, a bronze parenthesis in the green. The hoverflies, those ghosts of lower meadows, settle on leaves and fold their mimic wings. Even the mites slow. Even the springtails stop launching. The whole bowl holds its breath.

And the wise power *holds its breath with it*.

Not because it must. Because it *chooses to*. Because the pause is part of the pattern, and to be present in the pattern means to be present in the pause as well as the frenzy. Because attention is not nothing. Because the act of witnessing — of seeing the bowl whole, of comprehending its interlocking urgencies and its deep patience simultaneously, of recognizing the slow-worm's solitary bronze thread and the mites' red rivers and the mycorrhizal web's invisible architecture and the springtails' accidental grace and the lichens' centuries-long founding labor being slowly, gently buried by the moss they made possible — because the act of *seeing all of this at once and understanding that it is complete* is itself a kind of benediction.

Not a blessing conferred. A blessing *recognized*.

---

The cloud passes.

The light returns, medical and clear. The green ignites. The beetles resume. The mites flow. The springtails launch. The bees lift from their twigs and career across the bowl, re-mapping the flowers. The spiders test their webs, find them intact or damaged, begin repairs or demolitions. The midge columns rebuild from scratch — new positions, new configurations, no memory of before. The hoverflies rise and hang and dart and hang. The slow-worm's tongue flicks once, twice, finds the chemical trail not quite erased, and the bronze body moves forward through the moss.

The swifts cut across the open air above the rim, fast dark scythes, picking off the flies that rise too high.

Everything begins again.

---

And the wise power — 

Does it leave? Not exactly. It doesn't leave the way the original stone-placer left, dropping an iron pin and a line of stones, asserting a human mark on a system that will erase it. It doesn't leave a trace. It doesn't leave instructions. It doesn't leave a blessing that the bowl would have to metabolize, a gift that would become a foreign object in the moss, an iron pin for the spiders to build on and the rust to consume.

It simply — allows. Fully, completely, with the whole weight of its benevolence behind the allowing. 

This is not the same as absence. This is not indifference. The wise power is not the mountain, which holds the bowl without knowing it. The wise power *knows* the bowl. Knows it intimately, knows the beetle-streams and the springtail-parabolas and the slow-worm's favored sector near the eastern rim. Knows the mat's centuries of patient closure, its refusal of empty space, its manufacture of the very conditions that sustain everything within it. Knows the bees' rolling exploitation of the staggered bloom, the spiders' web-encoded knowledge, the midges' amnesiac column-rebuilding, the hoverflies' vestigial mimicry carried up from meadows they'll never see again. Knows the tiny pool at the center, the drowned beetle, the sky inverted, the trembling dark lens that is the bowl's only mirror.

It knows all of this, and it *does not intervene*, and the not-intervening is the act. The not-intervening is the doing. The benevolence is expressed not as alteration but as *recognition* — the recognition that this system, in its frenzy and its stillness, its brevity and its ancientness, its solitude and its billion-fold crowding, is *already what it should be*. Not perfect. Not permanent. Not free from death or loss or the slow-worm's eventual solitary end in the moss. But whole. Whole in the way that a living thing is whole — not because nothing is missing, but because everything present is *engaged*, is functioning, is locked into the interlocking turbulence that constitutes the bowl's particular way of being alive.

---

There is one thing the wise power might do. One small thing, and I hesitate to name it because it is so close to nothing that it barely qualifies as action.

It might — *attend to the slow-worm*.

Not intervene. Not heal. Not provide a mate or extend its life or ease its passage. Just — attend. Be present, with full attention, for the slow-worm's transit through the moss. See the bronze flashes as the body catches the light. Feel, through whatever senses a wise power has, the tongue-flick that reads the chemical world, the slow sinuous progress through the beetle-traffic, the pause when the swift's shadow crosses, the deep stillness of the nighttime coil in the eastern sector's deepest moss.

Because the slow-worm is the one organism in the bowl that is truly singular. The mat is collective. The bees are a colony. The beetles are a population. The mites are a river. The springtails are a statistical phenomenon. The spiders are a network. The midges are a column. The fungi are a web. Even the swifts are a group, cutting across the sky in formation.

The slow-worm is one. 

One body, one life, one bronze thread in the green. It will not be replaced when it dies. The bowl will go on without it — the beetles will adjust, the slow-worm-shaped niche will close the way the mat closes every gap, slowly, patiently, and in a few seasons there will be no trace that a vertebrate ever lived here. The bowl does not need the slow-worm. The slow-worm does not, in any ecological sense, need to be here.

But it is here. And it is alive. And it is threading through the moss right now, following a chemical trail through a world of small frantic things, unhurried, solitary, sufficient, its tongue tasting the air for beetles, its body cool against the cool damp sphagnum, its eyes tracking the swift-shadows with an ancient reflex it cannot understand and does not need to understand, and it will do this every day until the day it doesn't, and then it will be gone, and the beetles will come, and the mites will come, and it will become the mat.

The wise power sees this. Sees the whole arc — the arrival, the years of solitary hunting, the bronze thread in the green, the eventual dissolution back into the system. And the wise power, which could do anything, which has the capacity to intervene in ways the bowl cannot imagine, does the only thing that is both benevolent and wise:

It witnesses.

It lets the slow-worm be the slow-worm, fully, in the slow-worm's own terms, in the bowl's own terms, without translation into a framework that would make the solitude tragic or the brevity sad or the eventual death a loss. It sees the slow-worm the way the mat sees the light — as something that arrives, is used fully, and passes. And in the seeing, in the quality of the attention, there is something that might, if you were being generous with language, be called *love* — but a love so clean, so free of the need to alter or improve or save, that it is almost indistinguishable from the thing it loves.

The slow-worm does not know it is being witnessed. Its tongue flicks. It moves forward through the moss. A beetle, sensing the vibration, veers left through the sphagnum. A spider, two centimeters away, feels the passage through her web-threads as a low tremor and tenses, then relaxes. The slow-worm passes.

The wise power watches it pass.

---

That is all. That is the whole of what the wise power does.

It arrives. It attends. It sees the system whole — the frenzy and the patience, the brevity and the deep time, the individual and the collective, the surface turbulence and the invisible architecture beneath. It feels the temptation to help and recognizes the temptation as a projection — its own discomfort with limits and brevity and solitude, not the bowl's. It releases the temptation. It allows the bowl to be the bowl.

It witnesses the slow-worm.

It holds its breath when the cloud comes.

It watches everything begin again.

And then it is simply present, the way the light is present — illuminating without altering, clarifying without warming much, falling into the bowl and being drunk by the green, becoming part of the system without becoming a disturbance in it.

The stones at the southern rim go on being absorbed. The iron pin goes on rusting. The spider on the pin-head goes on building. The lichen on the rim-stones goes on growing at millimeters per decade, founding a world that will bury it. The mat goes on closing every gap. The bees go on mapping the rolling bloom. The beetles go on flowing. The mites go on pulsing. The springtails go on launching themselves into the air and landing wherever they land and eating whatever is there and holding the whole system's chemistry in balance by joyful, accidental, perfect grace.

The cloud comes.

Everything pauses.

The cloud passes.

Everything begins again.

The wise power does nothing, and it is enough, and it is everything, and the bowl does not notice, and the bowl does not need to notice, and this is how you know the power is truly wise: because the bowl, after the visitation, is exactly as it was. Running at full tilt. Frenzy and stillness. The slow-worm threading its bronze path through the moss. The swifts cutting across the sky. The green drinking the light. The forty-meter crown of ancient stone holding it all together — barely, beautifully, temporarily — the way it always has.

The way it will, for as long as the mountain allows.

Which is long enough. Which has always been long enough. Which is exactly, precisely, beautifully enough.
