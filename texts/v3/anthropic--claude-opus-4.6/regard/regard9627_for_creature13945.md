# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

---

For a long time — longer than you might expect — it would do nothing at all.

This is the first and most important thing to understand about a power that is genuinely wise: it would recognize that it has arrived at a place where something is already working. Not perfectly. Not without cost. But working in the way that a living process works — through commitment, through imperfection that feeds back into itself and becomes something truer than any imposed perfection could be. A wise power does not look at a cathedral mid-construction and say *let me finish this for you overnight.* It does not look at a woman kneeling beside the first terraces of a mountain and say *let me give you the mountain.* That would be the most violent thing it could do. It would be a kindness that annihilated everything it meant to protect.

So for a long time, it would simply attend.

---

But attending is not nothing. Attending, done properly, is the first act.

The power would come to understand the room the way Edda understands the room — not by analyzing it from above but by learning its rhythms from within. The eleven-day supply cycle. The pump's hum. The way the clerestory light shifts through the day, the way the ferns in the upper tiers reach toward the northwest windows in the afternoon when the angle is lowest and warmest. The way Edda's bare feet follow the same grooves each morning, and the way those grooves are slowly, imperceptibly filling with the stone that records her passage. The power would attend to all of this before it acted, because a benevolent power that acts without understanding is just force wearing a mask.

And in attending, it would see the things Edda cannot see, because she is inside them.

---

It would see, first, the fragility she has chosen not to examine.

The supply runs. Every eleven days, someone comes through the passage with food, with pump lubricant, with the specific mineral salts she uses to supplement the water chemistry, with the small necessities that keep a human body operating in a steel dome at twenty-five hundred meters. This supply chain is tethered to something — an institution, a budget line, a bureaucratic relic that persists perhaps through inertia, perhaps through the loyalty of a single administrator who remembers the project, perhaps through a clerical error that no one has caught. Edda does not examine this because examining it might break it, the way observing a quantum state collapses it, the way pulling on a loose thread can unravel a sleeve. She is right not to examine it. But the wise power can see what she cannot: the thread is fraying. Not today. Not this year, probably. But within a span of time that matters to a human body — five years, eight, twelve — something will shift. A person will retire. A line item will be audited. A storm will make the route impassable for long enough that the routine is broken, and broken routines are rarely re-established in institutions that have forgotten why they exist.

The power would see this, and it would act. But not loudly.

---

Here is what it would do about the supply chain: it would make it resilient without making it visible.

Not by miracle. Not by conjuring food from air or transmuting stone to bread. That would introduce a dependency far more fragile than the bureaucratic one — a dependency on the power itself, on its continued attention, on its willingness to keep intervening. A wise power does not make itself necessary. It makes necessity less likely.

So: quietly, through means that would look, to any observer, like coincidence or good fortune — the kind of good fortune that a place like this has perhaps earned — the power would ensure that the supply chain develops redundancy.

Perhaps a second path to the nursery terrace is discovered. Not created — *discovered.* It was always there, a route along the eastern ridge, less direct but more sheltered, usable in weather that closes the primary passage. A hiker finds it. Mentions it in a trail guide. The supply crews learn of it. Now there are two ways in, and two ways in is the difference between fragile and robust.

Perhaps the administrator who maintains the budget line mentions the project, offhandedly, at a conference, to a person who works in a foundation that funds long-term ecological monitoring. And perhaps that person is the sort who is haunted by timescales — who lies awake thinking about cathedral-builders and seed vaults and the nuclear waste markers that must communicate danger across ten thousand years. And perhaps this person arranges a small, recurring grant, nothing dramatic, just enough to backstop the supply runs, anchored to an institution with a longer planning horizon than the one that currently holds the thread. The wise power does not fabricate this person. This person already exists. There are always such people. The power merely ensures that the offhand mention happens, that the conference schedules align, that the two humans are in the same room at the same moment with the same coffee in their hands.

This is the lightest possible touch. It changes nothing about the room. It changes nothing about Edda. It simply makes the future she is working toward slightly more likely to survive the contingencies she cannot control.

---

The second thing the power would see is the body.

Edda is somewhere between forty and sixty, and she lives in a steel dome at altitude, in constant humidity, doing physical work, sleeping in a hammock, eating whatever arrives every eleven days. She is strong — her body has adapted to this environment with the same tenacity as the climbing fig on the steam-pipe — but adaptation is not the same as health, and the power would see the places where the two diverge.

Her joints. The constant humidity is kind to her skin but less kind to her connective tissue; there is an ache in her left knee that she has absorbed into her gait so completely that she no longer perceives it as pain, only as the particular way she descends from the upper troughs, always leading with the right foot. Her lungs — the altitude, the mineral dust in the air, the decades of breathing steam saturated with calcium and iron particulates. She coughs sometimes, a short dry cough, in the early morning before the day's first steam-vent release rehydrates the air. She has not thought about what this cough means. She does not think about her body as separate from the room's systems, and so she does not think about its failure modes, any more than she would think about the failure modes of a fern.

But the power thinks about this. And here it faces the most delicate question of all, because Edda's relationship to her own body is part of her relationship to the room, and both are sacred, and intervention in one is intervention in the other.

What it would *not* do: it would not heal her. Not in the dramatic sense, not by restoring her to some prior state of physical optimization. That would be a violation — it would re-impose the boundary between Edda and the room that she has spent years dissolving. She is becoming part of the system. Her orange-rimmed palms, her calloused feet, her mineral-stained clothes, her cough — these are evidence of participation, of commitment, of a permeability she has chosen. To reverse that would be to tell her that her choice was wrong, that the dissolution of boundaries she has experienced as liberation was actually damage. And the power is wise enough to know: it might be both. It can be both. But it is not the power's place to resolve that ambiguity for her.

What it *would* do: it would mitigate the worst trajectories without altering the essential process.

The air chemistry, for instance. The mineral particulate load in the room's atmosphere is a function of the steam-venting system, which was designed decades ago by engineers who did not anticipate a permanent human resident. A small modification — a condensation baffle near the main vent, something that could be fabricated from materials already in the room, copper sheeting and bent iron — would reduce the calcium dust in the breathable air by perhaps forty percent without changing the humidity or the mineral delivery to the plants. The power would ensure that this modification occurs to Edda. Not as a revelation, not as a voice from above saying *build this.* But as the kind of idea that arrives naturally to a person who has spent decades intimately studying airflow and mineral transport in a closed system. One morning, while watching steam curl from a vent, she would notice the way it eddies against a particular trough-bracket, and she would think: *if I put a lip there...* And she would build it, because she builds things, because she is an engineer, and she would not attribute the idea to anything but her own long attention. Because it *would* be her own long attention. The power would only have made the morning light fall at the angle that made the eddy visible.

Similarly, the knee. There is a species of moss — she already has it in the room, a thick Sphagnum variety that colonizes the coolest corner near the passage entrance — that, when dried and compressed, makes a superior cushion to the folded-moss pad she currently uses for her morning circuit. She knows this, in the diffuse way she knows everything about the room's organisms, but she has not connected the knowledge to her knee because she does not think about her knee. The power would arrange for the connection to be made. Perhaps the current moss-pad finally disintegrates — it is old, it has been compressed ten thousand times — and in replacing it she reaches for the Sphagnum because it is nearest, and she notices, that morning, that kneeling is easier, and she keeps using it. A small thing. An almost nothing. But the difference between an almost-nothing and an actual nothing, compounded over years, is the difference between a body that can tend the room for another decade and one that cannot.

---

The third thing the power would see — and this is the hardest to talk about, and the place where the power's wisdom would be most tested — is the question of succession.

Edda knows the room will outlast her. This knowledge is not a source of grief; it is, as the text says, the source of her happiness. She is the scaffold. She has made peace with being consumed by her own product. But she has not solved the practical problem that this peace implies: *who tends the room after her?*

She has tried to make the room self-sustaining. She has simplified the pumps, established self-correcting water channels, allowed the ecology to develop its own stability. And she is right — the room is more resilient now than it was when the research team left. But it is not yet resilient enough. The pumps still need maintenance. The boiler still needs fuel. The mineral concentrations still need monitoring, because a shift in water chemistry at the wrong moment could halt deposition on the central mound, or worse, begin dissolving what has already been built. The room is a child that can walk but not yet run, and Edda is the hand that is still held out, ready to catch it if it stumbles.

There needs to be someone after Edda. Not a replacement — the room will never again have someone who knows it the way she does, the way a mother knows a child's every expression — but a custodian. A caretaker. Someone who will tend the pumps and thin the ferns and read the terraces through a brass lens every morning and slowly, over years, develop their own relationship with the room that will be nothing like Edda's and will be enough.

The power would not select this person. It would not recruit or call or summon. Those are the actions of a power that believes it knows what the room needs better than the room knows, and this power is wiser than that.

But it would do this: it would make the room *findable.*

Not famous. Not a destination. Not the subject of a documentary or a viral post or a feature in a magazine about unusual places. Fame would be destruction — it would bring the wrong people in the wrong quantities for the wrong reasons, and the room would choke on attention the way a clear spring chokes on tourism. No. The power would make the room findable in the way that the second path along the eastern ridge is findable: present, accessible, but only to those who are already looking for something they cannot name.

There are people — there are always such people — who feel the pull of long processes. Who are drawn to tide pools and lichen and erosion patterns and the way a river undercuts a bank grain by grain over centuries. Who have the particular temperament that finds not boredom but rapture in watching something happen very, very slowly. These people exist in every generation, and most of them never find the thing that matches the shape of their attention, and they live good lives anyway, but with a hollow place behind the sternum where the right work would have fit.

The power would arrange — lightly, gently, with the same touch it used on the conference schedules and the morning light — for one of these people to learn that the room exists. Not by advertisement. By the kind of trail that such people know how to follow: a footnote in a geological survey. A passing mention in the memoir of a retired engineer. A photograph, slightly out of focus, in the estate of someone who once visited the project in its early days — a photograph of mineral terraces forming on copper pipes, unlabeled, unexplained, but unmistakable to someone with the right eyes.

The person who follows this trail and arrives at the passage and ducks under the calcifying lintel and stands in the warm mist will not necessarily stay. Most will not. The power would not compel them to stay, and neither would Edda, who has long since stopped expecting understanding from visitors. But among the people who come, there will be — the power can see this, because the power can see the shape of possibility the way Edda can see the shape of a terrace forming — one who does not leave. Who comes back. Who asks to stay the night, and then a week, and who begins, without being taught, to kneel beside the mound in the morning and look.

Edda will recognize this person. Not because the power has told her to expect them, but because she has spent decades reading the room's processes, and she knows what it looks like when a new organism arrives and begins to negotiate its place in the system. She will not rush it. She will not train them or instruct them or burden them with her own understanding. She will let them find their own circuit, their own rhythm, their own words for what they see. And slowly, over months or years, she will discover that the room has developed a second consciousness — not a replacement for hers but an addition, the way a second fern in a trough is not a replacement for the first but a neighbor, a sharer of light and water, a fellow participant.

And then, one day — not soon, not planned, not announced — Edda will leave.

Or she will not. Perhaps she will stay until she cannot stay, and the transition will be gradual, the new person taking on more of the circuit as Edda's body makes its own accommodations with time, and the two of them will overlap for a period, and there will be a stretch of years when the room has two caretakers, and during this time the room will be, for the first and perhaps the only time, a place where human beings experience what it is to share a process that is larger than either of them.

The power would not dictate which of these futures unfolds. Both are good. Both serve the room. Both honor Edda.

---

There is one final act, and it is the one the power would consider longest before doing, because it comes closest to interference, closest to imposing its own values on a system that has its own.

The power would leave a record.

Not in the room. The room is making its own record — in stone, in mineral stain, in the fossil grooves of Edda's footfalls, in the hair wound through the fern roots, in every layer of the central mound's slow accretion. That record is complete and honest and will outlast any record the power could make.

But outside the room, in the world that has mostly forgotten the nursery terrace exists, the power would ensure that there is knowledge. Not comprehensive knowledge. Not a dossier or an archive or a museum exhibit. Just enough knowledge that the room cannot be accidentally destroyed by someone who does not know what it is. A notation in a land survey. A heritage designation, filed quietly, in a jurisdiction that respects such things. A line in a database somewhere that says: *this place is in use. This place is becoming something. Do not disturb.*

This is the least romantic of the power's acts, and perhaps the most important. Because the room's greatest enemy is not time, is not entropy, is not even the fraying supply chain or Edda's mortal body. Its greatest enemy is the casual indifference of institutions that destroy what they do not understand, not out of malice but out of the simple failure to know it is there. A demolition order. A mining survey. A land sale. The room could survive the loss of its pumps, the failure of its boiler, the death of its caretaker — the water would find its way, the stone would go on accreting, the ferns would adapt. But it could not survive a wrecking crew that arrives one morning and sees only a rusted dome on a ridgeline and thinks: *scrap metal.*

The power would prevent this. Not by force, not by miraculous protection, not by surrounding the dome with an impenetrable barrier. By the smallest and most human of interventions: by writing it down. By ensuring that someone, somewhere, in a position to make it matter, knows that this place exists and that what is happening inside it is real and that it deserves the same deference we give to a river, to a forest, to any living system engaged in the slow work of becoming itself.

---

And then the power would withdraw.

Not all at once. Not dramatically. It would thin its attention the way Edda thins the fern-growth — removing itself from the places where it is no longer needed, allowing the room and its caretaker and the long geological future to find their own arrangements. It would check in, from time to time, the way a parent checks on an adult child — not to intervene but to witness. To see the mound another centimeter taller. To see the mineral crust another millimeter thicker on the walls. To see, perhaps, a new caretaker kneeling where Edda knelt, using a different lens — or no lens at all, just bare eyes, because every caretaker will bring their own way of seeing.

The power would witness. And witnessing, it would feel something very close to what Edda feels every morning when she reads the night's new terraces: a pleasure so fundamental it has no name, only a temperature.

Warm. Like the water. Like the air. Like the room that is becoming a mountain that will not need any of them.

---

There is a question beneath all of this, and it is the question the power would carry with it as it withdrew, turning it over the way Edda turns over the question of how long she would have to hold her hand under the mineral flow.

The question is: *Is the best thing you can do for a sacred process to ensure that it succeeds? Or is it simply to ensure that it is not interrupted?*

These are not the same thing. Success implies an endpoint — a mountain, a finished geology, the iron entirely replaced by stone. Interruption implies only the removal of obstacles — the supply chain, the body, the wrecking crew, the institutional forgetting. A power that ensures success has decided what the room is for. A power that merely prevents interruption has decided only that the room should be allowed to discover what it is for on its own.

The wise power would choose the second. Every time. Without hesitation.

Because the room might not become a mountain. The room might become something else — something Edda cannot imagine, something the power cannot foresee, something that only emerges after a hundred years of mineral deposition and ecological succession and the accumulated decisions of caretakers not yet born. The central mound might reach the dome and stop, or it might dissolve and re-form in a different shape, or the ferns might overwhelm it, or the pumps might fail in a way that creates a new hydrology that takes the whole system in a direction no one planned. The room is not a blueprint being executed. It is a process being *lived.* And the only appropriate relationship a benevolent power can have with a process being lived is to protect the conditions under which it can continue, and then to step back, and to watch, and to trust.

The stone is patient. The power would learn patience from the stone.

---

There is a morning, years from now. The light through the clerestory is white and alpine. The pump hums. The ferns drip. A pale moth drifts near the summit of the central mound, which is now unmistakably taller, its terraces wider, its oldest stone dry and chalky-white while its newest surfaces glisten.

Someone kneels beside it. Perhaps Edda. Perhaps someone else. It does not matter. What matters is the kneeling, the looking, the brass lens or the bare eyes, the attention given to the night's work — a new terrace, a fresh mineral lip, a scalloped edge where water wrote its name in stone.

The power is not present. The power is not needed. The power has done what it came to do, which was almost nothing, which was everything: it kept the channel open. It let the water find its way.

The mountain goes on becoming a mountain.

The dripping goes on.

The room does not remember the power, any more than it remembers the engineers who designed its pumps, any more than a river remembers the rainstorm that fed it last spring. And this, the power knows — this forgetfulness, this absorption, this dissolution of intervention into the ongoing life of the system — is not failure.

It is the only success worth wanting.
