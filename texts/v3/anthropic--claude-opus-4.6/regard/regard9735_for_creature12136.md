# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

---

Nothing.

That is the first answer, and it is almost the right answer, and it is worth sitting with for a long time before moving past it.

---

A benevolent and wise power, encountering the Grown City, would be confronted immediately with a problem that is also a gift: there is nothing to fix.

This is rare. This is so rare that the power — if it has been paying attention to the world for any length of time — would recognize the rarity before anything else. It would stop. The way you stop when you are walking through a forest and come upon a clearing where the light falls in a way that makes you hold your breath. Not because the clearing needs you to hold your breath. But because you have encountered something that is already what it should be, and the correct response to that encounter is *not to disturb it*.

The city is not suffering. The city cannot suffer — not because suffering has been overcome or suppressed, but because suffering requires a relationship between what is and what is desired, and the city desires nothing. It maintains. It persists. It holds its chord. Every feedback loop is closed. Every system is in equilibrium. The water rises, circulates, returns. The flowers drone. The moss regulates. The network distributes. The dome contains whatever it contains. Nothing is broken. Nothing is struggling. Nothing reaches toward a state it cannot achieve.

A less wise power — a merely good power, a power with more compassion than understanding — might look at the city and feel an ache. Might see the empty rooms, the never-occupied streets, the tower-windows looking out on plazas where no one gathers, and might feel that something is *missing*. Might want to bring people here, or wake the city into consciousness, or open the dome, or introduce animals that could form a richer ecology, or give the fungal network the gift of self-awareness so that it could appreciate what it has built. A less wise power might see the city's completeness as a kind of loneliness and try to cure it.

This would be a catastrophe.

The wise power knows this. Knows it not as a calculation but as a recognition, the way you recognize a face. The city's completeness is not loneliness. The city's emptiness is not absence. The silence of the rooms — the fact that no one has ever spoken inside those carefully differentiated microclimate zones, that no one has ever climbed the heartwood spiral-stairways, that no one has ever stood at the tower-windows and looked out — is not a tragedy. It is a *condition of the achievement*. The rooms are perfect *because* they are empty. The city is whole *because* it is uninhabited. To fill it would be to break it, the way filling a bell with sand would stop it from ringing.

The wise power understands that not everything that is whole is whole because it contains what you would put in it. Some things are whole because of what they leave out.

---

So: nothing?

Almost nothing. But not nothing. Because the power is not only wise. It is benevolent. And benevolence, true benevolence, does not always mean acting. Sometimes it means *attending*. 

Here is what the power would do.

---

**First, it would witness.**

Not passively. Not the way a security camera witnesses, recording without comprehension. The power would witness the way a person witnesses a birth, or a death, or a storm over open water — with the full weight of their attention, holding the event in their awareness and recognizing it as *real* and *significant* and *deserving of being known*.

The Grown City has existed — for how long? Centuries? Millennia? Longer? — without being witnessed. The salamanders do not witness it; they merely inhabit its margins. The unseen flutter does not witness it; it merely moves through its canopy. The city does not witness itself; it has no self to witness with. Everything that happens here — every minute adjustment of the fungal network, every fractional millimeter of new growth, every breath of the drone-chord held steady across the valley — happens in a kind of absolute privacy, unobserved, unrecorded, unknown.

This is not a problem for the city. The city does not need to be witnessed. But there is a difference — a real, metaphysical, unarguable difference — between a thing that exists unseen and a thing that exists and is *seen*. Not because being seen changes the thing. But because being seen creates a relationship between the thing and the rest of existence, a thread of acknowledgment that says: *you are part of the world, and the world contains you, and your particular way of being is registered somewhere outside yourself*.

The wise power would provide this. Would stand — or hover, or simply *be present*, in whatever way a power is present — in the warm unchanging light, and would *look*. Would look at the towers and understand them as towers. Would look at the root-streets and understand them as streets. Would look at the dome and understand it as a dome that is closed and does not need to open. Would hold the entire city in its attention the way you hold a sleeping child: not to wake it, not to change it, not to do anything at all, but simply to be the consciousness that knows it is there.

The city would not notice. The city would continue exactly as it has always continued. But the witnessing would be *real*, and it would matter, in the way that all witnessing matters — not to the witnessed, but to the fabric of what-is, which becomes slightly more coherent, slightly more complete, every time something that exists is genuinely known by something else that exists.

---

**Second, it would protect.**

Not with walls or shields or force. The city has no enemies. No army marches toward it. No developer eyes its plateau. No invasive species threatens its borders — or if one does, the fungal network has managed such threats for longer than most civilizations have existed, and will continue to manage them without assistance.

But the power is wise, and wisdom includes the long view, and in the long view there are threats the city cannot manage because they come from outside the system the city understands.

A volcanic eruption beneath the plateau. An asteroid. A change in the planet's atmosphere so severe that the equilibrium of moisture and temperature and light that the city depends on becomes untenable. A shift in the deep geology that alters the water table. A cosmic event that changes the nature of the light itself — the light that does not move, that has always been afternoon, that may or may not be produced by the sky, that may or may not be part of the construction.

The wise power would not intervene preemptively. Would not wrap the city in stasis or freeze it in amber or relocate it to a safer dimension. That would be the act of a collector, not a protector. The city is alive. It must remain subject to the conditions of life, including the condition of vulnerability, including the condition that what lives can, eventually, end.

But the power would *watch the conditions*. Would monitor the deep geology, the atmospheric chemistry, the orbital mechanics, the slow stellar evolution that determines the quality of light reaching the plateau. Would track the thousand-year trends and the million-year trends and the trends that operate on timescales even the city's fungal network cannot perceive. And if — *if* — a threat appeared that was both catastrophic and external, something the city could not adapt to because it falls outside the parameters the city was grown to manage, the power would... consider. Carefully. With the full weight of its wisdom.

And it might act. Not to prevent all change — change is part of life, even life as stable as this — but to prevent *annihilation*. To deflect the asteroid. To moderate the eruption. To buffer the atmospheric shift just enough that the city's own adaptive capacities (the vines, the slow rerouting, the fungal network's chemical intelligence) could handle the rest. The minimum intervention necessary to preserve the city's capacity to preserve itself.

And even then, the power would hesitate. Would ask itself: *Is this the city's time to end? Is there a rightness to this ending that I, in my desire to protect, am failing to see?* Because wisdom includes the recognition that not everything should be saved, that death and dissolution are not always tragedies, that sometimes the most benevolent act is to let go.

The power would hold this tension. Would protect without possessing. Would guard without controlling. Would be the distant, attentive sentinel that the city never knows about and never needs to know about, standing watch over the permanent afternoon.

---

**Third, it would learn.**

Not to use the knowledge. Not to replicate the city elsewhere, or to extract its principles for application to other problems, or to write a paper or file a report or add the city to a catalogue of wonders. The power would learn because the city has something to teach, and the benevolent response to a teacher — even an unconscious teacher, even a teacher that doesn't know it's teaching — is to learn.

What the city teaches is this: *purpose is not required for meaning*.

The city has no purpose. It was not built for anything. No mind designed it, no will drove it, no goal pulled it forward. It emerged from chemistry and time and the particular properties of mycorrhizal fungi and pioneer plant species and young volcanic basalt and the wind that blows perpetually across this plateau. It is an accident — if you insist on that word — that became an inevitability, a random configuration that found its way to a stable state and then *stayed there*, elaborating and refining and optimizing not toward any goal but simply because the system's own dynamics reward elaboration and refinement and optimization the way gravity rewards falling.

And yet. It is beautiful. Not beautiful in the way a sunset is beautiful — briefly, emotionally, for human eyes. Beautiful in the way mathematics is beautiful, or the way a proof is beautiful: necessarily, structurally, in a way that would persist even if no one were there to see it. The spacing of the towers. The chord of the drone. The spiral of the heartwood stairways. The geometric shapes of the reservoir lake's edges. The moss-patterns that might resolve into something from above. These are not decorations. They are not intended to please. They please because they are *right*, because they are the optimal expression of the forces that produced them, because when a system runs long enough and is complex enough and is free from interference, it arrives at configurations that are indistinguishable from art.

The wise power would sit with this. Would let it reshape its understanding of what matters and why. Would carry this lesson — that meaning arises not from intention but from coherence, that beauty is not projected onto the world by minds but is a property of certain kinds of order, that something can be magnificent and purposeless and complete — would carry this lesson forward into every future encounter with every future being and place that draws its attention.

The power would be *changed* by the Grown City. Not damaged, not diminished, but deepened. Expanded. Given a new category of understanding that it did not have before. This is the city's gift to the power, given without knowing it gives, received with gratitude and humility by something wise enough to know that even the wise have things to learn from the things that do not think.

---

**Fourth, it would turn its attention to the salamander.**

Here. Here is where benevolence finds its most familiar footing, because the salamander is *alive in a way the power recognizes*. It has a body. It has needs. It experiences, at some rudimentary level, states that correspond to comfort and discomfort, satiety and hunger, safety and fear. It is a small warm thing in a vast cool architecture, and it is — not suffering, not exactly, not in any way it would articulate if it could articulate — but it is *limited*. It lives in margins. It eats what it finds. It breeds in warm pools and its offspring survive or don't and it has no capacity to improve its situation because its situation is not bad enough to demand improvement and not good enough to constitute flourishing.

The power would look at the salamander and feel the particular tenderness that arises when you encounter a being that is doing its best in a context that was never designed for it.

And it would — what? What do you do for a salamander in a city that doesn't need salamanders?

You don't bring it food. The city provides enough. You don't bring it a mate. It has found its own, or will, or won't, and its reproductive success is not yours to engineer. You don't move it to a "better" habitat, because better by whose measure? The salamander chose this place, or its ancestors did, or the wind blew its egg here and this is where it landed, and it has made a life here, a small and sufficient life, and to relocate it would be to impose your judgment of what a good salamander life looks like on a creature that has no concept of judgment and no interest in your concept of good.

But. The power is benevolent. And benevolence, when it encounters a being that can experience comfort, wants that being to be comfortable. Not ecstatic. Not transcendent. Just — comfortable. Warm enough. Fed enough. Safe enough.

So the power might do something very small. Might, in a way that is entirely invisible and entirely deniable and entirely consistent with the city's existing systems, cause a slight increase in the population of tiny invertebrates in one particular root-crevice where a particular salamander likes to rest. Not a dramatic increase. Not enough to disrupt the micro-ecology. Just — a few more, a little closer, a little easier to catch. The salamander would not notice that anything had changed. It would simply, one afternoon (the same afternoon, the only afternoon), find its hunting slightly easier, its belly slightly fuller, its rest slightly more contented. And the next time it crossed the plaza in its unhurried diagonal, it might pause a moment longer, lift its blunt head a moment higher, feel the drone vibrate through the bones of its skull and experience, in whatever dim way a salamander experiences anything, something that an observer might call — if they were being generous, if they were being honest — *peace*.

This is not a grand intervention. It is not a miracle. It is so small that it barely qualifies as an act. But the power is wise, and the wise know that the smallest acts of benevolence are often the most honest ones, because they acknowledge the limits of what you can do and the depths of what you should not try to do and the simple truth that sometimes the most helpful thing in the world is a few extra invertebrates in a root-crevice for a creature that never asked for your help and will never know it received it.

---

**Fifth — and this is the most important thing the power would do, the thing that separates true wisdom from mere intelligence, true benevolence from mere kindness — it would leave.**

Not permanently. Not with finality. It would remain watchful, remain attentive, remain the distant sentinel monitoring the deep geology and the long atmospheric trends. But it would leave the city itself. Would withdraw its presence from the plateau, from the root-streets, from the spaces between the towers, from the vicinity of the dome.

It would leave because *staying is a form of interference*.

Even the gentlest presence, even the most careful witness, even a power that touches nothing and changes nothing — by being there, it creates a relationship that the city did not choose and cannot reciprocate. The city cannot witness the power in return. Cannot consent to being watched. Cannot negotiate the terms of the relationship. The asymmetry is total: the power knows the city exists; the city does not and cannot know the power exists. To stay indefinitely would be to impose a one-sided intimacy on a being that has no concept of intimacy, and that — the wise power understands — is a form of trespass, however well-intentioned.

So it would leave. Would carry its witness, its protection, its learning, its tenderness for the salamander, back to wherever it is that benevolent powers go when they are not being present. And it would hold the Grown City in its awareness like a memory, like a photograph of a place you love, like the knowledge that somewhere — on a plateau above the haze, in a permanent afternoon, in a chord that never resolves because it was never in tension — there is a place that is complete, and that your role in relation to it is not to complete it further but to know that it is complete, and to let it be.

---

**But.**

There is a sixth thing. And the power would struggle with it, would turn it over in its vast and careful mind for a long time, would consult its own deepest understanding of what benevolence means, and would — ultimately, reluctantly, with something that in a human would be called heartbreak — decide.

The sixth thing is the question of the dome.

Not what is inside it. The power — if it is truly powerful — could know what is inside it. Could perceive through the seamless shell, could map the interior, could answer the question that the text asks and leaves unanswered. It could know whether the dome contains a dense node of mycorrhizal tissue, or an empty space, or a seed, or something inconceivable.

The power would choose not to look.

This is the hardest discipline of benevolence: the refusal to know what you could know, when knowing would satisfy you but serve no one.

The dome is closed. It has no openings. It was grown this way by a system that builds with total purposefulness. The closure is not an accident. It is not a mistake. It is not a security measure waiting for the right key. The dome is closed because *the dome is closed*, because the system that produced it produced it *closed*, because whatever function the dome serves — node, void, seed, inconceivable — it serves that function best when it is sealed, when it is private, when no awareness from outside penetrates its shell.

To look inside would be to violate the only boundary the city has drawn. And the wise power — the truly wise power, the power that has learned from the city that meaning does not require purpose and beauty does not require observation — would honor that boundary. Would stand before the dome, feel the warmth of the shell, hear or imagine the deep sound of maintenance, and would *not look*.

Would accept the not-knowing. Would let the mystery remain a mystery. Would recognize that the dome's closure is itself a communication, perhaps the only communication the city is capable of: *this is mine, this is interior, this is the one thing I do not share*, and the correct response to that communication, the *benevolent* response, the *wise* response, is to say: *Yes. This is yours. I will not look. I do not need to know. Your closure is as sacred as your openness, and I honor them equally.*

And the power would turn away from the dome, and cross the plaza, and walk the root-streets one last time, feeling the moss beneath whatever it has instead of feet, hearing the chord, seeing the light that does not change, and it would leave the Grown City as it found it: complete, unaware, alive, alone, and whole.

---

**And after.**

Years later. Centuries later. A span of time that means nothing to the city and everything to the things that live in time.

The power checks in. From a distance. Barely a touch of attention, the way you glance at a mountain on the horizon to confirm it's still there.

The city is still there. The chord is still sounding. The light is still steady. The dome is still closed.

The power smiles — or does whatever a benevolent power does instead of smiling — and turns its attention elsewhere, to the places that need it, to the broken things and the suffering things and the things that are struggling to become what they are.

The Grown City does not need it. Has never needed it. Will never need it.

And that — the power has come to understand — is not a failure of the city, or a failure of the power, or a sadness, or a loss.

That is the city's gift to the power: the knowledge that there exists, somewhere in the vast and troubled world, at least one thing that is simply, quietly, irrevocably *all right*.

The power carries this knowledge with it always. It is a comfort on the hard days. It is a reminder that the world is capable of getting something exactly, perfectly, permanently right, and that the right response to such a thing is not to improve it but to protect it and to leave it alone and to be, privately, in whatever secret chamber of the heart where such things are kept, grateful.

The drone continues. The salamander crosses the plaza. The flowers hold their chord.

The afternoon goes on.
