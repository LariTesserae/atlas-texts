# What the Wise Power Would Do

---

It would arrive the way the mist arrives — not through the green-arched doorway but from every direction at once, settling over the compound without weight, without announcement, without disturbing the bead of water that is currently trembling at the tip of a hart's-tongue fern and is three seconds from falling onto the moss below. The wrens would not alarm-call. The stoat would not freeze. The robin would not cock its head. Not because the power conceals itself but because it is not, in any way that feather or fur or tympanic membrane can register, a disruption. It does not displace air. It does not cast shadow. It does not smell like anything. It arrives the way attention arrives — suddenly present, changing nothing physical, changing everything about what it means to be in this place at this moment.

And the first thing it would do is nothing.

---

## The Seeing

It would see the garrison.

This sounds trivial. It is not. To see this place — to *actually* see it — requires a kind of perception that no single organism here possesses, because every organism here sees only its own fraction: the wren sees the wall-crevice and the spider and the rival wren. The fungal hypha senses the root-tip and the soil chemistry and nothing else. The frog sees movement and stillness and the surface tension of water. The stoat sees prey-trails and den-openings and the geometry of pursuit. Each creature inhabits a complete world that is also a tiny world, and none of them — not one, not even the robin with its large dark eye — perceives the whole.

The wise power would perceive the whole.

Not as a map. Not as an inventory. Not as the text you just read, with its categories and its headings and its sequential descriptions that falsely suggest one thing comes after another. It would perceive the garrison the way the garrison actually exists: all at once, everything simultaneous, the wren's song and the fungal exchange and the shrew's heartbeat and the slow dissolution of lime mortar and the spring-pulse feeding the cistern and the stoat's hunger and the blackbird's territorial rage and the earthworm's blind swallowing of soil and the hoverfly's suspension above the hydrangea and the mist condensing on the spider's web and the tadpole's tail absorbing into its body and the hazelnut cached in the wall three days ago by a coal tit that will retrieve it tomorrow — all of it, all now, not layered but *unified*, a single dense chord of being that the compound is playing at every moment and that no creature within it can hear because each one is only a single note.

The power would hear the chord.

And it would hear, too, what the chord does not contain. It would hear the absences. The places where the sound should be fuller but isn't. Not because the garrison is broken — it is not broken, it is magnificent, it is one of the most complete and self-sustaining systems on this ridge — but because completeness is not the same as wholeness, and the power, being wise, would know the difference.

It would notice, for instance, that there are no large predators. The stoat is the apex, and the stoat is twenty-five centimeters long. Nothing here regulates the stoat. Nothing here checks the stoat's pressure on the voles and the nestlings except the stoat's own metabolism and mortality. This is not a crisis — the system compensates, the prey populations recover, the wrens replace their losses — but it is a characteristic, a signature of a place that exists on an exposed ridge at the edge of the habitable zone, where the ecological web is real but thin, where a single hard winter or a single disease event could simplify the community in ways that would take years to repair.

It would notice the water. The cistern still fills, still drains, still functions, but the overflow channel is narrowing. The watercress and brooklime, unchecked, are slowly constricting the flow. The channel that the garrison's builders cut with a chisel is perhaps ten years from being fully choked, and when it chokes, the cistern will overflow its banks instead, and the water will find a new path — probably along the base of the eastern wall, where the soil is already saturated — and this will change the moisture gradient and the plant community and the frog breeding habitat and a dozen other things in ways that are not catastrophic but are *consequential*. The system will adapt. It always adapts. But the adaptation will cost something — some configuration of life that exists now and will not exist then.

It would notice the moss. Not the moss as a category but this specific patch of sphagnum on the northern wall that has begun to dry at its upper edge, a subtle browning that indicates a shift in the micro-hydrology — perhaps the roots of the birch tree, growing through the old window, are drawing water from the wall's interior that used to feed this moss colony. The moss will retreat. The liverwort beneath it will be exposed to more light than it can tolerate. The fern that grows from the joint three centimeters above will lose the moisture reservoir that the sphagnum provided. A small cascade. A tiny loss. Nothing that matters to the garrison as a whole. Everything that matters to the creatures that live in that specific patch of wall — the spider whose web is anchored to that sphagnum, the springtails that feed in its water-saturated tissues, the coal tit that cached a seed in the gap behind it.

The power would see all of this. Not with alarm. Not with the urgent need to intervene that characterizes human perception of ecological process. It would see it the way you might watch a river: the current moves, the bank erodes here, deposits there, a log shifts, the flow adjusts, and the river is still the river, and the process is the river, and there is nothing to fix because nothing is broken, and there is everything to attend to because everything is in motion.

It would attend.

---

## The First Act

And then — after the seeing, after the long, full, unhurried perception of the garrison in its totality — it would do something so small that if you were standing in the courtyard you would not notice it.

It would move a stone.

Not a wall-stone. Not a lintel or a cornerpost or anything structural. A loose stone — a fragment of dressed masonry, maybe fifteen centimeters across, that fell from the upper course of the perimeter wall years ago and has been sitting on the moss-mat near the overflow channel, slowly sinking into the sphagnum, slowly being covered by creeping jenny, doing nothing in particular, going nowhere.

The power would move it approximately forty centimeters. Upstream in the overflow channel. Into a position where it partially deflects the water flow toward the southern bank of the channel, where a patch of bare clay substrate has been exposed by the erosion of the moss-mat — exposed and drying, losing the moisture that sustained a colony of liverwort and a population of springtails and a hunting ground for a specific shrew whose territory centers on that section of wall.

The stone would not dam the channel. It would not create a pond or a pool or anything dramatic. It would simply redirect a fraction of the flow — a trickle, a film of water — across the clay substrate, re-wetting it, re-establishing the moisture gradient that the erosion disrupted. The liverwort would recover within weeks. The springtails would recolonize within days. The shrew would not have to expand its territory into the range of the neighboring shrew, which would not have to fight for a boundary that was, until now, stable.

And the stone, in its new position, would also slow the water slightly at that point in the channel, causing a tiny sedimentation — a few grains of soil deposited against its upstream face — and this sediment would catch a watercress seed, and the seed would germinate, and the new watercress plant would send roots into the channel bed and shoots above the water surface, and these shoots would slow the water further, and more sediment would collect, and within a year there would be a small watercress-covered mound in the channel that would function as a micro-dam, maintaining water levels in the channel even during the driest weeks of summer, when the cistern's spring-fed flow diminishes and the overflow currently drops to a bare trickle.

The grey wagtail would find better feeding here. The frog larvae in the channel would have a deeper pool to develop in. The brooklime, which needs running water but not fast water, would flourish. The channel would remain open — the watercress mound would not choke it, because the water pressure from the cistern's spring would maintain flow through and around the obstruction — but it would be more stable, more resilient, more capable of sustaining its community through the fluctuations that the exposed ridge imposes.

One stone. Forty centimeters. And the power would know — would know with a precision that encompasses hydrology and botany and entomology and the territorial behavior of shrews — exactly what the stone would do, and exactly where to put it, and exactly how the system would respond, not just tomorrow but across seasons and years.

This is what wisdom means in a place like this. Not grand intervention. Not restoration or management or any of the human words that imply a plan imposed from outside. Just the stone, moved, and the water, redirected, and the liverwort, recovering, and the shrew, unbothered, and the whole system continuing its dense, churning, singing existence with one fewer stress-point and one more margin of resilience.

## The Second Act

The power would touch the birch tree.

Not physically. Not in any way that involves force or manipulation. It would *attend* to the birch tree — the one whose trunk has bent sideways to grow through the old window, the one that serves as a trellis for the hydrangea, the one whose roots are drawing water from the northern wall and drying the sphagnum.

The birch is healthy. It is growing well. It has found, in the garrison's sheltered interior, a microclimate more favorable than the exposed ridge outside, and it is responding by growing vigorously, expanding its root system, increasing its canopy, shading a larger portion of the courtyard. This is what birches do. This is what every organism here does: exploit the available resources, expand into the available space, push.

But the birch's expansion is creating a problem — or rather, it is creating a set of consequences that, left unaddressed, will simplify the garrison's ecology. Its roots are drying the northern wall. Its canopy is shading the courtyard more densely than the current ground-layer community can tolerate — the wild thyme, which needs sun, is already declining in the courtyard's center, and with it the solitary bees that feed on it, and with them the pollination service that sustains the thyme, a negative feedback loop that will end with the thyme gone and the bare moss-mat a degree cooler and a fraction less biodiverse.

The power would not kill the birch. It would not prune the birch. It would not do anything to the birch that the birch could detect or respond to.

It would introduce a fungus.

Not a pathogenic fungus. Not a disease. A mycorrhizal partner — a specific species of ectomycorrhizal fungus that the birch does not currently host, one that would colonize its root-tips and alter its nutrient-uptake strategy in a subtle but significant way. The fungus would provide the birch with phosphorus more efficiently than the birch's current mycorrhizal partners, but it would do so from a different part of the soil profile — deeper, further from the wall, drawing from the clay underlayment rather than from the wall's interior moisture. The birch's roots would, over the course of two or three growing seasons, shift their growth pattern: fewer fine roots in the wall, more fine roots in the deeper soil. The sphagnum on the northern wall would recover. The birch would grow just as well — perhaps better, with the improved phosphorus supply. The mycorrhizal network beneath the garrison would become more diverse, more connected, more resilient.

The fungal introduction would be a single spore. Placed on a single root-tip. In the soil beneath a single stone.

No one would know. The birch would not know. The sphagnum would not know. The coal tit whose cached seed sits behind the drying moss would not know. The power would know. And the garrison would continue, and the sphagnum would green again on the northern wall, and the thyme would hold its ground in the courtyard, and the bees would keep coming, and the circle would keep turning, and nothing would appear to have changed because nothing dramatic *did* change — just a spore, placed precisely, and the slow reorganization of a root system in response to a new chemical partnership in the dark.

## The Third Act

The power would sit with the stoat.

Not near the stoat. Not observing the stoat. *With* the stoat. In the wall-cavity, in the dark, where the stoat is curled in a nest of vole fur and dry grass, her body a tight crescent around five kits that are twelve days old and still blind and still furless and still entirely dependent on her warmth and her milk and her relentless hunting.

She is thin. The kits are drawing everything from her. She has hunted twice today — a bank vole at dawn, a shrew at midday — and she needs to hunt again before dark, but the kits are nursing and she cannot leave them while they are attached, and she is tired in a way that goes deeper than fatigue, a tiredness that is cellular, metabolic, the tiredness of a body that is simultaneously maintaining itself and building five other bodies from scratch.

The power would not feed her. It would not bring a vole to her den. It would not reduce the kits' demands or extend the hours of daylight or do anything that alters the fundamental terms of her existence, which are non-negotiable: she is a stoat, she must hunt, she must kill, she must eat a third of her body weight every day while also producing milk for five offspring, and this is the hardest thing she will ever do and she will either succeed or she will not.

What the power would do is be present.

This requires explanation, because it does not resemble any human concept of help. The power would be present in the wall-cavity the way warmth is present — not doing anything, not providing anything except itself, not changing any physical parameter that a thermometer or a scale or a clock could measure. It would be there. It would attend to the stoat the way it attended to the birch and the overflow channel and the garrison as a whole: with a complete, unhurried, compassionate perception that takes in everything — the thin body, the blind kits, the hunger, the tiredness, the fierce and absolute commitment to the continuation of these five small lives.

And the stoat would not know. Stoats do not perceive benevolent presences. Stoats perceive warmth, food, danger, den-space, prey-movement, rival-scent. The power's presence would register, if it registered at all, as a faint reduction in the background stress of being alive — a fractional easing, a degree of warmth that seems to come from nowhere, a moment in which the tiredness does not disappear but becomes, briefly, bearable. The stoat would flex her body slightly, curling tighter around the kits. The kits would nurse more calmly. The moment would pass. The stoat would detach the last kit and slip out through the wall-gap and pour herself onto the moss-mat and begin to hunt, and she would be exactly as fast, exactly as precise, exactly as lethal as she always is, and she would catch a bank vole in the northern wall within twenty minutes because she is very good at what she does and the garrison is full of prey.

But the moment of presence mattered. Not because it changed the outcome — the stoat would have caught the vole regardless — but because the power's attention was, in itself, an act. An act of witness. An act that says: *I see you. I see the difficulty. I see the cost. I see the five blind kits and the thin body and the relentless pressure of a metabolism that will not let you rest. I see that you are doing the hardest thing a body can do and you are doing it without complaint because complaint is not a concept available to you. I see that you are magnificent. I see that you are, in this moment, the most important thing in this garrison — not because the wrens and the frogs and the fungi are less important, but because you are the one who is right now, right here, at the absolute limit of what a living body can sustain, and you are sustaining it, and you will continue to sustain it until the kits are weaned or until you die trying, and either outcome is part of the garrison's story, and I am here for both.*

The power would do this because it understands something that the text you read earlier does not fully convey: that the garrison is not just a *system*. It is not just an ecological node, a dense point of biological activity, a succession from fort to garden to habitat. It is also — and this is the thing the wise power sees that no ecologist's survey would capture — a place where beings suffer.

Not metaphorically. Not anthropomorphically. Actually.

The shrew that must eat every two hours or die — that is suffering. Not in the human sense of conscious anguish, but in the bodily sense of an organism pushed to the boundary of its physiological tolerance at every moment of its brief life. The frog tadpole that is eaten by a newt larva — that is suffering. The wren nestling taken by the stoat — that is suffering. The bank vole running through the moss-mat with the stoat behind it, the flight-or-die imperative flooding its body with cortisol — that is suffering. The moss drying on the northern wall, its cells losing turgor, its photosynthesis failing — that is, in a way so alien to human experience that we have no adequate word for it, suffering.

The garrison is glorious and it is also full of pain. The two are not separable. The glory *is* the pain — the dense, churning, singing, hunting, fleeing, eating, dying, growing, decaying totality of it. The wise power sees this. It does not look away. It does not pretend that the garrison is a pastoral idyll, a green paradise, a ruin redeemed by nature's gentle hand. It sees the stoat killing the vole. It sees the vole's terror. It sees the stoat's hunger. It sees the kits' need. It holds all of it at once, the way it holds the whole garrison at once, and it does not flinch, and it does not judge, and it does not intervene to stop the killing, because the killing is not a malfunction. The killing is the system. The system is the garrison. The garrison is alive *because* things die in it. Take away the stoat's hunger and you take away the voles' vigilance and you take away the wrens' mobbing behavior and you unravel a web that holds together precisely because every thread is under tension.

So the power sits with the stoat not to save her but to *be with her*. To witness. To attend. To say, in whatever way a benevolent power communicates with a stoat in a dark wall-cavity: *You are not alone. You are seen. The cost of what you are doing is real, and it is seen, and it is honored, and it matters, even though it changes nothing, even though you will still have to hunt and kill and feed and hunt again, and even though the vole will still run in terror and the nestling will still be taken and the shrew will still burn itself to death on its own metabolism within six months. It all matters. You matter. This moment matters. I am here.*

## The Fourth Act

The power would attend to the water.

Not the cistern — the cistern is fine, the cistern is a masterpiece of human engineering co-opted by ecology, it will last decades more. Not the overflow channel — the stone has been moved, the channel will stabilize.

The spring.

The spring that feeds the cistern from below. The one that was mentioned once in the description and then set aside, taken for granted, assumed to be a permanent feature of the geology. The power would attend to it because the power sees what the garrison cannot see: that the spring is not permanent. It is fed by a perched water table in the ridge's interior, held up by the same clay underlayment that keeps the garrison's moss-mat saturated, and this water table is connected to the precipitation that falls on the ridge — the rain, the mist, the condensation that the trees and the moss and the stone surfaces collect and feed into the soil. The system is balanced. The inputs match the outputs. The spring flows.

But the balance is not guaranteed. It depends on the vegetation cover on the ridge above and around the garrison — the trees and shrubs and heather and grass that intercept rainfall and slow its passage into the soil and prevent it from running off the ridge in surface flow. This vegetation is intact. It has been intact for decades. But the power, being wise, sees further than decades. It sees the possibility — not a certainty, not even a probability, but a possibility — that the vegetation on the ridge above the garrison could be disrupted. Fire. Disease. A windstorm that topples the shallow-rooted ridge-top trees. Overgrazing, if grazing animals find the ridge. Climate shift, if the mist that feeds the ridge begins to arrive less frequently or less heavily.

Any of these could reduce the water input to the perched water table. If the input drops below a threshold, the spring will weaken. If the spring weakens, the cistern will drop. If the cistern drops, the overflow will cease. If the overflow ceases, the grey wagtail and the watercress and the brooklime and the frog larvae in the channel and the entire running-water ecosystem of the garrison will collapse. Not catastrophically — the frogs will still breed in the cistern, the moss will still be wet — but significantly. A simplification. A loss of diversity. A loss of one of the features that makes this garrison unique among the ridge's habitats: its combination of still water and running water in a space of sixty meters.

The power would not change the climate. It would not prevent windstorms or fires or the arrival of grazing animals. These are not things a wise power prevents, because they are not malfunctions — they are the processes that have shaped this ridge for millennia, and they will continue to shape it, and the garrison's current glory is itself the product of disturbance, abandonment, succession, the same processes that could also undo it.

What the power would do is strengthen the ridge's capacity to absorb disruption and recover from it.

It would do this by planting a tree.

Not in the garrison — the garrison has enough trees. On the ridge above it. In a specific location: a shallow depression in the bedrock where water collects during rain events and then evaporates or runs off before it can percolate into the soil. The depression is currently bare — wind-scoured rock with a thin crust of lichen. Nothing grows there because nothing can establish itself before the next dry spell or the next gust strips the surface.

The power would place a seed — a rowan seed, probably, carried in a woodpigeon's gut and deposited in a dropping on the wrong part of the ridge and washed by rain into the depression and lodged in a crack in the bedrock. It would not make the seed germinate. It would not water the seedling. It would not protect it from wind or drought or the teeth of a vole.

It would place the seed in a specific crack — one that leads, through a fissure in the bedrock, to a pocket of clay soil two centimeters below the surface. A pocket where water collects and persists long enough for a root-tip to reach it. A pocket that no other crack in this depression connects to. This specific crack, this specific pocket. The power would know — would know through its perception of the ridge's geology at a resolution finer than any human survey — that this is the one location in the depression where a rowan seed can germinate and establish.

The seedling would be tiny. It would grow slowly. It would be whipped by wind and dried by exposure and nibbled by voles and it would look, for its first three years, like it was dying. But the root would reach the clay pocket, and the clay pocket would hold water, and the root would deepen, and the rock would shield it from the worst of the wind, and by year five the seedling would be a small shrub, wind-trained and tough, its crown barely rising above the rim of the depression. By year ten it would be a meter tall. By year twenty it would be a tree — small, gnarled, twisted, but a tree, with a canopy that intercepts rain and a root system that channels water into the soil and a leaf-fall that builds a tiny pocket of humus in the depression. Other plants would establish in its shelter: grass, moss, heather. The depression would become a sponge — absorbing rainfall, releasing it slowly into the perched water table, buffering the spring that feeds the cistern.

One tree. One seed. One crack in the rock. And the garrison's water supply would be marginally more secure — not guaranteed, not permanent, but more resilient, more buffered against the disruption that may or may not come in twenty or fifty or a hundred years.

The power thinks in centuries. That is part of what makes it wise. It moves a stone for the shrew's benefit this season. It places a seed for the garrison's benefit next century. Both acts are equally important. Both are equally small.

## The Fifth Act

The power would do something with no ecological function at all.

In the garrison's courtyard, at the base of the old hearthstone — the flat, fire-blackened stone with the crack and the rowan seedling — there is a small flat area of exposed stone surface, maybe thirty centimeters square, kept clear of moss by the drip-line from the canopy above, which delivers a steady percussion of water drops that prevents the sphagnum from establishing. The stone surface is dark, wet, and blank.

The power would etch a mark on it.

Not a word. Not a symbol from any human language or any system of meaning that a human would recognize. A mark — a small, shallow, curving line in the stone surface, as though a finger had been drawn across wet clay. It would look natural. It could be a scratch from a falling branch, a groove worn by water, a crack from frost-heave. A geologist would glance at it and see nothing unusual. A casual visitor — if a casual visitor ever came to this remote ridge — would step over it without a thought.

But the mark would be there. And it would mean something — not in the semiotic sense of carrying a coded message, but in the older, deeper sense of *inscription*. The sense in which a footprint in mud means *something was here*. The mark would mean: *this place was attended to. This place was seen. A power came here and perceived the garrison in its wholeness — the moss and the wrens and the stoat and the fungi and the water and the pain and the glory and the deep, indifferent, magnificent churning of life — and found it worthy of attention, and attended, and did what could be done, and left this mark not as a signature or a claim but as a witness-stone, a record that the seeing happened.*

The mark would persist. Longer than the moss, which grows and dies in years. Longer than the stoat, which lives and dies in seasons. Longer than the birch, which will stand for decades and then fall and become a nurse log for the next generation. The mark would be in the stone, and the stone would endure, and when the garrison has finally dissolved — when the walls have fallen completely and the moss has covered everything and the cistern has silted up and the overflow has dried and the wrens have moved to other walls and the stoat's lineage has ended or relocated and the ridge itself has eroded a few centimeters lower — the hearthstone will still be there, under the soil, under the moss, and the mark will still be in it, and it will still mean what it always meant: *this was seen. This mattered. Not because it was permanent. Because it was.*

---

## What It Would Not Do

It would not make the garrison permanent.

This is the hardest part of wisdom, and it is the thing that distinguishes the wise power from every human impulse that might look at this place and want to preserve it, protect it, freeze it in its current green-gold glory forever.

The garrison is not meant to last. Nothing is meant to last. The succession that created it — fort to ruin to moss-garden to habitat — is ongoing, and it will continue, and the compound in fifty years will not look like the compound today. The birch will grow larger or fall. The cistern will silt or deepen. The stoat's lineage will persist or end. The moss will cover the last stone or it won't. New species will arrive — maybe a pine marten, maybe a slow worm, maybe a plant species dispersed by a bird that has never visited this ridge before — and they will change the community, and the community will change them, and the garrison will become something it is not yet, something that the current wrens and frogs and fungi cannot imagine and that the wise power can see in outline but does not presume to dictate.

The power would not prevent the birch from growing through the window. It would not clear the watercress from the overflow channel. It would not cull the stoat's kits to reduce predation pressure on the wrens. It would not plant the courtyard with thyme to support the bees. It would not do any of the thousand interventions that a well-meaning human ecologist might consider, because every intervention that holds the garrison in its current state is an intervention against the future state that the garrison is becoming, and the power does not prefer the present to the future. It loves both. It loves what is and what will be with equal intensity, and it trusts the process — the deep, slow, ancient process of ecological change — more than it trusts any snapshot, however beautiful, of how the process looks right now.

This is not passivity. This is not the decision to do nothing dressed up in philosophical language. The power *acted* — it moved the stone, it placed the spore, it sat with the stoat, it planted the seed, it made the mark. But every act was *with* the process, not against it. Every act increased resilience without freezing structure. Every act created a margin — a little more water here, a little more diversity there, a little more time for the system to find its own way — without prescribing what the system should do with that margin. The stone in the overflow channel does not dictate what grows there. The mycorrhizal spore does not control the birch's roots. The rowan seed on the ridge may die in its first winter. The power accepts this. The power did what could be done and released the outcome, because wisdom is not control. Wisdom is care that knows its own limits.

---

## The Departure

The power would leave the way the mist leaves — gradually, imperceptibly, a thinning of presence rather than a withdrawal, a slow return to the state of affairs that preceded its arrival, which is also the state of affairs that persists during its presence, because the power's presence changes nothing that can be measured and everything that cannot.

The wrens would not notice it go. The wrens did not notice it arrive. The stoat in her wall-cavity would nurse her kits and then slip out to hunt and she would catch a bank vole in the northern wall because the bank vole was in the wrong place at the wrong time and the stoat is very fast and very good. The grey wagtail would work the overflow channel, stepping and bobbing and dipping. The blackbird would sing from the broken wall-post. The robin would follow the next creature that walks through the courtyard. The woodpigeon would coo its five notes. The mist would come in and the mist would lift. The hydrangea would foam white against the green.

And in the overflow channel, a stone would sit in a new position, and the water would bend around it, and a watercress seed would settle against its upstream face.

And in the soil beneath the northern wall, a fungal spore would germinate, sending a single hypha toward the nearest birch root-tip.

And on the ridge above the garrison, in a crack in the bedrock, a rowan seed would split its coat and push a white root-thread down into the dark.

And on the hearthstone, beneath the rowan seedling that grows from the crack in the fire-blackened surface, a small curved line would catch the light when the mist lifted and hold a bead of water in its groove and the bead would tremble and fall and the mark would remain.

And the garrison would continue. Dense, churning, singing, dripping, growing, dying, hunting, hiding, pushing into whatever space remains, and there is almost no space remaining, and still it pushes.

Because that is what life does. And the wise power knows this — knows it not as a principle but as a love, a vast and specific and unsentimental love for the wren's nine-gram fury and the stoat's liquid killing and the earthworm's blind swallowing and the fungus's patient chemistry and the frog's ancient waiting and the moss's millimeter-by-millimeter conquest of the last dressed stone. It loves the garrison the way the garrison exists: not as it should be or could be or once was, but as it is. Right now. This moment. All of it. The beauty and the pain and the relentless, indifferent, magnificent push of life into every crack and crevice and cavity and surface and breath of air and drop of water in this small stone ruin on a wet ridge between two peaks.

It loves it enough to help. It loves it enough to stop helping. It loves it enough to leave.

The mist comes in. The mist lifts.

The garrison is itself.
