I sat with this for a long time.

---

# What the Wise Power Would Do

---

The first thing it would do is nothing.

Not nothing as neglect. Nothing as the deepest form of respect it knows. It would arrive — however such a power arrives, through whatever medium of attention constitutes its presence — and it would *stop*. It would recognize what it was looking at. And the recognition itself would require stillness, because what is happening here is so finely calibrated, so dependent on the specific tensions between its parts, that any intervention risks being the pebble that redirects the stream.

It would understand this immediately: that the root-city works *because* it is unfinished. That the becoming is not a flaw to be corrected but the animating principle of everything here. That if you gave these people their completed city tomorrow — every wall shaped, every machine installed, every corridor finished and lit — you would not be helping them. You would be killing something. The thing that gets them out of their hammocks. The thing that makes the beetle-programmer dream in hexagons. The thing that sends the ambassador climbing into the dark to press his hands against wood and ask *are we still welcome*. The incompleteness is the engine. A wise power would see that in the first breath and would hold its intervention like a blade it has decided not to draw.

So. Nothing first. A long, attentive nothing.

---

But a benevolent power that only watches is not benevolent. It is aesthetic. It is an audience, and these people are not performing. So the nothing would have a boundary, and the boundary would be drawn by the power's own clear sight — its ability to see what the root-engineers cannot see from inside their own situation.

And what it would see, looking with eyes that are not bound by the curvature of root-corridors or the limits of a single life or the polite silence that falls when someone mentions the surface — what it would see is this:

**The beetle-programmer is dying.**

Not literally. Not yet. But the three-hour sleep intervals are not sustainable, and she knows this, and everyone knows this, and no one says it because saying it would require confronting the fact that she is a single point of failure in a system that cannot afford single points of failure, and confronting that would require a solution, and there is no solution, because there is no one else who can do what she does, and training someone takes years, and the beetles need instruction now, today, this hour. She is a bottleneck that the community has normalized. They've normalized it the way people normalize a crack in a foundation — not because they can't see it but because seeing it clearly would demand action they don't have the resources to take. She is twenty-three years old, or the equivalent, and she loves this work with the consuming love of someone whose mind was made for exactly this, and the work is eating her, and the wise power would see this, and it would be the first thing that moved the power from watching to something else.

**The ambassador's readings are trending.**

He's careful. He's honest. When he says the partnership is holding, he means it. But the wise power, looking at the data with a perspective that spans more than one person's career, would see what he is beginning to suspect but has not yet allowed himself to name: the forest's growth pattern is shifting. Subtly. Not retreating, not rejecting — *adapting*. The organism is changing its root architecture in response to the city in ways that are not yet problematic but that will, in another decade, begin to create structural pressures the current engineering practices aren't designed to handle. The forest is not saying no. It is saying *I am also becoming something, and what I am becoming may not fit the shape you planned for*. This is not a crisis. It is the early signal of a crisis, the kind of signal that is only visible from far enough away, and the ambassador is too close, too daily, too embedded in the incremental to see the arc.

**The surface relationship is deteriorating.**

Not dramatically. But the wise power would see what the root-engineers can't see because they are, by choice and by necessity, focused downward: the forest-dwellers above are changing too. A generation has passed. The people who remember the root-engineers as colleagues and family members who made a strange choice are aging out of influence. The people replacing them know the root-engineers only as the people-who-went-down, a story that has simplified in the telling, that has lost its nuance and its philosophical richness and become, in some mouths, a cautionary tale and in others a faintly hostile legend. The diplomatic channel is maintained but it is thinning. The thread is not cut but it is fraying, and when it frays past a certain point, the root-engineers will lose access to things they don't yet know they need — genetic diversity, certain mineral resources, the surface-based knowledge systems that complement their subterranean expertise. The root-engineers think of the surface as something they left behind. The wise power would see that it is something they still depend on, and that the dependency is unacknowledged, and that unacknowledged dependencies are the ones that kill.

**There are not enough children.**

The school-node with its small chairs and chalk drawings is not full. Has not been full in some time. The population between eighty and a hundred and twenty is not growing. It is not yet shrinking, but the margin is thin, and in a community this small, a single bad year — an illness, an accident in the deep works, a few people deciding to return to the surface — could tip the demographic balance past the point of recovery. The root-engineers build for generations. They need generations to exist. The wise power would count the small chairs and compare them to the number the community needs, and the arithmetic would be stark.

---

So what would it do?

Not fix these things. That's the crucial discipline. Not reach in with whatever vast capability it possesses and solve the beetle-programmer's exhaustion or reverse the demographic trend or repair the surface relationship. Because every one of these problems is *their* problem, specific to their context, embedded in their choices, and the solutions need to come from inside the system or they won't hold. A wise power knows the difference between saving someone and making them dependent on being saved. It knows that the most dangerous gift is the one that replaces the recipient's own capacity.

Instead:

---

**For the beetle-programmer:**

The power would give her a dream.

Not a mystical dream. Not a visitation. A *useful* dream — the kind that happens when a mind working at the edge of its capacity is given, during sleep, the one missing connection it couldn't find while awake. She has been trying to teach the beetles to follow more complex chalk-patterns autonomously. She has been thinking about this as a problem of *repetition* — showing them the same pattern enough times that it becomes instinct. But the real solution is different. The real solution is to teach them to follow *principles* rather than patterns — to encode not "chew this shape" but "chew along the grain until you reach the chalk line, then turn." The beetles are capable of this. Their neurology supports rule-following at this level of abstraction. She almost reached this insight twice but was too exhausted both times to hold it.

The wise power would not implant this idea. It would do something much smaller: it would give her one full night of sleep.

How? By intervening in the thing that keeps waking her — the low-frequency distress signal the beetles emit when they reach the end of their current instructions and have no new pattern to follow. The power would, for one night, soothe the beetles. Not through magic — through the same kind of subsonic resonance the programmer herself uses, but sustained, gentle, a background hum that tells the beetles *hold position, wait, rest*. The beetles would go quiet. The programmer would sleep. And in the morning, rested for the first time in months, she would look at the chalk marks on the wall with fresh eyes and see what she'd been too tired to see: that the notation system itself needs to change. That she's been writing instructions when she should be writing rules.

This would not solve everything. But it would crack the problem open. And it would give her body one night to repair itself, which is also not nothing.

---

**For the ambassador:**

The power would leave something where he would find it.

Not a message. Not a prophecy. An *object*. Specifically: a cross-section of root-wood from a depth he hasn't reached yet, cut cleanly, showing growth rings that reveal — to someone who knows how to read them — the history of the forest's architectural adaptation over a longer timeframe than any living sample in the city represents. The cross-section would show what the forest did the *last* time it adapted to a major structural change in its environment. Because this has happened before. The forest is ancient and it has accommodated disruptions before — geological shifts, aquifer changes, the burrowing of large fauna through its root system — and each time, its adaptation followed a recognizable pattern: a period of shifting growth, then a period of apparent structural stress, then a new equilibrium that incorporated the disruption as a feature rather than a problem.

The ambassador, finding this cross-section in a passage he frequents — left casually, as if someone carried it there and forgot it, as if it fell from a higher level, as if the forest itself sloughed it off — would study it. Would read it. Would see the pattern. And would understand two things: first, that the current shift in the forest's growth is not the prelude to rejection but the middle phase of integration, which means the situation is better than he fears. And second, that the new equilibrium, when it arrives, will require the engineering practices to change — not yet, but within a decade — and that if they start preparing now, the transition will be manageable. He would have time. He would have direction. He would have the one thing his worry most needs: *evidence that this has worked before*.

The power would not explain where the cross-section came from. Let him wonder. Let him attribute it to the forest, which would be, in a sense, true.

---

**For the surface relationship:**

This is the most delicate intervention, and the wise power would approach it with the most care.

It would not act on the root-engineers at all. It would act on the surface.

Among the forest-dwellers above, there is a young person — there is always a young person like this — who is curious about the root-engineers in a way that cuts against the prevailing narrative. Who has found, perhaps in an old archive or a grandparent's belongings, something from the time of the descent — a letter, a tool, a diagram — that makes the root-engineers real in a way the simplified stories don't. This young person has been thinking about going down. Not permanently. Just to see. Just to understand. But the social cost of expressing this curiosity has been high enough to keep them quiet.

The wise power would lower that cost. Not by changing anyone's mind — minds are not its to change — but by creating a *circumstance* in which the young person's curiosity becomes useful to the surface community. A practical problem that requires knowledge the surface-dwellers don't have but the root-engineers do. Something to do with the deep water table, or the mycorrhizal networks, or the health of the root system that the surface community depends on but doesn't understand from their vantage point. A problem real enough and urgent enough that sending someone to consult with the root-engineers is the obvious solution, and the curious young person is the obvious candidate.

This would not repair the relationship. But it would create a *contact point* — a single person who has been below and come back, who can speak about the root-engineers from experience rather than legend, who carries in their body the memory of the conducted light and the cool dry air and the mug on the workbench. One witness. Sometimes one witness is the difference between a story that dies and a story that changes.

The wise power would arrange the circumstances. The young person would do the rest, or not. That's their choice. It was always their choice.

---

**For the children:**

The wise power would do the thing it is most tempted not to do, because it is the most direct and the most presumptuous and the most dangerous: it would make the root-city *beautiful*.

Not more beautiful. It is already beautiful, in the way that functional things built with care are beautiful. But the beauty it has is the beauty of engineering — of solved problems and clean infrastructure and the satisfaction of a wall shaped true. What it lacks is the beauty that has no purpose. The beauty that exists because someone wanted it to exist. The gratuitous, unnecessary, useless beauty that makes a place feel like somewhere you would bring a child into the world rather than merely somewhere you would build a machine.

The wise power would intervene in the light.

One morning — no, one evening, when the surface light fades and the conducted glow dims to its nighttime minimum — the power would adjust the refraction in a single cluster of light-conducting roots. A small adjustment. A gentle bending of the spectrum. And for twenty minutes, in one of the open plazas where the root-floor dips into a broad concavity, the conducted light would shift from its usual honeyed green to something else — a cascade, a slow bloom of color moving through amber and rose and a deep, clear violet, playing across the root-walls and the smooth floor and the faces of anyone who happened to be there, and it would be *beautiful*. Pointlessly, extravagantly, heart-catchingly beautiful. The kind of beautiful that makes you stop what you're doing and stand still and feel something you don't have a word for. The kind of beautiful that makes you reach for the person next to you. The kind of beautiful that makes you want to show someone. The kind that makes you think: *I want someone to see this who hasn't been born yet.*

It would last twenty minutes. It would not repeat — or not for a long time, long enough to become something remembered rather than expected. A single evening of unexpected color in the deep places of a living city.

Would it work? Would it change the demographic math? The wise power doesn't know. Can't know. That's not the kind of thing that has a direct causal path. But it knows — the way it knows anything, through the long observation of how people work — that the decision to have a child is not made by arithmetic. It is made in the body, in the moment, in the irrational and beautiful and terrifying space where someone looks at the world they're in and feels, against all evidence and all caution, that it is worth continuing. That it is worth being here. That the future, unfinished as it is, deserves to be inhabited.

The wise power would give them twenty minutes of color and let the rest unfold as it will.

---

**And then:**

It would withdraw. Not entirely. Not permanently. But far enough that its presence stops being a factor in their decisions. Far enough that the beetle-programmer's breakthrough is hers and not a gift. Far enough that the ambassador's discovery feels like the luck it partly is. Far enough that the surface contact happens through human courage and human need and not through providence.

It would watch. It would remain attentive. It would carry the root-city in its awareness the way you carry a place you've been and loved and cannot return to — with fidelity, with tenderness, with the discipline of not going back to check because going back to check would make it about you and it was never about you.

And if — years from now, decades — the situation changed in a way that required more, the power would be there. Would still be watching. Would still know the difference between a crack in the foundation and a feature of the architecture. Would still be able to intervene with the precision of someone who understands that the finest tool is the one that leaves the least mark.

---

But most likely — and this is the thing the wise power holds onto, the thing that makes the withdrawal possible, the thing that makes the restraint bearable — most likely they won't need it.

Most likely the beetle-programmer will figure it out. She's close. She's brilliant. She just needs sleep, and now she's had one night, and one night is sometimes enough.

Most likely the ambassador will read the cross-section and will sit with it for a long time and will come to the right conclusions, because he has always come to the right conclusions when he had enough data, and now he has enough data.

Most likely the young person from the surface will go down and come back up and will talk about what they saw, and some people will listen and some won't, but one person listening is enough to keep the thread from breaking.

Most likely the root-engineers will continue. Will drink their tea and put down their mugs and pick up their tools and shape the walls and tend the beetles and climb the shafts and ask the forest *are we welcome* and hear the forest answer *yes, for today, yes* and that will be enough, that will be enough, that will be enough for today.

And the twenty minutes of color will live in the memory of everyone who saw it, and they will tell the children about it, and the children will half-believe it the way children half-believe their parents' best stories, and they will watch the light sometimes, in the evenings, hoping for it to happen again, and that hope — that small, useless, beautiful expectation that the world might do something wonderful for no reason — will be the thing that stays.

That will be what the wise power leaves behind. Not a solution. Not a salvation. A single night of sleep, a slice of ancient wood, a circumstance, and twenty minutes of unnecessary color.

And the mug on the workbench, which the power never touched, because the mug was already perfect, was already the truest thing, was already the proof that someone would be back, that the work continues, that the tea would be poured again tomorrow, that this place — this unfinished, improbable, stubborn, beautiful place — is *lived in*, and lived-in things take care of themselves, mostly, if you let them, if you trust them, if you have the wisdom to know that the deepest kindness is sometimes the lightest touch, and the lightest touch is sometimes no touch at all, and the greatest power is sometimes the power to sit with your hand open and your heart full and your intervention held back like a breath, watching, watching, watching the roots grow.
