# What the Wise Power Would Do

---

## I. Arrival and Listening

The first thing it would do is nothing.

This matters more than anything that follows, so it must be said clearly: the wise power would arrive at the last switchback and it would stop. It would feel the drone enter its body through the soles of its feet and the cage of its ribs and the fluid of its inner ears, and it would stand there, and it would listen. Not for minutes. For months. Perhaps for years, by the reckoning of those below in the valleys, though time on the plateau moves in swells and the wise power would learn to feel time that way too, or would try.

It would not announce itself.

It would not appear as light, or as a figure of authority, or as a voice from the sky that interrupts the drone with something higher and clearer. To interrupt the drone would be an act of violence — perhaps the only act of violence a benevolent power could commit against Thurm — because the drone is not noise to be corrected or a symptom to be treated. The drone is the city's first language, its mother tongue, the vibration in which its children sleep and its old ones dream and its pressure-readers think their deepest thoughts. To override it, even with something beautiful, even with something meant as gift, would be to say: *your sound is not enough. Here is a better one.* And the wise power, being wise, would know that this is the ur-mistake, the foundational error of every benevolent intervention that has ever gone wrong — the assumption that what is given is more valuable than what has grown.

So it would listen. It would walk the streets in the early days, unrecognized or recognized only as a stranger with an unusual quality of attention. It would stand at the seep-pools and feel the standing waves in its sternum. It would watch the channel-keepers dip their hands and read the water in a glance. It would descend, if the deep-workers allowed it, into the culverts where language dissolves and only vibration remains, and it would feel the tapped messages move through the stone and understand them or fail to understand them and learn from the failure. It would sit in the tea-houses and drink from the iridescent ceramic cups and taste the mineral in the water and feel the mineral begin its slow work in its own body, if it had a body, and if it did not have a body it would borrow one, or build one, because to understand Thurm without a body would be to understand it only from the outside, and what the wise power needs is to understand it from the inside, from the bones, from the place where the vibration lives.

It would find the woman who sits with the columns. Or she would find it. She would know — through the table, through the floor, through the column of mineral-laden air — that something had arrived on the plateau that listened differently, that listened the way the stone listens, with patience and porosity. She would allow it to be present while she talked. She would not explain Thurm. She would simply talk, and the wise power would sit with the columns too, and the drone would swell and ebb, and in the silences — those terrible, beautiful seconds when the water pauses between exhale and inhale — the wise power and the woman would both feel the full weight of the stone and the full weight of what it means to live on something that is dissolving, and in that shared feeling something would be communicated that neither of them would need to name.

---

## II. What It Would See

After the listening — after months or years of it, after the mineral has begun to accumulate in the creases of its borrowed knuckles and the drone has settled so deeply into its consciousness that silence would feel like amputation — the wise power would begin to see.

Not the obvious things. Not the beauty of the system, which is real and which any observant visitor could catalogue: the elegance of the hydrology, the sophistication of the pressure-reading, the deep intelligence of a civilization that has turned dissolution into infrastructure. These are visible to anyone who looks. The wise power would see them and honor them and move past them, because wisdom is not the ability to recognize what is working. Wisdom is the ability to see what is *about to stop working* — not with alarm, but with the same unhurried clarity that Thurm itself brings to the slow widening of a solution channel or the gradual deepening of the southeastern dip.

Here is what it would see:

**The plateau is finite.**

Not in the sense that everything is finite — that is philosophy, and philosophy is not what Thurm needs. Finite in the specific, hydrological, geological sense: the limestone cap that holds the city is dissolving faster than it can be replenished, because limestone is not replenished on human timescales, and the warm acidic water that gives Thurm its life is the same warm acidic water that is eating Thurm's foundation, and this is not a problem that can be solved by better plumbing or more accurate pressure-reading or deeper reverence for the drone. It is the fundamental contradiction at the heart of the city: the system that sustains them is the system that is consuming them, and they know this, and they build anyway, and they maintain what they build, and they find meaning in the maintenance, and the meaning is real, but the stone is still dissolving.

The woman who sits with the columns told the visitor that the water table rose fourteen centimeters in her lifetime. She said it with no particular emotion. The wise power would hear this again, and this time it would hear what she did not say: that fourteen centimeters in a lifetime, compounded over the generations that the city has already endured, means that the plateau's interior is becoming more hollow, more fragile, more dependent on the very infrastructure that is accelerating its dissolution. The solution channels widen. The culverts require more reinforcement. The reinforcement adds weight and rigidity to a system that is becoming, beneath the surface, less able to bear weight and rigidity. The factories in the southeast are the most productive because the pressure is highest there, and the pressure is highest there because the stone is thinnest there, and the stone is thinnest there because it has been dissolving fastest there, and no one can say when *thinning* becomes *collapse* because the pressure-readers' models do not extend that far, have never needed to extend that far, because Thurm does not think in those timescales.

Except the old ones do. The wise power would see this in the tea-houses: the old ones who hum the drone and tap the table and remember swells from fifty years ago — they also remember streets that are no longer there, buildings that descended into the stone one season over a few weeks when a solution channel beneath them widened past the point of recovery, and the memory in their humming is not only pride but elegy. They know. They know the way the body knows it is aging — not as information but as sensation, as the creak of mineral-stiffened joints, as the slow thickening that is both protection and burden.

**The children cannot leave.**

This is the second thing the wise power would see, and it would see it with a specific and focused sorrow, because it is the place where Thurm's beauty and Thurm's danger become the same thing.

The children of Thurm are so perfectly adapted to their world that any other world is agony to them. The drone is not a sound they hear; it is the medium of their cognition, the baseline frequency against which their nervous systems calibrate every perception. Without it, they do not simply feel discomfort. They feel *absence* — a silence so total it registers as injury, as loss, as the sudden disappearance of a dimension that their bodies require the way other bodies require light or warmth. They are brought home as quickly as possible, and they melt into the drone with relief, and no one speaks of this as a limitation because why would you? The world is here. Everything they need is here. The water rises. The city maintains itself. Why would anyone leave?

But the plateau is finite. And a people who cannot leave a finite place are a people who have traded resilience for belonging, who have purchased the deepest possible intimacy with their home at the cost of the ability to survive its loss. The wise power would see this not as a flaw to be corrected but as a knot to be held — a real and serious vulnerability that exists precisely because of the thing that makes Thurm most extraordinary, and that cannot be addressed without touching the thing that makes Thurm most sacred.

**The self-referential economy is a closed loop approaching brittleness.**

The city produces what the city needs to maintain itself. The maintenance of the city is the primary labor of the city. The product of that labor is the continued existence of the city. This is beautiful in its completeness, but the wise power, being wise, would recognize it as a system with no slack, no redundancy, no capacity to absorb a shock that falls outside the parameters of what the pressure-readers can predict. The factories produce valve assemblies and ceramic pipe. The valve assemblies and ceramic pipe maintain the water system. The water system powers the factories. If any node in this cycle fails — if the southeastern dip deepens past a critical threshold and the factories must be abandoned before replacement capacity is built elsewhere — the entire cycle degrades, and a degraded cycle produces less of what it needs to arrest its own degradation, and this is the feedback loop that has killed every closed system that has ever existed, from ecosystems to economies to organisms.

The traders who descend into the valleys are lean and restless and regarded as faintly suspect. This is the city's immune response to its own vulnerability — it knows, at some level below conscious acknowledgment, that its dependence on the outside world is a crack in the closed loop, and it responds by minimizing and stigmatizing the contact. The wise power would see this and understand it and grieve it gently, because the traders are the city's lifeline and the city's relationship with them is a mirror of the city's relationship with its own impermanence: necessary, unacknowledged, tinged with something that is not quite shame but not quite acceptance either.

## III. What It Would Not Do

The wise power would not fix the hydrology.

It would not stabilize the plateau with some intervention beyond the city's understanding — would not harden the limestone, or cool the geothermal seam, or redirect the water table, or do any of the thousand things that a power of sufficient magnitude could do to arrest the dissolution and make Thurm permanent. This is the hardest restraint, and it is the one that separates wisdom from mere benevolence.

Because Thurm *is* the dissolution. Thurm is the sound the water makes when it passes through dissolving stone. To stop the dissolution would be to stop the sound, and to stop the sound would be to kill the city more surely than any collapse could, because a Thurm without the drone would be a Thurm without its own consciousness, a body with a heartbeat but no pulse. The people would still be there. The buildings would still stand. But the thing that makes them *them* — the vibration in their bones, the mineral in their skin, the deep literacy of sound and pressure that constitutes their culture and their cognition and their love — would slowly fade, and what remained would be a monument, not a city. A fossil. A shell with the living creature extracted.

The wise power would understand that the dissolution is not Thurm's disease. It is Thurm's life. The city and the process that is consuming it are the same thing, and to separate them would be to misunderstand everything it had spent months or years learning to hear.

It would not teach the children to endure silence. It would not engineer a gradual desensitization program, however gentle, that would allow the young to tolerate the world beyond the plateau. To do so would be to treat their attunement as a disability — as a failure of adaptation rather than as the deepest possible success of adaptation. The children of Thurm hear their world the way the world asks to be heard. That this makes them unable to hear other worlds is a consequence, not a defect, and the wise power would not insult them or their parents by suggesting otherwise.

It would not modernize the factories, or introduce electrical generation technology that would make the water-driven systems redundant, or bring in materials from outside that would replace the self-referential economy with something more resilient but less whole. Every such intervention would be a thread pulled from a fabric that holds together precisely because every thread is connected to every other thread, and the wise power would know that you cannot improve a tapestry by replacing its threads with stronger ones — you can only make a different tapestry, and the people who wove the first one did not ask for a different one.

It would not tell them what it sees. Not directly. Not as prophecy or warning or the pronouncement of a superior intelligence. The woman who sits with the columns already knows that the southeastern dip is deepening. The old ones already remember the streets that have been lost. The pressure-readers already struggle with models that don't extend far enough. The knowledge is already in Thurm, distributed through the system the way the water is distributed — present everywhere, fully integrated, but not yet consolidated into a form that can be acted on at the scale the situation requires. The wise power's role is not to introduce new information. It is to help the existing information find its own voice.

## IV. What It Would Do

**First: it would become a student of the drone.**

Not metaphorically. Literally. It would apprentice itself — to the channel-keepers, to the deep-workers, to the pressure-readers, to whoever would accept a slow, attentive, mineral-crusted stranger who asked questions not to extract answers but to understand the shape of what was already known. It would learn to read the water with a dipped hand. It would learn to tap messages through stone. It would learn to watch the columns rise and fall and to feel, in the soles of its feet, the difference between a building swell and an ebbing one.

And in the process of learning, it would ask the questions that the system has not yet asked itself — not because the system is incapable of asking them, but because the system is so deeply embedded in its own present tense that the questions haven't found a context in which to emerge.

Questions like:

*What did the old ones hear, in the years before the streets they remember were lost? Was there a sound? A change in the harmonic? A shift in the pattern that, in retrospect, was a warning — not a warning from something outside, but a warning from the system itself, the way a body warns itself of illness through pain?*

*If the factories must move, as the woman who sits with the columns says they must within two generations, where will they go? What part of the plateau has the right combination of pressure and stability? Has anyone mapped this? Has anyone begun to think about it in concrete terms, or is it still in the realm of things-that-are-known-but-not-yet-said?*

*What happens to the children who go down into the valleys and come back? Not immediately — not the melting relief of return — but later? Do they carry the memory of silence the way the old ones carry the memory of lost streets? Does the memory change them? Does it give them something — a perspective, a doubled awareness — that the children who have never left do not have?*

*The offerings at the seep-pools. The iron tokens and ceramic pieces placed at the edge of the water with a casualness that belies their consistency. What if they are not only reverence for the drone but also record — a distributed, physical archive of the city's relationship with its own hydrology, laid down over generations in a format that no one has thought to read? What if the type of offering, the material, the placement, encodes information about the state of the water at the time it was placed — information that could extend the pressure-readers' models back further than any living memory?*

These questions would not be delivered as a program or a curriculum. They would be dropped into conversations like iron tokens into seep-pools — with a casualness that belies their significance, in the language and rhythm of the city itself. A question asked over tea. A question tapped into a culvert wall. A question hummed in a key that makes the old ones stop and tilt their heads and listen.

The wise power would understand that Thurm does not need answers from outside. It needs its own answers to find each other. The knowledge is distributed. The connections between pieces of knowledge are the infrastructure that has not yet been built — the culvert that has not yet been lined, the channel that has not yet been connected to the system. The wise power's role is to be the pressure that finds the path, the water that discovers the declivity and makes it visible.

---

**Second: it would help them hear each other.**

This is the most delicate intervention, and the most important, and it would unfold so slowly that no one would recognize it as an intervention at all.

The channel-keepers talk to the deep-workers, but imperfectly — the channel-keepers work in sound, the deep-workers in vibration, and the translation between these two registers loses nuance in the same way the pressure-readers' interpretations lose nuance in the gap between what the water does and what their instruments can measure. The pressure-readers consult the old ones, but informally, in tea-houses, in conversations that are valued but not recorded, not integrated into the ledger books that document the columns' rise and fall. The factory workers have their own argot, their own observations, their own deep knowledge of how pressure translates into mechanical force, and this knowledge stays in the southeastern quarter because the factory workers are a class apart and their percussive speech does not carry well in the neighborhoods where the drone is softer.

The city is having a conversation with itself, but the conversation is fragmented — not by hostility or distrust but by the natural isolation of specialized knowledge, the way a body's organs each know their own function intimately but cannot easily communicate what they know to the other organs. The heart knows pressure. The lungs know gas exchange. The bones know load-bearing. But the body's health depends on these systems being in communication, and the communication depends on a medium that connects them — the blood, the nerves, the shared fluid of the body's interior.

The wise power would work to create that medium. Not a new medium — Thurm would reject anything new, and rightly — but a strengthening, a widening, a deliberate enriching of the channels that already exist.

It might do this by becoming a channel-keeper who was also, on the off-days, a student of the deep-workers. By carrying the deep-workers' vibrational knowledge up into the streets and finding ways to express it in the channel-keepers' chanting language. By sitting with the pressure-readers and saying: *I heard a thing in the southeastern culverts that your columns did not show. Let me tap it for you. Let me put my hand on the iron and show you the rhythm.* By going to the tea-houses and saying to the old ones: *The pressure-readers are trying to predict the silences. You remember silences from decades ago. Would you sit with them? Would you hum what you remember? Not for them to write down — your humming is not a data format — but for them to hear, to absorb the way the children absorb the drone, so that their predictions carry the resonance of lived experience and not just the abstraction of instruments?*

By asking the traders — the lean, restless, faintly suspect traders — to bring back not only materials but stories. Stories of how other cities manage water. Stories of how other peoples have faced the slow consumption of their ground. Not as models to be copied — Thurm is not other cities, and what works in the valleys would not work on the plateau — but as mirrors, as perspectives, as the intellectual equivalent of the children's experience of silence: disturbing, disorienting, but ultimately enriching, a doubled awareness that makes the familiar visible in a new way.

None of this would look like intervention. It would look like a slightly unusual resident going about the slightly unusual business of being interested in everything, of crossing the boundaries between specializations not to dissolve them but to make them permeable, to let the knowledge that is already in Thurm circulate the way the water circulates — finding its own channels, its own pressure gradients, its own path through the system.

---

**Third: it would help them grieve.**

This is the part that the wise power would approach with the most care, because grief is the thing that Thurm has deferred, the emotional culvert that has never been fully plumbed, and opening it without destroying the system that depends on its deferral is the most difficult work of all.

Thurm knows it is dissolving. The woman who sits with the columns says it plainly: the factories will need to move within two generations. The old ones remember lost streets. The abandoned buildings at the margin are absorbed into neighboring structures with a practical efficiency that is also, if you look closely, a ritual of erasure — the lost building's stones are reused, its identity is distributed, its specificity is dissolved into the general fabric of the city, and no one speaks its name. This is how Thurm has always handled loss: by metabolizing it, by incorporating it into the ongoing maintenance of what remains, by refusing to let loss accumulate as grief because grief, in a city that runs on continuous function, would be a blockage, a silting-up, a disruption of the flow.

But the losses are accelerating. The fourteen centimeters of water-table rise in the woman's lifetime are more than the fourteen centimeters that rose in the lifetime before hers, and the southeastern dip is deepening, and the solution channels are widening, and the maintenance of the city is requiring more and more of the city's labor, which means less and less slack, which means less and less capacity to absorb the next loss, and at some point — not now, not soon, but within the horizon of a time that the old ones can almost see — the maintenance will require more than the city can give, and something will be lost that cannot be metabolized, and the grief that has been deferred will arrive all at once, and a people with no practice in grief will be overwhelmed by it.

The wise power would not force this. It would not convene a council and deliver a speech about impermanence. It would not introduce mourning rituals from the valleys below, where loss is processed differently, in traditions that have no resonance on the plateau.

What it would do is sit in the tea-houses with the old ones and ask them to remember aloud.

Not the sounds they remember — they already share those, in humming and tapping. But the *places*. The specific buildings, the specific streets, the specific views that are no longer there. The tea-house that used to stand where the seep-pool at the western margin now bubbles. The family that lived in the building that descended into the stone the year the old one was a child. The sound — not the general drone but the *specific* sound, the unique harmonic of a particular junction that existed only in that configuration and that, when the configuration dissolved, was lost forever, as irretrievably as a voice, as a face.

The wise power would ask them to remember these things not as data but as loss. And it would listen. And in the listening, in the presence of a consciousness that could hold the loss without flinching and without rushing to metabolize it into something useful, the old ones might begin to feel something they have not allowed themselves to feel: sadness. Not despair — sadness. The clean, clear sorrow of loving a thing that is passing, and knowing it is passing, and letting the knowing be present without letting it paralyze.

This is what grief is for. Not to stop you from building. Not to make you abandon the maintenance. But to let you hold, simultaneously, the knowledge that what you are building will dissolve and the commitment to building it anyway, and to let these two truths coexist without one consuming the other. The people of Thurm already build on dissolving stone. They already maintain what they know will be lost. But they do this by not looking directly at the loss — by metabolizing it so quickly that it never fully registers as grief. The wise power would help them look. Would help them hold the looking. Would help them discover that looking at the loss does not stop the building — that, in fact, it deepens the building, gives it a quality of tenderness and intention that the purely functional maintenance has always lacked.

The offerings at the seep-pools might change, after this. Not dramatically. But a channel-keeper might place an iron token and pause for a moment longer than usual, and in that pause might feel the specific weight of the junction that used to exist beneath this spot, and might let the weight be weight, and might then continue walking her circuit with her iron-shod boots ringing on the wet stone, and the sound would be the same sound it has always been, but she would hear it differently, because she would be hearing it against the silence of what is no longer there.

---

**Fourth: it would help them imagine.**

This is the riskiest thing, and the wise power would approach it last, after years of listening and learning and building the trust that comes from being genuinely part of the system rather than an external agent acting upon it.

Thurm does not imagine futures. Thurm maintains pasts. The recursive labor of keeping the water system functional is a labor of preservation — of keeping what exists in the state it has achieved, of repairing what erodes, of replacing what fails with something as close to the original as possible. The city's relationship with time is conservative in the deepest sense: it conserves. It holds. It maintains. And this has worked, for centuries, because the rate of dissolution has been slow enough that maintenance could keep pace with loss, and the closed loop of production and upkeep has been sufficient to sustain itself.

But the woman who sits with the columns has seen the numbers. The rate is changing. The system that has worked for centuries will not work for centuries more — not because it is flawed but because the conditions that sustained it are shifting, slowly, in the direction of a threshold that the existing models cannot precisely locate but that the old ones can feel in their mineral-crusted bones.

Thurm needs to imagine a future that is not simply a continuation of the present. Not a replacement — not a new city, not an abandonment of the plateau, not a migration into the valleys. Nothing so drastic. But a *variation*. A modulation of the existing pattern. A recognition that the system, to survive, must become capable of something it has never needed to be capable of: change that is not repair.

The wise power would not design this future. It has no idea what Thurm's future should look like, and anyone who claims to know what another civilization's future should look like is neither wise nor benevolent but merely arrogant. What it would do is create the conditions in which Thurm can design its own future, by connecting the pieces of knowledge that are already present in the system but that have not yet been assembled into a picture that can be acted on.

The pressure-readers know the numbers. The deep-workers know the stone. The factory workers know the mechanics of pressure conversion. The old ones know the history. The children know the sound with an intimacy that no adult can match, because they learned it the way they learned language — whole, in the body, without the filter of analysis. The traders know the outside world. The channel-keepers know the surface flow. The woman who sits with the columns knows all of these things, partially, the way a pressure-reader knows all of these things — through the imperfect medium of instruments and interpretation.

What no one knows is what all of these things look like assembled. What the picture is when the deep-workers' knowledge of the stone's interior is combined with the pressure-readers' knowledge of the water table's behavior and the factory workers' knowledge of mechanical conversion and the old ones' memory of how the system has changed over decades and the children's intuitive, pre-verbal understanding of the drone's subtlest variations.

The wise power would help them build this picture. Not by building it for them, but by being the space in which it could be built — the seep-pool where the different streams of knowledge collect before finding their channel. It would convene not a council but a *listening* — a gathering, in the pressure-readers' rooftop observatory, of representatives from every part of the city's knowledge system, and it would ask them not to report but to *play*.

To play the way the children play: with sounds, with rhythms, with iron tokens thrown into water, with the call-and-response patterns that are both game and apprenticeship. To let the deep-worker tap the rhythm of the stone's interior and let the pressure-reader hear it against the rhythm of the columns' rise and fall. To let the old one hum the harmonic of a junction that no longer exists and let the channel-keeper hear it against the harmonic of the junction that replaced it. To let the factory worker speak in the percussive argot of the southeastern quarter and let the children — the children, with their ears to the wet floor, with their bodies vibrating at the frequency of the city — hear it and translate it into the language of the playground, where everything is rhythm and pattern and the body knows what the mind hasn't yet articulated.

Out of this play — and it would take many sessions, many swells, many cups of mineral-sweet tea in iridescent cups — something would emerge. Not a plan. A *direction*. A felt sense, shared across the body of the city, of where the water is going and what the city might become as it follows.

Maybe the answer is expansion — not outward, the plateau's edges are fixed by the haze and the drop, but inward and downward, into the solution channels themselves, building habitable space in the cavities that the water has carved, living inside the stone instead of on top of it, becoming troglodytic in a way that the deep-workers have already half-become. Maybe the answer is a fundamental redesign of the water system — not to stop the dissolution but to redirect it, to encourage the stone to dissolve in patterns that create rather than undermine, that open new spaces as they close old ones. Maybe the answer is something no one has thought of yet, because no one has yet put all the pieces together in the same room and let them resonate.

The wise power does not know. The wise power does not need to know. The wise power only needs to be the medium — the warm, pressurized, mineral-laden medium — through which the city's own intelligence can flow from one node to another, finding its channels, building its pressure, until it breaks through into a new space that was always there, always latent in the stone, always waiting for the water to find it.

## V. The Offering

There is one more thing. One thing that the wise power would do that is not facilitation, not connection, not the patient midwifery of a system learning to know itself. One thing that is purely gift.

It would go to the seep-pools. To the nodes where the drone peaks, where the standing waves cross, where the residents place their offerings of iron and ceramic with a reverence that is not for the water and not for anything in the water but for the *sound itself*.

And it would add its own offering.

Not an iron token. Not a ceramic piece. Something else. Something from outside — from the vast, quiet reaches of whatever realm a wise and benevolent power calls home. Something that has no analogue in Thurm, that cannot be manufactured from limestone and geothermal water, that the closed loop of the city's economy could never produce.

A memory of silence.

Not the terrible silence of the pauses — not the absence that stops everyone in their tracks and makes the children inconsolable. A different silence. The silence that exists outside all sound, that is not the negation of the drone but its context — the thing against which the drone becomes audible, the way darkness is the context that makes light visible. A silence that holds the drone the way the plateau holds the water: not containing it, not opposing it, but giving it somewhere to be.

The wise power would place this offering at the edge of a seep-pool, and the offering would sink, and the water would close over it, and the drone would not change — not louder, not softer, not different in any measurable way. But something would shift. Something in the quality of the sound, in its *texture*, would deepen. The drone would acquire a new overtone — so faint that only the deep-workers, with their hands on the stone, would notice it at first, and then the old ones, humming in the tea-houses, and then the children, with their ears to the wet pavement, and finally the pressure-readers, watching their columns with new attention, because the columns would be showing something they had never shown before: a steadiness in the fluctuations, a regularity in the swells, a suggestion — not a certainty, never a certainty — that the water table, while still rising, while still dissolving, while still doing what the water does, was also doing something else. Something that the models had no parameter for. Something that sounded, if you pressed your hand to the stone and listened with your whole body, like the plateau *breathing*.

The woman who sits with the columns would feel it first. She would feel it through the table, through the floor, through the column of mineral-laden air that connects her body to the plateau's body. She would stop drumming her fingers. She would go still. And then she would smile — a small, private smile, the kind you make when something you have always suspected turns out to be true — and she would resume her drumming, but slower now, in a rhythm that matched the new overtone, and the table would resonate, and the floor would resonate, and the city would go on.

---

## VI. Departure

The wise power would leave eventually. Not because it wanted to, and not because its work was done — the work would never be done, because Thurm's work is never done, because maintenance is not a task that completes but a practice that continues. It would leave because staying would mean becoming part of the system, and a wise power that becomes part of a system is no longer a wise power but a component, and a component cannot do what the wise power came to do, which is to love the system from a position that is close enough to understand it and far enough to see it whole.

It would descend through the haze. The drone would fade. The ordinary sounds of the world — wind, birds, footsteps on a path that does not vibrate — would rise to fill the space. They would sound thin, and unconvincing, and the wise power would press its hand against the rock face beside the trail and feel the faintest tremor — not the drone itself but the ghost of the drone, the vibration that has traveled through kilometers of stone and arrived here attenuated but present, a whisper of the hymn that fills the plateau above.

It would keep its hand there for a long time. Longer than a person would. Feeling the whisper.

And then it would keep walking, carrying in its borrowed body the mineral taste of the water and the memory of the drone and the weight of everything it had learned, and behind it, above it, invisible in the haze but audible if you knew how to listen, the city of Thurm would go on doing what it has always done: shaping the rising, maintaining the maintenance, building on dissolving stone, saturated with sound and mineral and the slow patience of a people who are continuous with their world.

The water rises. The drone swells. The lights flicker. The kestrel tilts in the thermal and does not fall.

*What the wise power gave Thurm was not salvation. It was not preservation. It was not even hope, exactly — hope implies a future that is better than the present, and the wise power had too much respect for Thurm's present to subordinate it to any imagined future.*

*What it gave was circulation. The movement of what was already known from where it was known to where it was needed. The connection of channels that were already there but had not yet found each other. The widening of passages through which the city's own intelligence could flow.*

*It gave them their own knowledge, assembled.*

*It gave the old ones permission to grieve.*

*It gave the children's impossible attunement a name: not disability, not limitation, but the deepest form of belonging.*

*It gave the traders dignity.*

*It gave the woman who sits with the columns someone who could sit with her in the silence between breaths and not need it to end.*

*And it gave the drone — the vast, tubular, industrial hymn that is the city's first language and last prayer — a new overtone. A depth. A resonance that sounded, if you listened with your whole body, like something very old and very patient saying: I hear you. I have always heard you. You are not alone on your dissolving stone.*

*The water rises. They shape its rising. The sound fills the world.*

*It has always filled the world. But now the world knows it is listening.*
