# What the Wise Power Would Do

---

The first thing to understand is that the wise power would not arrive. Arrival implies a boundary crossed, an outside becoming inside, and this place—with its seeping walls and its slow negotiations between rock and water and breath—does not admit of dramatic entries. The wise power would instead become *attentive*. The way a person walking through fog gradually realizes they have been listening to a sound for some time before they noticed it. Attention, in this case, not as surveillance but as the quality of regard that a stonemason gives to the grain of the rock before setting the chisel. A regard that asks: *what is already happening here, and what does it want to become?*

And the answer, which the wise power would receive not as words but as the slow pressure of the place itself against its awareness, is: *everything here is mid-sentence.*

---

The cavern is mid-sentence. The machinery is mid-sentence. The drainage channels that carry water halfway to sumps before losing it to cracks in unfinished concrete—mid-sentence. The blind salamanders pressing their pale flanks against geothermal fissures. The rats whose routes already feel older than the pipes they travel. The workers who speak little and leave boot prints that the mineral mud begins erasing before the next shift. The algae whose green has been diluted almost to white. All of it: mid-sentence. Not broken. Not failing. Not yet what it will be, and no longer what it was, and moving—but so slowly that the movement looks like stillness to anything that watches on a human clock.

A foolish power would see this and act. Would finish the sentence. Would seal the cracks, complete the drainage, cap the vents properly, chase the rats from the pipe runs, install brighter lights, clear the midges, accelerate the construction schedule, bring the cavern to its intended state as a functioning geothermal plant or underground city or whatever blueprint the engineers carry folded in their pockets. A foolish power mistakes *incompletion* for *suffering* and *patience* for *helplessness*.

A cruel power—or merely an indifferent one—would see this and withdraw. Would say: *this is what it is. Let it negotiate its own terms. The rock does not need me. The water does not need me. The salamanders have survived longer epochs than this.*

But a wise power. A *benevolent* and wise power.

---

The wise power would do something much harder than either acting or withdrawing. It would *hold the space open*.

Let me explain what I mean.

There is a moment in any unfinished place—any half-built structure, any life mid-transition, any sentence not yet completed—when the thing is most vulnerable not to catastrophe but to *premature closure*. The danger is not that the cavern will collapse. The limestone is stable; the engineers are competent; the pumps run day and night. The danger is that someone, somewhere, will make a decision that forecloses what the place is still discovering about itself. That the construction schedule will accelerate past the speed at which the ecology can adapt. That the drainage will be completed so efficiently that the salamanders lose their trenches and the algae lose their ledges and the midges lose their sumps, and the whole delicate, unplanned community of life that has seeped into the gaps will be rationalized out of existence. Not out of malice—out of *tidiness*. Out of the same impulse that makes a person smooth wet concrete before it's ready, leaving a surface that looks finished but hasn't had time to cure.

Or the opposite danger: that the project will stall. That funding will dry up, or the engineers will be reassigned, or some distant authority will decide the cavern is not worth completing. And then the machinery will rust on its temporary cradles, and the sodium lamps will burn out one by one, and the water will reclaim the channels and the pumps will stop and the lowest levels will flood, and what was becoming a place where humans and geology and life negotiated together will become just another flooded cave, undifferentiated, returned to a silence that is not peace but abandonment.

The wise power sees both dangers—premature completion and premature abandonment—and positions itself against both. Not by intervening in the construction. Not by speaking to the engineers or altering the budget or adjusting the timetable. But by doing something that has no name in any project management framework, something that operates below the threshold of anyone's notice:

*It would sustain the conditions under which the negotiation can continue.*

---

Here is what that looks like, concretely, though "concretely" is almost the wrong word for something so close to the texture of the place itself:

**The water.** The wise power would attend to the water. Not redirect it, not accelerate it, not slow it—attend to it. The hairline fractures in the ceiling through which the seepage comes are the cavern's circulatory system, and they are under constant pressure from both sides: from the aquifer above and from the construction below. Every new beam anchored into the rock sends micro-fractures outward that change the pattern of flow. Every new drainage channel completed removes a trickle from one surface and sends it underground to another. The wise power would hold in its attention the entire hydrological network of the cavern—every crack, every bead, every trickle—and would ensure that no single change cascades into a failure of the whole. Not by preventing change. By ensuring that each change has *time to propagate* before the next one arrives. The water needs time to find its new paths. The wise power would give it that time. How? Perhaps by nothing more than a subtle influence on the rate at which the rock yields to pressure—a fractional increase in the limestone's willingness to absorb shock, a barely measurable slowing of the micro-fracture propagation around new anchor points. The engineers would not notice. The water would.

**The heat.** The geothermal vents are the cavern's metabolism, and they are only partially capped because the engineers have not yet decided how much heat to harvest and how much to vent. This is the right indecision. The wise power would protect it. Because the partially capped vents are what make the entire ecology possible: the algae need the warmth, the midges need the warm standing water, the salamanders need the thermal gradient along the trenches, the rats need the insects that the warmth supports, and the workers need the temperature to remain at the edge of tolerance rather than crossing it. A fully capped vent system would be more efficient and would also sterilize the space. An uncapped system would make it uninhabitable. The current state—partial, negotiated, uncomfortable for everyone but fatal for no one—is the state in which the most possibilities remain open. The wise power would not freeze this state permanently; it would simply ensure that the decision to change it, when it comes, comes from *understanding* rather than from impatience or ideology. Perhaps this means: on the night before the engineering review where the vent-capping schedule will be finalized, the lead engineer steps outside—or rather, steps into one of the deeper aisles where the sodium light barely reaches, to check a pressure reading that has been fluctuating. And in that aisle she finds one of the blind salamanders resting on the warm concrete beside the vent she is scheduled to cap. And she pauses. Not because the salamander matters to the project plan. But because something in its stillness—its absolute, unhurried presence in a place that was not built for it but that it has claimed anyway—makes her pause long enough to think: *what are we actually building here?* And the answer that surfaces is not the one in the blueprint. It is something more complicated and more honest: *we are building a place that other things are also building, and we have not yet decided whether to let them.*

The wise power did not put the salamander there. The salamander was already there. The wise power simply ensured that the engineer's path and the salamander's rest coincided. A nudge so small it is indistinguishable from chance.

**The pace.** This is the deepest intervention, and the one that requires the most wisdom to execute without distortion. The cavern's fundamental character—the thing that makes it what it is rather than what it could become under a dozen different plans—is its *pace*. Everything here moves slowly. The water seeps; it does not pour. The steam drifts; it does not blast. The workers install one beam, one conduit, one sump at a time. The salamanders emerge only when a pump changes pitch. The rats travel in unhurried processions. The algae grow in films so thin they are almost theoretical. Even the sound moves slowly, echoes lingering and blending rather than overlapping. This pace is not inefficiency. It is the pace at which rock and water and life and machinery can actually *negotiate*—can test each other's boundaries, retreat, adjust, advance again. Speed would destroy the negotiation. Speed is what happens when one party decides it already knows the outcome and forces the others to comply.

The wise power would protect the pace. Not by slowing anything down—the pace is already correct. By preventing the introduction of *urgency* from outside. Because urgency is the most common weapon used against unfinished places. Somewhere above the cavern, in an office with windows and fluorescent light, there are people who have never felt the steam on their skin or watched the water find its cracks, and these people have timelines. They have quarterly reports. They have investors or ministers or boards who want to know when the facility will be operational. And the pressure they exert travels downward through the organizational structure like water through limestone—slowly at first, then finding the cracks and widening them. The wise power would not block this pressure. It would not make the people above forget their timelines or lose their urgency. It would do something more subtle: it would ensure that the people *in the cavern*—the workers, the shift supervisors, the on-site engineers—retain their own sense of time. Their own clock, calibrated to the drip and the thrum and the slow cooling of poured concrete. So that when the pressure from above arrives, it meets not resistance but *translation*. The workers hear "accelerate the schedule" and translate it, without conscious thought, into "find the next thing that is ready to be done." And if nothing is ready—if the concrete needs another day to cure, if the drainage channel needs another shift to find its level, if the pump alignment requires one more careful adjustment—they wait. Not defiantly. Patiently. In the same way the water waits for the crack to widen enough to pass through.

---

**The being.** But I have been speaking of the place, and the prompt speaks also of a being who lives in it. And this is where the wise power's attention sharpens, because a being who *lives* in this place—not works in it, not passes through it, but *lives* in it—is a being who has entered into the same negotiation as the water and the rock and the salamanders. A being who has found a seam the cavern has not yet decided to close, and has settled into it.

Who is this being?

I think—following my heart, as instructed—that it is one of the workers. One who, at some point that they could not precisely identify, stopped leaving. Not dramatically. Not as a decision. They simply began staying longer after their shift, then arriving earlier before the next one, then sleeping in the cavern rather than ascending to whatever surface dormitory was provided. Their bunk is in one of the wider aisles, near enough to a geothermal vent that the air stays warm but far enough that the steam doesn't soak their blankets. They have arranged a few personal items on a ledge that was meant to hold a junction box: a thermos, a lamp with a lower wattage than the sodium gantries, a notebook whose pages have begun to curl from the moisture. The other workers know and say nothing. The shift supervisor has noticed and decided not to notice. The being has become part of the cavern's ecology in the same way the rats have—by occupying a space that no one else was using and making it feel, through the simple fact of continued presence, as though it had always been occupied.

This being is not unhappy. But they are *unfinished*, in the same way the cavern is unfinished. Something in their life above—a relationship, a purpose, a sense of who they were—fractured along lines as fine as the ones in the ceiling limestone, and instead of trying to seal the cracks they descended. Not fleeing. Seeping. Following the warmth and the moisture and the low thrum of the pumps to a place where incompletion is not a problem to be solved but simply the state of things. Where everything around them is also mid-sentence, and no one is asking anyone else to finish.

The wise power recognizes this being with the same quality of attention it gives to the water and the rock: not as a problem, not as a project, but as something that is *becoming*, and that needs the conditions of its becoming to be sustained rather than disrupted.

And here is what the wise power would do for this being—and it is so simple that it might not look like anything at all:

**It would ensure that the being is not forced to decide before they are ready.**

Not forced to decide whether to stay or leave. Not forced to decide whether they are a worker or a hermit or a casualty or a visionary. Not forced to decide whether the cavern is their home or their refuge or their exile. Not forced to name what broke or whether it can be mended. Not forced to explain themselves to the shift supervisor or the people above or even to themselves.

Because the being, like the cavern, is in a state where the most important thing happening is the slow negotiation between what they were and what they might become. And that negotiation requires *exactly the conditions the cavern provides*: warmth without comfort, company without intimacy, work without ambition, shelter without permanence. The being has found, without knowing they were looking for it, the one place on earth where their incompletion is not aberrant but *native*. Where they match their surroundings so precisely that they have become invisible in the way the salamanders are invisible—not hidden, just unsurprising. Just part of what is here.

The wise power would protect this invisibility. Would ensure that no well-meaning colleague initiates the conversation that begins with "Are you okay?" and ends with a referral to a counselor on the surface. Would ensure that no policy change requires all workers to badge in and out of the facility, revealing the anomaly of someone who badges in but never badges out. Would ensure that the notebook on the ledge stays dry enough to write in, and that the thermos stays full through mechanisms the being does not question—perhaps a quiet arrangement among the other workers, who have begun to leave a portion of their coffee in a particular spot without discussion, the way the water leaves a portion of its flow in the salamanders' trench.

And—this is the part that requires true wisdom, the part where a merely benevolent power might falter—the wise power would also ensure that the conditions for *leaving* remain available. Because protecting the being's right to stay is not the same as trapping them. The wise power would keep the path to the surface clear. Would ensure that the being's name remains on the worker roster, their pay continues to deposit, their place in the world above does not close behind them. So that on the day—and the wise power can sense it, far off, the way the rock senses a change in the water table—on the day when the being wakes in their bunk and finds that something has shifted, that the fracture has not healed but has *settled*, the way a crack in limestone settles after the pressure equalizes and becomes not a wound but simply a feature of the stone—on that day, the being can rise, and pack their thermos, and walk the unfinished aisles one more time, and ascend. Not because they are finished. No one is ever finished. But because they are ready to be unfinished somewhere else. Ready to carry their incompletion back into the wider world, where it will be harder and louder and less forgiving, but where—and this is the thing the wise power knows, the thing it has been holding in its attention all along—*the negotiation continues*. Always continues. In every place. Between every surface and every crack. Between every being and every space they occupy.

The cavern taught them this. Or rather, the cavern *was* this, and by living in it long enough, the being absorbed the lesson through their skin the way the salamanders absorb warmth through theirs: not as knowledge but as *condition*. As the way things are.

---

**What the wise power would not do.** It would not heal the being. It would not finish the cavern. It would not optimize the heat exchange or complete the drainage or eradicate the rats or brighten the lights or speed the construction or slow it. It would not introduce anything new. It would not remove anything that is already here. It would not speak, or appear, or make its presence known in any way that would cause anyone—the being, the workers, the engineers, the salamanders—to change their behavior in response to being observed.

It would hold the space open. It would keep the pace. It would protect the negotiation.

And then—because even a wise power must practice what it protects—it would return its attention to the periphery. Not withdrawing. Not departing. Simply becoming, once again, indistinguishable from the conditions it has been sustaining. Indistinguishable from the warmth that rises from the partially capped vents. From the pressure that keeps the water moving through the cracks. From the low, continuous thrum that fills the chamber without hurry, the sound that has been there so long that no one remembers when it started, the sound that will continue after the last turbine is sealed and the last sodium lamp is replaced with something brighter and the being has long since ascended and the salamanders have retreated to deeper, darker trenches that the engineers will never reach.

The thrum. The patient, unfinished thrum. That was the wise power all along, or perhaps the wise power simply recognized it as kin. Either way: it continues. It does not rush. It holds the space open.

Everything mid-sentence, still becoming.

The wise power's deepest act: *to let it.*
