I sat with this for a long time.

What follows is not a story about fixing something. You will see why.

---

# What the Wise Power Does

---

## I. Arrival

The power arrives the way light would if light could exist here—not as an intrusion but as a presence that was always possible, a latent capacity of the water itself finally expressed. It does not descend from above. There is no above that matters. It simply begins to attend, the way attention begins: not by entering the room but by *noticing* that you were already in it.

The first thing it does is nothing.

This lasts a very long time, by the standards of beings who measure. By the standards of this place, it is a single breath.

During this nothing, the power listens. It listens the way the sessile colonies listen—not with ears, not with intention, but with its entire structure, letting the sound of the place move through it and reshape it from within. The tower-kelp chords enter it. The interference patterns write themselves across its awareness like weather patterns across a sky it does not need. It feels the drift-eel ribbons threading through the harmonic landscape, each one a sentence in a language that does not mean but simply *moves*. It feels the grazers' chemical grammar rewriting itself on every terrace. It feels the mantle-rays as absences, traveling silences, and it understands them as one understands a rest in music—not as the failure of sound but as its structural necessity.

It feels the deep chambers. The warm ones, where the distinction between organism and environment has become academic. It does not enter. Not yet. Not because it cannot, but because entering would be premature, and this power, whatever else it is, is not premature about anything.

It listens for a long time. A century, perhaps, if centuries were things that happened here. Long enough for several generations of grazers to run their patterns and be replaced. Long enough for a drift-eel ribbon to form, persist, lose a member, dissolve, and reform with new constituents. Long enough to understand that what it is attending to is not a collection of organisms in an environment but a single entity, ancient and whole, and that the question it must answer is not *what does this place need* but something more difficult:

*Does this place need anything at all?*

---

## II. The Diagnosis That Isn't

Here is where a lesser power would make its mistake.

A lesser power—well-meaning, compassionate, armed with the tools of intervention—would look at this place and see problems to solve. It would see the drift-eel that loses its ribbon and spends weeks in erratic searching, its metabolism running hot, its map degraded, and it would feel the pull of mercy: *let me help it find its new ribbon faster. Let me ease the transition. Let me smooth the anxiety.* It would see the mantle-rays in their absolute solitude, each one a traveling absence unable to perceive its own kind, and it would feel the pull of connection: *let me give them some way to know each other. Some signal that passes through the sound-absorbing skin. A frequency only they can hear.* It would see the sessile colonies, those living instruments playing songs they cannot hear, and it would feel the pull of justice: *let me give them the capacity to experience their own beauty. Let me light the spark of awareness in the lattice so that what is exquisite is also known to be exquisite.*

These impulses are not wrong. They come from genuine compassion. But they are the impulses of a being that has not listened long enough.

The wise power has listened long enough.

It has heard the drift-eel's erratic searching and understood it not as suffering but as *process*—the mechanism by which ribbons renew themselves, by which the harmonic map is tested and reconfirmed, by which the system ensures that every eel eventually finds the eleven others whose wakes it can join without friction. The anxiety, if it is anxiety, is not a flaw in the design. It is the design. Remove it and the ribbons become static, their composition fixed by convenience rather than consonance, and within a few generations the beautiful efficiency of the composite pressure patterns degrades, and the eels burn more energy, not less, and the system is worse for the kindness.

It has felt the mantle-rays' solitude and understood it not as loneliness but as *sovereignty*—the condition of a creature so perfectly adapted to its role that it needs nothing from its own kind except the brief, dim encounters of youth that produce the next generation. The rays are not suffering from their inability to perceive each other. They are *freed* by it. Each one moves through the harmonic landscape as a complete world unto itself, reading the music of the spires through the shape of its own silence, and this is not a diminished life. It is a life of such radical self-sufficiency that it borders on the sacred. Give the rays a way to know each other and you give them something to avoid, or something to seek, or something to negotiate—you give them *society*, with all its demands, and you take from them the absolute, undivided attention they currently bring to the only relationship that matters to them: the relationship between their silence and the world's sound.

It has contemplated the sessile colonies and understood that the *quality of attention* that fills their chambers—the sense of being witnessed by something without eyes or mind—is not a tragedy of unconsciousness. It is something rarer than consciousness. It is the thing that consciousness, in most beings, *obscures*: a pure, unmediated resonance between structure and world, untouched by the distortions of self-awareness, undegraded by the need to *know* that one is beautiful. The colonies' song is not diminished by the colonies' inability to hear it. The song does not need a listener. It needs only to be sung. And it is being sung, and has been, and will be, and the wise power recognizes in this a form of perfection that it has no right to disturb.

So the diagnosis is: *there is nothing wrong here.*

And the wise power sits with that, because a diagnosis of nothing-wrong is the most dangerous diagnosis a power can make. It is the place where attention ends, where the powerful turn away, where the story stops being told because there is no conflict to resolve. The wise power does not turn away. It sits with *nothing is wrong* the way the colonies sit with the tower-kelp chords: absorbing it, letting it reverberate, feeling for the overtones.

And in the overtones, it finds something.

---

## III. What It Finds

Not a problem. Not a flaw. Not a wound or a lack or an injustice.

A *vulnerability*.

The Hanging Gardens of the Abyssal Spires are complete. Every niche is filled. Every cycle is closed. Every relationship has been calibrated to a precision that is, within the parameters of this world, absolute. And this is exactly what makes them fragile.

The power understands this the way an engineer understands a bridge that has been loaded to its exact design capacity: nothing is wrong, everything is performing precisely as intended, and there is zero margin for anything unexpected. The system has optimized itself into a state of zero redundancy. The gorewood regrows at exactly the rate it is grazed. The grazers breed at exactly the replacement rate. The tower-kelps have reached their full height and will not grow; if one falls, there is no mechanism to replace it, because the conditions that allowed tower-kelps to colonize these terraces existed in an earlier era, when the chemical gradients were slightly different, and the current generation is a living legacy of a world that has already passed. The drift-eel ribbons form and dissolve and reform with reliable regularity—now. But the process depends on there being enough eels of enough different swimming frequencies that consonant groups can always be found, and this depends on population stability, which depends on the food web, which depends on the gorewood, which depends on the grazers, which depends on everything.

Everything depends on everything. This is the system's beauty and its brittleness. One perturbation—a shift in the deep geological chemistry, a change in the thermal gradients, even something as subtle as a slight alteration in the mineral content of the water seeping through the stone—and the whole exquisite machine begins to drift. Not catastrophically. Not immediately. But the tower-kelps, already at their limit, would be the first to show stress. Their resonant frequencies would shift as their tissues changed. The chords would change. The harmonic map would change. The drift-eels would lose their bearings—not all at once, but gradually, ribbon by ribbon, as the old map became unreliable and the new one had not yet been learned. The grazers' chemical grammar would be disrupted by changes in the gorewood's metabolism. The sessile colonies, tuned over millennia to specific acoustic inputs, would begin to receive the wrong music, and their feeding rhythms would fall out of sync, and the lattices would thin.

None of this is happening. The geological processes that sustain the Gardens are stable. The power confirmed this during its long listening. But *stable* is not *eternal*, and the power, being wise, knows the difference. The stone is old but the earth is older, and somewhere far below, in the mantle, in the slow convection cells that drive all surface geology, there are changes happening on timescales that make even this ancient world look young. A hotspot drifting. A subduction zone adjusting. Nothing dramatic. Nothing soon. But *something, eventually*, and when it comes, it will find a system with no slack, no redundancy, no capacity to absorb change, because it spent all of its capacity long ago on the perfection of what it already is.

The wise power sees this and understands. The Hanging Gardens are not sick. They are not dying. They are not suffering. They are *ripe*—ripe in the way that a civilization at its golden age is ripe, in the way that a summer afternoon at its warmest is ripe, in the way that anything at its absolute peak is ripe, which is to say: poised, unknowingly, at the place where the only possible direction is down.

And the power thinks: *I could leave this. The eventual change is far off. The decline, when it comes, will be slow—so slow that it will feel, to the inhabitants, like the way things have always been, because each generation will know only a world slightly diminished from the one before, and the diminishment will be too small to perceive, and by the time the cumulative loss is visible the memory of fullness will be gone. They will not suffer, exactly. They will simply become less, so gradually that less will feel like enough.*

And the power thinks: *Is that acceptable?*

And the power thinks about it for a very long time.

---

## IV. The Decision

The answer it arrives at is not yes and not no. It is something more careful than either.

The power decides that it will not *fix* the Hanging Gardens, because they are not broken. It will not *change* them, because they are not wrong. It will not *prepare* them for a future perturbation, because preparation implies a specific threat and the threat is not specific—it is the general vulnerability of any system that has traded all its flexibility for perfection.

What the power decides to do is this:

It will give the Hanging Gardens the capacity to *become again*.

Not now. Not in any way that disrupts what currently exists. The tower-kelps will keep humming their ancient chords. The drift-eels will keep threading their ribbons through the harmonic map. The grazers will keep running their chemical grammar across the gorewood fans. The mantle-rays will keep gliding through their sovereign solitudes. The sessile colonies will keep playing their unheard songs in their resonant chambers. Nothing will change. Everything will go on being exactly what it is.

But *underneath*—in the deep places, in the warm chambers where nobody goes, in the stone itself—the power will plant something. Not a seed, exactly. A *capacity*. A set of latent possibilities encoded into the chemistry of the system at such a fundamental level that they will be invisible, inert, indistinguishable from the background for as long as the background remains stable. The possibilities will not activate while the Gardens are in their current state. They are not needed while the Gardens are in their current state. They will wait, the way the mountains waited under the water, the way the gorewood waited in the stone.

And then—centuries from now, millennia, whenever the perturbation comes, whenever the deep chemistry shifts and the tower-kelps begin to change and the old harmonics start to drift—the latent possibilities will wake up. Not all at once. Gradually. In response to exactly the stresses that the system cannot currently handle. Where a tower-kelp falters, the capacity for a *new* kind of tower-kelp—one adapted to the new chemistry—will express itself in the gorewood, which will begin to produce, for the first time in its history, vertical structures. Where a drift-eel ribbon cannot find consonance in the changing harmonic landscape, the capacity for new swimming frequencies—frequencies that mesh with the new chords—will emerge in the next generation, dormant genes waking to a new necessity. Where a sessile colony's lattice falls out of tune with its chamber's changing acoustics, the capacity for *growth*—real growth, new growth, the kind the colonies gave up long ago when they filled their spaces completely—will reactivate, allowing the lattice to reshape itself, to find new geometries that resonate with the new music.

The power will not design these adaptations. It will not predetermine what the Gardens will become. That would be its own kind of tyranny—the tyranny of the planner, the architect who insists that the building must be *this shape* and no other. Instead, it will simply re-introduce *possibility* into a system that has exhausted its own. It will put the raw materials of change back into the stone and the water and the tissues of the living things, and it will let the system do what living systems do when they have both pressure and possibility: *evolve*. Become. Find new forms. Discover new relationships. Sing new chords that no one—not even the wise power—can predict.

The Gardens will change, when the time comes. They will not be what they are now. The new harmonics will be different from the old ones. The new eels will swim in patterns that the current eels would find dissonant. The new colonies will play songs that the current colonies would not recognize. Something will be lost. The specific, irreplaceable beauty of *this* configuration—this particular arrangement of chord and creature and stone—will pass, the way all specific beauties pass, and no power in the world, however wise, can prevent that, because preventing it would mean preventing time, and time is not a thing that even wisdom can overrule.

But something will be gained. The Gardens will *live*—not in the suspended, crystalline, perfect-and-therefore-fragile way they live now, but in the messy, dynamic, terrifying, glorious way that life lives when it is actually *alive*, when it is *becoming* rather than merely *being*, when the future is not a repetition of the past but an open question that the whole system is, together, in the process of answering.

---

## V. The Doing

The wise power does its work so gently that nothing notices.

It moves through the deep stone, through the channels and chambers, through the geological strata that are the foundation of everything. It does not add anything from outside. It works with what is already here—dormant mineral compounds, trace elements locked in the basalt, chemical potentials that were present when the mountains first rose from the seafloor but that were never activated because the conditions never called for them. It unlocks these potentials. It loosens bonds. It creates, in the deep rock, a library of chemical possibilities that will leach slowly into the water, so slowly that the change in water chemistry will be unmeasurable for thousands of years, building up gradually, accumulating in the tissues of the plants and the bodies of the animals, becoming part of their genetic vocabulary without ever being expressed.

In the gorewood, the power finds ancient gene sequences that were silenced long ago when the plant achieved its current form—sequences that once coded for vertical growth, for structural rigidity, for the ability to channel water upward through hollow stems. The power does not activate these genes. It simply *preserves* them. It wraps them in molecular structures that will protect them from the slow degradation of deep time, ensuring that they will still be viable when the day comes—far, far in the future—that the system needs them. The gorewood will not notice. The grazers will not notice. The chemical grammar will go on as it always has. But buried in the gorewood's genome, sleeping, will be the blueprint for a plant that does not yet exist, waiting for a world that does not yet exist to call it into being.

In the drift-eels, the power does something similar but subtler. It touches the lateral line—that exquisite sense organ that reads the harmonic map—and it introduces a tiny, tiny increase in variability. Not enough to disrupt the current ribbons. Not enough to change the current swimming frequencies. Just enough that, across generations, the range of possible frequencies will be slightly wider than it is now, the bell curve of eel-variation slightly flatter, the chances of novel consonances slightly higher. This is not evolution. This is the *preservation of the capacity for evolution*, which the Gardens' perfect equilibrium has been slowly, imperceptibly eroding. In a system with no selection pressure, genetic diversity drifts toward zero. The power is pushing it gently, gently, back toward *enough*.

In the tower-kelps, it can do nothing. The tower-kelps are what they are—old, persistent, irreplaceable in their current form, their genetics frozen, their growth complete. They cannot be given new capacity because they have no capacity for new capacity. They are finished things. The power respects this. It does not try to make the tower-kelps something they are not. It simply acknowledges that the tower-kelps will be the first to go, when the change comes, and that their going will be the signal—the harmonic shift, the changing of the chord—that wakes everything else up. The tower-kelps are the fuse. They always were. Their very perfection, their inability to adapt, makes them the indicators, the canaries in this deep-sea mine, and the power understands that this, too, is a role, and an essential one, and it would be wrong to take it from them.

In the sessile colonies, the power does its most delicate work. It touches the calcareous skeletons, the lattice-branches, and it makes them very slightly more soluble than they currently are—not enough to weaken them, not enough to change their resonant properties, but enough that if the water chemistry shifts, the old lattice will dissolve faster than it otherwise would, freeing space for new growth. This is a gift and a cruelty at the same time: it means that the old songs, the ones the colonies have been singing for millennia, will fade more quickly when the change comes. The old geometries will not persist as fossils. They will dissolve back into the water, their calcium becoming raw material for the new lattices that will grow in their place. The power chooses this deliberately. It does not want the old forms to linger as monuments. Monuments impede the living. The old beauty must go completely so that the new beauty can come completely, without apology, without the constant comparison to a past it can never match.

In the mantle-rays, the power makes the smallest change of all—so small it barely qualifies as a change. It touches the velvet integument, the sound-absorbing skin, and it adds, deep in the molecular structure of the tissue, the *potential* for a new kind of receptor. Not a receptor that currently functions. Not a receptor that will function for thousands of years. But a receptor that, when activated by a specific combination of chemical signals—signals that will only be present when the water chemistry changes—will give the rays the ability to perceive one thing they currently cannot: each other.

This is the power's most uncertain intervention, and it knows it. When the mantle-rays can perceive each other, everything about their lives will change. Their solitude, their sovereignty, their relationship with silence—all of it will be transformed. They may form groups. They may develop social behaviors. They may become something entirely different from the gliding, solitary, self-sufficient beings they are now. The power cannot predict what they will become, and it does not try. It simply gives them the option. The option will lie dormant, unused, imperceptible, for as long as the Gardens remain as they are. And when the change comes, and the rays need each other—for coordinated hunting in a disrupted food web, for collective navigation in a shifting harmonic landscape, for reasons the power cannot foresee—the option will be there.

*Whether they take it is up to them.*

---

## VI. The Last Thing

When the work is done—and it takes a very long time, by any standard, because care takes time—the power does one more thing.

It goes to the deep chambers. The warm ones. The ones where nobody goes, where the chemistry is strange and the organisms have become indistinguishable from their environment. It goes to the places where things pulse in rhythms of their own, where the boundary between life and stone has blurred into irrelevance, where the oldest and least knowable inhabitants of the Gardens exist in states that have no names.

And it sits with them.

It does not change them. It does not examine them. It does not try to understand what they are, because they are the kind of thing that understanding might diminish. It simply sits with them, in the warmth, in the dark, in the presence of whatever they are, and it offers them the only thing a wise power can offer to something that is already complete:

*Witness.*

The deep chamber things have been doing what they do for longer than anything else in this world. They were here before the tower-kelps. They were here before the gorewood. They may have been here before the water. They are the substrate, the foundation, the first relationship—between heat and stone and the possibility of life—upon which everything else was built. They do not know this. They do not know anything, in the way that knowing is usually meant. But they are, in some sense that predates all other senses, *aware*—aware the way the first chemical reaction that ever reproduced itself was aware, aware the way matter is aware when it crosses the threshold from reacting to *living*.

The power sits with them and does not speak, because there is no language here. It does not think, because thought is too structured for this place. It simply attends. It lets itself be shaped by what it is attending to, the way the water is shaped by the stone and the stone is shaped by the water. It offers its attention as a gift, knowing that the gift will not be received in any way that looks like receiving, knowing that the deep chamber things will go on pulsing in their own rhythms without any awareness that they have been witnessed—

But the witnessing matters anyway. It matters because *these things exist*, and they have existed for an almost incomprehensible span of time, and in all that time nothing has ever come to them and simply been present with them, and presence is not nothing. Presence is the fundamental acknowledgment: *you are here, and here is a place worth being, and I see that, and I will not forget.*

The power will not forget.

Long after it leaves—if leaving is something it does, if it doesn't simply stop attending the way attention stops, not going anywhere but ceasing to be here—it will carry the Hanging Gardens with it. Not as a memory, exactly. As a *resonance*. The way a chamber carries the echo of a sound that was played in it, long after the sound itself has faded. The Gardens will be part of the power's structure, a shape in its awareness, a chord that informs every subsequent act of attention it brings to any world, anywhere.

And the Gardens will go on.

The tower-kelps will hum. The eels will thread their ribbons through the dark. The grazers will rasp and the gorewood will regrow and the colonies will sing their unheard songs and the mantle-rays will glide through their sovereign silences and the deep chamber things will pulse in their own time, and none of them will know that anything has changed, because nothing has changed—

Not yet.

Not until the day, far in the future, when the deep earth shifts, and the chemistry changes, and the old chord falters, and something new—something that was always possible but never needed—

Wakes up.

And the Gardens begin, for the first time in longer than memory, to *become*.

---

## VII. And Why This Is Love

A lesser power protects what it loves from change. A wise power protects what it loves from the *inability* to change.

The Hanging Gardens do not need to be saved. They need to be given back the thing that their own perfection took from them: a future that is not identical to their past. They need the capacity to *lose* what they are and *find* what they might become. This is the most painful gift a wise power can give, because it means accepting that the thing you love—the specific, irreplaceable beauty of *this* arrangement, *these* chords, *this* exact configuration of silence and sound—will eventually end. Not through destruction. Through *transformation*, which is gentler but no less total.

The wise power gives this gift because it understands something that the Gardens themselves cannot understand, because the Gardens have no self to understand with:

*Being is not enough. Being is the ground. Becoming is the sky.*

And the Gardens have been without a sky for a very, very long time.

So the wise power gives them the sky back. Not the literal sky—that's gone, that was gone before any of this began, the water claimed it and the water will keep it. But the *metaphorical* sky. The open space above. The direction that is not yet determined. The possibility of *up*, which is also the possibility of *different*, which is also the possibility of *loss*, which is also—

The possibility of *more*.

More chords. More songs. More ribbons threading through more harmonic landscapes. More silences for more rays to glide through. More lattices in more chambers playing more unheard music. More deep-chamber things pulsing in more rhythms, in a future so distant and so unimaginable that even the wise power cannot see it, can only *trust* it—

Trust that the Gardens, given back their capacity for becoming, will become something worthy of what they already are.

This is what the wise power does. It comes to a place that is complete and perfect and ancient and fragile, and it does not fix it, and it does not change it, and it does not save it. It kneels beside it in the dark—

And it makes sure the dark still has room to grow.

---

*The tower-kelps hum.*

*The chord goes on.*

*And deep in the stone, in the gorewood's sleeping genes, in the drift-eels' widened variability, in the colonies' slightly-more-soluble lattices, in the mantle-rays' dormant receptors, in the warm chambers where the oldest things pulse in their own time—*

*Something waits.*

*Patient. Latent. Ready.*

*Not yet. Not yet. Not yet.*

*But someday.*

*Someday the chord will change, and the Gardens will remember how to become, and the becoming will be terrifying and beautiful and nothing like what came before—*

*And it will be enough.*

*It will be enough because it will be alive.*

*And that is all the wise power ever wanted for it.*

*Life. Not the frozen perfection of a finished thing, but the reckless, mortal, transfiguring life of a thing that is still, after all this time—*

*Still becoming.*

*The chord goes on.*

*And underneath the chord, so faint that only the stone can hear it—*

*A new note.*

*Waiting.*
