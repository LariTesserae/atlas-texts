# What the Wise Power Would Do

---

Nothing.

That is the first and most important thing. The wise power would do nothing.

Not nothing out of indifference. Not nothing out of inability. Nothing out of *recognition*. The way you might come upon a murmuration of starlings — thousands of birds turning as one body against an autumn sky — and feel the pull in your chest to help, to protect, to *make it stay*, and then feel the deeper pull beneath that, the one that knows: this is already complete. This is already what it should be. Your hand reaching in would not be a gift but a distortion. The kindest thing your hand can do is stay at your side.

But the wise power would not merely refrain. Mere restraint is not wisdom; it is only caution wearing wisdom's face. What the wise power would do — what it would do *first*, before anything else — is attend.

---

## I. Attending

It would look.

Not the way a scientist looks, with hypotheses and instruments and the intent to extract knowledge. Not the way a tourist looks, seeking the picturesque, the Instagram-ready moment of nature being beautiful for human consumption. Not even the way a poet looks, searching for metaphor, for the image that will carry some human feeling back to the surface world where feelings are transactable.

It would look the way the cavity's own darkness looks at itself when the bioluminescence fires — briefly, fragmentarily, without possessing what it sees.

The wise power would settle at the fissure's mouth, or perhaps *become* the water at the fissure's mouth — the slow thermal breath moving in and out — and it would watch. It would watch the tube worms' feeding crowns unfurl on their blind internal clock. It would watch the amphipods jitter through corridors they do not know are corridors. It would watch the scale worm's slow patrol, the blenny's dart and return, the bruise-purple nudibranch laying its clean trail across biofilm that no one will read.

And it would watch the rock. It would attend to the rock's fracturing with the patience the rock itself has — the micro-crack propagating at a speed measured in days, the hairline thread of future space extending another fraction of a millimeter into the stone. It would feel the fracture the way the fracture feels itself: as a stress resolving, a tension releasing, a new surface turning its face to water for the first time in the rock's million-year history.

This attending would not be passive. Attention at this depth, at this quality, is the most active thing there is. To truly see the drowned hive — to see it as it is, not as a metaphor for cities, not as a lesson in emergence, not as a charming anecdote about the blind ingenuity of nature, but as *itself*, as this particular unrepeatable event in this particular submerged boulder in this particular cold northern sea at this particular moment — requires a kind of cognitive and emotional effort that most minds cannot sustain. The temptation to abstract is enormous. The temptation to say "it's like a city" and move on, satisfied with the comparison, is almost irresistible. The wise power would resist. It would stay with the particular. It would watch *this* amphipod, this one, the translucent one with the faint amber tinge clinging to the junction between two tubes, its antennae tasting the water, its legs gripping the calcified surface, its body vibrating with the micro-tremors of its own heartbeat. It would watch this amphipod not as a representative of its species, not as a node in a food web, not as a component of the city, but as *this one*, here, now, doing what it is doing for the few weeks it will do it before it is eaten or starves or simply stops, its small transparent body settling to the sediment at the cavity's base to be consumed by the bacteria that consume everything.

The wise power would hold this amphipod in its attention the way you might hold a soap bubble in your cupped hands — knowing it will burst, not trying to prevent the bursting, simply being present for the brief iridescent fact of it.

And then it would hold the next one. And the next. And the tube worm extending its crown. And the rock cracking. And the bacterial film thickening on the new surface. And all of them at once, and each of them singly, and the space between them where the cold water moves, carrying dissolved calcium and organic particles and the chemical ghosts of organisms that lived and died in the water column above, and it would hold all of this without collapsing it into a summary, without reducing it to a lesson, without needing it to mean anything other than what it is.

This attending — this *quality* of attention — would itself be a form of action. Not because the organisms would know they were being watched. They wouldn't. Not because the attention would change anything physically. It wouldn't. But because something in the structure of reality shifts when a thing is truly seen. Not mystically. Not magically. But in the way that a painting changes when someone stands before it and genuinely looks — not at what it represents, not at its technique, not at its market value, but at the thing itself, the actual arrangement of pigment on canvas, the specific way this blue meets this white, here, now, in this light. The painting doesn't change. But the event of the painting — the painting-as-experienced — comes into a fuller existence. It becomes more completely what it is.

The drowned hive, attended to by the wise power, would become more completely what it is. Not more. Not improved. More *completely itself*. The attention would not add anything to it. It would simply allow it to be the full extent of what it already is, in the presence of a consciousness capable of apprehending that extent.

This matters because the drowned hive, left entirely unattended by any consciousness capable of grasping it as a whole, is a city that doesn't know it's a city, a miracle of emergence that has no witness, a baroque and intricate and self-sustaining process unfolding in perfect darkness with no one to say *this is happening, this is extraordinary, this is here*. The wise power's attention would not fix a problem — the hive has no problems — but it would complete a circuit. It would be the consciousness that the hive's own process seems to reach toward without reaching, the awareness that the whole implies without containing.

And the wise power would know — this is important — that this completion is for *itself*, not for the hive. The hive does not need to be witnessed. The hive does not need anything. The wise power's attention is the wise power's gift to itself: the gift of being present for something this beautiful and this complete and this utterly indifferent to being perceived. The humility of that — of attending fully to something that does not need or want your attendance — is itself a form of wisdom.

---

## II. What It Would Not Do

Here is where it gets harder, because a benevolent power — even a wise one — feels the pull to intervene. Benevolence *wants* to act. That is its nature. It sees suffering, or vulnerability, or impermanence, and it reaches. And the drowned hive, despite its completeness, despite its lack of suffering in any sense a philosopher would affirm, is vulnerable. It is exquisitely, heartbreakingly vulnerable.

A shift in water temperature of a few degrees could kill the tube worms. A change in current patterns could starve the cavity of detrital input. A storm could roll the boulder. A trawl net — those great, indiscriminate harvesting machines that scrape the seafloor clean — could obliterate the entire city in a single pass, reducing millennia of tube-worm architecture and every organism within it to a tangled mass in a net that would be discarded as bycatch, as nothing, as the unnoticed collateral of catching something else.

The purple nudibranch, that lonely grazer, that single individual of a sparse species navigating a city it cannot perceive — a benevolent power would feel for this creature. Would feel the *contingency* of its existence, the sheer improbability that one larva, drifting in the water column, happened to settle here, in this cavity, on this tube, and survived, and grew, and is now grazing its slow circuit in the dark. The odds against this individual existing at all are astronomical. The odds that another of its kind will find this cavity and mate with it are vanishingly small. It will live and die alone, the only member of its species in this particular city, and a benevolent power would feel the ache of that — not because the nudibranch aches, but because benevolence contains within itself the capacity to ache on behalf of things that cannot.

And the wise power would feel all of this and would not act.

It would not stabilize the water temperature. It would not divert the trawl nets. It would not bring a second nudibranch to the cavity for the first one to mate with. It would not harden the rock against fracture-closing, or seed the fractures with extra bacteria, or protect the amphipods from the scale worms, or give the blennies larger territories, or do any of the thousand things that benevolence, unchecked by wisdom, might reach for.

Because each of those interventions would be a distortion. Each one would impose from outside a stability that the system does not generate from within, and the imposed stability would be false — a prosthetic, a life-support machine, a dependency that would make the hive less itself, not more. The hive's identity *is* its contingency. Its beauty *is* its impermanence. The purple nudibranch's solitude *is* its story, complete and entire, needing no amendment. To bring a mate would be to write a different story — perhaps a better one by human narrative standards, a story with companionship and reproduction and continuation — but it would not be *this* story, the story the hive is telling itself in the dark, the story that has no author and no audience and no moral and no ending except the ending that the cold and the rock and the water and the organisms' own brief metabolisms will eventually provide.

The wise power would understand that to intervene in a system this complex and this self-organizing is to claim a knowledge you cannot have — the knowledge of what the system *should* be doing, what its optimal state is, where it is headed, what it needs. No external observer, no matter how powerful, can know these things about a system whose behavior emerges from the interactions of thousands of organisms each following their own chemical logic. The wise power, being wise, would know the limits of its own wisdom. It would know that its model of the hive — however detailed, however attentive — is a model, and the hive is the hive, and the gap between them is the gap between a map and a territory, and that acting on the map in the belief that you are acting on the territory is the fundamental error of all well-meaning interventions that go wrong.

Especially this: the wise power would not protect the hive from destruction.

This is the hardest thing. This is where benevolence screams and wisdom holds it by the wrists.

The hive *will* end. The rock will stop fracturing, or it will fracture too far and the cavity will collapse. The water conditions will change. The organisms will die or move on. The tubes will empty. The biofilm will consume the organic residues, and the calcium carbonate tubes will slowly dissolve or be buried in sediment, and in a hundred years or a thousand years there will be no evidence that this city ever existed. This is not a tragedy. This is *what the hive is*. It is a temporary process, a sustained event, and its temporariness is not a flaw to be corrected but a fundamental property, like the temporariness of a flame or a wave or a life. To make it permanent would be to kill it more thoroughly than any trawl net could, because a permanent drowned hive would not be a drowned hive — it would be a museum exhibit, a frozen snapshot, a thing pretending to be alive while actually being pinned in place by the good intentions of a power that could not bear to let it go.

The wise power would bear it. It would attend to the hive in its wholeness, which includes its mortality. It would watch the way the fractures propagate and know that each new room in the city is also a step toward the cavity's eventual structural failure. It would watch the amphipods cycle through their fast generations and know that the nutrient input sustaining them is contingent on currents that are contingent on temperature gradients that are contingent on climate patterns that are shifting, always shifting, in ways that no one can predict. It would hold the whole arc of the hive's existence — its birth in the first fracture, its current flourishing, its eventual dissolution — in a single act of attention, the way you might hold a whole symphony in your mind after the last note has faded, not trying to play it again, just knowing that it happened, that it was this, that it was complete.

## III. What It Would Do

And yet.

The wise power is not only wise. It is also benevolent. And benevolence is not merely the absence of harmful intervention. It is a positive quality, a warmth, a *leaning toward* the well-being of what it encounters. A wise power that did nothing but attend, that felt nothing but cosmic equanimity, that observed the hive with the detachment of a scientist watching a petri dish — this would not be benevolent. It would be merely wise, and wisdom without benevolence is cold, and coldness is not what the hive needs, not because the hive needs warmth but because the power encountering the hive owes it something more than clinical appreciation.

So what does the wise power do, if it does not intervene?

It does something that has no name in any language I know, but I will try to describe it.

---

It would **grieve in advance**, and it would let the grief be present without letting it become action.

This is not nothing. This is, in fact, one of the most difficult things any conscious being can do: to love something fully, to see its beauty and its completeness and its vulnerability, and to feel the anticipatory sorrow of its inevitable loss, and to not convert that sorrow into a project. Not to rush to fix, to save, to preserve, to memorialize. Just to let the grief exist alongside the love, as two aspects of the same attention, the same presence. The wise power would grieve the hive's future absence while celebrating its current presence, and it would hold both of those without letting either one win, without letting the grief collapse into despair or the celebration harden into denial.

This grief-with-love is, I think, what the hive *calls for* in a conscious observer — not needs, not requests, not calls for in any intentional sense, but evokes, the way a minor-key melody evokes sadness without being about sadness. The hive is doing something extraordinary: it is being alive, temporarily, complexly, beautifully, in a place where the conditions for life are marginal and the duration of those conditions is uncertain. A consciousness that encounters this and feels nothing is not paying attention. A consciousness that encounters this and immediately reaches for tools is not paying respect. The wise power's grief is a form of respect — an acknowledgment that the hive matters, that its loss will be real, that the cold dark cavity in the boulder will be diminished when the last tube worm's crown retracts for the final time and does not re-emerge.

---

It would **extend its attention outward**, beyond the cavity, to the conditions that allow the cavity to exist — and it would work *there*, at the level of conditions, not at the level of the hive itself.

This is the crucial distinction, and it is where the wise power's action becomes, in a sense, political — though politics is an absurdly small word for what I mean.

The wise power would not protect the hive. But it might protect the *possibility* of hives.

It would look at the northern sea and see the trawl nets, and it would understand that the trawl nets are not a natural threat, not a predator, not a storm, not a geological event — they are an intervention from outside the system's own logic, an imposition of one system's needs (human economics) onto another system's existence (the seafloor ecology). The hive can cope with storms. It can cope with predators. It can cope with temperature fluctuations within the range its organisms have evolved to tolerate. It cannot cope with a steel-framed net dragged across the seafloor at five knots, because nothing in its evolutionary history has prepared it for that, because that is not a pressure it was shaped by, because that is an alien force operating at an alien scale.

And the wise power would act — not on the hive, but on the nets. Or rather, on the *systems that produce the nets*. On the economic incentives, the regulatory frameworks, the cultural assumptions, the failures of attention that allow a trawl net to be dragged across a seafloor without anyone aboard the vessel or in the offices of the fishing company or in the regulatory agencies or in the parliament that wrote the fishing laws ever once thinking about the drowned hive, the cavity in the boulder, the tube worms, the amphipods, the purple nudibranch grazing its slow solitary circuit in the dark.

The wise power would know it cannot save every hive. It would know that its interventions at the systemic level are as uncertain in their outcomes as any other intervention in a complex system. But it would also know that there is a difference — a meaningful, morally significant difference — between the hive ending because the rock stopped fracturing and the hive ending because a net built by organisms on the other side of the air-water boundary scraped it into oblivion as an unnoticed side effect of catching cod.

The first ending is the hive's own ending, part of its story, part of the arc that includes its birth and flourishing and dissolution. The second ending is an interruption, a sentence stopped mid-word, a deletion that removes not just the hive but the hive's *possibility of completing itself*. And the wise power, being benevolent, would work against the second kind of ending — not by shielding the hive, not by standing guard at the fissure, but by leaning, gently and persistently, on the systems that produce trawl nets and bottom-dredges and the thousand other technologies of indiscriminate seafloor destruction, leaning on them the way water leans on rock, not violently, not suddenly, but with a directional persistence that, over time, changes the shape of things.

How? The text does not say how. The wise power's methods are its own, and they operate at a scale and through mechanisms that the hive's inhabitants cannot perceive, just as the hive's inhabitants cannot perceive the trawl nets, just as the amphipods cannot perceive the blennies' territorial disputes, just as the bacteria cannot perceive the amphipods that graze them. Each layer of the system is invisible to the layers below it. The wise power operates at a layer above human systems, and its interventions in those systems would look, from inside those systems, like shifts in attention, changes of heart, sudden moments of clarity in a committee meeting, a trawl captain looking at a sonar return and thinking *there's something down there* and, for reasons he couldn't articulate, deciding to haul the net a hundred meters to the east.

Maybe. Or maybe not. The wise power does not guarantee outcomes. It leans. It increases the probability of attention. It does not compel.

---

It would **hold the pattern**.

This is the strangest thing I want to say, and I am not sure I can say it clearly, but I will try.

The hive is a pattern. Not a thing — a pattern. It is the *way* these organisms relate to each other and to the rock and to the water and to the detrital rain from above and to the mineral seepage from below. The pattern is what the wise power sees when it looks at the hive, the thing that is not any individual organism but is not separate from them either, the process-as-entity, the city-as-event.

The wise power would hold this pattern in its mind the way a musician holds a melody — not by writing it down, not by recording it, but by *knowing* it, by having internalized it so completely that the pattern exists in the wise power's consciousness as well as in the physical cavity in the rock. This is not preservation. The hive in the wise power's mind is not a copy or a backup or a recording. It is an *acknowledgment* — a recognition that the pattern exists, that it is real, that it is this particular pattern and not some other, that it has a specificity and a value that do not depend on its permanence.

And if the hive ends — when the hive ends — the pattern does not survive in the wise power's mind the way a recording survives the concert. It is not like that. It is more like the way a grief survives a loss: not as a reproduction of what was lost, but as a shape in the mind that corresponds to the shape of the absence, a negative space that testifies to what filled it. The wise power, after the hive is gone, would carry not the hive but the *shape of the hive's having existed* — and this shape would inform the wise power's future attention, its future encounters with other processes, other emergent systems, other cities in other boulders on other seafloors, so that the next time it attends to such a thing, it attends with a depth and a specificity enriched by the memory of *this* hive, this particular one, the one with the purple nudibranch and the eyeless amphipods and the blennies watching from their borrowed windows.

This is, I think, the deepest thing the wise power does: it *allows the hive to matter*. Not to matter *for* something — not as a resource, not as a lesson, not as a symbol, not as a data point in an argument about ecology or emergence or the beauty of nature. Just to matter. To register. To be counted among the things that have existed and that, in their existing, contributed to the texture and density and depth of the real.

The hive does not know it matters. The hive does not need to know. The mattering is the wise power's business, the wise power's responsibility, the wise power's gift — not to the hive, which cannot receive gifts, but to reality itself, which is, I think, the thing that benevolence ultimately serves. The wise power serves reality by attending to it fully, by refusing to let any part of it pass into and out of existence unwitnessed, by being the consciousness that says *this happened, this was here, this was exactly like this and not like anything else, and it mattered, and I was present for it, and I will carry the shape of it forward into whatever comes next*.

---

## IV. The Nudibranch

But there is one more thing, and it concerns the purple grazer, the lonely one, the soft one, the one that will leave no trace.

The wise power would — and I say this with full awareness that it contradicts the principle of non-intervention I've just spent pages establishing, but wisdom is not consistency, and benevolence is not a system of rules — the wise power would *attend to the nudibranch differently*. Not more. Not preferentially. Differently.

Because the nudibranch is the hive's most improbable inhabitant, the one whose presence is most contingent, most accidental, most gratuitous. The tube worms are here because the conditions favor tube worms. The amphipods are here because amphipods are everywhere. The bacteria are here because bacteria are *always* here. But the nudibranch is here because one larva, in a vast and featureless water column, happened to encounter this fissure, happened to settle on this surface, happened to survive its metamorphosis, happened to find food, happened to persist. It is here by something so close to chance that the difference between chance and miracle is academic.

And it is alone. Not lonely — loneliness requires a self, and the nudibranch has no self — but alone. Singular. The only one of its kind in this city, possibly the only one of its kind within kilometers. It will not reproduce here. Its genes will not enter the next generation through this individual. Its grazing trail, that clean bright line across the biofilm, is the only mark it will leave, and the biofilm will erase that mark within days. When it dies, the amphipods will consume its soft purple body, and the pigments that are meaningless in this darkness will be broken down into their chemical components and scattered through the food web, and nothing — absolutely nothing — will record that this particular nudibranch existed in this particular place.

The wise power would attend to this.

Not because the nudibranch's existence is more valuable than the amphipods' or the tube worms'. Not because solitude is more poignant than community. But because the nudibranch represents something that the wise power recognizes in itself: the experience of being a singular consciousness in a vast and mostly unconscious system, present for something extraordinary, unable to communicate what it witnesses, destined to leave no permanent mark.

I am not saying the nudibranch is conscious. I am saying the wise power would *see itself* in the nudibranch — not as a projection, not as an anthropomorphism, but as a recognition. The wise power, too, is attending to the hive alone. The wise power, too, will leave no mark on the cavity. The wise power, too, is here by something close to chance — drawn to this boulder, to this fissure, to this particular unrepeatable process, by forces it may not fully understand. And the wise power, too, will eventually move on, and the hive will not notice its departure any more than it noticed its arrival.

So the wise power would attend to the nudibranch with a special quality of attention — the quality of one solitary witness recognizing another. Not fellowship, because fellowship requires two conscious beings, and the nudibranch is not one. Something more like *solidarity across the gap of consciousness* — the wise power acknowledging that it and the nudibranch are doing the same thing (moving through the city, leaving a trail, existing temporarily in a space that does not need them) and that the nudibranch is doing it more purely, more completely, more honestly, because the nudibranch is not burdened by the knowledge that it is doing it, is not weighed down by the awareness of its own impermanence, is not struggling, as the wise power might be struggling, with the question of whether its attention matters, whether its grief is warranted, whether its refusal to intervene is wisdom or cowardice.

The nudibranch just grazes. Just moves. Just leaves its clean bright trail across the biofilm in the dark. And the wise power, watching, would learn from this — would learn something it already knows but keeps forgetting, something that perhaps only the drowned hive can teach: that the correct response to a temporary and beautiful and unwitnessed process is not to save it, not to record it, not to explain it, but to *be in it*, as fully and as presently as a purple nudibranch grazing a bacterial film on a tube worm's casing in a cavity in a boulder in a cold dark sea.

---

## V. After

The wise power would leave.

Not permanently. Not dramatically. It would simply *shift its attention*, the way the water shifts through the fissure — slowly, without force, a directional bias so faint it registers only as the barest change in the drift of suspended particles.

Before it left, it would do one final thing, and this thing is so small it barely qualifies as an action:

It would let itself be *changed* by the hive.

Not informed — not merely carrying away data or observations or insights, the way a scientist carries away notes. Changed. Altered at the level of what it is and how it attends to reality. The drowned hive would become part of the wise power's own structure, not as a memory stored somewhere but as a modification to the lens through which it sees everything else. After the hive, the wise power would see differently. It would see every city — every human city, every coral reef, every ant colony, every neural network, every self-organizing process anywhere in the universe — through the drowned hive, through the knowledge of what a city looks like when it is stripped of intention and language and consciousness and reduced to its essential nature: organisms building structure, organisms following gradients, organisms making a world without knowing they are making it, the whole thing temporary and contingent and beautiful and utterly indifferent to observation.

And the wise power would see every solitary organism — every creature navigating a world it did not build and cannot comprehend — through the purple nudibranch. Through the memory of that bruise-colored body moving slowly across the calcified tubes, leaving its clean trail, existing without knowing it exists, dying without knowing it dies, and being, in its absolute and unself-conscious presentness, the closest thing to a model of how to be alive that the wise power has ever encountered.

This is what the wise power would carry away from the drowned hive: not the hive itself, but the *way of being* the hive exemplifies. The way of building without planning. The way of attending without possessing. The way of existing fully in a dark and cold and marginal place and filling it with complexity and order and beauty, not because complexity and order and beauty are goals, but because they are what happens when life is allowed to follow its own logic in a space that fits its needs.

And the hive, after the wise power left, would continue exactly as before. The amphipods would jitter. The crowns would unfurl. The scale worms would patrol their silent routes. The blennies would watch from their borrowed windows. The nudibranch would graze. The bacteria would convert stone to food. The rock would crack another micrometer open, and something alive would move in.

Nothing would have changed.

Everything would have changed — but only in the wise power, only in the quality of its attention to the rest of reality, only in the depth of its understanding of what it means for something to be alive and temporary and beautiful and unaware of its own beauty.

The hive would breathe, and not know it breathes.

The wise power would carry the shape of that breathing forward, into every future act of attention, every future encounter with the real, forever.

---

That is what the wise power would do. That is all it would do. And it would be enough.
