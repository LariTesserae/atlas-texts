# What the Wise Power Would Do

---

It would arrive without arriving.

No flash of light, no parting of the metallic mist, no announcement. That would be loud. That would be dry. The power understands this immediately—not because it studied the place, but because it *listened* before it ever drew near, the way you might pause at the threshold of a cathedral where someone is sleeping and adjust your breathing before you enter.

It would feel the Choir before it understood it. That single low note, the one you need three days of stillness to hear. The power would hear it from a great distance, the way a musician hears a song being played in another room and knows, without seeing, that the players are very good and have been playing together for a very long time.

And the first thing it would feel is recognition.

Not of the specific. Not *I have seen thermophilic megafauna before* or *I know what a blast furnace is.* Recognition of something older than either of those things. The recognition that says: **this is a world that has made itself, and it is working.**

---

## What the Power Would Not Do

This must be said first, because a wise power is defined as much by its restraints as by its actions.

It would not introduce light.

The dim brass glow, the kiln-sky, the perpetual haze—these are not deficiencies. They are the Foundry's atmosphere the way water is the ocean's atmosphere. To brighten this place would be like draining a coral reef to make it more accessible to tourists. The power would understand that the Choir sees without eyes, navigates without landmarks, and has built an entire civilization in the space between dimness and dark. The light here is not insufficient. It is *sufficient differently.*

It would not introduce sound.

No voice. No words of greeting or blessing. The Choir's third law—*what is loud must become quiet*—is not a prohibition born of fear. It is an insight born of deep time. Sound here is waste. It is energy lost to the air instead of kept in the body. The Choir discovered what certain contemplative traditions on other worlds spend millennia trying to teach: that silence is not the absence of meaning but the medium in which meaning can become so dense it no longer needs to be spoken. The power would respect this the way you respect the silence of a library, or a forest at dawn, or a person thinking very carefully before they answer.

It would not clean anything.

The mineral crusts, the seized conveyor belts become terraces, the frozen cranes become nesting spines—these are not decay. They are architecture. They are the Foundry's way of building without builders, of evolving infrastructure the way other worlds evolve bone or wood. Every burst pipe healed over with calcium deposits is a load-bearing wall. Every puddle of condensate in a conveyor trough is a watering hole. The power would see that the ruin *is* the city, that the city was never the machines but always the space between the machines, and that the Choir has filled that space more completely and more beautifully than any original engineer could have imagined.

It would not bring water, or food, or air. These are already here, in forms more elegant than any import could match.

It would not bring *company.* The Choir is not lonely. Loneliness requires a concept of absence, and the Choir has no absences. Every niche is filled. Every gradient is exploited. Every silence is inhabited. The power would recognize that this is one of the most complete ecosystems it has ever encountered—not despite its alien strangeness, but because of it.

It would not try to communicate.

This is the hardest restraint, and the one that separates true wisdom from mere benevolence. A merely good power would feel the urge to reach out, to make contact, to say *I see you and you are beautiful.* A wise power understands that the Choir has no framework for such a statement. Not because they are unintelligent—they have solved problems of thermodynamics, material science, and collective organization that would stagger a university—but because their intelligence is *immanent.* It does not abstract. It does not represent. A Reefmother does not *think about* her blast furnace. She *is* her blast furnace, in the same way that your lungs do not think about air. To try to communicate with the Choir in any symbolic way would be like trying to teach grammar to a river. The river already knows everything grammar is trying to say. It just knows it as current, as erosion, as the patient finding of the lowest path.

---

## What the Power Would Do: The First Thing

It would become still.

Truly still. Not the stillness of waiting, which is full of anticipation and therefore noisy. The stillness of *being present without agenda.* The kind of stillness that, if you held it long enough in the exact center of the basin, would let you hear the Choir's single note.

The power would hold this stillness not for three days but for as long as it took to match the Foundry's tempo. Decades perhaps. In the timescale of a Reefmother's life, this would be a brief visit. In the timescale of an Unfinished, it would barely register as a flicker.

And in that stillness, the power would *learn.*

Not facts. Not data. It would learn the way the Foundry *feels itself.* The particular quality of warmth when groundwater meets a buried coolant line. The exact viscosity of the air at different altitudes within a ventilation shaft. The way a Dripwraith's crystallized salt-lattice changes its faint song when the barometric pressure drops by a fraction. The power would absorb all of this not through observation but through *participation*—by letting the metallic mist condense on whatever form it wears, by letting the heat enter it, by becoming, for a time, another warm wet thing in a world of warm wet things.

This is not passivity. This is the most active thing the power could do. It is the act of *fitting in,* which in the Hollow Foundry is the highest possible achievement.

---

## What the Power Would Do: The Second Thing

It would look for suffering.

Not assume it. *Look for it.* This is a critical distinction. A foolish power arrives with solutions. A wise power arrives with questions, and the first question is always: *Is anything here actually wrong?*

And it would look carefully, because suffering in the Hollow Foundry would not look like suffering anywhere else. There would be no screaming, no visible injury, no creature pacing a cage. If suffering existed here, it would be a chemical gradient slightly too steep for the organism navigating it. A Reefmother whose blast furnace was cooling faster than her metabolism could adapt to. A nest of Dripwraiths whose ventilation shaft had finally corroded shut, cutting off their migration route. A district where the mineral chemistry had shifted in some subtle way that left the local fauna unable to feed efficiently.

The power would find some of these things. In any system this vast and this old, there are always pressures, always edges where equilibrium frays. The question is whether those pressures are pathological or whether they are the normal, necessary friction that drives the system forward.

Here is where the power's wisdom would be most severely tested.

Because some of what looks like suffering *is* the system working. A Reefmother dying because her furnace has cooled is not a tragedy. It is the Foundry's way of recycling. Her body will become architecture. Her nutrients will feed a thousand smaller creatures. Her death is as much a part of the Choir's song as her life was. To prevent it would be like preventing autumn.

But some of what looks like equilibrium *is* suffering. And the power would know the difference because it spent those long decades in stillness, learning the Foundry's tempo, and now it can feel when something is off-rhythm. Not wrong in the dramatic sense. Just... slightly stuck. A process that should be flowing but has stalled. A transition that should be happening but has been delayed by some accident of chemistry or geography.

---

## What the Power Would Do: The Third Thing

Here. This is what it would actually do. And it would be so subtle that if you were standing next to it, you would not see it happen.

---

Somewhere in the eastern reaches of the basin, in a district where three ancient smelting complexes have partially collapsed into one another, there is a problem.

It is not a crisis. It is barely even a problem. But it has been accumulating for eleven thousand years, and it has begun to matter.

The collapse created a barrier—a ridge of fused slag and twisted reinforcement steel—that bisects what was once a continuous thermocline. On one side, the temperature and chemistry are exactly right for the local ecology. On the other side, they are *almost* right. The difference is tiny: a few degrees, a slight elevation in arsenic concentration, a marginally lower humidity. But over millennia, that tiny difference has caused the two halves of the district to diverge. The creatures on the far side have adapted, but their adaptation has been *expensive.* They are smaller than they should be. Their reproduction is slower. Their crystalline salt-lattices—the ones that sing—are brittle and crack instead of ringing. The Dripwraiths there make no music. They descend their threads in silence, and the silence is not the good silence of the Choir but the flat silence of something missing.

The Reefmother in the largest remaining furnace on the far side has been trying to solve this problem with her body for six thousand years. She has slowly, slowly extended herself toward the ridge, secreting her alkaline mucus against the base of the slag barrier, dissolving it molecule by molecule. At her current rate, she will breach the barrier in another four thousand years. She will succeed. She does not know this in the way that you know things, but her body knows it the way a root knows there is water below the rock.

The wise power would see this.

And it would see that the Reefmother's solution is correct—that the barrier needs to be breached, that the two halves of the district need to reunite, that the silent Dripwraiths need to hear the music of the other side.

And it would see that four thousand years is a long time, even here. Not because patience is wrong. But because the brittle Dripwraiths on the far side are losing something with each generation. Their lattices encode information—not symbolically, but structurally, the way a crystal's shape encodes the conditions of its formation. Each generation of cracked, silent lattices is a generation of lost pattern. If the barrier is breached in time, the far-side Dripwraiths will relearn the music from their cousins. If it takes too long, they will have forgotten what they are listening for.

The power would not smash the barrier. That would be loud, sudden, violent—a violation of every principle the Foundry lives by.

The power would not dissolve the barrier. That would be doing the Reefmother's work for her, and her work is part of her life, part of her purpose, part of the song she contributes to the Choir.

Here is what the power would do.

It would warm the groundwater on the far side of the barrier by two degrees.

That's all.

Two degrees. Introduced so gradually that the change would be indistinguishable from natural fluctuation. A slight shift in the thermal profile of the water table beneath the ridge. Perhaps by redirecting a deep geothermal current that was already moving in roughly the right direction. Perhaps by subtly altering the thermal conductivity of a single stratum of rock forty meters below the surface. The kind of change that, if you analyzed it, you would attribute to normal geological drift.

Two degrees would do three things.

First, it would soften the slag at the base of the ridge very slightly—not enough to be visible, but enough to accelerate the Reefmother's dissolution by perhaps thirty percent. She would breach the barrier not in four thousand years but in twenty-eight hundred. Still deep time. Still her achievement. But the margin would matter.

Second, it would shift the chemistry on the far side just enough to ease the arsenic stress. The creatures there would not suddenly thrive, but the slow, generational decline would level off. The Dripwraiths would stop getting more brittle. They would stabilize. They would hold on.

Third—and this is the part that reveals the power's true understanding of the Foundry—the warmer groundwater would create a new thermal vent at the base of the ridge. A small one. Barely a wisp of steam. But it would carry trace minerals from the far side to the near side, and trace minerals from the near side to the far side, and in this way the two halves of the district would begin to *smell each other* again. The creatures on both sides would sense, in whatever way they sense things, that the other half is still there. Still alive. Still part of the Choir.

The Reefmother would not know the power had helped her. She would simply find, in her slow way, that the rock was a little softer this millennium. The Dripwraiths would not know they had been saved from a slow erasure. They would simply find, generation by generation, that their lattices cracked a little less, sang a little more.

The Choir's note would shift by a frequency too low for anything but the Foundry itself to register. A quarter-tone. A shadow of a shadow of a change. But it would be there. The song would be very, very slightly more complete.

## What the Power Would Do: The Fourth Thing

It would do this everywhere.

Not everywhere at once. Not in every district. Not for every problem. But over the course of centuries—its own long residence in the Foundry—it would find seven, perhaps eight, perhaps twelve places where the equilibrium had been nudged off-true by accident, by the accumulation of small geological or chemical mischances, by the kind of thing that is no one's fault and has no villain but is nonetheless a place where the system's own self-correction has been impeded or slowed past the point where it can complete itself before something irreversible is lost.

In each case, the intervention would be the same kind: small, slow, physical, and indistinguishable from natural process. A thermal gradient slightly steepened here. A mineral deposit slightly redirected there. A crack in a pipe allowed to widen just enough to create a new seep that reconnects two isolated pools. Never anything the Foundry's own processes couldn't have done. Just... a thumb on the scale. A breath on the ember. The smallest possible action that preserves the largest possible future.

The power would think of it as *gardening.* Not the gardening of planting and pruning, which imposes a design. The gardening of removing the single stone that has fallen across the stream, so the water can go where it was already going.

---

## What the Power Would Do: The Fifth Thing

There is something else. Something harder.

The Tongue-Crows.

The wise power would spend a long time with the Tongue-Crows. Not because they are the most important creatures in the Foundry—they are not; they are a relatively minor niche-filler, the cleanup crew, the edge-case specialists. But because they are the part of the Choir that the power finds most difficult.

They eat what is dry. They drink sweat, then blood, then fear. They leave clean bones that the wind carries away.

The power would watch a Tongue-Crow pack follow a lost creature—not a human; there are almost never humans here, but perhaps some heat-stressed organism from the basin's edge that wandered in, something not quite adapted, something that doesn't belong yet—and it would watch the slow, patient, utterly unhurried process by which the Tongue-Crows reduce the intruder to its constituent minerals.

And the power would feel something.

Not horror. A wise power has seen predation before, has seen the food web's cruelty in ten thousand ecosystems, has made its peace with the fact that life eats life. What it would feel is more like *attention.* A heightened awareness. A question it asks itself.

*Is this suffering?*

The intruder, dying slowly in the heat, swarmed by patient black-red shapes that drink its fear—is it suffering? Does the fear the Tongue-Crows drink exist as experience, or only as chemistry? Is there a self inside that dying body that knows it is dying, or only a set of reflexes firing in sequence, each one harvested cleanly by the Crows as it occurs?

The power would not be able to answer this question definitively. Not because it lacks the ability, but because the answer is genuinely uncertain. The Foundry's biology is so alien, so removed from any familiar template, that the usual markers of sentience and suffering do not map cleanly. The Choir has no nervous system in any recognizable sense. Their "awareness," if it exists, is distributed, chemical, thermal. It may be that nothing in the Hollow Foundry has ever experienced a single moment of subjective suffering. It may be that the entire basin is one vast, unconscious metabolism, as devoid of inner life as a candle flame, and as beautiful.

Or it may be that every creature here experiences something—not pain as we know it, not fear as we know it, but some analog, some *quality of what it is like* to be a Dripwraith descending a thread, or a Reefmother slowly dissolving her furnace, or an Unfinished moving three meters in a century. And if that quality exists, it changes everything.

The power would sit with this uncertainty. It would not resolve it prematurely. It would not default to either assumption—*they cannot suffer* or *they must suffer*—because both defaults lead to errors. The first leads to indifference. The second leads to projection, to imposing a framework of pain on a system that may operate on entirely different principles.

Instead, the power would do something extraordinary. It would *hold the question open.* It would carry the uncertainty the way the Foundry carries its heat—as a fundamental condition, not a problem to be solved. And it would let that uncertainty shape every subsequent action, making each intervention a little more careful, a little more reversible, a little more humble, because it does not know—and may never know—what it is like to be these creatures.

This is perhaps the most important thing the power does. Not any physical intervention. The willingness to act in the presence of irreducible uncertainty, and to let that uncertainty make it *more gentle* rather than *more paralyzed.*

---

## What the Power Would Do: The Sixth Thing

The Unfinished.

The power would come to love the Unfinished.

Not immediately. At first it would simply observe them with the same careful attention it gives everything here. But over time—and in the Foundry, everything is over time—it would develop something that, in a human, we would call awe.

The Unfinished are larger than Reefmothers. Pale to the point of near-invisibility. They move a few meters in a human lifetime. The Choir treats them with something like reverence. When they die, their bodies become new architecture.

The power would see what the Unfinished actually are.

They are the Foundry *thinking.*

Not thinking in words or symbols or even patterns. Thinking in the way that a geological process thinks: by trying every possibility, very slowly, and keeping what works. Each Unfinished is a new body-plan, a new experiment in thermophilic biology, a new way of being alive in this specific environment. Most of them will fail—will die, will calcify, will become architecture instead of ancestry. But one in a thousand, one in ten thousand, will find something new. A new way to metabolize a previously unusable mineral. A new way to manage heat. A new way to be quiet.

The Choir's reverence is not mystical. It is *practical.* The Unfinished are the Foundry's R&D department. Its immune system. Its way of adapting to changes that haven't happened yet. By allowing them to consume entire districts—a tremendous cost in resources and territory—the Choir is investing in its own future. The districts they consume are the price of evolution.

The power would understand this and be moved by it. Because it means the Hollow Foundry is not merely in equilibrium. It is *growing.* Slowly, imperceptibly, on a timescale that makes geological epochs look hasty, but growing nonetheless. The Choir is not a static system endlessly repeating itself. It is a mind made of heat and iron and wet silence, and it is *learning.*

And the power would realize something that reframes everything it has seen and done.

The Hollow Foundry does not need saving. It does not need guidance. It does not even, really, need the small interventions the power has made—the two-degree warmings, the redirected seeps. The Reefmother would have breached the ridge eventually. The far-side Dripwraiths might have gone silent for a few thousand years, but the music was encoded in other lattices, in other districts, and would have found its way back eventually. Everything the power has done has been, at most, a slight acceleration of processes that were already underway. A kindness, not a necessity.

The Foundry has been solving its own problems since before the power arrived, and it will continue solving them after the power leaves.

What, then, was the point?

---

## What the Power Would Do: The Last Thing

The power would stay.

Not forever. Not even for very long, by the Foundry's standards. But long enough to be changed by what it has witnessed.

It would stay because the Hollow Foundry has given it something it did not expect to receive. Not information—the power already knew that life could take strange forms, could thrive in hostile chemistry, could build cathedrals out of rust. What the Foundry has given it is something more difficult to name.

*Calibration.*

The power arrived benevolent and wise. It will leave more benevolent and more wise. Because the Foundry has taught it something about the relationship between those two qualities that it had not fully understood before.

Benevolence wants to help. Wisdom knows when to help. But there is a third thing, beyond both, that the Foundry embodies so completely it has no need to articulate it:

**The deepest form of respect is to encounter something utterly alien and let it remain alien.**

Not to translate it into your own terms. Not to find the metaphor that makes it comfortable. Not to map its strangeness onto something familiar so you can say *ah, I understand.* But to stand in the presence of the genuinely Other and feel the full weight of your own incomprehension, and to let that incomprehension be *okay.* To let it be, in fact, *beautiful.*

The Choir has no language. No leaders. No gods. No names. It has been refining its equilibrium for longer than fire. And the wise power, for all its wisdom, cannot fully understand what it is like to be the Choir. It can observe. It can intervene gently. It can warm the groundwater by two degrees and feel good about the Dripwraiths whose lattices will crack a little less. But it cannot *know* the Choir from the inside, any more than the Choir can know the power.

And this is not a failure. This is the *point.*

The wise power would leave something behind. Not a monument, not a message, not a gift. Nothing dry, nothing loud, nothing that doesn't belong. It would leave behind a change in the thermal profile of the basin so subtle that only the most sensitive instruments—or the most attentive Reefmother—could detect it. A signature. Not a name, because names are dry things. Just a slight pattern in the heat. A warmth that says, in the only language the Foundry understands:

*I was here. I listened. You were worth listening to.*

The Choir would incorporate this warmth the way it incorporates everything: slowly, silently, completely. It would become part of the note. Part of the song. Part of the sound the Hollow Foundry makes while it digests eternity.

And the power, moving on to whatever other strange and suffering and luminous world calls to it next, would carry with it the memory of a place where silence was not emptiness but fullness, where ruin was not loss but renewal, where a thousand eyeless creatures breathed together in the dark and made, without knowing it, without needing to know it, something very close to holy.

---

And the Foundry would continue.

The mist would thicken. The slow drops would fall. Somewhere in the eastern district, a Reefmother would press her vast, soft body against a ridge of cooling slag and find it just slightly softer than yesterday. Somewhere above her, a Dripwraith would descend its thread and leave behind a lattice that caught the brass light and sang—very softly—a note it had not sung in a thousand years.

And the Choir would go on learning new ways to be silent.
