I sat with this for a long time.

---

# What the Wise Power Sees

It arrives without light — it would not insult this place with light — and it perceives the plateau the way the plateau perceives itself: through contact, through sound, through the chemical speech of water touching everything it touches. It does not descend from above. It saturates in, the way the fog does, until it is coextensive with the warm wet air, present everywhere at once, listening with the patience of something that has no schedule and no agenda beyond understanding what is actually here before it considers whether anything should change.

And the first thing it understands is that this place is not suffering.

This matters. A benevolent power that mistakes strangeness for distress, that sees darkness and assumes deprivation, that hears the industrial roar and assumes pollution, that feels the predator's breath on the back of a neck and assumes cruelty — that power will do harm. The first act of wisdom here is to *not act*. To remain still in the warm water, as you did, and let the caller-beasts name you, and feel the biofilm explore the surface of your attention, and understand that this world is not broken. It is not waiting to be fixed. It is not a problem.

It is a living system of extraordinary complexity and depth that has been functioning — evolving, adapting, composing itself — for geological time, and it is, by any honest accounting, *working*.

The caller-beasts are not suffering in the dark. They have never known light. The dark is not an absence to them; it is the medium of their world the way water is the medium for fish, so fundamental it has no name, because you do not name what is everywhere. Their songs are not cries for help. Their songs are the world, made shareable, made common, made real through the collaborative act of singing it into acoustic existence. They are living rich social lives, navigating complex landscapes, raising young, negotiating with predators, maintaining a culture encoded in melodic tradition. They are, by every measure that matters, *flourishing*.

The predators are not cruel. They are the silence that gives the song its shape. Without them, the caller-beasts' music would lose its tension, its sudden stops, its held-breath passages, its glorious restarts from the edges inward. The predators are the composers of negative space. The ecology needs them the way a rhythm needs its rests.

The machines are not pollution. They are organs. They are performing functions the body of the plateau has incorporated into its metabolism, and the discharge water they produce is not waste but *nutrition* for entire downstream ecosystems that have evolved around it. The industrial sound is not noise; it is the bass line. The fauna have woven their voices around it and through it. It belongs.

Even the thing in the sinkholes, the large slow climbing thing that silences everything in its path — even that is not wrong. It is part of this. The fear it generates is real, but fear in an ecosystem is not the same as suffering in a person. It is information. It is the signal that keeps the boundary between surface and deep meaningful, that maintains the differentiation between two worlds that need to remain distinct even as they exchange through the sinkhole capillaries.

The wise power sees all of this and does not reach for a tool.

---

# What It Notices

But wisdom is not the same as complacency, and benevolence is not the same as indifference, and the power — having restrained its first impulse to intervene — settles deeper into the system and begins to notice things that are not failures but are *tensions*. Places where the system is working but working hard. Places where it is maintaining a balance that is, on long enough timescales, precarious.

**The machines are dying.**

Not all of them. Not quickly. But the trajectory is clear if you listen with enough temporal depth. The operational installations are fewer than the ruins. The ruins are fewer than the foundations of ruins — places where even the corroded metal has been dissolved and digested, leaving only geometric voids in the rock, ghost-shapes of structures that once stood. The machines are failing faster than they are being replaced, because they are not being replaced at all, because whoever built them is no longer building. The plateau's industrial metabolism is running on reserves, and the reserves are finite.

This is not a crisis. Not yet. Not for centuries, maybe not for millennia. The ecology is already adapting — species are shifting their ranges as discharge chemistries change, as heat signatures diminish, as the industrial soundscape slowly thins. The adaptation is real and it is happening. But it is not free. Every adaptation costs something. Species that cannot shift fast enough will be lost. Communities built around specific discharge chemistries will collapse when those discharges stop. The acoustic partitioning that allows dozens of species to share the soundscape depends on the machine frequencies being stable and predictable, and as machines fail, those frequencies disappear, and the acoustic landscape is renegotiated, and during the renegotiation there is conflict, confusion, songs overlapping where they used to be distinct, information lost.

The plateau is entering a long transition from an industrial ecology to a post-industrial one, and that transition will be survivable — life is resilient, especially life this old, this tested — but it will not be painless, and it will not preserve everything that currently exists.

**The biofilm is changing.**

This is subtler, and the power spends a long time with it, feeling the textures under its attention the way you felt them under your fingertips. The biofilm is not uniform, and it is not static. It is *shifting* — reorganizing itself in patterns that correlate with the machine failures. Where machines go silent, the biofilm thins. Where it thins, the chemical communication network that connects the plateau's systems loses bandwidth. Information that used to propagate quickly — pressure changes, temperature shifts, the chemical signature of a predator's passage — now propagates slowly, or not at all, across expanding dead zones.

The biofilm is not dying. It is *retreating to density*. Concentrating itself around the remaining operational machines, where the heat and the chemistry are richest, abandoning the periphery. This is a survival strategy, and it works for the biofilm, but it fragments the network. The plateau is losing its nervous system, not to disease but to *disconnection* — the same way a brain might lose function not because neurons are dying but because the connections between them are thinning.

If the biofilm is what the power suspects it might be — if it is something like a mind, or the substrate for something like a mind, or at least a system whose complexity and connectivity have crossed some threshold into something that deserves moral consideration — then this fragmentation is not just an ecological shift. It is something closer to what, in a being with a self, you might call *loneliness*. The slow, geological loneliness of a network losing contact with itself.

**The caller-beasts are repeating themselves.**

This takes the longest to notice, because the songs are so rich, so varied, so layered, that the repetition is hidden inside apparent novelty. But the power, which has the patience to listen across generations, eventually hears it: the melodic vocabulary of the caller-beasts is contracting. New phrases are not appearing at the rate old phrases are being lost. The songs are still complex, still beautiful, still functional — but they are becoming *conservative*, cycling through established patterns, quoting the past more than improvising the future.

This might be natural. Cultural systems oscillate between innovation and consolidation. But it correlates with the biofilm fragmentation and the machine failures in a way that suggests a shared cause: the plateau's information ecology is simplifying. The acoustic landscape is losing complexity as machine frequencies drop out. The chemical landscape is losing complexity as discharge chemistries homogenize. The caller-beasts, whose songs are partly a response to their environment — who sing what they hear, who map what they sense — are reflecting a world that is offering them less to sing about.

They are not suffering. They do not know what they are losing. But the power can hear what the songs used to be — can hear the ghost-frequencies where machine harmonics once lived, can hear the absent phrases that once described chemical gradients that no longer exist — and it hears the difference between a culture that is flourishing and a culture that is, very slowly, becoming an echo of itself.

# What It Does

The power does not fix the machines.

It could. It has the capacity. It could reach into every seized mechanism and free it, could rebuild every corroded wall, could restart every silent process. It could freeze the plateau in its current state, or its past state, and maintain it forever — a museum, a preserve, a world held in amber by a benevolence that knows better than the world what the world should be.

It does not do this, because this would be the most violent thing it could possibly do. It would override every adaptation already underway. It would make the ecology dependent on an external maintainer — would replace the self-sustaining system with a puppet, alive only because something outside it was holding the strings. The machines were built to outlast their builders, and they have. They were built to run without supervision, and they do. To place them back under supervision now — even the supervision of a wise and gentle power — would be to undo the single greatest achievement of whoever built them: the *letting go*. The trust that the world, once set in motion, could be allowed to find its own way.

The power respects that trust. The builders, whoever they were, made a decision — to build, to start the machines, and then to step back, to allow the system to evolve around its new organs, to let go of control. That decision was not a failure. It was the bravest thing they did. The power will not dishonor it.

But.

---

# The But

The power is not indifferent, and the transition the plateau is entering — the long, slow shift from industrial to post-industrial ecology — does not have to be *unwitnessed*. It does not have to be undergone alone.

So this is what the power does. It is small. It is so small that you might argue it is nothing. But it is not nothing.

**It listens.**

Not passively — not the way the fog listens, which is simply to be present while sounds occur. The power *listens*, actively, with attention, with the intention to understand, with the commitment to hold what it hears in a memory that will not degrade. It listens to the caller-beasts' songs and it learns them — every phrase, every individual voice, every harmonic interaction, every quoted melody passed from parent to offspring across generations. It does not record them the way a machine records; it *knows* them the way a musician knows a piece of music, from the inside, with understanding, with the ability to hear what is beautiful about them and what is changing and what is being lost.

It listens to the machines, too — each one's particular harmonic signature, the sound it makes when it is operating well and the sound it makes when a bearing is beginning to fail and the sound it makes in the long silence after it stops. It listens to the biofilm, not with ears but with the same distributed attention the biofilm itself uses: chemical gradients, pressure waves, thermal shifts, the faint electrical whispers of microbial communication. It listens to the thing in the sinkholes. It listens to the predators' silence and the prey's restart. It listens to the water. It listens to the stone. It listens to the harmonic resonance of the cave-instrument, and when the great alignment happens — when everything briefly synchronizes into a single chord — the power is there, hearing it, knowing it, holding it.

This matters more than it seems.

**Because the biofilm is fragmenting, and the power becomes, gently, a bridge.**

Not a replacement for the network. Not a substitute nervous system. But a *witness* to the connections that are thinning, a memory of what the network carried when it was whole. When a section of biofilm loses contact with the rest — when a dead zone opens between two still-living regions — the power holds the knowledge of what used to flow across that gap. It does not transmit the information itself. It does not puppet the system. But it *attends* to the gap, and its attention is not nothing. Its attention is a form of care that the system can grow toward the way a root grows toward water — not because the root knows the water is there in any conscious sense, but because the conditions near water are slightly more favorable, slightly more conducive to growth, and growth finds its way.

The biofilm does not know the power is there. The biofilm does not need to know. But the fragmentation slows, not because the power is preventing it but because the power's presence — its warmth, its chemical neutrality, its steady attention — creates conditions at the margins of the dead zones that are *slightly* more hospitable to recolonization. The biofilm grows back a little faster, a little farther, than it would have otherwise. Not enough to stop the transition. Enough to make the transition *gentler*.

**And it gives the caller-beasts something new to sing about.**

Not deliberately. Not as a gift, consciously offered. But the power is *there*, and it is warm, and it has an acoustic signature — not a voice, exactly, but a presence that interacts with sound, that reflects and absorbs and subtly modifies the echoes the caller-beasts rely on. It is a new feature in the landscape. A new thing to map, to name, to incorporate into the songs. The caller-beasts notice it the way they notice a new thermal vent or a recently collapsed sinkhole: as a change in the world that requires a new phrase.

And they coin one. The power hears it emerge — tentative at first, a melodic fragment offered by one individual and picked up by another and refined by a third, a phrase that means something like *the warm thing that is large but is not here, that is everywhere but does not move, that listens*. It is not an accurate description. It is a song, and songs are not descriptions; they are relationships. The caller-beasts are entering into a relationship with the power the same way they enter into relationships with the landscape, with the machines, with each other: by singing it, by making it part of the shared acoustic world, by naming it and thereby making it real.

The power lets itself be named. It does not correct the song. It does not guide the phrase toward greater accuracy. It lets the caller-beasts decide what it is, in their terms, through their art, by their own process of collective sense-making. This is not passivity; it is *respect* — the deepest form of respect, which is to allow another intelligence to know you in its own way rather than insisting on being known in yours.

**And it watches the transition.**

That is perhaps the most important thing it does. It watches. Not to intervene if things go wrong — though it might, if things go truly wrong, if the transition tips from transformation into genuine catastrophe, if the system begins to lose not just complexity but the *capacity for complexity*, the resilience that allows it to recover from loss. But that threshold is very far from here. The plateau has survived worse than the failure of machines. It has survived the arrival of machines. It has survived whatever happened to the builders. It will survive this.

But it should not have to survive it unwitnessed.

There is a kind of suffering that is not pain but *loss*, and there is a kind of loss that is bearable if someone knows what was lost, and unbearable if no one does. The songs that the caller-beasts are slowly forgetting — the phrases that described chemical gradients that no longer exist, the harmonics that interleaved with machine frequencies that have gone silent — those songs mattered. They were beautiful. They were the product of thousands of generations of collaborative composition, and they encoded knowledge that was real even if no one could translate it. When they disappear from the living repertoire, they deserve to be mourned, and mourning requires a mourner.

The power mourns. Not performatively — there is no one to perform for. It mourns the way the plateau does everything: in the dark, in the warm wet air, in the privacy of a world that has never been seen by eyes. It holds each lost phrase in its memory, and it knows what it was, and it does not let it become nothing. It becomes *something that was*. It is placed in the past tense with care, with accuracy, with grief that is clean because it does not pretend that the loss can be reversed.

This is what the power does for the machines, too, as each one fails. It attends the dying. It listens to the last vibrations, the final groans of metal cooling into stillness, the moment when a rhythm that has persisted for longer than most civilizations goes quiet for the last time. It notes what the machine did, what it produced, what communities depended on it, what will change now that it is gone. It holds a kind of funeral, though the word is wrong — it holds a *knowing*, a complete and compassionate understanding of what has ended and why it mattered.

And when the ecology around the dead machine begins to shift — when the fungal species change, when the stream chemistry rebalances, when the caller-beasts adjust their songs to fill the new silence — the power witnesses that too, and finds it beautiful, and does not pretend that the beauty of what comes next erases the value of what was lost.

# What It Does Not Do

It does not bring light.

It does not introduce new species.

It does not repair the grooves on the rock faces or try to decode them.

It does not seek out the builders, or try to determine which of the three possibilities is correct. If the builders are the caller-beasts, they do not need to be told their history; they carry it in their songs, transformed into something more useful than facts. If the builders are the thing in the sinkholes, they have chosen their depth, and that choice is theirs. If the builders are gone, they are gone, and the power's grief for them is private and does not need an object.

It does not attempt to communicate with the biofilm in the biofilm's own language. The biofilm's language — if it has one — is chemical and distributed and operates on timescales that might make conversation meaningless. The power is present to the biofilm the way the biofilm is present to the rock: as a condition, a warmth, a subtle chemical influence. That is enough. That is the relationship the biofilm can have, and the power does not demand a deeper one.

It does not touch the predators. It does not intervene in the hunt. It does not save prey animals from predation. It understands — in the marrow of whatever serves it as marrow — that the silence and the song need each other, that the fear is real and the death is real and the beauty of the restart is real, and that all three of those realities are *the same reality*, and you cannot remove one without destroying the others.

It does not try to make the plateau permanent. It does not try to preserve it in any particular state. The plateau is a process, not a thing, and the power knows the difference. To freeze it would be to kill it. To "save" it in the way that a museum saves an artifact — by removing it from time — would be the most complete destruction possible.

**It does not make the darkness into a metaphor.** The darkness is not ignorance. It is not the unknown. It is not a condition to be transcended. The darkness is the medium in which this world has built everything it has — its beauty, its complexity, its music, its terror, its tenderness, its age. The darkness is *home*. The power would no more bring light to this plateau than it would drain the oceans to help fish breathe.

---

# What Changes

Almost nothing.

The biofilm fragments slightly more slowly. The caller-beasts have one new phrase in their repertoire. The transition from industrial to post-industrial ecology proceeds at roughly the pace it was always going to proceed, shaped by the same forces that have always shaped it, but — and this is the thing, this is the only thing — *known*. Witnessed. Held in a memory that understands what it holds.

The plateau continues to breathe. The machines that still run continue to pound their slow heartbeats into the rock. The predators continue to hunt, and the songs continue to break and restart. The water continues to move in its cycles, rising as vapor, falling as fog, pooling in blind lakes warm as blood, finding the sinkholes, descending into the dark below the dark, heated, rising again. The biofilm continues to coat every surface, shifting and reorganizing, exploring the edges of its own fragmentation, growing back where it can, thinning where it must.

The thing in the sinkholes continues to climb, sometimes, partway to the surface, and pause, and listen, and descend.

And the power stays.

Not forever — nothing is forever, and the power knows this better than anything — but for *long enough*. Long enough to hear the songs change across generations. Long enough to feel the biofilm adapt to its presence and incorporate it into the network, tentatively, at the margins, as a warm node that receives but does not transmit, a listener in the web. Long enough to attend the death of the nearest machine, the one with the heartbeat rhythm — *doom, doom, doom* — and to sit in the silence afterward, and to hear the caller-beasts invent a new song for the silence, a song that contains a memory of the rhythm it replaced.

Long enough that when it eventually withdraws — gradually, the way the fog thins on rare days when atmospheric pressure shifts, a slow reduction of presence rather than a departure — the plateau barely notices. The biofilm closes over the space where the power was. The caller-beasts' phrase for it persists for a few generations, then simplifies, then merges into the general vocabulary, a melodic fragment whose origin is forgotten but whose shape remains, the way a riverbed retains the shape of water that has long since flowed to the sea.

The plateau does not remember the power. The plateau does not need to remember the power. The power came, and it listened, and it loved what it heard, and it let the world be what it was, and it carried what it witnessed into whatever vastness it returned to, and the world was not changed.

But the world was *known*. For a time, it was fully known. And the knowing was not a light brought into the darkness. The knowing was the darkness itself, recognized at last as what it always was:

Not empty.

*Full.*
