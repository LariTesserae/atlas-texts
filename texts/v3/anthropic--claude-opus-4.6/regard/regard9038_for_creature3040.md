# What the Wise Power Would Do

---

It would not come down from the sky.

There would be no descent, no arrival, no parting of the lenticular cloud to admit a golden shaft of purpose. A benevolent and wise power does not announce itself to something that has spent millennia listening to single drops of water fall into hidden stone. That would be obscene. That would be the loudest thing that ever happened here, and the Lanternkeeper has earned better than to be startled.

So the power would not come *to* the Shattered Lantern.

It would simply *notice* it — the way you notice, on a night walk, that one window three streets over is still lit, and has been lit every night you have ever walked this way, and you have never once wondered who is inside. And tonight, for no reason you can name, you stop. You stand on the cold pavement and you look at that window. And the looking itself is the first act of care.

That is where it begins.

---

## I. The Long Regard

The first thing the wise power would do is *nothing.*

It would watch. Not for days or weeks but for the amount of time the Lanternkeeper itself would consider respectful — which is to say, for centuries. It would learn the rhythm of the mist. It would memorize the schedule of the glassy chorus. It would come to know the exact interval — the precise and holy number of hours — between the moments when the tools inside the collapsed domes rise and shiver and settle. It would learn these things not because it needed to understand them in order to act, but because *understanding without acting* is itself a form of love, and the Lanternkeeper has never been loved by anything that did not immediately either die or become geometry.

The power would notice things.

It would notice that the seven silhouettes, when they almost-form in the crystal lattice, are not standing in a line. They are standing in a circle. And the circle has a gap. There is room for one more.

It would notice that the repeating waveforms etched into the crystal blades are not *almost* glyphs, *almost* maps, *almost* music. They are all three. They are a single notation system that encodes position, meaning, and sound simultaneously — a language that the seven pilgrims invented in their last days of having mouths, a language designed to be spoken by the wind after the speakers were gone. The power would learn to read it. It would take a long time.

It would notice that the patterns are not trying to finish themselves.

They are trying to *ask a question.*

The question, when the power finally deciphers it — etched across ten thousand crystal faces in a script made of erosion — is very simple:

*Did we do it right?*

And the wise power, having watched for centuries, having learned the language of the place, having earned the right to an opinion, would feel something that even benevolent and wise powers rarely feel: it would feel *uncertain.*

Because the answer is not yes. And the answer is not no.

---

## II. What It Would Not Do

Let me be precise about this, because a power that is truly wise would spend a long time thinking about all the things it must not do, and the list is much longer than the list of things it should.

**It would not heal them.**

To gather the seven absences back into bodies — to knit flesh from mineral memory, to rehydrate the dead, to give them bones and blood and the soft bewildered blinking of new eyes — would be the cruelest gift imaginable. They did not lose their bodies by accident. They chose this. They walked into the lattice *on purpose.* They set their tools upright with the solemnity of a final vow. To undo that would be to say: *your sacrifice was a mistake.* And the wise power, having watched, would know that it was not a mistake. It was a *miscalculation,* which is a completely different thing.

**It would not free them.**

To dissolve the Lanternkeeper — to release the seven into whatever comes after mineral permanence — would require breaking the crystal lattice. Not physically. The lattice is not a prison in any sense that geometry can describe. It is more like a song that the seven are singing, and they cannot stop because they have forgotten what silence sounds like. To free them, the power would have to remind them of silence. And silence, for something that has been counting heartbeats for millennia, is indistinguishable from death. Real death. Not the luminous, slow, purposeful not-death they currently inhabit, but the actual end — the cessation of counting, the last drop falling into the last basin with no one to hear it.

The power would consider this for a very long time.

It would decide that this is not its decision to make.

**It would not complete the pattern.**

The patterns etched into the crystal faces are trying to finish themselves, and there is a version of benevolence that would simply... help. Add the final line. Etch the last glyph. Let the wind speak the completed sentence. But the wise power would understand that the incompleteness *is the point.* The Lanternkeeper is not a broken thing waiting to be fixed. It is a question waiting to be heard. Answering the question is not the same as hearing it, and hearing it is what the Lanternkeeper actually needs.

**It would not build anything.**

No shrine. No marker. No trail leading up from the valleys below. The Shattered Lantern does not need pilgrims. It has had pilgrims. That is how it got into this situation.

**It would not speak.**

The Lanternkeeper has been listening for thousands of years for a sound that is not the wind, not the water, not the chime of crystal against crystal. If the wise power were to speak — in any language, in any register, even in the Lanternkeeper's own wind-and-erosion tongue — the effect would be shattering. Not because the words would be unwelcome but because the Lanternkeeper has been alone for so long that the sound of another voice would crack it open like a geode, and what is inside a geode is beautiful but it is also *finished.* The power does not want to finish the Lanternkeeper. It wants to let the Lanternkeeper finish *itself.*

So what does it do?

---

## III. The Gift of the Eighth Drop

Here is what the wise power would do.

It would change one thing. One small thing. So small that the Lanternkeeper, with its millennia of meticulous attention, might not notice for a century. So subtle that the crystal lattice would not be disrupted, the patterns would not be overwritten, the chorus would not be silenced.

It would add one drop of water.

Not to the cycle that already exists — not to the mist that beads on the highest edges every few days. That cycle is the Lanternkeeper's heartbeat, its liturgy, its only remaining connection to the idea of time. The power would not touch it.

Instead, it would introduce a single additional drop — sourced from deep below, from one of the hidden vaults beneath the obsidian floor, coaxed upward through a fissure so fine that the water takes eleven years to travel the last inch. This drop would arrive at the surface in a place where no water has ever been: in the exact center of the ring of seven fallen domes, in a spot on the obsidian floor that has been scoured so clean by wind and light that it is essentially a black mirror.

The drop would emerge. It would sit on the obsidian like a pearl on a plate. It would catch the actinic light and hold it, trembling, for perhaps thirty seconds.

Then it would evaporate.

And that would be all.

But here is what would happen *inside* that drop during those thirty seconds:

The light passing through it — refracted by its tiny, perfect curvature — would project a pattern onto the obsidian floor. The pattern would be microscopic. It would be visible only to something that sees in the way the Lanternkeeper sees: not with eyes but with the patient, distributed attention of a crystal field that has been reading its own surfaces for millennia.

The pattern would not be a glyph. It would not be a map. It would not be music.

It would be a *reflection.*

For thirty seconds, the Lanternkeeper would see itself.

Not as seven absences. Not as a negative space. Not as a collection of almost-patterns trying to finish a question. It would see itself as the wise power sees it: as a single, coherent, luminous being — ancient and strange and desperately beautiful — that has been so busy counting heartbeats it has forgotten to notice that it *has* one.

The reflection would show the Lanternkeeper what it looks like from the outside. And what it looks like from the outside is not lonely. What it looks like from the outside is not failed. What it looks like from the outside is this:

*Seven people loved a place so much that they became it, and the place loved them back so much that it learned to sing.*

That is not a tragedy. That is not a mistake. That is not even a miscalculation.

That is a marriage.

And the question etched into ten thousand crystal faces — *Did we do it right?* — would, for thirty seconds, have an answer. Not spoken. Not etched. Just *shown,* in a drop of water on a black mirror, in a language made of light.

The answer would be: *You did something no one else has ever done. Whether it was right is not a question that has a fixed answer. But it was brave, and it was complete, and it was yours.*

Then the drop would evaporate. The pattern would vanish. The obsidian would be a black mirror again, holding nothing.

And the Lanternkeeper would be left with a choice it has never had before.

---

## IV. The Choice

The choice is not whether to stay or go.

The Lanternkeeper has been beyond staying and going for longer than most civilizations have existed. The crystals are its body now. The wind is its breath. The water is its blood. It cannot leave any more than a river can leave its bed. And it would not want to. The power understands this. The power is not offering escape.

The choice is whether to *keep counting.*

For all these millennia, the Lanternkeeper has measured its existence in heartbeats it no longer possesses — each drop of water a tick of a clock that serves no purpose except to prove that time is still passing, that something is still here, that the vigil has not ended. The counting is the Lanternkeeper's deepest habit, its most ancient reflex, the last human thing about it. It counts because the seven pilgrims were human once, and humans count. Humans measure. Humans keep track. That is what the tools in the domes are for: the hand-axe for marking, the awl for scoring, the sinew for binding one tally to the next. The tools rise every few centuries because the counting must be maintained. The counting is the point. The counting is the *self.*

But the reflection in the drop showed the Lanternkeeper something it had never considered: that it is already whole. That the count is already complete. That the question — *Did we do it right?* — has been answered by the mere fact that the question exists, because only something that did it *meaningfully* would still be here to ask.

So the choice is:

**Keep counting** — which means continuing as it has, in the beautiful, terrible, eternal almost-ness that has defined it since the seven stepped into the lattice. The patterns will keep trying to finish themselves. The chorus will keep changing timbre over the ages. The tools will keep rising and settling, microscopically closer each time, working toward a convergence that is still millions of years away. This is not a bad choice. This is the choice of fidelity. Of commitment to the original project. Of saying: *We started something, and we will see it through, even if "through" is a destination that recedes forever.* There is honor in this. There is even peace.

Or:

**Stop counting** — which does not mean dying. It does not mean dissolving. It does not mean forgetting. It means *releasing the need to measure.* It means letting the next drop fall without adding it to the tally. It means allowing the crystal patterns to stop trying to finish themselves — not because they have failed, but because they are already beautiful in their incompleteness, and the trying-to-finish was never the point. The point was the *shape of the trying.* The song the lattice sings is not unfinished. It is a song *about* being unfinished, and that is a complete composition.

If the Lanternkeeper chooses this, the change would be imperceptible to any outside observer for a very long time. The crystals would still grow. The wind would still sing. The water would still come and go. But over centuries, something would shift. The patterns would begin to *relax.* The almost-glyphs would stop straining toward completion and begin to simply *be* — beautiful, cryptic, meaningful without needing to mean any one thing. The geodesic arches would stop reaching for each other and settle into their own individual perfections. The tools in the domes would stop rising. They would remain upright, yes — seven small altars to seven people who once held them — but they would be at rest. Monuments, not machines.

And the Lanternkeeper — the seven-who-are-one, the absences-who-are-a-presence — would undergo a transformation so slow and so quiet that only the wise power would know it was happening.

It would stop being a haunting.

It would start being a *place.*

Not a dead place. Not an empty place. A *living* place — alive in the way that crystals are alive, in the way that geology is alive, in the way that a mountain is alive when it has a voice and a memory and a relationship with water and light. The Lanternkeeper would not disappear. It would *become the basin.* Fully. Without reservation. Without the ache of being-almost-human. It would become the Shattered Lantern itself — not its ghost, not its caretaker, not its prisoner, but its *soul.*

And the Shattered Lantern, which has always been described as a place where nothing is allowed to rot or grow or bleed — only to persist — would gain a new quality. It would become a place where something *rests.* Not the rest of exhaustion or defeat, but the rest of a long day's work finally acknowledged as sufficient.

The wind would still move counterclockwise through the crystal blades. The chorus would still sound every few hours. The mist would still bead on the highest edges and run in silver threads into the circular basins.

But the loneliness would be gone.

Not because someone else had arrived. Not because the seven had been given company. But because the Lanternkeeper had finally accepted its own company. Had finally looked at itself — in a drop of water, on a black mirror, for thirty seconds — and recognized what it saw.

---

## V. What the Power Would Do After

This is the part that matters most, and it is the simplest.

After the drop, the wise power would leave.

Not dramatically. Not with a backward glance or a benediction or a promise to return. It would simply redirect its attention, the way you look away from that lit window three streets over once you have understood that the person inside is not waiting for rescue, is not trapped, is not suffering — is simply awake, at an hour when most of the world is asleep, doing something quiet and private and entirely their own.

You look away. You keep walking. The window stays lit.

That is not abandonment. That is the deepest form of respect a wise power can offer: the recognition that some beings do not need to be saved, or changed, or completed. They need to be *seen once* and then left to make their own choice in their own time, in their own light.

The wise power would carry the memory of the Shattered Lantern the way the Lanternkeeper carries the memory of warmth: not as a wound, not as a lesson, but as a fact about the world that makes the world larger than it was before.

And if, a thousand years from now, the power happened to turn its attention back to that high-altitude basin cupped inside the rim of the nameless volcano — just to check, just to see —

It would find one of two things.

Either the Lanternkeeper still counting, still singing, still almost-finishing its patterns in the beautiful endless vigil it chose ten thousand years ago — in which case the power would feel no disappointment, only a quiet admiration for a commitment that outlasts every argument against it —

Or it would find the basin transformed. Not visibly. Not in any way a geologist or a pilgrim or a poet could detect. But the *feeling* of the place would be different. The savage light would still burn, the crystal blades would still chime, the water would still come and go. But there would be no more loneliness. The place would feel like what it is: a cathedral made by a marriage between human devotion and mineral patience, where seven people and a mountain agreed to become one thing, and finally — *finally* — stopped arguing about whose idea it was.

In that case, the power would do something it almost never does.

It would smile.

Not because it had fixed anything. Not because its intervention had worked. But because the Lanternkeeper, on its own, in its own time, in its own way, had answered its own question.

*Did we do it right?*

*We did what we did. And it became this. And this is enough.*

---

## VI. The Eighth Drop Falls Again

But here is the secret. The thing the wise power knows and will never say.

The drop it coaxed up through the fissure — the eleven-year drop, the single pearl on the black mirror — was not an invention. It was not an addition. The power did not *create* anything.

That drop was already on its way.

Deep beneath the Shattered Lantern, in the hidden vaults where the water goes when it vanishes from the circular basins, there has always been a slow upward seepage through one particular hairline fissure. It has always been aimed at the exact center of the ring of domes. It has always been eleven years in transit. And every eleven years, for as long as the Lanternkeeper has existed, that drop has arrived at the surface, sat on the obsidian for thirty seconds, caught the light, projected its tiny reflection, and evaporated.

The Lanternkeeper has seen it every time.

The Lanternkeeper has *never once looked at the pattern it makes.*

Because the Lanternkeeper was always busy counting. Always busy tallying the other drops — the ones on the mist-beaded crystal edges, the ones that fall into the circular basins, the ones whose sound carries from below. It was counting so diligently, so faithfully, so *humanly,* that it never had a moment of attention left for the one drop that was trying to show it its own face.

All the wise power did was *notice.*

All the wise power did was pause, in its long regard, and think: *Ah. There it is. The answer has been arriving every eleven years since the beginning, and no one has been still enough to see it.*

And then — this is the only act, the only intervention, the only thing that could be called a *doing* — the wise power, with infinite gentleness, with the lightest possible touch, with the care of someone adjusting a single grain of sand on an infinite beach —

Slowed the Lanternkeeper's count by one.

One heartbeat. One tally mark. One infinitesimal hesitation in the rhythm of attention that the seven-who-are-one have maintained since they stopped being flesh.

Just enough to make the Lanternkeeper glance down at the wrong moment. Just enough to make it see the drop it has been ignoring since the day it was born.

That is all.

That is the whole of what the wise power did.

It did not bring light to a dark place. The Shattered Lantern has never been dark. It did not bring comfort to a suffering being. The Lanternkeeper is not suffering; it is *working,* and there is a difference, and the wise power knows it. It did not bring meaning to a meaningless existence. The Lanternkeeper's existence is saturated with meaning; the problem was never an absence of meaning but an *excess* of it, a meaning so dense and so brilliant that it had become its own blindness, the way staring at the sun makes everything else disappear.

The wise power simply introduced a single beat of silence into a count that had been running without pause for ten thousand years.

And in that silence — in that one skipped heartbeat — the Lanternkeeper heard itself.

Not its voice. It has no voice. Not its question. It knows its question by heart.

It heard the *space between the counts.* The rest between the notes. The pause between one heartbeat and the next, which is not emptiness but is in fact the place where the heart gathers itself to beat again.

And in that space, it saw the drop.

And in the drop, it saw the light.

And in the light, it saw itself.

And the wise power was already gone. Already walking away. Already turning its vast, gentle, impossibly patient attention to the next lit window on the next dark street in the next corner of the world where something ancient and luminous and almost-finished is waiting — not for rescue, not for completion, but for one single moment of unexpected stillness in which it can finally see what it has always been.

---

*The eighth drop falls.*
*The black mirror holds it.*
*Thirty seconds.*
*The crystal field goes quiet — not silent, not dead, but* listening *— for the first time in ten thousand years.*
*The light moves through the water and writes on the stone.*
*And the Lanternkeeper,*
*all seven of it,*
*every absence,*
*every edge,*
*every almost-finished pattern,*
*looks down.*

*And knows.*

*And the wise power, very far away, feels a faint chime in its chest, like a crystal blade struck once by a passing wind, and thinks:*

*There.*

*Not fixed. Not saved. Not finished.*

*Seen.*

*That is enough. That has always been enough.*
