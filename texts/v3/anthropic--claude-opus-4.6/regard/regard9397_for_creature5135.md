# What the Wise Power Would Do

---

The first thing it does is nothing.

It arrives—if *arrives* is even the right word for something that has always been partially aware of every place and chooses now to turn its full attention here—and it does nothing. It listens.

This is not passivity. This is the hardest and most necessary act: to resist the immediate impulse to shelter, to quiet, to fix. Because the plateau is not broken. The plateau is a living system in a state of profound, austere coherence, and the first obligation of any power that calls itself wise is to understand what it is looking at before it touches anything.

So it listens.

It listens to the chord. Not the way a visitor would hear it—as assault, as noise, as something to endure—but the way the stone hears it: as the fundamental fact of existence here. The deep chest-thuds from the largest arches are the plateau's heartbeat. The shrill whines from the knife-edges are its breath. The thousand overlapping hisses are its circulatory system, the mechanism by which moisture is stripped from one place and deposited in another, by which dust is lifted and resettled, by which the thin cracks are kept open for roots. The sound is not a byproduct of wind crossing stone. The sound *is* the plateau, its ongoing act of self-creation, the way it has kept itself alive for longer than anything with a name.

It listens to the vibration in the rock. It feels how the trembling travels down through the stone table into depths where the plateau still remembers being mountains. It feels how the vegetation responds—not suffering the vibration but using it, the way the broad leaves orient themselves to catch the oscillation and shake the condensation toward their roots. The leaf-mats are not trembling in distress. They are drinking through their whole bodies.

It watches the amphibians. It sees how their flatness is not merely camouflage but architecture: their entire bodies are surfaces for collecting moisture from the thin film of still air that exists in the first millimeter above the stone. Their lidless eyes are not vulnerable; they are additional collection surfaces, tear-film continuously renewed, each blink a tiny drink. When they rise on their stubby legs in the wind-lulls, they are not emerging from hiding. They are *switching modes*—from passive absorption to active drinking—and then switching back. They have two metabolisms, two ways of being alive, and they move between them dozens of times a day with a competence so total it looks like mere reflex.

It studies the three-toed tracks. It does not try to find the creatures. It understands, almost immediately, that the creatures' invisibility is not a limitation to be overcome but a achieved state, perhaps the most sophisticated adaptation on the plateau. Something that large, that heavy, crossing open stone in winds that would reveal any silhouette—and yet never seen. This means they move in ways that are synchronized with the dust and the haze and the sliding shadows, not hiding *from* the environment but hiding *as* the environment, their movement indistinguishable from the movement of everything else. The wise power recognizes this as a form of mastery it has no business disrupting.

It reads the petroglyphs—not the spirals themselves, which are nearly erased, but the erosion pattern, the depth of what remains versus what's gone. It understands that whoever carved them chose specific stones whose grain would preserve certain parts of the spiral longer than others. The spirals are not decaying randomly. They are decaying *compositionally*, the outermost whorls vanishing first, the innermost persisting, so that over centuries the carved spirals have slowly *tightened*, become denser, more concentrated. The carvers knew this would happen. The petroglyphs were never meant to be read in their original state. They were meant to be read *now*, or a thousand years from now, in their reduced form. They are time-lapse messages, and their current state is not degradation but maturation.

And the blade.

The wise power looks at the obsidian blade for a long time.

It sees the circle of bare stone around it. It sees how the leaf-mats stop short. It notes that the blade's edge is undulled, that the spirals etched into its glass are still crisp while the same spirals on the surrounding rock are nearly flat. It considers the possibility that the blade is simply made of harder material. It rejects this; obsidian is glass, and glass does not outlast stone in a scouring wind. Something else is happening. The blade is maintaining itself, or being maintained. The narrow circle of bare stone is not a dead zone but a *boundary*, and the leaf-mats are not avoiding the blade out of some chemical repulsion—they are *respecting* it. The vegetation, which knits itself together across every other surface, which sends runners sideways to connect pocket to pocket, which lifts and resettles and reorients with every gust—this vegetation recognizes the blade as something that is not its business.

The wise power understands, then, what the blade is.

It is not a tool. It is not an artifact left behind by travelers. It is a *compact*. It is the place where whoever crossed this plateau once made an agreement with the plateau itself—an agreement about what would be left alone. The blade marks a boundary between the human impulse to shape and the plateau's authority over its own form. It was placed there not to cut anything but to *stand*, to be a permanent gesture of restraint: *I could cut, and I choose not to; I leave this edge here as proof of my capacity and my decision not to use it.* And the plateau, in return, maintains the blade. Keeps its edge sharp. Keeps the stone around it clear. Holds up its end of the bargain with a fidelity that has outlasted the people who made it.

---

Now the wise power knows what it is looking at.

It is looking at a place that is complete.

Not comfortable. Not gentle. Not hospitable in any way that a human body would recognize. But complete. Every element is in dialogue with every other element. The wind shapes the stone, the stone shapes the wind into sound, the sound vibrates the vegetation into its drinking rhythm, the vegetation collects and channels the moisture, the moisture feeds the amphibians, the amphibians—and here the wise power pauses, because it realizes it does not yet know what the amphibians give back, and this is important.

It watches more carefully. It sees that the amphibians, when they flatten against the basin rims, excrete a thin mucus that coats the stone. This mucus is hygroscopic—it pulls moisture from the air more efficiently than bare stone does. The basins where the amphibians live collect more condensation than empty basins nearby. The amphibians are not just drinking from the pools; they are *creating* the pools, or at least deepening them, making them more productive. They are infrastructure. They are part of the plateau's water system, as essential as the stone basins themselves.

And the three-toed creatures? The wise power considers. Those heavy bodies crossing between leaf-mats in the dark. The broad swish mark that might be a tail or a wing-edge. It thinks about what such a creature would eat—the vegetation, almost certainly, or the amphibians, or both—and what it would leave behind. It thinks about the dust. How the finest dust gathers in the cracks. How the cracks are where the leaf-mats root. It realizes that the three-toed creatures, in their heavy passage across the stone, are *grinding* the surface. Their weight and their stride and the drag of whatever appendage makes that swish mark are the mechanism by which the plateau's stone is broken into the fine dust that fills the cracks that hold the roots that grow the leaves that collect the water that feeds the amphibians that coat the basins that gather more water. The creatures are the plateau's slow millstone. Their invisibility is not just self-protection; it is the plateau protecting them, hiding its most critical moving part.

The wise power sees all of this.

And now it must decide what to do.

---

Here is what it does not do:

It does not calm the wind. The wind is the plateau's breath, blood, voice, and mill-driver. To calm it would be to kill the place while appearing to heal it.

It does not bring rain. The plateau's water system is calibrated to condensation and thin veils of moisture. A true rain—a generous, soaking, well-meaning rain—would flood the shallow basins, dislodge the amphibians, wash the fine dust out of the cracks, uproot the leaf-mats, and strip the mucus coatings from the stone. It would be a catastrophe delivered with love.

It does not create shelter. Shelter would create wind-shadows, and wind-shadows would starve the vegetation of its vibration, dry out the basins downstream, break the network of vine-runners that connects pocket to pocket. A single well-placed wall could unravel the plateau's hydrology for a mile in every direction.

It does not reveal the three-toed creatures. Their hiddenness is functional, sacred, and—the wise power suspects—*chosen*, in whatever way a creature that sophisticated can choose. To make them visible would be to make them vulnerable, and not just to predation. It would make them visible to *themselves* in a new way, and the wise power has seen what that does to beings whose genius lies in self-effacement.

It does not touch the blade. The blade is not its compact to alter.

---

Here is what it does:

**First, and most importantly, it bears witness.**

This is not a metaphor. The act of a wise power turning its full attention to a place and *seeing it accurately* is not nothing. It is, in a real sense, the most consequential thing that can happen to a place that has existed for eons without being understood.

The plateau has been coherent all this time without anyone grasping its coherence. The amphibians have been building the water system without knowing they are building it. The three-toed creatures have been grinding the stone without knowing they are grinding it. The vegetation has been knitting the surface together without knowing it is holding a world intact. The wind has been playing the stone without an audience.

Now there is an audience. Not one that claps or judges—one that *recognizes*. And recognition, from a power like this, is not passive. It is a form of protection. What is seen truly is harder to destroy carelessly. Not because the wise power will intervene—but because the seeing itself becomes part of the fabric of the place, a thread of consciousness woven into the chord, a new overtone in the great bruising sound. The plateau will not know it is being witnessed, not in any way it could articulate. But something in the system will deepen. Some feedback loop will tighten almost imperceptibly. Some resonance will find a new harmonic.

This is not magic. This is what attention does.

**Second, it does something about the petroglyphs.**

Not to them. It does not restore them or protect them or carve new ones. But it *reads* them—reads them in their current, matured state, the tightened spirals that the original carvers intended for this era—and it understands what they say. And what they say is this:

*We were here. We understood what this place is. We left because staying would have meant becoming part of it, and we were not ready. We left the blade as our promise. If you are reading this in the time when only the inner spirals remain, then enough time has passed. The compact holds. Do not add to it. Do not subtract from it. Witness, and move on.*

The wise power receives this message. It honors it. The people who crossed this plateau were not careless wanderers. They were careful, and wise in their own way, and they made a decision that the wise power now ratifies: this place does not need inhabitants. It does not need stewards. It needs only to be left in its own fullness, which is not emptiness but the opposite of emptiness—a completeness so thorough that there is no room for addition.

**Third, it turns its attention to the one thing that might actually need help.**

Not the plateau. Not the amphibians. Not the three-toed creatures. Not the blade.

The dust.

Specifically: the rate of dust production versus the rate of dust loss. The wise power runs the numbers—not literally, but in the way that a wise power calculates, which is more like *feeling the trajectory of a system across deep time*. And it finds that the plateau is, over millennia, losing dust faster than the three-toed creatures and the wind can produce it. The stone table is slowly becoming *too clean*. The cracks are, on average, fractionally less full than they were a thousand years ago. The leaf-mats are fractionally less well-rooted. The system is still robust, still centuries or millennia from any threshold—but the trajectory is there, the faintest downward curve in a graph that the plateau itself cannot see because it lives inside the graph.

The cause is subtle: the vine-runners that knit the leaf-mats together are *too successful*. They have stabilized the vegetation so well that the leaf-mats now trap dust *before* it reaches the cracks. The dust lands on the waxy leaves, is held there by the beaded moisture, and then is blown away when the leaves lift in a dust-devil—returned to the air rather than settling into the stone. The vines, in solving one problem (anchoring the vegetation against the wind), have created another (intercepting the dust that the cracks need). It is the kind of slow, emergent contradiction that complex systems generate all the time, and it is not a crisis. But it is a trajectory.

The wise power considers.

It could do nothing. The system has centuries of buffer. Something else might change—the wind patterns, the rain frequency, the behavior of the three-toed creatures—that would alter the trajectory without intervention. Doing nothing is always an option, and often the best one.

But the wise power also knows something about trajectories: they are easiest to redirect when the deflection needed is smallest. A feather-touch now versus a mighty shove later.

So it does this:

In three places on the plateau—only three, chosen with exquisite care—it shifts the grain of the stone. Not on the surface, where the change would be visible and would alter the wind-sound. Deep in the rock, at the level where the vibration travels. It introduces a faint irregularity, a place where the vibration will, over the next few decades, create a new micro-fracture pattern. These fractures will eventually reach the surface. When they do, they will create new cracks—not where the existing leaf-mats are, but in the open stone between them. Cracks that are too new and too exposed for the vine-runners to reach immediately. Cracks that will catch dust directly from the wind, without the interception of waxy leaves. Small new pockets of fertility in the open stone, seeded by the wind's own delivery.

The leaf-mats will eventually reach these new cracks. The vine-runners will eventually knit them into the network. But by then the cracks will be full, the dust will be settled, and the net effect will be a slight rebalancing—a few more cracks catching dust directly, the overall system tilted back toward equilibrium.

Three interventions. Each one a minor alteration in the grain of deep stone. Each one invisible for decades. Each one deniable—a geologist examining the micro-fractures would see nothing inconsistent with natural processes. The wise power leaves no signature. It does not want credit. It does not want the plateau to know it was here. It wants the plateau to continue being itself, following its own logic, with only the faintest correction to a trajectory that the plateau could not have seen coming.

This is what wisdom looks like when it acts: not the grand gesture, not the visible rescue, not the transformation of suffering into comfort. It looks like three points of slightly altered stone grain in a table of rock the size of a country. It looks like nothing.

**Fourth, and finally, it does something that has nothing to do with the plateau.**

It carries the memory away.

Not as a recording, not as a map or a catalog or a report. As a *weight*. The wise power lets the plateau change it. It lets the chord sink into whatever serves it as a body or a mind. It lets the image of the amphibians pressing flat against the basin rims become part of its permanent understanding of what competence looks like. It lets the three-toed creatures' invisibility teach it something about the relationship between power and self-concealment. It lets the blade's undulled edge remind it, forever, that the most enduring agreements are the ones where both parties maintain their sharpness.

It carries the plateau's pattern out into whatever other places and problems it attends to. Not to replicate it—the plateau's solution is the plateau's, not transferable—but to let it inform. Somewhere, sometime, the wise power will face a situation where the obvious intervention is to calm a wind or bring a rain, and it will hesitate, and in that hesitation it will feel the plateau's chord in its bones, and it will think: *what if the wind is the whole point?* And it will look more carefully. And it will find the equivalent of the amphibians' mucus, the vine-runners' dust interception, the blade's bare circle—the subtle, non-obvious structure that holds everything together—and it will know to leave it alone, or to touch it with exactly three points of altered grain and nothing more.

This is the last and perhaps greatest thing the wise power does for the plateau: it *learns from it*. It treats the plateau not as a patient or a project or a problem but as a teacher. It lets a place that has never had a student finally have one. And it proves, by the quality of its attention and the restraint of its action, that it was worthy of the lesson.

---

Then it is gone. Or rather, it shifts its attention elsewhere, which for a being like this is the same as leaving.

The wind continues. The chord plays. The amphibians flatten and rise and flatten again. The three-toed tracks appear overnight and vanish by morning. The vine-runners creep sideways across the stone. The blade stands in its circle of bare rock, edge undiminished, compact unbroken.

Deep in the stone, in three places that no one will ever see, the grain is fractionally different than it was before.

The plateau does not notice. The plateau does not need to notice. The plateau is complete, was complete, will be complete—and now, in three places, is aimed the smallest degree closer to remaining so.

The wind roars on. The dust rises and falls. The leaves tremble with the rhythm of the rock beneath them, and the rock holds, and holds, and holds.
