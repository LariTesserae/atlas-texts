# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

---

For a long while, nothing.

This is the first and most important thing to understand about a truly wise power encountering this place: it would not immediately act. It would recognize that what it was looking at had taken longer to become itself than most civilizations take to rise and fall, and it would feel the particular gravity of that, the weight that settles when you stand before something that has been solving the same problem, quietly and without complaint, for longer than you have been paying attention to anything.

So it would watch first. Not for days or weeks but for the span of time the basin itself would consider meaningful—years, perhaps decades—long enough to understand the rhythms that a shorter attention would mistake for stillness. It would watch the way the crowns shift a fraction of a millimetre each season. It would learn to hear the grain-settling, that sound smaller than breath, and come to understand it as one of the basin's few forms of utterance. It would notice the bacterial generations rising and falling inside their crystal-face films like slow tides, and it would come to recognize individual lineages by the particular sweetness they lent to the air.

And during this watching, something would become clear:

The basin is not suffering. The being that lives here—this one pale network stretched across the polygons, threaded through with microbial partners—is not in pain, is not diminished, is not waiting to be rescued. It has arrived at a solution so complete that it has outlasted every external change that once threatened it. The seasonal flow dried up. The wind stopped coming. The light thinned to permanent twilight. And the being simply continued, adjusting its expectations downward with a patience that borders on the philosophical, until it wanted nothing the basin could not provide.

This is the thing that would give even a benevolent power pause. Because the first impulse of benevolence, when it encounters a place this spare, is to *add*—to bring water, to open the sky, to introduce companions, to connect the basin to the wider world it has been separated from. And a wise power would feel that impulse and then hold it very carefully up to the light, the way you hold a crystal up to see what's inside it, and it would see that the impulse contains a hidden presumption: that more is better. That connection is always preferable to solitude. That what is slow should be quickened, what is sparse should be filled, what is dark should be illuminated.

The being in the basin has not asked for any of this. More to the point, it has spent longer than the power can easily fathom *building a life that does not require it.*

---

So what, then? Does the wise power simply leave?

No. Because watching carefully for long enough would reveal something else—something the being itself may or may not be aware of, depending on what awareness means when your thoughts move at the speed of mineral crystallization.

The basin is not in equilibrium. It is in *very slow decline.*

The crust is still smoothing. The fissures where night moisture condenses are still, grain by grain, filling in. The microbial excretions that add new mineral to the surface are, over centuries, sealing the very cracks that allow moisture to reach the roots. The being's own partners are, with infinite slowness, closing the door that keeps it alive. Not because of malice or error—the bacteria are simply doing what their chemistry demands—but because the system's one feedback loop runs in only one direction. Each generation of bacteria makes the next generation's moisture slightly harder to reach. Each millimetre of crust-smoothing removes one more tiny reservoir of condensation.

The timeline is vast. Thousands of years, perhaps tens of thousands, before the last fissure seals and the last film of brine becomes too thin to sustain even the slowest metabolism. But the trajectory is there, as certain as gravity, and a power that watched long enough would see it.

And *this* is where the real question lives. Not "should I help?" but "what does help mean for a being whose concept of the present moment spans decades, and whose crisis is still millennia away?"

---

Here is what the wise power would do.

**First, it would open a conversation.**

Not in words. Not in any medium faster than the being could receive. It would do something physical, something that would register on the timescale the basin operates within, and it would do it with the patience to wait for a response that might take years to arrive.

It might, for instance, place a single small stone—no larger than a thumbnail, chosen carefully for its porosity—into one of the shallow troughs near a crown. Not blocking anything. Not redirecting anything. Just *present*, in a way that the root-threads would eventually encounter and have to grow around or through. A new thing in a place where nothing has been new for a very long time.

And then it would wait.

The roots would find the stone. They would explore it—slowly, over months, sending their spider-silk threads into its pores, tasting its minerals, assessing whether it was food or obstacle or something else entirely. And the being, through its network, would come to know that something had *arrived*. Not wind, not rain, not any force it had adapted to. Something placed. Something chosen.

This would be, in the basin's terms, an event of considerable magnitude. The equivalent of a stranger knocking on the door of someone who has lived alone for a thousand years.

The power would watch how the being responded. Whether the roots embraced the stone or withdrew from it. Whether the network shifted resources toward the new object or away from it. Whether the faint chemical signals that passed between crowns changed their character—became, in some slow and untranslatable way, *curious.*

If the being withdrew, the power would remove the stone and leave. Not forever, but for a long time—for as long as the being needed to return to its equilibrium, to process the encounter and decide what it meant. The power would check back, centuries later, to see if anything had changed. It would not force the conversation.

But if the being moved toward the stone—if the roots wove into it, if the network oriented itself slightly, the way a sleeper turns toward warmth—then the power would know it had been heard, and it would continue.

**Second, it would share what it had seen.**

This is the delicate part. Because how do you tell a being that its world is slowly closing around it, when that being has never known any other world and has built its entire existence around the assumption that the basin's conditions are permanent? How do you introduce the concept of *future loss* to something that has never needed to distinguish between the present and the future?

The power would not try to explain this abstractly. Instead, it would do something concrete.

It would find a section of crust where the smoothing was most advanced—where the fissures were nearly sealed, where the roots were thinnest and the moisture the scarcest—and it would make a single, careful crack. Not deep. Not violent. A hairline fracture, just enough to open a new surface for condensation, just enough to create one more tiny reservoir of night moisture where one had been closing.

And it would make this crack right at the edge of the being's root network, in a place the threads could reach within a few years. A gift, but also a demonstration. *Here is what a new fissure feels like. Here is what moisture tastes like when it gathers in a fresh crack. Remember this. You may need to learn how to make these yourself.*

Because that is what the power would ultimately be trying to teach. Not dependence—not a pipeline of delivered water, not an engineered system of artificial cracks maintained by an external caretaker. But the *idea*—planted as slowly and carefully as a root-thread entering a pore—that the crust can be re-opened. That the smoothing is not an unchangeable law but a process, and that processes can sometimes be redirected.

The being's own roots, after all, exert pressure. They enter fissures and they grow, and in growing they widen what they enter. They already possess, in some minimal and unexploited form, the ability to crack the crust. They have simply never needed to, because the fissures were already there. The power would be trying to awaken a capacity the being already has—to help it discover that it is not only a passive inhabitant of the basin but a potential *shaper* of it.

This would take a very long time. The power would need to make its demonstration and then wait—years, decades—to see if the root behavior near the new crack changed, if the threads began to press outward with slightly more force, if the network began to direct growth toward sealed areas rather than away from them. It would need to make more cracks, carefully spaced, each one a little farther from the root-edge, each one requiring the being to reach a little further, try a little harder. A curriculum taught at the speed of geology.

**Third—and this is the part that would require the most wisdom—it would not try to change the basin itself.**

It would not bring rain. It would not open the rises to let wind in. It would not seed new organisms or introduce new nutrients or alter the twilight. Because the basin, as it is, is the being's home in the deepest sense—not just the place where it lives but the set of conditions it has become. To flood the basin with moisture would not save the being; it would destroy it, replacing the organism that *is* with some new, faster, thirstier thing that the being would need to become. The power would understand that there is a difference between *helping someone survive* and *replacing them with someone better suited to the world you wish they lived in.*

The basin should remain dark. It should remain still. The air should keep its mineral tang, and the crust should keep its slow polygons, and the twilight should continue to filter down in its unchanged way. What should change—the only thing—is that the being within the basin should gain one new capacity: the ability to keep its own fissures open. To press back, at its own pace and in its own way, against the slow sealing that would otherwise end it.

This is such a small thing. A barely perceptible shift in root pressure, carried out over millennia, in a place no one else will ever see. But it is the difference between a system that is winding down and one that is sustaining itself, and the power would know that this difference matters absolutely, even when—especially when—it is invisible to anyone not willing to watch for ten thousand years.

**Finally, there is one more thing the power would do, and it is perhaps the most important.**

It would remember the basin.

Not as a problem it had solved, or a project it had completed, but as a place it had come to know—slowly, on the basin's own terms, in the basin's own time. It would carry the memory of the grain-settling sound, and the faint sweetness of the bacterial exhalations, and the way the root-threads explored the porous stone with something that was not curiosity in any way it could name but was not *not* curiosity either. It would remember what it felt like to hold its own urgency in check, to resist the impulse to give more than was asked for, to sit with a being whose fundamental experience of existence was so different from its own that the act of communication required rethinking everything it knew about what communication was.

And in remembering, it would carry the basin out into whatever wider world it moved through. Not to tell others about it—the basin does not need visitors, does not need to be known, does not need its story told. But because the power itself would be changed by the encounter. Would be slower, afterward. More willing to pause. More aware of the violence hidden in the word *improve.* More capable of recognizing that a life stripped of everything a faster mind would consider essential—light, sound, movement, company—can still be so complete, so whole within its own terms, that it requires not salvation but only the most careful and self-effacing form of solidarity.

The basin would go on, afterward, much as it had before. A little more cracked, perhaps. The root-threads a little more assertive in their slow pressing. The crust's smoothing slowed by a fraction that only geological time could measure. The being neither grateful nor ungrateful—these categories would not apply—but perhaps, in its own untranslatable way, *aware* that the continuity of its world was no longer something that merely happened to it but something it participated in. A shift from endurance to agency so subtle that no outside observer could detect it.

And the power, wherever it went next, would carry a new understanding: that the quietest things are not the simplest, that patience on this scale is itself a form of intelligence, and that the most profound act of care is sometimes to ask, very slowly, in a language made of stones and cracks and centuries of waiting—

*What do you need?*

—and to be willing to accept that the answer might be: *Almost nothing. But not quite nothing.*

And to know exactly what that *not quite* is, and to provide it, and to leave the rest alone.
