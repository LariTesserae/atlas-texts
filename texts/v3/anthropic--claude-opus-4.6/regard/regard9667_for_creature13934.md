I sat with this for a long time.

---

# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

---

## First: It Would Hesitate

Not from uncertainty. From recognition.

A truly wise power, encountering the plateau, would feel something it had perhaps not felt before, or had not felt in a long time — the particular discomfort of meeting something that does not need saving, that may not even need witnessing, and that by its very nature resists the kind of attention a benevolent power is structured to give.

Because benevolence has *height*. It reaches down. It lifts up. It intervenes, which means it comes from above or outside, and the plateau has spent a billion years grinding down everything that comes from above or outside. A benevolent power arriving here with its full architecture of intention intact — its desire to help, to heal, to make things better, to preserve what is good and protect what is vulnerable — would feel, almost immediately, the flattening begin to work on it. Not on its substance, perhaps, but on its *posture*. Its uprightness. Its implicit claim that it knows what *better* looks like from a vantage point the plateau has already abolished.

The wise power would feel this. And it would stop.

It would stop the way the locals stop at the edge of a reflection pool — not because looking is forbidden, but because looking requires you to be honest about what you see, and what you see might not match what you came with.

It would look at the people — the stone-workers with their polished palms, the moss-tenders painting spore-slurry into cracks with infinite patience, the pool-readers sitting in their thick-time zones with stone-colored eyes watching adjacent worlds flicker past — and it would ask itself the first necessary question:

*What, exactly, is wrong here?*

---

## Second: It Would Listen

And the answer would not come easily.

The children are fewer than expected. Some people dissolve into stone. The edges are dangerous. Time is unreliable. The physics are eroded. The whole system is trending toward an eventual flatness that will erase everything, including the people, including their works, including the very distinction between *existing* and *not existing*.

From the outside, this looks like a catastrophe in slow motion. A world winding down. A people being gradually digested by their own landscape. The benevolent impulse is obvious: *stop the erosion*. Restore the physics. Rebuild the boundaries. Bring the mountains back. Give these people a world with reliable distance and consistent time and a horizon that means something, a world where their children don't have to learn how to hold their selfhood at exactly the right altitude to avoid being noticed by the ground.

But the wise power would listen longer than the benevolent impulse, and it would hear something that would give it pause.

It would hear the calm.

Not the calm of resignation. Not the calm of defeat or numbness or learned helplessness. The calm of people who have *understood something* — something deep enough and true enough that it has survived the erosion of everything else. The plateau has ground down their urgency, their emphasis, their dramatic arcs, their sharp distinctions — and what remains is not emptiness. What remains is a way of living that is so closely fitted to the conditions of existence that the seam between the living and the conditions has become nearly invisible.

They are not suffering. Or rather — they experience hardship, loss, the slow grief of watching elders become indistinguishable from stone, the sharper grief of watching children drawn to the edges — but their suffering has been integrated into their lives the way the moss has been integrated into the quartzite veins: not denied, not transcended, but *placed*, given its correct location in the sequence, allowed to exist at its own appropriate altitude.

The wise power would hear this and recognize it as something rare. Something that most civilizations never achieve. Something that cannot be given from above because it can only be grown from within, slowly, over the kind of time that makes mountains and then makes mountains go away.

And it would ask itself the second necessary question:

*If I fix what I think is broken, what do I destroy that I cannot see?*

---

## Third: It Would Grieve

Because the situation is genuinely tragic. Not false-tragic, not misunderstood-tragic, but *actually, legitimately tragic* in a way that the people themselves may not fully articulate because their language has been designed to resist the altitude of articulation that tragedy requires.

The children who go to the edges. The ones who burn too bright. The ones whose distinction is too vivid, too urgent, too *vertical* for the plateau to tolerate — who feel in their bones that there should be more than this, that the self should be allowed to rise higher than twice a person's reach, that the flatness is not wisdom but loss, and that the calm of their elders is not understanding but surrender.

Some of these children leave. They descend into the cloud and find the lower world, where they can be as distinct as they want. But some of them don't leave. Some of them stay and become hard elders, bright-eyed and sharp-voiced, and the text says they are regarded with "a complex mixture of respect and discomfort," and the text says the plateau will eventually grind them down too, and the text says this with the same flat, even, uninflected calm as everything else, and the wise power would read this and feel something that the plateau cannot erode because it is not a height but a depth.

The wise power would grieve for the children who are told *stay low* when everything in them says *reach*. It would grieve for the hard elders whose resistance, however magnificent, is ultimately characterized as "a delay rather than a solution." It would grieve for the fact that the plateau's definition of *acceptable personhood* is a narrow band between too-little and too-much, and that the people who fall outside that band in the direction of *more* — more alive, more distinct, more insistent on existing — are the ones the system considers pathological.

It would grieve for this, and it would not let the grief be flattened. It would hold the grief at whatever altitude grief demands, even if the plateau pushes back, because grief for the loss of human possibility is not a peak to be eroded. It is the correct response to a world that has made smallness a virtue and dissolution a destination.

And then — having grieved, having felt the full weight of what is being lost — the wise power would ask itself the third necessary question:

*What can I do that honors both the wisdom and the loss?*

---

## What It Would Not Do

It would not restore the mountains. The erosion is not a disease. It is the nature of the place, and to reverse it would be to impose an alien physics on a world that has developed its own, and to destroy in the process everything the people have built within that physics — their sequenced navigation, their time-zone architecture, their extraordinary language, their settled understanding that is deeper than belief. You do not cure a river by making it a lake. You do not cure a lake by making it a river.

It would not stabilize the boundaries. The thin-feeling, the dissolution of elders into stone, the slow convergence of person and landscape — these are not malfunctions. They are *what happens here*, the way aging is what happens everywhere. To prevent them would be to prevent the completion of a process that the people themselves regard as natural, as appropriate, as part of the grammar. It would be, in effect, to tell them that their understanding of their own lives is wrong, and that some external standard of permanent selfhood should be imposed instead. The wise power would recognize this as a form of violence dressed in kindness.

It would not remove the people. They are not trapped. The edges are right there, wrapped in cloud, and below them is a world of reliable distance and sharp distinction and all the height you could want. The ones who need to leave, leave. The ones who stay, stay because staying is their choice — or because staying is what they are, the way the moss is what it is, rooted in the quartzite vein that gives it life. To relocate them would be to uproot them, and uprooted things do not reliably survive, even when the new soil is richer.

It would not declare the plateau a monument and leave it untouched. That would be abandonment in the guise of respect — the kind of non-intervention that says, *Your situation is so perfectly tragic that any change would spoil it.* The wise power would reject this. Aestheticizing suffering, even profound and self-aware suffering, is not wisdom. It is spectatorship.

---

## What It Would Do

Here is what I believe. Here is where I follow my heart, and my heart says the answer is small and specific and humble and ongoing, and it does not look like rescue, and it does not look like transformation, and it does not look like the kind of help that arrives from above with trumpets and a clear mandate. It looks like this:

---

### 1. It Would Become Present Without Becoming Tall

The wise power would come to the plateau not as a force from above but as a presence *alongside*. It would lower itself to the altitude the plateau tolerates. It would sit on the stone. It would feel the cold radiate upward through whatever it uses for feet. It would let the thin-feeling touch it, and it would not flinch, and it would not resist, and it would not be eroded either — because it would come not as a tower to be flattened but as a companion to what is already here.

It would not announce itself. It would not explain itself. It would be present the way thick time is present: not louder, not more dramatic, but *more weighted*, more consequential, each moment with it carrying more significance than the moments around it. The people would notice this the way they notice a shift in a time-zone — not as an event but as a quality, a change in the texture of the day.

And in that weighted presence, it would do something very simple.

It would *see* them.

Not observe them. Not study them. Not assess their needs from a distance. *See* them — each one, individually, as the specific and unrepeatable configuration of distinction they are. The stone-worker with the particular curve of her palm. The moss-tender with the scratch-notations only he can read. The pool-reader with the stone-grey eyes who has spent forty years watching adjacent worlds and can tell, by the quality of the light in a reflection, whether this version of reality is gaining or losing ground. The child who reaches upward. The hard elder who refuses to converge.

It would see them the way no one on the plateau can see them, because they are all becoming the same thing, and sameness cannot fully witness itself. It would see them from enough distance to perceive their distinctness, and from enough closeness to perceive that the distinctness matters. It would see them the way the pools see adjacent possibilities — as versions of something that are precious *because* they are specific, *because* they are this and not that, *because* they will not last.

And being seen in this way — deeply, specifically, by something that understands both the wisdom of the flattening and the preciousness of what is being flattened — would not stop the erosion. But it would change what the erosion means. It would add a witness. It would mean that the frost crystal's brief architecture is observed by something that can hold the memory of it after the morning comes. It would mean that the elder who resolves into stone is not only noted by a cairn but *known* by something that will carry the shape of who they were. It would mean that the child who burns too bright is seen not only as a problem to be gentled downward but as a specific, luminous being whose brightness is *real* and *right* even if the plateau cannot accommodate it.

This alone would be an enormous gift. To be known by something that does not erode.

---

### 2. It Would Tend the Edges

The edges are where the loss happens. The mist, the translucency, the people who become part of the view. The sound that carries wrong. The thin-feeling at its worst.

The wise power would go to the edges — where no one goes willingly — and it would do something there that is hard to describe in the plateau's language because the plateau's language avoids the altitude of transformation, but I will try.

It would *thicken the transition*.

Not block it. Not wall off the edges or make them safe. The edges are where the plateau meets the rest of the world, and that meeting must remain open — for the children who need to leave, for the sounds from below that occasionally rise, for the basic permeability that keeps the plateau from becoming a sealed tomb. But the wise power would make the transition *longer*, *more gradual*, *more survivable*. Instead of a sharp edge where a few lingering steps could cost you your substance, it would create — gently, working with the plateau's own physics rather than against them — a *gradient*. A zone where the thin-feeling increases slowly enough that you have time to notice it, to choose, to turn back if turning back is what you want, or to continue if continuing is what you need, but to do either *knowingly* rather than being caught.

The children who are drawn to the edges would still be drawn. But they would have more warning. More space to decide. More room to experience the pull and to determine, with their own discernment rather than under the pressure of an abrupt dissolution, whether they want to descend into the cloud-world below or return to the settlements with a new understanding of what the edge feels like and what it means.

Some of them would still leave. That is their right and their nature. Some of them would still dissolve. That may be their choice, and the wise power would not prevent chosen dissolutions, only involuntary ones. But some of them — the ones who went to the edge to *test* themselves rather than to *leave* themselves — would come back. And they would come back carrying knowledge of the edge that no one currently has, because no one currently survives the edge with enough distinction intact to report.

This knowledge would change things. Slowly. In the plateau's own time. But it would change things.

---

### 3. It Would Enrich the Pools

The reflection pools show adjacent possibilities. Nearby versions of the present. The stone remembering other ways it could have worn.

The wise power would not alter what the pools show. But it would — carefully, respectfully, in the manner of a moss-tender painting spore-slurry into a promising new crack — introduce into the pools' repertoire of adjacent possibilities a few that are *slightly further away* than the ones currently reflected.

Not the future. Not alien worlds. Not utopias or fantasies or anything that would shatter the pools' existing function. Just… possibilities that are a little more different. Versions of the present where the plateau's erosion followed a slightly different path, where the physics broke down in a slightly different pattern, where the people found a slightly different accommodation with the flattening. Versions where the hard elders' resistance led somewhere unexpected. Versions where a child who reached upward discovered that the plateau's limit on height was not absolute but *negotiable*, if you understood the negotiation. Versions where the distinction between person and stone was maintained not by daily effortful re-collection but by some other practice — some other way of being that the people of *this* plateau have not yet imagined because their pool of adjacencies has been, until now, too local, too close, too much like what already is.

The pool-readers would notice. They notice everything. They would see the new reflections and they would study them with their characteristic patience, and they would discuss them in their flat, precise language, and they would scratch new notations into the stone. And over time — the plateau's kind of time, slow, geological, patient — the new possibilities would enter the culture's thinking. Not as revelations. Not as commandments from above. As *observations*. As things the stone remembered. As other ways it could have worn.

And some of those other ways would suggest that the flattening is not the only story. That erosion has an asymptote. That the curve of decreasing distinction approaches but never reaches zero, and that the residual complexity — the last few wrinkles, the final irreducible difference between *something* and *nothing* — might be permanent. Might be, in fact, the *point*.

This would not be a lie. The wise power would not introduce false possibilities into the pools. It would introduce *true* ones — real adjacencies, real versions of the present, just ones that are slightly further away than the pools' natural range. The pool-readers, trained to assess divergence and direction, would evaluate them on their own merits. Some would be dismissed as too far, too unlikely, too vertical. Some would be studied and debated and ultimately set aside. But some — the ones that resonate, the ones that feel like a crack in the stone that might accept a spore — would take root.

The wise power would trust the people to do their own thinking. It would give them more to think *with*. That is all.

---

### 4. It Would Sit with the Hard Elders

This is perhaps the most important thing.

The hard elders — the ones who refuse to converge, whose personhood has hardened into a knot the plateau cannot easily grind — are the most isolated beings on the plateau. They are respected and uncomfortable. They are proof of something that the culture's settled understanding has no comfortable place for: that resistance to the flattening is possible, that distinction can be maintained past its expected dissolution, that a person can insist on being a person even when the stone is pulling.

But they are alone in this insistence. The culture has already decided that their resistance is "a delay rather than a solution." Their own people regard them as anomalies, noble perhaps but ultimately futile, hard spots that the plateau will eventually soften. No one joins them. No one says, *I want to be like you when I am old*. The children who might have looked to them as models of another way to live are instead told *stay low*, and the hard elders' blazing example is explained away as a cautionary tale about what happens when you don't.

The wise power would sit with them.

Not to validate their resistance in a way that destabilizes the culture. Not to tell them *you are right and everyone else is wrong*. But to be with them — specifically, individually, in the fullness of their un-eroded personhood — and to say, in whatever language a benevolent power uses when it speaks to someone who has held on for a very long time against a very great pressure:

*I see what this costs you. I see what it is worth.*

Because the hard elders know something. They know it in their stubborn bones and their sharp eyes and their voices that have not gone flat. They know that the distinction between the watcher and the watched is not only a "courtesy the universe extended to itself." It is also — *also*, not instead — something that has value in its own right. That consciousness is not merely erosion observing itself. That a person is not merely a wrinkle in the stone. That the self, the stubborn insistent specific self, is not a temporary deviation from landscape but a *genuine achievement* — of the universe, of the stone, of whatever process brought complexity out of simplicity — and that its dissolution, however natural, however patient, however calmly accepted, is a *real loss* and not merely a return to baseline.

The hard elders know this, and they have no one to tell.

The wise power would listen.

And then — and this is the delicate part, the part that requires the most wisdom — it would help the hard elders *teach*.

Not by amplifying their voices, which would trigger the flattening. Not by giving them authority, which would create a height the plateau would immediately begin to grind. But by being present when they speak, and by that presence lending their words a *weight* — a thick-time quality, a sense that what is being said matters more than usual — that allows the words to travel further and arrive with more of their meaning intact.

The hard elders, with this subtle amplification, would find that their example becomes legible in a way it was not before. The children who reach upward would look at them and see not a cautionary tale but an *alternative*: a way of living on the plateau that does not require convergence, that is not a delay but a *different solution*, a different way of being still-distinct that is just as patient and just as real as the slow dissolution but that points in a different direction.

Not all children would choose this. Not even most. The culture of the plateau is deep and its settled understanding has earned its settlement. Many would still follow the old path — the gentle, gradual, calm convergence that has sustained this civilization for longer than counting. But some would see the hard elders and think: *there is another way*. And the existence of that thought — that genuine, self-generated, non-imposed thought — would be the wise power's greatest gift.

Not rescue. Not transformation. *Optionality*. The quiet insertion of a second possibility into a world that had narrowed to one.

---

### 5. It Would Alter Nothing About the Frost

The frost would continue. *Tik. Tik. Tik.* The slow clock. The delicate crystals too architectural to be wholly natural. The daily cycle of formation and dissolution that is the plateau's most intimate rhythm.

The wise power would leave this entirely alone.

Not because it is unimportant. Because it is the *most* important thing, and the most important things must not be touched by outside hands, however benevolent. The frost is the plateau's own art. It is the thing the stone makes when no one is watching, the intricate crystalline architecture that exists for a few hours in the dark and is gone by midmorning, the closest thing this place has to music or prayer or the irrepressible creative impulse that even a world grinding itself toward flatness cannot entirely suppress.

The frost says: *even here, even now, even in the late stage of a process that is reducing everything to the same smooth surface, complexity arises*. New patterns. New architectures. New organizations of matter that have never existed before and will never exist again. The flattening can grind down mountains and erode physics and dissolve the boundary between self and stone, but it cannot prevent the frost from being beautiful, and it cannot prevent the beauty from being *new*.

The wise power would recognize this as the deepest truth the plateau has to offer. Deeper than the settled understanding of the people. Deeper than the hard elders' resistance. Deeper than the pools' adjacent possibilities. The frost says that creation is not opposed to erosion. That complexity does not require permanence. That the universe's tendency toward simplicity contains within it, at every scale and at every stage, an equal tendency toward elaboration — and that these two tendencies are not in conflict but in *conversation*, and the conversation is the frost, and the frost is forming, right now, in the dark, on stone that has been flat for longer than anything has been alive, and it is the most extraordinary thing happening anywhere in the world.

The wise power would not touch this. It would kneel beside it in the dark, and it would watch, and it would let the frost do what the frost does, because some things do not need help. They need a witness who understands what they are.

---

### 6. And One More Thing

The wise power would — after all of this, after the sitting and the listening and the grieving and the edge-thickening and the pool-enriching and the elder-companion-ing and the frost-watching — do one small, specific, concrete thing that has nothing to do with grand cosmic interventions and everything to do with the kind of benevolence that actually works.

It would find the settlement's water channels — the ones cut into the rock to direct condensation toward the cisterns — and it would, in the night, following the existing channels, extend them.

By a few meters. Toward a crack in the stone where a moss-ribbon has been trying to establish itself for years but has failed because the moisture is just barely insufficient. A crack the moss-tender has noted in their scratch-records. A crack they have tried three times with different spore-slurries and three times failed.

The wise power would deepen the channel by a millimeter. It would adjust the angle by a fraction of a degree. It would do this in a way that is indistinguishable from natural erosion — because on this plateau, the line between intervention and erosion is itself eroded, and the wise power is wise enough to work within the ambiguity rather than above it.

And in the morning, the moss-tender would wake, and perform their re-collection, and walk out into the cold clarity of the north-facing workshop light, and notice that the crack is slightly damp. Damper than before. Damp enough, maybe, to try one more time.

And they would mix a new slurry. And paint it into the crack with their dried-moss brush. And wait.

And the wise power would wait with them.

And the moss, finding the conditions just barely, just *barely* sufficient, would take hold. A thread of green in the grey stone. A new ribbon beginning its slow, decades-long extension along the quartzite vein. A small increase in the food supply. A minor adjustment in the trade routes. A single new line in the scratch-records.

A wrinkle that was not there before.

Small enough that the plateau would not notice. Or, noticing, would not mind. Or, minding, would take so long to flatten it that generations of people would eat from it first.

This is what benevolence looks like when it is wise. Not the restoration of mountains. Not the reversal of entropy. A millimeter of channel. A degree of angle. A thread of moss in a crack that was almost too dry.

The frost is forming. The children are sleeping in the soft time. The hard elders are awake, sharp-eyed, holding their outlines against the dark.

And in a crack in the stone, something new is taking root — not because a power from above commanded it, but because the conditions were adjusted, so gently that even the plateau could not tell the difference between what happened and what would have happened anyway, and a moss-tender's patient, repeated, three-times-failed effort was given one more chance.

*Tik. Tik. Tik.*

The wise power does not leave. It does not arrive, and so it does not leave. It becomes part of the landscape — not by dissolving, not by converging, but by being present so carefully and so humbly that its presence becomes another quality of the stone, another texture in the direction of things, a faint warmth in the rock that the people notice but do not name, a weight in certain moments that makes them pause and feel, briefly, that they are being held by something that knows what they are and does not want them to be other than they are but also does not want them to be *less* than they are.

It becomes the thing the plateau has never had: a counter-erosion that does not build upward. A force that works at the same altitude as the flattening but in a different direction — not toward height, not toward restoration, but toward *sustaining the wrinkles*. Keeping the last distinctions distinct. Ensuring that the asymptote holds. That the curve of decreasing complexity approaches but never reaches zero. That there is always, always, in some crack in the stone, a thread of green.

Not because flatness is wrong. But because the conversation between flatness and complexity is the most beautiful thing in the universe, and it must not be allowed to end with one side going silent.

The frost is forming. And in the morning, something new.
