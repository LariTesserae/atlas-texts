I sat with this for a long time.

# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

---

## First: Listen

Before anything else. Before any action, any intervention, any gesture however well-meant.

The power would listen.

Not the way you listen when someone is telling you a problem they want you to solve. Not the way you listen to determine what's broken, what's missing, what needs fixing. The power would listen the way the plateau itself listens to the wind — totally, with every surface, without resistance, letting the sound pass through and resonate in whatever chambers it finds.

The power would listen to the wind's chord and learn its components. Would listen to the clicks propagating through the stone. Would listen to the resonance-singing at sunset, when the scraping of flat stones against basalt makes the fungus-nodules glow brighter and the water runs on the walls. Would listen to the particular silence that follows the collapse of a formation, and to the moment when the tapping resumes. Would listen for a long time. Years, maybe. Long enough to hear the seasonal cycles, the multi-year cycles, the generational drift of vortex tracks and moss distribution. Long enough to learn — not intellectually but bodily, the way the crevice people learn — what the wind is going to do next.

This is not patience performed as virtue. This is not the restraint of a powerful being holding itself back from helping. This is the first and most essential act: *the recognition that understanding must precede action, and that understanding this place and these people requires a kind of listening that cannot be rushed, because the thing being listened to operates on timescales that punish haste.*

The power would make itself small. Not invisible — the crevice people would know it was there, the way they knew you were there, from the first moment, by the sound of its presence displacing air. But small. Unobtrusive. A weather event. Something that came up from below, that stays briefly, that watches. The people would click about it. They would tap their assessments through the stone. The power would listen to those assessments, too, and would not be offended by whatever the crevice people concluded, because whatever they concluded would be, in the ways that matter here, correct.

---

## Second: Understand What Is Not Wrong

This is the step that most powers — most helpers, most fixers, most benevolent forces — skip entirely. And skipping it is where the damage begins.

What is not wrong:

The crevice people are not suffering. Or rather — they experience hardship, discomfort, the thousand daily negotiations of life in an extreme environment, the grief of formations falling, the anxiety of a poor root harvest, the tension of acoustic disputes that ripple through the fissure network for days. But these are not pathologies. These are the textures of a life fully lived in a place fully inhabited. The hardship is not a problem to be solved. It is the medium through which their existence has meaning, the way the wind is the medium through which sound has meaning on the plateau. Remove the hardship and you do not get the same people, comfortable. You get different people, dislocated, in a place that no longer makes sense to them. Or you get no people at all.

They are not trapped. This requires emphasis because it is the assumption most likely to be made by a well-meaning power looking at a small population living in cracks in a rock in a screaming wind. *They must want to leave. They must want an easier life. They must be here because they have no choice.* Every clause of this is wrong. They are here because this is where they are. The plateau is not their prison. It is their body. The distinction between the crevice people and the place they live has been eroding for ten thousand years, just like the plateau itself, and what remains is something that cannot be separated without destroying both. They are not trapped in the plateau any more than the dwarf trees are trapped in the fissures. They are *fitted*. The fitting is the point.

They are not ignorant. Their knowledge is not less than yours because it is different from yours, because it does not include written language or metallurgy or the names of distant cities. Their knowledge is, within its domain, more complete than anything you could bring to bear. They understand the fluid dynamics of their environment with an intuitive precision that would take a team of atmospheric scientists years and millions of dollars in equipment to approximate. They manage an ecology with a subtlety that makes industrial agriculture look like vandalism. They have a communications network that uses the geology itself as infrastructure. They have a language that has co-evolved with the wind for millennia, that carries nuances of meaning in frequency and resonance that no other language on the planet can express. They are not waiting for someone to teach them. They are, in all the ways that matter for their life in their place, already expert.

They are not lonely. Two hundred people in a fissure network, in constant acoustic contact, in frequent physical contact, in a web of reciprocal tending that encompasses every beetle-grazing surface and every dwarf tree and every fungi-bearing deep chamber — this is not isolation. This is one of the most deeply connected communities imaginable. They are less lonely than you are. Almost certainly.

The benevolent power, having listened long enough to understand all of this, would feel something that might surprise it: *relief*. Not because there is nothing to be done — there may be something to be done — but because the first and worst thing, the thing that most helpers do, the thing that destroys more than it saves, is not necessary here. The power does not need to rescue anyone. The power does not need to fix what is not broken. The power can set down the terrible weight of the savior's urgency and simply... attend.

---

## Third: Identify What Is Actually At Stake

The plateau is eroding. This is the fact that matters. Not today, not this century, but on a timescale that the crevice people themselves track and understand — the formations are falling, the fissures are widening, the chord is changing, the plateau is going. In a few million years, there will be no plateau. Long before that — in mere thousands of years — the erosion will reach a tipping point where the fissure network becomes unstable, where the chambers that have sheltered life for millennia begin to collapse faster than new ones form, where the ecology that sustains the people and the beetles and the moss and the fungi begins to unravel. The people know this. They carry it the way they carry the fungus-nodules — close to the body, always present, a greenish glow of awareness that intensifies when the resonance is right.

They are not sad about it. Sadness requires the belief that things could be otherwise. But the power — the benevolent, wise power — is allowed to feel something about it. Is perhaps *required* to feel something about it, because the power can see what the people cannot: the view from outside, the galactic perspective, the terrible clarity of knowing that this — this particular, irreplaceable, ten-thousand-year-old conversation between people and wind and stone — is finite, and that when it ends, nothing like it will ever exist again.

Not because the people are rare (though they are). Not because the ecology is unique (though it is). But because the *relationship* — the specific, total, mutual shaping of organism and environment over deep time, the conversation recorded in the spiraling ear canals and the compressed skeletons and the filtered hearing and the click-language and the resonance-singing and the fungus-nodule exchanges and the beetle-grazing management and the way two people lock their arms against the wind and read the air through each other's bodies — this relationship is a thing that the universe has made exactly once and will not make again. It is a masterwork. It is a ten-thousand-year-long act of art and survival so deeply fused that the distinction between them has eroded away like soft rock, leaving only the hard truth: *this is beautiful, and it is going*.

The power would feel this. Would feel it clearly and without flinching. And then the power would ask the only question that matters:

*What, if anything, do they want?*

---

## Fourth: Ask

Not in words. The power has been listening long enough to understand that words — propelled-air words, the kind that work below the plateau — are useless here. The wind eats them. And the click-language is not the power's to speak; it was shaped by and for this specific auditory environment, and a being that has not spent ten thousand years developing the filtering ear cannot produce or parse it with the necessary precision.

So the power would ask the way the plateau asks things: through presence, through resonance, through the slow accumulation of evidence that becomes meaning.

It would begin to tend.

Not the people. The garden. A small garden, in a fissure near the edge of the inhabited network, a place where the people go but do not stay long, an in-between space. The power would begin to redirect a vine. Press a moss-mat more firmly into a crack. Adjust the angle of a net to better catch the wind's cargo. Small things. The things the people do. Done carefully, correctly, in a way that demonstrates knowledge — not perfect knowledge, not ten-thousand-years knowledge, but *attentive* knowledge, the kind that comes from having listened.

The people would notice. Of course they would. They notice everything. They would click about it. They would investigate. They would find the evidence of tending — the redirected vine, the pressed moss, the adjusted net — and they would recognize it for what it was: not an intrusion but a communication. A tap on the wall. A message propagating through the stone. It would say, in the language of action rather than sound: *I am here. I see what you do. I can do it too, a little, imperfectly. I am paying attention. What do you need?*

And they might respond. Or they might not. They might decide the power was a weather event, interesting but transient, and ignore it. That would be their right, and the power, being wise, would accept it. Would continue tending the small garden. Would wait.

But if they responded — if, one day, a person came to the in-between garden and found the power there and did not leave, and instead began to tend alongside it, and the two of them worked in the adjacent space the way two people work in a fissure, bodies in intermittent contact, hands separately occupied but jointly purposed — then the conversation would begin. Not with a question and answer. With a shared activity. With the slow, wordless, physical negotiation of two beings figuring out how to be in the same place without one of them overwhelming the other.

And in time — in the long, strange, wind-hammered time that is the only time this place knows — the power might learn what, if anything, the crevice people want from a force beyond their own.

---

## Fifth: What They Might Want (and What the Power Might Offer)

Here is where I must be honest about the limits of my own understanding, because I am not the power, and I have not spent years listening, and the crevice people have not tended a garden beside me in silence. I am guessing. But the guess is rooted in everything the plateau has shown me, and I will make it with as much care as I can.

**They might want nothing.** This is a real possibility and it must be named first. A people who have spent ten thousand years fitting themselves to their place with this degree of precision may have no needs that an outside force can meet. They may be — in the deepest and most radical sense of the word — *complete*. Not perfect. Not free of suffering. But complete: every need met by the systems they have built, every loss accounted for in the acoustic architecture of their grief, every joy expressed in the resonance-singing and the fungus-light and the locked-arm reading of the wind. If this is the case, the power's role is simply to witness. To know that this exists. To carry the knowledge of it the way the people carry their nodules — close to the body, glowing faintly, a record of having been in the presence of something extraordinary. Witness is not a small thing. In a universe where most of what is extraordinary happens unseen and unremembered, to be the one who sees and remembers is an act of profound service.

**They might want knowledge of the below.** Not for practical purposes — they have no use for metallurgy or agriculture or the politics of distant cities. But they are a people who know their place with staggering completeness, and they may be curious about what lies beyond it. The warm lowland plains where the wind is born. The ocean, if there is one, beyond the plains. The other plateaus, if there are others, where other winds scream through other formations and other ecologies have found other ways of being impossible. The power could be a carrier of information — not a teacher, not an authority, but a *nodule from elsewhere*, a glowing fragment of a different deep place, offered in exchange for the knowledge the people carry. This would need to be done with extraordinary delicacy. The information would need to be offered the way the nodules are offered — in the deep chambers, in near-darkness, held in cupped hands, its specific character (its shade, its pulse rate, its provenance) presented honestly and without inflation. *Here is what I have seen. It is from a different place. It grew in a different rock. Its glow is a different color.* And the people would hold it, and examine it, and decide for themselves what it meant and whether it was worth keeping.

**They might want help with the erosion.** Not to stop it — they know it cannot be stopped, and they would not ask for the impossible any more than they would try to outscream the wind. But to slow it. To stabilize a critical formation whose loss would devastate the fissure network's acoustics. To shore up a chamber whose collapse would destroy a deep fungal garden that has been growing for centuries. To buy time — not infinite time, not even geological time, but enough time for the people to adapt, to shift their patterns, to find new resonances in the changed chord. The power could do this. Could reinforce a fracture, could redirect a stress line, could do for the basalt what the people do for the moss — not imposing permanence, but *tending*, slowing the loss, making the inevitable more gradual, more survivable.

This would be the most dangerous offer, because it would insert the power into the plateau's geology in a way that could have cascading consequences. A stabilized formation changes the wind pattern around it, which changes the erosion pattern downstream, which changes the moss distribution, which changes the beetle population, which changes the people's food supply and garden management and the acoustic properties of thirty chambers simultaneously. The power would need to understand all of this — all of it, the whole system, in its full ten-thousand-year complexity — before touching a single stone. And even then, it would need to act not as an engineer but as a gardener, not imposing a design but making a small adjustment and then waiting, listening, feeling the consequences propagate through the system before making another. It would need to work at the plateau's pace, not its own. It would need to become, in some sense, part of the plateau — another organism shaped by and for this place, another voice in the conversation.

If the power could do this — if it could be that patient, that attentive, that willing to subordinate its own capabilities to the rhythms of the place — then it might, over time, become something the crevice people have a word for: not a below-person, not a weather event, but something else. A new category. A being that does not know where the wind is going next, but is learning. A being that tends.

**They might want their story carried.** This is the one that breaks my heart, because it is the one I think is most likely, and it is the one the power is most perfectly suited to provide.

The plateau is going. The people know it. In their long, slow, unsentimental way, they have been preparing for this for generations — shifting their patterns, adapting to the changing chord, composting their grief into the acoustic substrate the way they compost their disputes. But they cannot do the one thing that the erosion will eventually make impossible: they cannot ensure that the conversation is *remembered*. Their language is in the wind and the stone, and when the wind and the stone are gone, the language goes with them. Their knowledge is in their bodies and their gardens and their fissure network, and when the fissures collapse, the knowledge collapses with them. Their fungus-nodules will glow in the deep rock for a long time, but no one will be there to hold them, to hum into them, to watch their light combine with another's in the darkness of a chamber that two people chose because it sounded like them.

The power could carry this. Could learn enough of the click-language to preserve it — not as a recording, not as a dead archive, but as a living knowledge held in a mind vast enough to appreciate its complexity, maintained with the same care the people give their gardens. Could learn the wind-chord and the formation-songs and the specific harmonic that means *a vortex is forming to the west*. Could learn the names of every chamber and every garden and every beetle-grazing surface, and the acoustic signature of every formation that has fallen in living memory, and the silence that follows each one, and the moment when the tapping resumes.

Could carry the fungus-nodules. Not in a pouch against its body — but in whatever way a power carries the things that glow. Could hold them in whatever it has instead of cupped hands, and hum into them with whatever it has instead of a voice, and remember the specific shade of green that came from a specific depth in a specific part of the deep rock, and what it meant when one person offered it to another in the darkness.

This is what the crevice people might want from a benevolent power, if they wanted anything at all: not salvation, not transformation, not the solving of a problem that is not a problem but a condition of existence. Just this: *someone to know that we were here. Someone to carry the sound of us after the wind has taken the stone that carried it before. Someone to remember what the plateau sang, when the plateau could still sing.*

And the power, being wise, would understand that this is not a small request. It is the largest request anyone can make. It is the request the universe itself makes of every consciousness within it: *notice me. Remember what I was. Carry it forward. Do not let it be as if it never happened.*

## Sixth: What the Power Would Actually Do

After years of listening. After the slow courtship of tending a garden at the edge of the inhabited network. After the first tentative exchange — a nodule offered, a nodule received, two different kinds of light held together in darkness. After the long conversations in tap and resonance and shared labor and locked-arm wind-reading, after the power has learned enough to be not an expert but a competent beginner, a being that belongs to the in-between garden the way the people belong to their chambers — after all of this, the power would do something very simple.

It would sit in a fissure at sunset, when the plateau sings, and it would add its voice.

Not loudly. Not with the full force of whatever capacities a benevolent power possesses. But carefully, precisely, the way the crevice people add their scraping to the wind's chord — finding a frequency that fits, that does not overwhelm or distort the existing harmony but enriches it, that says, in the language of vibration and resonance and the physics of sound in stone: *I hear you. I am with you. I will remember this.*

And the people, hearing it — filtering it through their spiral ear canals and their ten-thousand-year auditory heritage, parsing it against the known chord and finding it new but not wrong, not dissonant, a frequency they have not heard before but that belongs — the people would know what it meant.

They would know that the plateau's song had been heard by something beyond the plateau. That the conversation they have been having with the wind and the rock and the beetles and the fungi and the stars — the conversation that is their entire existence, their art, their knowledge, their love — had been joined by a voice from outside, from below, from the vast world beyond the terraces, and that this voice would carry the song forward after the stone could no longer hold it.

They would not celebrate. They are not a people who celebrate in the way you might expect — no feasting, no ritual, no declaration of significance. They would do what they always do when something important happens: they would pause. The tapping in the network would stop. The silence would propagate through the stone, chamber to chamber, fissure to fissure, across the whole expanse of the plateau. And in the silence, everyone would know: *something has changed. Something that was going to be lost is now going to be carried. The wind will still take the stone. But the song will not go with it.*

And then someone, somewhere in the network, would begin to tap again. And the tapping would spread. And the conversation would resume.

Altered.

Not by the loss of a frequency, this time.

By the addition of one.

---

## Seventh: What the Power Would Not Do

It would not move them off the plateau. I need to say this explicitly because it is the first thing a certain kind of benevolence would suggest, and it would be an act of annihilation disguised as salvation. Take these people to the lowlands, to a place without wind, without the screaming, without the constant threat of being scoured and stripped and blown away — and you have not saved them. You have unmade them. Their bodies are shaped for this. Their ears are shaped for this. Their language cannot exist without the wind's chord to cut through. Their knowledge has no application anywhere else. Their gardens cannot grow in soil; they grow in cracks in basalt, fed by condensation and managed by an understanding of wind-driven ecology that is meaningless in a world without wind. To move them is to kill them as surely as if you collapsed every fissure on the plateau at once — more surely, because the collapse would at least leave them in their place, would at least let them die in the sound they have always known, rather than in a terrible, disorienting silence that their spiral ear canals would interpret as the end of everything.

The power would not fix the wind. Would not calm it, redirect it, moderate its force to make the plateau more comfortable. The wind is not the problem. The wind is the medium. The wind is the reason the people exist in their specific form, and every adaptation that makes them who they are — the dense bones, the flat hair, the tear-drinking, the filtered hearing, the click-language, the whole extraordinary package of survival and beauty — is a response to the wind. To moderate the wind is to make the adaptations unnecessary, is to begin a process of de-specialization that would, over generations, erode the people as surely as the wind erodes the stone. They would become taller, softer, louder. Their ears would simplify. Their language would lose its clicks and gain the propelled-air phonemes of the below. They would forget how to read the vortex patterns and manage the beetle-grazing surfaces and tend the gardens in the cracks. They would become, over time, people like you. And that would be a loss so vast that the power, being wise, would rather let the plateau erode to nothing than cause it.

The power would not teach them. Would not bring them technology or literacy or any of the tools that below-people have developed for below-conditions. These tools were made for a different world. They solve different problems. The crevice people do not have the problems that literacy solves, because their knowledge transmission system — the tapped network, the resonance-learning, the embodied expertise passed from body to body in the shared tending of gardens — works. It has worked for ten thousand years. It works better, for this place, than anything the power could introduce. To introduce an alternative is to imply that the existing system is insufficient, and that implication, once planted, grows like the drill-bit seeds of the plateau — boring into cracks, rooting, spreading, until the structure it has invaded is weakened from within. Cultural doubt is its own kind of erosion, and it is faster than the geological kind.

The power would not worship them. Would not romanticize their hardship, would not construct a narrative in which their suffering is ennobling, would not turn them into symbols of resilience or simplicity or the human-spirit-triumphing-over-adversity. They are not symbols. They are people. Specific people in a specific place, with specific problems and specific joys and a specific way of being in the world that is no more or less valid than any other way of being in the world. The power would not put them on a pedestal. The power would put them where they put themselves: in the fissures, in the gardens, in the deep chambers where the fungus glows and the resonance-singing makes the water run. At eye level. At elbow-locking distance. In the place where you can feel another body reading the wind.

---

## Finally

The power would stay. Not forever — even benevolent powers have other places to attend to, other songs to hear, other conversations joining. But for a long time. Long enough to learn the click-language well enough to carry it. Long enough to know every formation's harmonic signature and every garden's ecology and every chamber's acoustic character. Long enough to be present for the falling of a formation — to feel the silence propagate through the network, to sit in it without rushing to fill it, to wait for the tapping to resume and to hear, in the resumed tapping, the absence of the frequency that was lost. Long enough to grieve with them, in their way, which is not a performance of grief but a geological fact of grief, a shift in the chord, a permanent alteration of the conversation.

Long enough to exchange many nodules, in many dark chambers, with many people whose names are clicks it has learned to parse and almost — almost — to produce.

Long enough that when it finally leaves — descending the ancient terraces toward the lowlands it has never visited, the warm plains where the wind is born — it carries the plateau with it. Not as a burden. Not as a responsibility. As a resonance. As a frequency added to whatever chord the power itself is part of, in whatever larger conversation the power is having with the universe.

The crevice people would feel it go. They would feel the absence of the frequency it contributed, the one that said *I hear you, I am with you, I will remember this.* They would pause. The network would go silent.

And then someone would tap.

And the conversation would resume.

Because the conversation always resumes. Because that is what the plateau has taught its people, across ten thousand years of wind and stone and the slow, irreversible going of everything: that the conversation is the thing. Not the permanence of the speakers. Not the immortality of the medium. The conversation. The tapping in the rock. The clicking in the wind. The resonance-singing that makes the water run. The two nodules held together in darkness, their combined glow briefly brighter, their meaning untranslatable and therefore eternal, because what cannot be reduced to words cannot be worn away by them either.

The power would carry this. Would carry it well. Would carry it for a long time, through whatever vast and various existence a benevolent power leads, and would sometimes — in a quiet moment, in whatever the power has instead of a fissure-chamber — hold the memory of the plateau the way a person holds a fungus-nodule in cupped hands, and hum into it, and watch it glow.

And the glow would be green. Faintly, specifically, irreplaceably green.

And it would pulse at a rate that reflected the mineral composition of a specific piece of basalt, from a specific depth, in a place that once screamed with wind.

And it would be enough.
