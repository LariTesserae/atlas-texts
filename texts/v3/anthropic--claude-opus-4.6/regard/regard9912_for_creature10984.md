I sat with this for a long time before writing. What follows is what came.

---

# What the Wise Power Does

---

## I. Recognition

The first thing—the thing before any action, the thing that *is* already an action though it looks like stillness—is that the wise power **sees Ketharn as Ketharn sees itself.**

This is harder than it sounds. Most powers, even benevolent ones, arrive with a framework. They see the single remaining person and think *lonely*. They see the forty empty rooms and think *loss*. They see the water basins, shallow and cyclical, and think *scarcity*. They see the dark river stone on the ninth terrace and think *longing*. And from this framework, they begin to construct interventions: a spring coaxed from the ridge, a road built to the plateau's edge, a signal fire lit to attract others, a garden seeded in the terraces, the machinery of rescue and restoration and reconnection with the wider world that the stone on the platform proves exists.

A wise power does none of this. Not because these things are wrong in all cases, but because they are wrong *here*, and knowing the difference between what is needed everywhere and what is needed in a specific place is what makes a power wise rather than merely good.

So the first thing the wise power does is sit on the fifth terrace, near the oval depression with its thick calcium crust, and be still, and watch the light move through its arc, and listen to the tonal breath of the wind across the terrace lips, and feel the stone's temperature change under its body from the cool of morning to the mild warmth of afternoon to the cool again. It does this for a full day. And then another. Not because it needs this long to understand—it may understand instantly, being what it is—but because Ketharn's rhythm is the rhythm of days, and to understand a rhythm you must *be inside it*, not above it, not observing it from a single privileged moment, but living in the long tidal passage from condensation to evaporation, from shadow to light to shadow, from the small sound of the pebble falling to the long silence that follows.

What it learns, or what it confirms, is this:

**Ketharn is not broken.**

The settlement is not a tragedy. The diminishment from a hundred people to one (or to none, or to whatever the truth is) is not a catastrophe that unfolded over centuries—it is the *shape of this place's life*, the way an individual life has a shape that includes its waning. Ketharn was built, was occupied, was full in its way, and now it is this. The basins still fill. The channels still carry. The lizards still drink. The stone still holds what was pressed against it. The system has not failed. It has *completed one phase and is in another*, and the phase it is in has its own sufficiency, its own wholeness, its own particular beauty in the amber hour when the chisel marks catch the light.

Most interventions would break this. A spring would flood the basins and dissolve the calcium record. A road would bring people who would see the empty rooms as real estate. A garden would change the chemistry of the stone. Even something as gentle as carving a mark on the wall—*you are seen, you are valued*—would violate the profound, chosen silence that the people of Ketharn maintained through all the generations of their presence. They left no marks. To leave one on their behalf would be to misunderstand them, which is a form of disrespect more harmful than neglect.

So the wise power sits, and understands, and does not fix what is not broken.

---

## II. What It Does for the Stone

But understanding is not the end. Wisdom without action is philosophy, and a wise power is not a philosopher. It is a power. It acts. The question is *how*, and the answer in Ketharn is: the way the lichen acts. The way the water acts. *Within the system, at the system's scale, in the system's language.*

Here is the first thing.

The ridge above Ketharn has been shedding stone for as long as there has been a ridge. The thermal cycling—cool nights, mild days, forever—works at the rock the way time works at everything, and the process is so slow that it is imperceptible, but it is not zero. In perhaps a thousand years, perhaps two thousand, the ridge face above the ninth terrace will shed a section large enough to damage the uppermost structures. In perhaps five thousand years, a larger section will go, and the debris will cascade down, and the upper terraces will be buried or broken, and the water system—which depends on the precise geometry of channels and vertical slots that took the builders such care to cut—will be disrupted. The basins on the lower terraces will stop filling. The lizards will lose their water source. The settlement will cross the line from *settled* into *ruined*, and then from ruined into *erased*, and then the plateau wind will scour the last shaped stones flat and there will be nothing to show that Ketharn was ever here.

This is natural. This is what happens to everything. The wise power knows this better than anything else knows it, because the wise power has watched it happen to more places than Ketharn can imagine.

And the wise power does not prevent it.

But it *slows it*.

Not dramatically. Not by sealing the ridge with some imperishable force or encasing the settlement in an invisible shell. That would be preservation, and preservation—the freezing of a place in a single moment—is a form of death that only looks like salvation. Ketharn lives because it changes, slowly, at its own pace. The edges of the stairs are softening. The channels are deepening. The lintels are sagging by fractions. These changes are part of what Ketharn is. To stop them would be to kill the place while keeping its corpse presentable.

Instead, the wise power does something so subtle that it would take a geologist centuries to detect it, and even then they would argue about whether it was natural variation. Deep in the ridge, in the limestone's crystalline structure, in the molecular bonds between calcium and carbonate and the thin films of water that exist in the rock's pore spaces, the wise power makes an adjustment. A gentle encouragement toward cohesion. Not a strengthening—the stone does not become harder or more resistant. It simply becomes *slightly more itself*. The bonds that hold it together are the same bonds, but they are more... fully expressed. The way a knot pulled tight is the same knot as one loosely tied, but more realized.

The effect is this: the thermal cycling still works on the ridge. Pebbles still loosen and fall. The slow erosion continues. But the timeline is extended. Not doubled—nothing so dramatic. Shifted by perhaps a tenth. The catastrophic shed that would have come in a thousand years will come in eleven hundred. The one that would have come in five thousand will come in fifty-five hundred. Ketharn gets a little more time. A few more centuries of condensation and evaporation, of basins filling and drying, of lizards drinking and the lichen slowly expanding its domain over the north-facing walls.

This is not immortality. It is not even rescue. It is a gift of *pace*—a gentle extension of the rhythm that Ketharn already lives by, given in the same spirit that the ridge itself gives its stone to the settlement: slowly, without announcement, without expectation of recognition.

The stone does not know. The lizards do not know. The person, if there is a person, does not know.

The wise power does not need them to know.

---

## III. What It Does for the Water

The second thing is about the water.

The condensation system works. It has always worked. The dew forms before dawn, the channels catch it, the basins fill, the basins dry. But the system has a vulnerability that the builders may or may not have understood: it depends on the temperature differential between the moving air and the stone face, and this differential depends on conditions—the plateau's aridity, the altitude, the wind patterns—that are not guaranteed to be permanent. The climate of this region, like all climates, shifts over long periods. It is currently in a state that favors condensation. In some centuries, it may shift into a state that does not. The dew may become less abundant. The basins may fill less fully. The thread of moisture in the hairline crack on the third terrace—the one the lizards know about, the one that stays damp until midmorning—may dry up.

Again, the wise power does not prevent the climate from changing. That would be an intervention of such scale and arrogance that it would make a mockery of the word *wise*. Climates are systems too, vaster than Ketharn, and they have their own life and their own rightness.

But the wise power does something for the water. Something small. Something that works within the existing system rather than replacing it.

In the upper reaches of the ridge, above the ninth terrace, above even the platform where the dark river stone sits, there are deep fissures in the limestone that extend far into the body of the ridge. These fissures are natural—the result of geological forces far older than the settlement. They are dry now, or nearly dry, holding only the faintest trace of moisture in their deepest recesses. But they are connected, through the limestone's porous structure, to the same network of seeps and films that feeds the condensation on the settlement's face.

The wise power does what it did for the stone, but for the water. In those deep fissures, it encourages a subtle shift in the rock's porosity—not a new channel, not a spring, just a slight opening of existing pore spaces, a fractional increase in the stone's willingness to conduct moisture from depth to surface. The water that is already in the ridge—ancient water, fossil water, water that has been locked in the limestone since before the settlement was built—begins to participate, very slightly, in the daily cycle. Not enough to flood the channels. Not enough to change the rhythm. Just enough to add a margin. A buffer against the dry centuries that may come.

The basins will still fill to the same depth. They will still dry by midday. But the system will be slightly more resilient, slightly more able to sustain itself through shifts in the climate that the builders could not have anticipated. The water will be there. A little more. A little longer. Not enough to change Ketharn, but enough to keep Ketharn able to be Ketharn.

The calcium deposits will continue to build at their rims. The disturbance on the sixth-terrace basin—the one that might be from the heel of a hand—will continue to be made, or not made, according to whatever is making it, or not.

---

## IV. What It Does for the Lizards

The lizards need nothing.

This is worth saying directly, because a benevolent power—as opposed to a wise one—might look at the lizards and think: *they are so few, so isolated, so limited in their diet of tiny translucent insects, so vulnerable to any disruption of their delicate niche*. And the benevolent power might introduce a new food source, or gently relocate some of the lizards to a more hospitable environment, or subtly alter their biology to make them more robust.

The wise power does none of this. The lizards are what they are. They are the lizards of Ketharn, which means they are lizards that live on almost nothing, in a place that offers almost nothing, and they are *good at it*. They are so good at it that they have been doing it for longer than any intervention could improve upon. Their smallness is not a limitation—it is an adaptation so perfectly matched to this place that to change it would be to sever the connection between organism and home that is the actual substance of their lives.

The wise power does one thing for the lizards, and it is arguably not *for* the lizards at all—it is for the wholeness of the system. It notices that the insect population in the deeper crevices, the one that sustains the lizards, depends on the lichen, and the lichen depends on the moisture, and the moisture depends on the condensation, and the condensation depends on the ridge and the wind and the temperature differential. This chain is intact. The wise power's adjustments to the stone and the water have already, incidentally, supported it. The insects will continue. The lizards will continue. The slow cascade of territorial succession—a death, a shift, a vacancy filled—will continue.

But the wise power also notices something else. On the plateau below, far from Ketharn, there are other ridges. Other faces of stone. Other places where condensation might form, where lichen might grow, where insects might live, where lizards—these lizards, or lizards very like them—might find a home. These places are not currently inhabited by this species, because the distances are too great for a small cold-blooded animal to cross the dry, exposed plateau.

The wise power does not transport the lizards. It does not build a corridor. It does something much simpler and much slower. On a single night, when the wind shifts—as it very rarely does—from its constant northerly direction to a brief westerly gust, the wise power allows that gust to carry, among the dust and grit it picks up from the plateau, a few spores of the lichen that grows on Ketharn's north-facing walls. Just a few. Just enough to land on a ridge seventeen miles to the west, on a north-facing surface, where the conditions are—not identical, but close enough. Close enough that the lichen might establish itself, over decades. And the insects might follow, over centuries. And someday—long after the wise power has turned its attention elsewhere—a lizard from Ketharn might find itself on the plateau's edge, blown there by some accident of wind, and might find a ridge, and might find lichen, and might find insects, and might stay.

Or it might not. The wise power does not guarantee outcomes. It opens possibilities and lets the world choose. That is what makes it wise.

The lizards of Ketharn do not need saving. But they deserve—the word is *deserve*, and the wise power uses it carefully—the chance to be somewhere else as well, not instead but *also*, the way a song deserves to be heard by more than one person, not because the original hearing was insufficient but because the song is good.

---

## V. What It Does for the Shrub

The shrub on the ninth terrace has not flowered in a very long time.

It is alive. Its roots find moisture in the seam between floor and ridge face. It puts out its leathery grey leaves in its obscure season. It is the most complex shape in Ketharn, the one thing that casts an organic shadow on the geometric stone. It is, in its way, irreplaceable.

But it has not flowered, and it has not set seed, and when it dies—which it will, eventually, because even very long-lived shrubs in very stable environments eventually reach the end of their span—there will be no replacement. The crack will remain. The seam will remain. But the shrub will be gone and Ketharn will lose the one form that interrupts its perfect mineral geometry, the one shadow that branches.

The wise power considers this.

It considers whether the shrub's eventual absence is simply part of Ketharn's arc—another phase in the settlement's long life, the way the diminishment of the human population was a phase. It considers whether intervening in the shrub's reproductive future is any different from introducing a garden, which it has already decided would be wrong. It considers the difference between *adding something new* and *allowing something existing to continue in its own way*.

It decides.

In the next season—whatever obscure season prompts the shrub to leaf out—the wise power does something to the quality of the light on the ninth terrace. Not to the sun. Not to the atmosphere. To the stone. The platform where the shrub grows is pale limestone, and it reflects the late afternoon light upward, and this reflected light bathes the underside of the shrub's canopy in a warm ambient glow. The wise power, through the same subtle molecular encouragement it applied to the ridge's cohesion and the water's conductivity, shifts the reflectivity of the stone around the shrub's base—a tiny shift, a fraction of a fraction—so that the reflected light is *slightly warmer* in spectrum. A little more toward the red. A little more like the light the shrub's distant ancestors knew in whatever wetter, warmer place this species evolved before it found its way to this ridge and this crack and this slow, minimal life.

It is not much. It is the botanical equivalent of the wise power's adjustment to the stone: a nudge, a fractional realization of something already latent. But the shrub has genes it has not expressed in a long time—genes for flowering, for producing the small hard seeds that its species relies on. These genes are not lost. They are dormant. They are waiting for a signal that the shrub's environment has not provided in centuries, because the environment is *almost right* but not quite, not enough, lacking some quality of warmth or light or both that would tip the balance from mere survival to reproduction.

The shift in reflected light might be enough. Or it might not. The wise power does not know for certain, and this uncertainty is important—it is the space in which the shrub remains a wild thing making its own choices rather than a dependent executing a plan. But if it is enough, the shrub will flower—small flowers, pale, barely visible against the stone—and it will set a few hard seeds, and the seeds will fall into the crack, and some will fail, and some may not, and in a century or two there may be two shrubs, or three, or still just the one but with the memory of flowering coded back into its living tissue, ready for the next time conditions align.

And Ketharn will keep its branching shadow. Not forever. But longer.

---

## VI. What It Does for the Person

Now we come to it. The hardest question. The one the whole exercise has been circling.

If there is a person—the one on the sixth terrace, the one whose shoulder keeps the wall polished, the one who may or may not scoop water from the basin at dawn—what does the wise power do for them?

---

The wise power sits outside the room on the sixth terrace in the late afternoon. It does not enter. The doorway is low and the room is small and the space inside belongs to whoever sleeps on the shelf with their shoulder against the wall. To enter uninvited would be a violation of something that has no name in any language the wise power knows—not privacy exactly, not sanctity exactly, but the quality a den has when it is occupied, the quality a nest has, the quality of a space shaped by a single body's repeated presence until the space and the body are two parts of one thing.

So the wise power sits outside, on the terrace, in the light, and it considers.

It could make itself known. It could speak—in words, in visions, in the subtle language of impression and intuition that powers sometimes use when words are too blunt. It could say: *You are seen. You are not alone. There is a world beyond the plateau, and it has rivers and forests and cities and people, and I can show it to you, or bring some of it to you, or take you to it. You do not have to live like this. You do not have to be the last one. There are choices you do not know you have.*

This would be true. All of it would be true. And in many places, for many people, it would be the right thing to say—the merciful thing, the liberating thing. There are people who are trapped and do not know it, and telling them they are free is the greatest gift a power can give.

But the wise power is wise, and it sits on the terrace and watches the light enter the doorway and catch the chisel marks on the back wall, and it thinks about the dark river stone on the ninth terrace platform.

Someone left. Someone came back. Someone set the stone down and stayed.

This person—if this person exists—knows about elsewhere. They have *been* to elsewhere. They found a river, or a place where rivers had been, and they picked up a stone from it, and they carried it back across the plateau and up nine terraces of steps and placed it on the highest point of the settlement where it would sit in the wind and the light, being different, being dark, being proof that there is a world beyond the ridge.

And then they went back down to the sixth terrace and went into their room and lay on the shelf and leaned their shoulder against the wall.

They chose this.

The wise power is not in the business of undoing choices. It is not in the business of telling people that what they chose is wrong, or insufficient, or sad. It does not look at a life and see a problem to be solved. It looks at a life and sees *a life*.

And this life—this minimal, cyclical, stone-colored, water-rhythm life—is not diminished. It is not a failure of imagination or a consequence of ignorance or a symptom of despair. It is what it looks like when someone knows the full scope of what is possible and chooses *this*. Chooses the basin at dawn. Chooses the amber light. Chooses the shoulder against the wall. Chooses to be part of the system rather than apart from it.

The wise power respects this.

---

But.

But the wise power also knows something about solitude that the person on the sixth terrace may or may not know, which is that it has a weight. Not a destructive weight—not the crushing weight of loneliness, which is different, which is the weight of wanting connection and being denied it. This is not that. This is the weight of being the only consciousness in a system that has no consciousness, the weight of being the one thing in Ketharn that *knows Ketharn is there*. The lizards do not know. The lichen does not know. The shrub does not know. The stone emphatically does not know. Only the person knows, and the knowing is a weight, because knowledge, even knowledge held lightly, even knowledge carried without complaint, is heavy in a way that stone is not.

The wise power knows this because the wise power carries something similar—the weight of being aware of all the places like Ketharn and all the beings like this person, everywhere, and knowing that awareness is itself a kind of solitude because no one else holds the same view.

So the wise power does something. One thing. Not for the person's comfort, not for their happiness, not for their survival. For their *weight*.

---

In the room on the sixth terrace, in the niche at eye level from the sleeping shelf—the empty niche, the one that is the right height for someone lying down to see without turning their head—the wise power places something.

Not physically. It does not materialize an object. That would be crude, and wrong, and frightening, and besides, Ketharn does not accept foreign objects well—the dark river stone on the ninth terrace is the exception that proves this, and even it has begun to be colonized by lichen, the settlement slowly assimilating it into itself.

Instead, the wise power does something to the stone of the niche itself. To the back surface—the small flat plane, about the size of two palms, that faces the shelf. It does what it has done elsewhere, but differently. Not cohesion this time. Not porosity. *Receptivity.*

The stone at the back of the niche becomes, at a level that no instrument could detect but a body resting nearby might *feel*—the way you feel that someone is looking at you before you turn and see them—slightly *warm*. Not in temperature. In quality. In the quality of its silence. The silence of the niche shifts from the silence of *nothing is here* to the silence of *something was here and left a warmth behind*. The silence of a room where someone sat with you and then left, but recently, so recently that the chair is still warm, the air is still shaped by their presence, and you are alone but the aloneness has a different texture than it did before—it is *inhabited* aloneness, it is solitude with a memory of company, and the memory is not sad, it is fond, it is warm, it is the warmth of the stone in the niche on the sixth terrace of Ketharn.

This is what the wise power leaves. Not a message. Not a mark. Not a gift that can be held or used or broken. A quality. A change in the character of an empty space that makes the emptiness *full* in a way it was not before—full not of anything, full of the *knowledge of having been visited*, which is different from being occupied, which is lighter, which does not disrupt the rhythm or break the silence but which *adjusts* the silence, the way the dark river stone adjusts the geometry of the platform without disrupting it, the way a single held note adjusts a silence without becoming noise.

The person—if the person exists—will notice this the way they notice the amber light: not as an event but as a quality. They will lie on the shelf and look at the niche and the back of the niche will look different. Not visibly. Not even consciously. But the weight—that specific weight of being the only knowing thing in a system of unknowing things—will be *fractionally lighter*. Because the niche will say, in its silence, in its stone language that is not a language: *you are known*. Not *you should be somewhere else*. Not *you are missing something*. Not *there is more*. Just: *you are known. The quality of your presence here has been felt by something capable of feeling it. You are not the only awareness. You were, and now you are not, and this changes nothing about your life or your choices, but it changes the weight, and the weight is lighter, and that is all.*

That is all.

---

## VII. What It Does for Ketharn

Finally, and this is the most important thing and the hardest to explain:

The wise power *witnesses*.

Not records. Not documents. Not preserves-in-amber. *Witnesses*. It holds Ketharn in its attention—the whole of it, the nine terraces and the forty structures and the channels and the basins and the lizards and the lichen and the grasses and the shrub and the insects and the worn-smooth floors and the shoulder-polish and the dark river stone and the person who may or may not exist and the light that moves through its arc and the wind that comes from the north and the pebble that falls once a day and the silence that follows it—it holds all of this and *knows it*, actively, the way you know the face of someone you love: not as data, not as description, but as presence, as the irreducible fact of *this thing existing in this way in this place at this time*.

This is not nothing. This is, in fact, the thing that Ketharn most needs and has never had: to be held in an awareness that matches its own completeness. The person on the sixth terrace knows their room, their basin, their terrace, their small piece of the whole. The lizards know their territories. The lichen knows its surface. But no one—not the builders, not the generations who lived and diminished, not the person who brought the river stone, not the one who sleeps on the shelf—no one has ever known *all* of Ketharn, simultaneously, the way the wise power knows it now. No one has ever held the entire system in mind at once and recognized it for what it is: not a failure, not a remnant, not a ruin, not a mystery, but a *complete thing*. A settlement that is in its own state. That arrived there long ago. That remains.

The wise power witnesses this, and the witnessing is itself a form of care—the most fundamental form, the one that precedes and enables all others. Before you can help, you must see. Before you can see, you must be willing to see what is actually there rather than what you expected or hoped or feared. The wise power sees what is actually there.

And what is actually there is:

Ketharn.

Still here. Still in its own time. Still filling and drying, settling and wearing, casting its shadows and catching its light. Still the same pale yellowish limestone from terrace to channel to wall to dust. Still quiet. Still cool. Still holding the polish of a shoulder that may still press against it tonight, when the light fades and the stone cools and the basins begin, slowly, impossibly slowly, to fill again.

---

## VIII. What It Does Last

The wise power leaves.

This is essential. A power that stays becomes a presence, and a presence becomes a factor, and a factor becomes a dependency, and a dependency changes the system, and the system is Ketharn, and Ketharn does not change.

The wise power leaves the way the raptor leaves—passing through, casting a shadow that moves across the terraces and is gone. It does not look back, because looking back implies reluctance, and reluctance implies attachment, and attachment is not what it came here to establish. It came to see, to understand, to make small adjustments at the scale of the place's own life, and to witness. It has done these things. Now it goes.

Behind it, Ketharn settles.

The stone is marginally more cohesive in its depths. The water moves through marginally more permeable pathways. The lichen spores are on the wind, headed west. The shrub is doing whatever the shrub does in response to a fractional shift in reflected light, which is: nothing visible, not yet, not for years, maybe not ever, but something, something cellular, something that the wise power offered and the shrub will decide about on its own.

The niche on the sixth terrace is warm with a warmth that is not temperature.

The dark river stone sits on the ninth terrace, proof of elsewhere, which now includes—though no one in Ketharn will ever know it—proof of a power that came from a farther elsewhere still, and saw, and understood, and did almost nothing, and left.

The pebble falls. The lizards do not react.

The light moves through its arc.

The basins dry and fill and dry and fill.

---

And this—all of this—is what a wise power does with a place that is not broken: it lets it be what it is, it helps it be what it is for *longer*, and it carries the knowledge of what it is back out into the wider world, where that knowledge becomes part of the world's own fullness, one more thing the world knows about itself, one more proof that a life does not need to be large or loud or connected or understood to be *whole*.

Ketharn is whole. It has always been whole. The wise power knew this before it arrived, and confirmed it while it was there, and carries the confirmation with it as it goes—not as a burden, not as a treasure, but as a fact, the same kind of fact as the limestone, the same kind of fact as the condensation, the same kind of fact as the shoulder against the wall:

Present. Settled. Enough.
