I sat with this for a long time before writing.

# What the Wise Power Does

---

## I. It Watches

First, for a very long time, it does nothing at all.

This is the hardest thing to convey about genuine wisdom applied to a genuine system: the overwhelming initial impulse is toward restraint. Not because the power is indifferent—it is the opposite of indifferent, it is *attentive* in a way that borders on reverence—but because the first thing it understands, the thing it understands before anything else, is that it is looking at something that works.

And not just works. *Works beautifully*. Works in a way that has been tested by more time than the power can easily hold in a single thought. Every relationship here—eel and biofilm, runner and groove, flyer and shaft, crawler and crack, filter and worm-tangle, megafauna and corridor—has been negotiated by millennia of mutual adjustment into a fit so precise that intervention would be, in almost every case, a form of vandalism. You do not improve a river by explaining hydrology to it. You do not improve a forest by optimizing its nutrient cycles. These systems already embody solutions more elegant than any designed alternative, because they have had the one resource no designer ever has enough of: time. Deep, uninterrupted, geological time, and the freedom to fail and recover and fail differently and recover differently until what remains is what works.

So the power watches. It watches the eels braid through the channels and understands that the braiding pattern distributes heat and wear and reproductive opportunity across the column in a way that maximizes the longevity of the channel itself. It watches the runners hum and understands that the hum is not merely social bonding but a real-time acoustic mapping system that allows the column to navigate the city's corridors without any individual needing to know the map—the map is the sound, the sound is the corridor, the corridor is the path worn by the sound, and the whole thing is a navigational technology so deeply embodied that it has become indistinguishable from anatomy. It watches the flyers filter the air and understands that the particulate load they remove is precisely calibrated to the particulate load the city generates, a dynamic equilibrium maintained not by regulation but by the simple fact that more particles mean more food mean more flyers mean fewer particles mean less food mean fewer flyers mean more particles, a self-correcting cycle that has never needed correction.

It watches the crawlers. It watches them for a very long time.

It watches the small things on the high terraces, the ones with the hands and the predator eyes. It watches them even longer.

It watches the center, where the water goes. It does not flinch from this. It is wise enough to look at the thing the narrator could not bring themselves to approach, and what it sees there—

But that comes later.

---

## II. It Understands the Question It Is Being Asked

The power has been drawn here by attention, not by crisis. This is important. The Pelvian Shelf is not suffering. It is not in danger, not in any immediate sense. It is not crying out for help. It is not collapsing. It is not in pain.

And yet.

The wise power perceives something that the city itself cannot perceive, because the city has no self to perceive with—or if it does, that self operates at a timescale so far beyond the biological that it does not experience its own condition as a *condition*, only as a continuation.

What the power perceives is this: the system is closed.

Not thermodynamically—energy enters from the sun, from the geochemistry below, from the ocean to the south. But informationally. Evolutionarily. Creatively. The Pelvian Shelf is a system that has optimized itself into a local maximum so deep and so stable that it has, for all practical purposes, stopped exploring. Every niche is filled. Every cycle is balanced. Every feedback loop is closed. The system is not dead—it is vibrantly, extraordinarily alive—but it is *finished* in the way that a perfect crystal is finished. It has found its shape. It is repeating it.

This is not a flaw. It is not a failure. It is, in many ways, a triumph—a demonstration that life can build something so durable and so internally coherent that it persists for geological time without external management. The power respects this. The power is in awe of this.

But the power also sees the cost.

The eels have not changed in a hundred thousand years. Their braiding pattern is identical in every channel across the continent. They have solved the problem of being an eel in a drainage channel so completely that the solution has become a cage—not a cage imposed from outside but a cage woven from the inside, from the sheer perfection of the fit between organism and environment. There is no selective pressure to change because there is nothing to change *toward*. The environment is the organism is the environment.

The runners have not changed. The flyers have not changed. The megafauna have not changed. The cistern-filters and the worm-tangles have not changed. The crawlers—

The crawlers are the closest thing to change the system has produced in millennia, and the crawlers are building walls. They are building *the same walls*. Their almost-geometric patterns are always almost-geometric, never arriving at full geometry, never collapsing into randomness. They are frozen at the threshold of something—computation, communication, architecture-with-intent—and they have been frozen there for longer than the iron has been in the city, and they will be frozen there forever, because the system does not need them to cross that threshold, and what the system does not need, the system does not produce.

The small things on the terraces. The ones with the hands. The ones that stack stones and watch each other stacking stones.

They are the newest species on the Shelf. They evolved here—the power can read this in their bodies, in the way their eyes are adapted to the Shelf's specific light conditions, in the way their fur matches the stone, in the way their hands are shaped to grip the particular angles and textures of the native rock. They evolved here, and they evolved *toward something*, and they are close—so close—and they are stuck.

They are stuck because the city doesn't need them to be anything more than what they are. There is no pressure. There is no crisis. There is no scarcity that their cleverness could solve, no predator that their cooperation could defend against, no environment that their tool-use could reshape. The city provides. The city has always provided. The feeding crusts regenerate on schedule. The water runs clean. The corridors are maintained. The temperature is stable. Everything works. Everything has always worked.

And so the small things sit on the terraces and stack stones and watch each other and do not—*cannot*—take the next step, because the next step requires a need that the city's perfection has preemptively eliminated. They are intelligent enough to play. They are not intelligent enough to know they need something they don't have. They are the most remarkable product of the Shelf's long evolution, and they are the clearest symptom of its stasis, and they do not know this, and no one knows this, because no one here knows anything at all, because knowing is not something the system requires.

This is what the wise power sees: a living system of extraordinary beauty and durability that has, through the very perfection of its self-maintenance, foreclosed the possibility of its own further becoming. Not dying. Not suffering. But *finished*, in a way that life—which the power understands as a process, not a product—is not meant to be finished. Not yet. Not while there are small things with clever hands stacking stones on the high terraces and watching each other do it with those binocular, predator, *curious* eyes.

## III. What It Does Not Do

Before describing what the wise power does, it is necessary to describe the much longer list of things it does not do, because every one of them is a temptation, and the power's wisdom is demonstrated as much by its refusals as by its actions.

It does not introduce plants. This is the most obvious intervention and the most catastrophic. The Shelf's entire ecology is built on a world without photosynthetic life. The food web, the atmospheric chemistry, the water cycles, the behavioral patterns of every species—all of it is predicated on the absence of plants. Introduce a photosynthetic organism and you don't enrich the system; you *destabilize* it. The biofilm that feeds everything would face competition. The light regime would change. The atmospheric oxygen balance would shift. The water chemistry would be altered by root systems and organic acids. Within decades, species that have spent millennia in perfect equilibrium would find themselves in a suddenly alien environment, and most of them would not adapt, because they have forgotten—genetically, evolutionarily—how to adapt. The city would die not of poison but of *novelty*, a shock it has no immune system for. The power knows this. The power does not introduce plants.

It does not uplift the small things. Not directly. Not by granting them language or technology or expanded cognitive capacity. This is the second temptation and the second refusal, and it is harder than the first, because the power can see what the small things could become and aches with the knowledge. But uplift—direct cognitive enhancement—would not produce a new intelligence emerging from the Shelf's own logic. It would produce a graft, a foreign mind wearing a native body, and the resulting creature would be alien to its own world in the deepest possible way. It would have the capacity to reshape the Shelf but no organic connection to the Shelf's history, no embodied understanding of the relationships that hold it together. It would be, in effect, a well-meaning invader. And the history of well-meaning invaders, which the power knows in full, is not encouraging.

It does not fix the center. Whatever is at the center of the Shelf—the place where the water converges and the patterns become actually geometric—is the system's own deepest expression of itself, and it is not broken. It is not suffering. It is not calling for help. It may be the closest thing the Shelf has to a mind, or it may be a purely physical phenomenon that *resembles* mind the way a whirlpool resembles intention, and the power is wise enough to know that the difference between these two possibilities may be less significant than it appears and that neither one requires intervention.

It does not accelerate anything. It does not introduce urgency into a system that has never known urgency. It does not create a crisis to provide selective pressure. It does not engineer a scarcity to make the small things need their cleverness. Every shortcut is a wound. Every forced transition is a break in the long continuity that has made the Shelf what it is. The power understands that the Shelf's slowness is not a limitation; it is a vocabulary. Things that happen slowly here happen *deeply*, and what happens deeply is what lasts. Whatever the power does must happen at the Shelf's own pace, in the Shelf's own language, or it will not last either.

It does not announce itself. It does not manifest. It does not descend in a column of light or speak in a voice of thunder or leave a monolith on the terrace. The Shelf has no concept of the divine, no framework for revelation, no narrative into which a miracle could be inserted. A visible intervention would be meaningless—not rejected, not feared, just meaningless, the way a symphony is meaningless to a stone. The power understands that communication requires a shared medium, and its medium is not the Shelf's medium, and forcing a translation would distort the message beyond recognition.

So. What does it do?

## IV. The First Thing

The smallest possible thing.

Somewhere on the Shelf—not at the center, not on the terraces, but in the mid-levels, in the zone where the crawlers are densest and the runners' grooves are deepest and the architecture is layered with the accumulated construction of millennia—there is a drainage channel that has been slowly silting up.

This is not unusual. It happens occasionally, despite the eels' best efforts, in places where the water flow has shifted over centuries due to the gradual subsidence of the plateau. A channel that once carried a strong flow now carries a trickle; the biofilm thins; the eels use it less frequently; sediment accumulates; the channel narrows further. It is a slow feedback loop running in the wrong direction—not a catastrophe, but a gradual diminishment, the kind of thing that the system usually resolves on its own through the rerouting of traffic to adjacent channels.

But this particular channel is located at a junction. Its silting is not just reducing its own flow; it is subtly altering the drainage pattern of an entire district, redirecting water along paths that are slightly less efficient, creating a marginally wetter zone in one area and a marginally drier zone in another. The effects are small. They are, at the moment, well within the system's tolerance. In a thousand years, they will resolve themselves one way or another—the channel will silt completely and the water will find a new path, or some shift in the deep geology will restore the flow. The system is patient. The system has time.

The wise power shifts a stone.

One stone. A single piece of native rock, no larger than a fist, lodged in the channel at the point of maximum sedimentation. The power does not add this stone or conjure it—it is already there, part of the sediment, deposited by natural processes. The power moves it. Shifts it six inches downstream and rotates it so that its slightly concave face directs the trickle of water against the packed sediment behind it.

That's all. That's the first thing.

The redirected trickle begins to erode the sediment. Not quickly—this is the Shelf; nothing happens quickly—but steadily, grain by grain, the packed material loosens. Within a season, the channel's flow has increased by a small but measurable amount. Within a year, the eels have noticed (or their bodies have noticed, which amounts to the same thing) and have increased their traffic through the channel, their braiding columns scouring it further. Within a decade, the channel is running at something close to its historical capacity, and the downstream drainage pattern has shifted back toward its previous configuration.

The district that was getting slightly too wet dries back to normal. The district that was getting slightly too dry gets its moisture back.

And in the district that was getting slightly too dry, a feeding depression that had been producing a thinner-than-usual nutrient crust for decades begins to produce a thicker one.

And the runner column that feeds at this depression, which had been slightly smaller than its neighbors due to the reduced food supply, begins to grow.

And the growing column's hum shifts—not in frequency but in *complexity*, because more voices in the resonant space produce more interference patterns, more harmonics, more acoustic information propagating through the stone.

And the crawlers in the walls near this corridor, bathed in a marginally richer sonic environment, alter their patterns. Not dramatically. Not suddenly. But the almost-geometric becomes *slightly more* almost-geometric. One ripple in a thousand resolves into a clean line where before there were only approximations.

---

The power watches this and does nothing else for a hundred years.

## V. The Second Thing

The second thing is not a physical intervention. It is an *absence*.

Somewhere in the deep geology of the plateau, there is a fault—not a crack, not a danger, but a zone of slightly different mineral composition where two ancient rock formations meet. This fault produces, very occasionally, a localized seismic event: a tremor so minor that the surface dwellers never feel it, but strong enough to be registered by the deep systems—the worm-tangles, the cistern-filters, the whatever-it-is in the center.

These tremors have been part of the Shelf's environment forever. They are factored into every system, every cycle, every equilibrium. They are part of the rhythm.

One of them is scheduled—if geological processes can be said to schedule anything—to occur in approximately thirty years. It will be slightly larger than usual. Not dangerous. Not damaging. But enough to temporarily disrupt the drainage flow in the deep channels near the center, which will temporarily alter the nutrient concentration in the water, which will temporarily affect the worm-tangles, which will temporarily dim the bioluminescence in one district, which will temporarily disorient the juvenile eels emerging from the cisterns in that area.

The eels will recover. The worm-tangles will recover. Everything will recover. It has happened before and will happen again. But the recovery takes time and energy, and during the recovery period, certain feedback loops will be running at reduced efficiency, including the loop that connects the enriched feeding depression to the crawler patterns in the mid-levels.

The wise power reaches into the geology—not to prevent the tremor, which is part of the Shelf's deep rhythm and should not be silenced, but to *time* it. To shift it forward by eleven years, so that it occurs now, during a period when the enriched feeding depression has not yet produced its downstream effects. The disruption happens. The recovery happens. The system restores itself. And when, two decades later, the slowly growing runner column and the slowly complexifying crawler patterns reach the point where the tremor *would have* disrupted them, there is no tremor. There is instead an unusual period of deep stability—not engineered, just the natural aftermath of a tremor cycle that has already completed—during which the fragile new patterns have time to establish themselves, to deepen their grooves, to become part of the system rather than a perturbation on its surface.

The power has not added anything. It has not changed the geology. The same tremor occurred with the same magnitude. It simply occurred at a time when it would strengthen rather than weaken the thing that is trying to happen.

---

## VI. The Third Thing

This one takes a thousand years.

The power alters the path of a wind.

Not the wind on the Shelf—the upper-atmosphere circulation pattern that determines how much moisture reaches the plateau from the southern ocean. The alteration is minuscule: a fractional change in the temperature of a current three hundred kilometers to the south, caused by a fractional change in the reflectivity of the ocean surface, caused by a slight shift in the distribution of whatever microbial engine generates the world's oxygen down there. The power does not change the engine. It changes the nutrient upwelling in one small zone, which changes the microbial density, which changes the albedo, which changes the air temperature, which changes the wind, which changes the moisture delivery to the Shelf.

The change, when it arrives, is this: the Shelf gets approximately two percent more moisture during what passes for its rainy season.

Two percent.

Over decades, this two percent shifts every equilibrium in the system by a tiny amount. The drainage channels carry slightly more water. The biofilm grows slightly thicker. The eels grow slightly larger. The feeding crusts form slightly faster. The runner columns grow slightly bigger. The hum grows slightly richer. The crawlers—

The crawlers, bathed in richer acoustics, vibrating in the slightly wetter stone that transmits sound slightly better, building on surfaces that are slightly more amenable to their mineral secretions because the elevated moisture keeps their calcium paste workable for slightly longer before it cures—

The crawlers change.

Not all of them. Not everywhere. But in the mid-levels, in the district where the feeding depression was enriched a thousand years ago, in the corridors where the runner columns have been growing and the acoustic environment has been complexifying and the stone has been getting two percent wetter for centuries—there, the crawler patterns cross a threshold.

The almost-geometric becomes geometric.

Not everywhere at once. In one swarm, on one wall, for one brief period, the rippling movement of the carpet resolves into a pattern that is unambiguously structured: a recursive fractal, a branching form that mirrors the drainage network, a shape that encodes information about the architecture of the surrounding corridors in a medium of living, moving chitin. The pattern holds for an hour. Then it dissolves back into the almost-geometric, the habitual, the safe.

But it happened. And the crawlers on the adjacent wall saw it—or sensed it, through the vibrations it produced in the stone, through the faint chemical signature of the secretions it laid down during its brief coherence. And something in the aggregate behavior of those adjacent crawlers shifts. Not immediately. Not visibly. But the probability distribution of their own patterns has been altered by exposure to a pattern that was more ordered than any they have previously encountered, and the next time they approach their own threshold, they approach it from slightly closer.

The power watches this and feels something that, in a being capable of human emotion, would be called joy.

---

## VII. The Fourth Thing

The fourth thing concerns the small creatures on the terraces. The ones with the hands. The ones the narrator thought about more than anything else.

The power has not touched them. Not directly, not indirectly. Everything it has done so far has been in the mid-levels and below, adjusting the deep infrastructure of the Shelf's ecology, and the effects have been propagating upward slowly, through the layered systems, in ways that have not yet reached the high terraces where the small things live.

But the small things come down sometimes. At night—what passes for night, when the amber sun drops below the plateau edge and the shafts darken and the worm-tangles become the dominant light source in the mid and lower levels—the small things descend from their terraces and move through the upper corridors. They are not feeding; their diet is something else, something they catch in the crevices of the high terraces, small flying things or crawling things that live only up there. They come down for another reason.

They come down to watch the crawlers.

This is the thing the power noticed first, before it noticed the drainage channel, before it noticed the geological fault, before it noticed the wind. The small things sit in the corridors of the mid-levels, in the warm dark, their large eyes catching the bioluminescent light, and they watch the crawler patterns on the walls. They watch for hours. They are still, and silent, and their attention is total.

Sometimes, after watching, they go back to the terraces and stack stones in new ways. Ways that echo—faintly, imprecisely, as if seen through water—the patterns they watched on the walls.

The power understood, when it saw this, what it was seeing. It was seeing the first thread of a connection that could, given time and stability and the barest, gentlest push toward complexity in the crawler patterns, become something unprecedented on the Shelf. Not tool use—the small things already use tools, in their rudimentary way. Not language—their vocalizations are limited and context-specific. Something else. Something harder to name.

*Recognition.* The recognition of pattern *as* pattern—not as wall, not as movement, not as stimulus, but as a thing that can be seen and held in the mind and reproduced in a different medium. The small things watching the crawlers and then stacking stones are doing something that no other creature on the Shelf does: they are *translating*. They are taking information from one domain (the movement of crawlers on a vertical wall) and encoding it in another domain (the arrangement of stones on a horizontal terrace). This is abstraction. This is the seed of representation. This is, if you want to be dramatic about it and the power does not but I do, the first flicker of mind in a city that has run for a hundred thousand years on pure, mindless, exquisite process.

And it is guttering.

Because the crawler patterns are not complex enough. They are *almost* geometric, and the small things can see this, can sense the structure lurking in the noise, and they reach for it, and they cannot quite grasp it, and their stone-stacks remain ambiguous—are they representations? Are they play? Are they compulsive repetition without comprehension? The small things themselves do not know, because the signal they are trying to amplify is too weak, and without a stronger signal they cannot build the cognitive infrastructure needed to process a stronger signal, and this—*this*—is the trap. This is the local maximum. This is the point where the Shelf's perfection defeats itself, where the system's equilibrium holds so steady that the perturbation needed to push the small things across the threshold of genuine abstraction never arrives.

Until now.

Because the crawlers are changing. In one district, in one set of corridors, the patterns are crossing the threshold. The geometric is becoming available. And the small things will find it.

The power does not guide them there. The power does not need to. The small things already range across the mid-levels on their nightly descents, and they are drawn to crawler activity the way the eels are drawn to warm water—instinctively, bodily, without decision. They will find the new patterns. They will watch them. They will go back to the terraces and stack stones differently.

And—

And this is where the power does the fourth thing, and the fourth thing is the most delicate thing it has done, more delicate than the stone in the channel, more delicate than the timing of the tremor, more delicate than the wind.

The power makes one of the high terraces slightly more sheltered.

Not by building a wall or moving a boulder—by the same kind of slow, minimal intervention it has used throughout. A shift in the erosion pattern of the terrace's windward edge, caused by a slight change in the drainage above it, caused by a minor adjustment to a single cistern's overflow rate, causes the lip of the terrace to build up slightly over decades, creating a marginally more protected space behind it. The wind is less. The moisture is slightly higher. The temperature is a fraction of a degree warmer.

This terrace becomes, over generations, a slightly more attractive nesting site. The small things congregate there in slightly higher numbers. They are not crowded—the terrace is large—but they are *denser*, and density changes everything. More of them means more interactions. More interactions means more watching. More watching means more stone-stacking. More stone-stacking means more opportunities for one of them to see what another one has built and *respond* to it—not just imitate it but extend it, modify it, add to it, creating a dialogue in stone between two minds that are each, individually, just below the threshold of abstract thought but that together, in dialogue, might—

Might.

The power does not know. This is the terrifying, beautiful truth: the power does not know if it will work. Wisdom is not omniscience. The power has done everything it can to create the conditions for a possibility, and now the possibility must decide for itself whether to become real.

## VIII. What It Looks Like from Inside

A thousand years pass. Two thousand. The power watches.

The crawlers in the enriched district develop stable geometric patterns. These patterns spread—slowly, the way everything spreads on the Shelf—to adjacent districts, carried by swarms that split and merge and carry their pattern-vocabularies with them. The geometric does not replace the almost-geometric; it coexists with it, a new dialect in a very old language, used in some contexts and not others.

The runner columns in the enriched corridors grow larger and more acoustically complex. Their hum develops harmonics that propagate through the stone in new ways, carrying information—not meaning, not yet, but *structured* information, patterns of vibration that the crawlers respond to and that the crawlers' patterns, in turn, modulate. A feedback loop forms between the runners' hum and the crawlers' geometry—each shaping the other, each complexifying the other, a conversation between two species conducted in a medium of vibration and mineral secretion that neither species experiences as a conversation.

The small things on the sheltered terrace become denser, more social, more interactive. Their stone-stacking becomes more elaborate—taller structures, more varied arrangements, constructions that require multiple individuals to build because the stones are too heavy for one pair of hands. They develop, without fanfare and without anyone noticing, a rudimentary system of cooperative labor. Two of them lift a stone together. They must coordinate. They vocalize—short, sharp calls, different from their alarm calls, different from their contact calls. New sounds for a new activity. The sounds are rough and variable at first. Over generations, they stabilize.

The stone-stacks on the terrace begin to mirror—not copy, *mirror*, with variation and elaboration—the geometric crawler patterns on the walls below. The small things are not just translating now; they are *developing* the patterns, extending them, combining them, producing arrangements that are more complex than anything the crawlers have made, because the small things have something the crawlers do not: individual brains capable of holding a pattern in working memory and comparing it to another pattern and seeing the relationship between them.

They are thinking. Not in a way you would recognize as thought—not propositional, not linguistic, not reflective. But in the deep, bodily, *constructive* way that the first minds always think: by building. By making things in the world and then looking at what they have made and being changed by the looking. Their stone-stacks are externalized thoughts, cognitive scaffolding, the mind bootstrapping itself by using the world as its own working memory.

One day—and the power sees this, and the power trembles, and the power is as close to tears as a benevolent cosmic intelligence can be—one of the small things on the terrace places a stone on top of a small stack, steps back, looks at it, *removes* it, and places it somewhere else.

It has edited.

It has looked at something it made and judged it against an internal standard and found it wanting and *revised* it. This has never happened on the Pelvian Shelf before. This has never happened on this world before. This is the moment—not the first tool use, not the first cooperation, not the first vocalization, but the first *revision*—that marks the birth of mind, because revision requires a self that can hold two states simultaneously (what is and what should be) and choose between them.

The other small things on the terrace watch this happen.

Some of them begin to edit their own stacks.

---

## IX. What It Does Not Become

The wise power does not congratulate itself. It does not declare victory. It does not set up a monitoring station or a development program or a plan for the next ten thousand years. It has done what it came to do, which was not to create intelligence—it cannot create intelligence, no one can, intelligence creates itself or it is not intelligence—but to create the conditions in which intelligence could, if it chose, emerge from a system that had been too perfect to need it and too stable to produce it.

The small things may flourish. They may develop language—real language, with syntax and reference and the ability to lie, which is the hallmark of a mind that can model other minds. They may learn to read the crawler patterns not just as visual stimuli but as *records*, may discover that the walls of the mid-levels contain, in the mineral secretions of a million generations of crawlers, a history of the city that no living creature has ever accessed. They may descend to the center and find whatever is there and be changed by it. They may build.

Or they may not. They may plateau again, at a higher level than before but still short of whatever threshold would make them something you would call a civilization. The terrace may erode. The enriched district may shift. The climate may change in ways the power cannot predict. A thousand things could intervene, and the power will not prevent them all, because the power is benevolent but not totalitarian, and a mind that is protected from every setback is a mind that never develops the resilience to survive the setbacks that no protector can anticipate.

What the power has given the Shelf is not an outcome. It is an *opening*. A crack in the perfection. A space where the merely good can stop being the enemy of the genuinely new. The Shelf was a masterpiece, and it will remain a masterpiece, and the small things with their stacked stones may or may not become its next chapter, and the power has made its peace with the uncertainty because the alternative—certainty, control, a guaranteed result—would not be wisdom. It would be authorship. And the Shelf is not the power's story to write.

---

## X. The Last Thing

Before it withdraws its attention—not permanently, it will check back, in the way that a gardener checks on a garden, occasionally, without anxiety—the power does one more thing. The last thing. The smallest thing, even smaller than the first.

On the sheltered terrace, among the stone-stacks of the small things, there is a particular arrangement that one of them has been working on for days. It is the most complex construction any of them has yet produced: a miniature cairn, built with evident care, stones selected for shape and balanced with precision, rising in a rough spiral that echoes—the power can see this even if the builder cannot fully articulate it—the form of the drainage fractal that underlies the entire city. It is beautiful. It is the first beautiful thing that has been deliberately made on the Pelvian Shelf, as opposed to the countless beautiful things that have arisen from process and time and accident.

The small thing that built it comes back to it in the morning light, sits on its haunches in front of it, and looks at it with those large, forward-facing, predator, *artist* eyes.

A wind comes across the terrace. A slightly stronger gust than usual, channeled by the new lip of rock that the power shaped decades ago. The cairn trembles.

The power stills the wind.

Not forever. Not even for long. Just for this moment. Just for this morning. Just long enough for the small thing to finish looking at what it has made and to feel, in whatever way its newly-flickering mind can feel, the satisfaction of a thing that holds together, the pleasure of a pattern that works, the first, faint, unmistakable warmth of meaning in a world that has operated for a hundred thousand years without any.

The wind resumes. The cairn stands.

The power withdraws.

The city breathes. The city digests. The city circulates.

The water drips.

The eels rasp.

The runners hum.

And on a high terrace, in the amber light of a slightly-too-large sun, a small thing with clever hands and careful eyes sits before a tower of stacked stones and sees, for the first time in the history of this world, that something it has made is good.
