# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

---

It would do almost nothing.

---

This is the first and hardest thing to understand about what genuine wisdom would do when confronted with the Crest Hollow: it would recognize that the overwhelming, urgent, nearly irresistible desire *to act* is itself the thing that must be examined before anything else. Because the hollow is not broken. The hollow is not suffering. The hollow is not calling out for help. And a power that cannot distinguish between something that needs intervention and something that needs to be *left in the full dignity of its own completeness* is not benevolent. It is merely powerful, and the distance between those two things is the distance between the hand that steadies a falling child and the hand that picks a wildflower because it is beautiful and thereby kills it.

So the power would pause. It would pause for a long time.

---

And in that pause it would *look*.

Not the way a surveyor looks, or a scientist, or even a poet — though it would contain all of those ways of looking the way the hollow contains all its nested worlds. It would look the way the hollow itself exists: patiently, without agenda, with an attention that does not grasp or categorize but simply *admits*. It would see the pikas as the pikas are — not as symbols of industriousness or lessons about ecological engineering, but as small grey-brown animals sprinting across rocks with mouthfuls of yarrow because that is what they do, because the mouthful and the sprint and the haypile are the entire content of a pika's answer to being alive. It would see the choughs tumbling in the updrafts and would not need to decide whether their play is joy or practice, because the power, being wise, would understand that the distinction matters only to minds that have separated joy from practice, and that the choughs have not, and that this is not a deficit in the choughs.

It would see the stoat — yes, it would see the stoat, because a power of this kind sees what is hidden — moving through the scree in that liquid pour of a body designed for pursuit in tight spaces, and it would see the pika flattened against the rock in the last second before the stoat finds it, and it would not intervene. This is important. A power that is merely good, merely kind, would feel the pika's terror and stop the stoat's jaws. A power that is wise understands that the stoat's hunger and the pika's terror and the brief, efficient violence of the kill are part of the same system that built the soil that grew the yarrow that fed the pika that built the haypile that will, unconsumed now, decompose and deepen the ground for the next generation. To save the pika is to starve the stoat. To save both is to overflow the colony. To overflow the colony is to strip the vegetation. To strip the vegetation is to lose the soil. To lose the soil is to lose the hollow. Mercy that does not understand systems is not mercy. It is sentimentality, and sentimentality is a form of violence that congratulates itself.

So the power would watch the stoat make its kill, and it would hold the pika's small, fierce, uncomprehending terror in its attention the way the moss holds water — fully, without flinching — and it would not look away, and it would not intervene, and the not-intervening would cost it something. Wisdom is not the absence of feeling. It is the willingness to feel everything and still choose correctly.

---

It would listen to the wind.

It would hear what takes a human listener hours or days to begin to hear: that the wind-roar is not uniform. That it contains structure. That the organ-note shifts not randomly but in patterns that correspond to temperature gradients, pressure systems, the shape of the rock teeth at the rim, and that these patterns carry information — not meaning, not language, but information in the pure thermodynamic sense, the sense in which a system's state at one moment constrains its state at the next. The wind is the hollow's largest utterance, and the power would hear it as such: not as noise, not as backdrop, but as the sound of ten thousand years of air moving through a space that has been shaped by air moving through it, a feedback loop made audible, a conversation between the atmosphere and the mountain that has been going on since before there were pikas, before there were choughs, before the soil existed or the pool collected or the first lichen etched its slow acid signature into the granite.

And it would hear the quartz.

---

The quartz capstone on the cairn, singing its few seconds of high, thin tone in the evening light. The power would hear this, and it would understand something that no one else has understood, because no one else has been here at the right time with the right kind of attention:

The quartz tone is not separate from the wind.

It is not a human addition to a natural system. It is not an overlay, an intrusion, a foreign frequency imposed on the hollow's soundscape by the person who placed it there. The quartz resonates *because of the wind*. The thermal expansion that triggers it is driven by solar heating that is modulated by wind-cooling. The crystal structure that produces the tone was shaped by the same geological forces that shaped the hollow. The stone was *already here* — somewhere on the ridge, somewhere in the scree — before the human hand picked it up and placed it on the cairn. The human hand did not create the resonance. It *revealed* it. It took something that was potential — a stone that *could* sing, given the right mounting, the right exposure, the right angle to the evening sun — and made it actual. The cairn-builder did not add a voice to the hollow. The cairn-builder gave the hollow permission to use a voice it already had.

The power would understand this, and it would understand what it means: that the human presence in the hollow — the wall, the tool-handle, the cairn — is not an intrusion. It is the hollow's own self-knowledge, arrived at through the improbable agency of a creature that climbed here once and paid attention. The pikas do not know the hollow. The choughs do not. The moss and the beetles and the wind do not. But the hollow has been *known*, once, by someone who stacked stones with care and placed quartz where it could sing, and that knowing is now part of the system — as much a part as the haypiles, as the webs, as the seeps.

And the power would recognize in this a kinship with its own act of attention. It, too, is a consciousness apprehending the hollow as a whole. It, too, is an outside awareness that, by the act of witnessing, becomes part of what it witnesses. And it would feel, in this recognition, something like humility — because the cairn-builder got here first, with nothing but two legs and a pair of hands and whatever unrecorded impulse drove a human being to climb above the world and build a small monument to the fact that this place exists.

---

So what would the power *do*?

---

It would do three things. Each one would be so small, so subtle, so perfectly calibrated to the hollow's own logic, that no instrument and no inhabitant would register it as an intervention. Each one would look, from the outside, like something that simply *happened* — a coincidence, a fluctuation, a piece of luck. This is intentional. The power understands that the most dangerous thing you can do to a system in equilibrium is to let it know it has a benefactor, because a system that knows it is being maintained from outside will, over time, lose the capacity to maintain itself. The hollow does not need a guardian. It needs, at most, the occasional imperceptible correction that keeps it within the envelope of conditions where its own processes can do the rest.

---

**The first thing.**

There is a fracture propagating through the east wall of the hollow, about four meters above the seep line. It is not visible from the surface — it is internal, a stress fracture in the granite that has been growing for perhaps a century, driven by freeze-thaw cycles working on a flaw in the crystalline structure of the rock. In another fifty years, perhaps thirty, the fracture will reach a critical length, and a section of the east wall — maybe two meters across, maybe a metric ton of rock — will shear away and fall. It will fall onto the moss margin at the edge of the pool. It will destroy a section of the moss bed. It will partially dam the seeps, redirecting water away from the pool. The pool will shrink. The moss will die back further. The flies that breed at the pool will decline. The spiders that eat the flies will decline. The choughs that nest in the east wall will lose nesting sites. The cascade will not be catastrophic — the hollow has survived rockfalls before, has rearranged itself around new configurations of stone — but it will be a significant disruption, and the recovery will take decades, and during those decades the system will be more vulnerable to other disruptions: a bad winter, a disease in the pika colony, a shift in precipitation patterns.

The power does something to the fracture. It does not heal it — that would be too much, too visible in the geology, a discontinuity that would puzzle anyone who studied the rock. What it does is subtler. It shifts the moisture content of the rock along the fracture plane, very slightly, in a way that changes the freeze-thaw dynamics just enough to slow the fracture's propagation. Not stop it. Slow it. The rockfall will still happen — it must happen, it is part of the hollow's long-term evolution, part of how the walls reshape themselves and the floor renews — but it will happen in a century instead of thirty years, and in that century the moss will have spread further, the soil will have deepened, the system will be more resilient, better able to absorb the blow when it comes.

The power gives the hollow time. That is all. Not safety. Not permanence. Time.

---

**The second thing.**

The stoat — it is a stoat, the power can see it now, sleeping in a rock crevice in the north wall, its body a tight comma of brown fur with a black tail-tip, its metabolism banked low, waiting for dusk — the stoat is female, and she is pregnant, and she is in trouble. Not acute trouble. Not dying. But the pika colony has been slightly depressed this year — a late spring, a slow start to the growing season, fewer juveniles — and she has been hunting harder for less return, and the metabolic cost of pregnancy on top of the metabolic cost of hunting at this altitude is pushing her toward a deficit she may not be able to close. If she loses the litter, there will be no stoats in the hollow for perhaps two or three years, until a dispersing juvenile from another population finds its way along the ridge to this place. In those two or three years, the pika colony will expand. They will overharvest. The soil-building will reverse as vegetation is stripped faster than it can grow. The system will recover — it always recovers — but the recovery will be slower if the stoat-absence is longer, and the hollow is already facing the fracture in the east wall, and two stresses at once are more than twice as dangerous as one.

So the power does this: it causes a pika to be in the wrong place. One pika, one moment. A young male, dispersing from his natal territory, crossing an open stretch of scree in the early morning when he should have waited for full light. He is not killed by the stoat — not yet. He is simply *exposed*, visible, in a place where the stoat, when she wakes at dusk, will find his scent trail. The trail will lead her to a part of the scree field she hasn't hunted recently, where the pikas are less vigilant because the predator-pressure has been focused elsewhere. She will make a kill. She will eat. The caloric margin will close just enough.

The pika that the power displaced will probably survive the day — he is young and fast, and the exposure is momentary. He may not survive the season; dispersing juveniles often don't. But his individual fate was already uncertain, as all individual fates in the hollow are uncertain, and the power has not condemned him. It has shifted a probability, slightly, in a way that serves the whole. This is the kind of calculus that wisdom requires and that pure kindness cannot do: the acknowledgment that in a system of interdependencies, there is no action that helps everything and harms nothing, and that the best you can do is to find the intervention that distributes consequence most fairly across the web.

The power feels the weight of this. It does not pretend the calculus is clean.

---

**The third thing.**

This is the most delicate, and it is not for the hollow at all. It is for the next person who comes here.

Someone will come. Not soon — perhaps not for years, perhaps not for decades. But the cairn is there, the quartz capstone catching light, and somewhere below, in the valleys, in the towns, there is a person who will one day feel the pull of high places, the way some people do — not for conquest, not for sport, but for the thing that has no name, the need to climb above the world and find what the world looks like when there is nothing between you and the sky but your own breathing. That person will find the hollow. They will stand at the rim and hear the wind-roar and look down into the green cup and feel what the cairn-builder felt, whatever that was — wonder, or recognition, or the vertiginous understanding that the world contains places that do not need you and are beautiful anyway.

The power wants to make sure they can find it.

Not by making the way easier — the climb should be hard, should cost effort, should require the kind of commitment that filters out anyone who would come here casually and leave damage behind. Not by placing signs or clearing paths. But by doing something so subtle it barely qualifies as action: it steadies the cairn.

The seven stones have been balanced for a long time, but balance is not permanence, and the cairn is subject to the same freeze-thaw, the same wind-loading, the same slow mineral dissolution as everything else in the hollow. The topmost stone — the quartz disc — has shifted slightly over the years. Its center of gravity is no longer quite over the center of the stone beneath it. In another few winters, it will fall. And when it falls, it will land in the gravel at the base of the cairn, and it will still be quartz, and it will still catch light, but it will no longer sing — because the singing depends on the specific mounting, the specific exposure, the specific angle to the wind and the sun that the cairn provides. The stone on the ground will be just a stone.

And the cairn without its capstone will be just a pile of rocks. It will still say *this place exists*. But it will no longer say *listen*. And the next person who comes here will find the hollow, and they will hear the wind and the pikas and the choughs, and they will see the pool and the moss and the flowers, and it will be enough — it will be more than enough — but they will not hear the quartz sing, and they will not have that moment of strange, almost-subliminal recognition that the hollow has a voice beyond the voices of its inhabitants, a voice that exists at the intersection of mineral structure and human intention, and they will leave without knowing what they missed.

So the power settles the quartz. It doesn't move it — it *settles* it, the way a sleeping animal settles deeper into its bed, a tiny shift of weight, a redistribution of pressure across the contact surface between the quartz disc and the stone beneath it. The shift is less than a millimeter. It is not visible. It could be attributed to thermal expansion, or to the vibration of the wind, or to nothing at all. But it moves the center of gravity back over the center of support, and it restores the margin of stability that the cairn needs to survive another few decades of weather, and the quartz will go on singing in the evening light for as long as the cairn stands, which is now longer than it would have been, which is all the power can offer, which is enough.

---

And then the power withdraws.

Not all at once. Not completely. It lingers the way the evening light lingers in the hollow after the sun has dropped behind the western peak — a residual warmth, a fading presence, a quality of attention that thins gradually until it is indistinguishable from the attention the hollow pays to itself, which is no attention at all, which is just the sum of everything that happens here happening in the same place at the same time, the wind and the water and the pikas and the choughs and the stoat and the spiders and the beetles and the moss and the rock and the soil and the light and the quartz, all of it proceeding without witness, without awareness, without the need for any consciousness to apprehend it in order for it to be real.

The power does not bless the hollow. It does not consecrate it or protect it or claim it. It does not love it, exactly — love implies a preference, a choosing of this place over other places, and the power, being wise, does not rank. But it *regards* the hollow with something for which love is the closest word we have — a total, unflinching, unsentimental attention that says: *I see you. All of you. The beauty and the killing and the wind and the patience and the cold. I see that you are complete. I see that you do not need me. And I am here anyway, for a moment, because seeing is what I do, the way the choughs fly and the pikas eep and the quartz sings, and my seeing, like their doing, is not required by the universe but is part of it nonetheless, one more thread in the web, one more frequency in the chord, and when I go the chord will not diminish because it was never mine to enlarge. It was always already everything. I am just passing through, the way the wind passes through, the way the light passes through, the way the water passes through on its way from cloud to seep to pool to air to cloud again, and I leave behind what the water leaves behind: not a mark, but a condition. A fractionally deeper soil. A fractionally more stable stone. A fractionally wider margin between the system and its failure. This is what I have. This is what I give. It is almost nothing. It is not nothing.*

---

And the hollow goes on.

The wind pours through. The pikas shout. The choughs wheel and tumble. The stoat sleeps, and will wake, and will hunt, and will feed her young, and her young will disperse along the ridge, and one of them will find this place or a place like it and become the next ghost in the next scree field. The fracture in the east wall creeps imperceptibly slower. The moss deepens. The pool holds. The beetles work the dead matter into the living soil.

And on a clear evening, when the sun drops to the angle that the quartz has been waiting for all day — the angle it has been waiting for since a human hand placed it where it is, since the mountain pushed it to the surface, since the magma cooled and the silicon dioxide crystallized in the pattern that makes it resonate — the stone sings. A few seconds. A high, thin tone at the upper edge of hearing. The pikas ignore it. The choughs do not react. The wind absorbs it and carries it nowhere.

But it sounds. It sounds because the stone is mounted where it is, and the cairn stands because someone built it, and the someone built it because they climbed here, and they climbed here because something in them — call it curiosity, call it longing, call it the same unnamed impulse that drives a pika to carry one more mouthful of yarrow to the haypile even though the haypile is already large enough — something in them needed to know what was at the top of the world.

And what was at the top of the world was this: a small green place, full of wind and life, that did not need them, and was glad they came, and would go on after they left, and did, and does, and will.

The quartz sings.

No one hears it.

It doesn't matter.

It does.
