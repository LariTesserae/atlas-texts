# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

---

## I. Arrival

It would not descend from above.

This matters. A power arriving from the sky — breaking through the pewter cloudbank, parting the low clouds with light, descending in radiance — would be an act of *resolution*. It would impose a direction on the light, a hierarchy on the space, a clarity that the city has not asked for and does not need. The sky over Kethane-Above is edited for a reason. The dimness is not a failure. It is a condition of becoming. To pierce it would be to answer a question the plateau has not yet finished asking.

So the power would not come from above.

It would come the way everything meaningful comes to Kethane-Above: on the cable car.

---

It would board at the bottom station — wherever below is, whatever solidity that entails — and it would sit in the riveted steel capsule and it would feel the cloudbank close around it. The cold. The disorientation. The sounds like static-speech, like something vast breathing. And the power would *listen*, because a wise power listens before it acts, and what it would hear in the cloudbank is this: a membrane. Not a barrier. A *threshold of translation*, where the rules of one world give way to the rules of another, and the passage between them is not seamless but *deliberate*, requiring attention, requiring willingness, requiring that you let the settled world release its grip on your assumptions before the unsettled world can receive you.

The power would let itself be received.

It would step out of the cable car at the north station, where Dekken Farr stands in his gray luminescence watching gauges and listening to things he has never described, and Dekken Farr would look at the power and know immediately what it was — not because the power announced itself, not because it glowed brighter than the citizens or moved with supernatural grace, but because Dekken Farr experiences the next few seconds before they arrive, and the few seconds containing this being's presence have a quality he has never encountered: they feel *complete*. Not resolved in the way below is resolved — hard, definite, finished. Complete the way a chord is complete. Every note present, every tension acknowledged, nothing collapsed into premature certainty.

Dekken Farr would look at the power and say nothing and step slightly aside, which is the most generous welcome he has ever offered anyone.

And the power would step onto the plateau, and it would feel the ground note come up through its feet, and it would understand.

---

## II. What It Would See

The power would walk Kethane-Above the way the newcomers are asked to walk it: as a witness. It would take the role seriously. A wise power does not skip steps. It does not arrive knowing. It arrives *willing to know*, which is different, which requires the humility to let a place be more complex than your mercy.

It would walk the leftward-pulling street and feel the gravity tug at its body — even its body, even a body that could presumably override such things — and it would *let itself lean*. It would not correct the anomaly. It would lean the way the workers lean, weight shifted, and it would notice the asymmetric wheels on the carts and the trapezoidal doorways and it would understand that these are not problems awaiting solutions. These are *adaptations*. These are the physical vocabulary of a people who have learned to conjugate themselves to a shifting grammar, and the grammar is not wrong. The grammar is *young*.

It would enter the Soft Quarter and run its hand along the moss-furred buildings, feeling the dense nap of it, warm and wet and alive, and it would see the climbers surface with their black mirror eyes and regard it, and it would regard them back, and neither would flinch, and both would know: *we are part of this. We are both expressions of what the plateau is becoming.*

It would stand in an empty lot after rain and hear the spiral-stalks hum, that sub-audible pressure, and it would recognize the sound. Not because it had heard it before. Because it recognized the *type* of sound. The sound of something organizing itself. The sound of pattern emerging from potential. The sound, if you will, that the universe made once, when it was young and undecided and buzzing with the energy of what it might become. The power would hear this and feel a tenderness so deep it is almost grief — the tenderness of recognizing something ancient in something new, of seeing a process you know the full arc of still in its earliest, most vulnerable stages.

It would visit the old quarter and see the temporal echoes. The translucent workers walking to shifts that ended decades ago. The rain falling on dry streets. The woman with her laundry, snapping in a wind that isn't now. And the power would see what Lenne Vas sees, what the old ones see: that these are not hauntings. They are *all the moments this place has been*, held in suspension, unstacked, because the plateau has not yet learned to sequentialize time, because *sequentializing time is a decision* and the plateau has not yet made it, and until it does, all moments are equally present, equally real, equally part of the ongoing draft.

The power would find this beautiful. And it would leave it alone.

---

## III. What It Would Not Do

This is the most important section. The heart of it. Because the first and most dangerous impulse of any benevolent power — even a wise one — upon encountering Kethane-Above would be to *fix* it.

The reality is unstable. The rules are shifting. The people are slowly becoming translucent. The ground is undecided. The light is wrong. The gravity misbehaves. The time layers. The distances lie. The rock has not committed to being rock. The city is built on an unfinished sentence.

A lesser power — a power that was benevolent but not wise, or wise but not patient — would see this as suffering. Would see the workers' tiredness, the miners' transformation, the old ones' slow dissolution into luminous ambiguity, and would think: *these people need stability. These people need the ground to be ground and the sky to be sky and their bodies to be opaque and their time to flow forward and their world to make sense.*

And it could do this. A power of sufficient scope could *decide* the plateau. Could reach into the nacreous rock and say: *you are basalt now, everywhere, permanently.* Could straighten the gravity, regularize the distances, sequentialize the time, brighten the light, seal the borders between biology and geology, between present and past, between flesh and stone. Could make Kethane-Above into a normal city on a normal mountain, and the citizens would wake up one morning with opaque skin and predictable streets and a sky that behaved, and they would breathe the thin air without their adapted lungs and they would gasp, and their children's fingers would not glow, and the burrowers would be just animals, and the ground note would fall silent, and the old ones —

The old ones would die.

Not metaphorically. Essant, who is visible mainly by the warmth of the air around her, who sees all times at once, who speaks with the precision of someone choosing words from every moment she inhabits — Essant has become what she is *through* the plateau's indeterminacy. Her body, her consciousness, her way of being alive — these are adaptations to an unfinished world. Fix the world and you unmake the adaptation. Resolve the rock and you resolve her, and what she resolves *into*, in a world that demands opacity and sequence and fixed edges, is nothing. Is absence. Is a woman-shaped hole where a woman-shaped luminescence used to be.

And not just Essant. The miners, whose translucent skin is a negotiation with the pith they handle. The engineers, whose flexible rulers and polytonal tuning forks are instruments of a science that only exists here. The children, who play in dilated lots with an intuitive physics that is native to them and that would become *disability* in a fixed world — an attunement to signals that no longer exist, a readiness for changes that no longer come. The cable-car operators, whose looseness, whose temporal sensitivity, whose gray-luminous skin are the marks of a life lived in translation between decided and undecided worlds — fix the world and the translation becomes meaningless, and they become people who are slightly out of step with a reality that no longer needs them to be in step, and this is its own kind of death.

The wise power would see all of this in the first hour. It would see that Kethane-Above is not a disaster. It is not a wound. It is not a place where something has gone wrong.

It is a place where something is going *slowly*.

And the power would understand — because wisdom is largely the ability to distinguish between *suffering that should be relieved* and *difficulty that is producing something irreplaceable* — that what Kethane-Above needs is not resolution. Not rescue. Not the imposition of completed rules upon an incomplete world.

What Kethane-Above needs is almost nothing.

And almost nothing is the hardest gift to give wisely.

---

## IV. What It Would Do: The Small Things

The power would stay for a while. Not forever. Not as a ruler or a guardian or a savior. As a resident. As a witness who has been given the role and who takes it seriously.

And during its stay, it would do small things. Things so small they might not be recognized as the acts of a power at all. Things that look, from the outside, like the ordinary kindnesses of a neighbor who is paying attention.

---

**The air.**

The power would do something about the air. Not fix it — not thicken it to sea-level density, which would disrupt the deep-chested adaptation that generations have developed, which would make the citizens' lungs suddenly too large for their atmosphere and cause a different kind of discomfort. But the eighty-percent air — the slight insufficiency of every breath — this is not a feature of the plateau's becoming. This is not part of the indeterminacy. This is altitude. This is physics, even here. And there is a difference between the productive difficulty of living in an unfinished world and the grinding, unnecessary difficulty of never quite getting enough oxygen.

The power would — gently, over the course of months, so gradually that no one could pinpoint when it began — *enrich* the air. Not to full sea-level density. To perhaps ninety, ninety-two percent. Enough that the slight headache newcomers feel for their first weeks would ease. Enough that the workers on the conveyor lines would find, without understanding why, that their shifts feel fractionally less exhausting. Enough that the children, running in the empty lots, would have a tiny surplus of breath, and this surplus would become laughter more often, and the laughter would ring in the dim air, and the spiral-stalks would hum in response, and the ridge-soarers on the rooftops would tilt their discoid faces toward the sound with what might be curiosity.

The deep-chestedness would remain. It would remain because it is useful, because it is adaptive, because it is part of who the people of Kethane-Above have become. But it would no longer be a response to deprivation. It would be a capacity. A reservoir. Lungs that were shaped by necessity would discover that they now had *room* — room to breathe slowly not because they must but because they choose to, room to be deliberate not from scarcity but from abundance.

This is the kind of gift a wise power gives: not the removal of a challenge, but the transmutation of a deprivation into a strength.

---

**The pockets.**

The miners fear the pockets. The sudden resolution events, where undecided rock makes a snap decision and anyone caught inside that decision is subject to its terms. Crushed. Sheared. Transformed. The miners' ritual — touching the rock, waiting for the consideration, announcing themselves — mitigates this, but it does not eliminate it. Pockets still happen. Miners are still injured. Some are killed, though the city does not keep precise records of this, or the records are temporally layered and contradictory.

The power would not eliminate pockets. The plateau is deciding what it is, and part of deciding is the occasional sudden commitment — the moment when indeterminacy collapses into specificity. This is natural. This is necessary. To prevent it would be to arrest the plateau's development, to keep the rock in permanent adolescence, never committing, never becoming.

But the power would do this: it would *slow the pockets down*.

Not by much. Not enough to make them undangerous — danger in the mines is honest danger, and the miners' respect for the rock is built on their understanding that the rock is not tame, and this respect is itself part of the relationship that makes the mining work. But the snap decisions — the instant resolutions — these are not necessary. The plateau does not *need* to decide in a millisecond. It can decide in a minute. In five minutes. In long enough for the vibration in the rock to change its character, for the ground note to shift its timbre, for the miners to feel the consideration change — from the warm, open hum of thinking-rock to the tighter, more directional frequency of rock-that-is-about-to-commit — and to move. To step back. To get their hands and faces and luminous forearms out of the space where the decision is about to happen.

The power would reach into the plateau — not to its mind, which it does not have in that way, but into its *tempo*, its rhythm of becoming — and it would introduce a hesitation. A pause. The geological equivalent of a breath before speaking. And this pause would save lives, dozens of them over the decades that follow, and it would cost the plateau nothing — an instant's patience before each resolution, a moment of grace between the last second of possibility and the first second of certainty.

The miners would notice. They would feel the change in the ground note, the new quality of *warning* before a pocket event. And they would adjust their rituals accordingly, adding a new gesture — perhaps a double press of the palms, or a murmured word — and they would attribute the change to the rock's growing familiarity with them, to the deepening of a relationship measured in generations, and they would be right. They would be right because the power's gift is not separate from the relationship — it is an amplification of what was already there. The rock already paused for the miners who announced themselves. The power would simply make the pause longer, wider, more forgiving.

---

**The light in the old quarter.**

The temporal echoes in the old quarter are thickening. They have been thickening for a century. They are now, in places, nearly opaque — translucent figures so solid that newcomers cannot always tell them from present-day residents, which leads to moments of confused, poignant interaction: a newcomer greeting a woman who turns out to be a moment from thirty years ago, the greeting passing through her like sound through glass, the newcomer left standing with an extended hand and a feeling of disorientation that the old quarter's residents recognize and treat gently.

The power would not thin the echoes. The echoes are *part of the old quarter*. They are its memory, its depth, its visible history. The old ones who live among them navigate them like a rich, layered text, and to simplify that text — to reduce its layers, to push moments back into sequence — would be to impoverish the place and the people who have learned to read it.

But the power would adjust the *light* in the old quarter. Subtly. So that the temporal echoes and the present-day residents are distinguishable not by solidity (both are translucent, the echoes by nature and the old ones by adaptation) but by *warmth*. The present-day residents would glow, very faintly, with a warmth — not physical heat, not visible light exactly, but a quality, a *thereness* — that the echoes would lack. The difference would be perceptible to anyone paying attention: the echoes would be cool, silvery, still; the living would be warm, faintly amber, breathing.

This would help the newcomers. It would help the old ones, too, though they would be slower to admit it. Essant, on her bench, would notice that the echoes around her had acquired a new quality of *pastness* that made her own presentness — tenuous as it is, ambiguous as it is — feel more definite by contrast. She would still see all times at once. But the time she *is* in would feel, very slightly, more like home.

---

**Harath Senn's theory.**

The chief engineer is right. The power would know this immediately, would see it in the way the stamp mills' vibrations propagate through the rock and subtly influence its crystallization patterns, in the way the burrowers' tunnels leave trails of resolved stone, in the way generations of human warmth and weight and chemical presence have nudged the plateau's indeterminacy in directions it might not have gone alone. Harath Senn is right: the city is not a passenger on the plateau's process. It is a participant. The mountain is deciding what it is in the context of being lived on, and the living matters, and the choices matter.

The power would not tell Harath Senn she is right. Not directly. A wise power does not validate by fiat. Validation by fiat is a form of resolution — it collapses the productive uncertainty of intellectual inquiry into the dead certainty of revealed truth, and Harath Senn's theory is more valuable as a living question than it would be as an answered one.

But the power would do this: it would find a way to bring Harath Senn and Lenne Vas together.

These two women — the engineer and the philosopher — are working on the same problem from opposite sides. Harath Senn understands the *physical* mechanics of the plateau's becoming: how human presence affects geological process, how infrastructure votes on reality, how the burrowers' tunnels resolve the rock. Lenne Vas understands the *experiential* dimension: what it means to live inside becoming, how the citizens' consciousness has been shaped by indeterminacy, what *ground-sense* is and why it matters. Together, they would constitute the closest thing to a complete understanding of Kethane-Above that any two humans could achieve.

But they don't talk to each other. Harath Senn regards Lenne Vas's work as poetic but imprecise. Lenne Vas regards Harath Senn's work as precise but unimaginative. They move in different circles, breathe the same eighty-percent air (ninety-two, now, gradually), walk the same leftward-pulling streets, and do not converge.

The power would create a reason for them to converge.

Not a crisis — not a forced collaboration against some shared threat. Kethane-Above does not need crises; it has a slowly accumulating reality-loss that provides all the urgency anyone could want, distributed over centuries in a way that permits patience. Instead, the power would create a *question*. A new anomaly — subtle, slow, in the power's nature to introduce without violence — that neither the engineer nor the philosopher could fully explain alone.

Perhaps: a place where the rock has resolved into something unprecedented. Not basalt, not crystal, not chalk, not any mineral that has a name. Something that, when Harath Senn tests it with her tuning fork, produces a note that is harmonically related to the ground note but an octave above it — a note that has never been heard in the plateau before, a note that suggests the rock has not merely *decided* what it is but has decided in a way that is *responsive*, that incorporates information, that sounds like — if you are willing to hear it this way, and Harath Senn is an engineer and therefore resistant to hearing things this way, but the note is clear, the note is unmistakable — it sounds like an *answer*.

An answer to what? Harath Senn doesn't know. She can describe the frequency, the harmonic relationship, the mineral composition of the new rock. She can say what it is. She cannot say what it *means*.

For that, she needs Lenne Vas. Whose changed palm feels vibrations at frequencies no one else can detect. Who has been trying to communicate with the plateau for years. Who has claimed the plateau responded — not in words, but in a change in the quality of its consideration.

The power would place this new anomaly where both women would find it. And then it would wait.

---

**The children.**

The power would spend time with the children.

Not teaching them. Not guiding them. Not enhancing their abilities or accelerating their development. Just *being with them*, in the empty lots, in the dim light, watching them play their impossible games in the dilated spaces, watching them press their small glowing hands against the rock and listen with a seriousness that is also joy.

Because the children are the plateau's future citizens, and they are also, in a sense, the plateau's future *collaborators* — the generation that will be most thoroughly woven into its process, most native to its rules, most capable of participating consciously in its becoming. And what the children need from a wise power is not instruction but *recognition*. Someone who sees what they are doing — listening to the rock, reading the ground note, navigating spatial anomalies with laughing precision — and understands that it is not childish play but *practice*. Rehearsal for a role that no one has fully defined yet because the world in which the role will be performed has not finished defining itself.

The power would play their games with them. Would throw the ball in the dilated lot and watch it travel its absurd trajectory and would adjust its throw the way the children adjust theirs — not by calculation but by feel, by ground-sense, by the body's willingness to learn from a world that is still learning what it is. And the children would look at the power with their wide, dark, adapted eyes and would see — not a god, not a savior, not even an adult who is pretending to understand — but a *player*. Someone who is inside the game. Someone who is, like them, willing to be unfinished.

And this would be a gift beyond reckoning: the knowledge, transmitted not in words but in shared laughter, in the arc of a ball through impossible space, in the touch of glowing fingers on thinking rock, that *being unfinished is not a flaw to be corrected. It is a capacity to be inhabited. It is the beginning of everything.*

---

## V. What It Would Do: The Large Thing

But there is one large thing.

One thing that a wise and benevolent power would see that the citizens of Kethane-Above cannot see, because they are inside it, because you cannot see the shape of the sentence you are part of.

The plateau is going to finish.

Not soon. Not in any single lifetime, perhaps not in many lifetimes. But the process has a direction. The rock is resolving. The rules are, slowly, becoming less provisional. The spatial anomalies drift but they drift toward stability — a leftward-pulling gravity that increases by imperceptible degrees is a gravity that is *settling*, finding its final angle, approaching a permanent state. The temporal layering thickens but it thickens in a way that suggests accumulation toward a critical mass, after which the plateau will have to choose how time works here, will have to either commit to simultaneity or commit to sequence. The material sympathy is spreading but it is also *refining*, becoming more predictable, more systematic, approaching something that could be called a law rather than an anomaly.

The plateau is going to finish. And when it finishes, one of two things will happen.

In the first case, the plateau finishes *around* the city. The rules that solidify are rules that have been shaped by centuries of human presence — by the stamp mills and the burrowers and the warmth and weight and attention of generations — and the resulting reality is one that humans can inhabit, not comfortably (this place will never be comfortable) but *coherently*. The spatial anomalies settle into a stable, navigable non-Euclidean geometry. The temporal layering resolves into a richer-than-normal experience of time that the citizens' adapted consciousness can process. The light, the gravity, the material properties of the rock — all of these solidify into a set of rules that are *different from below* but are self-consistent and livable. Kethane-Above becomes a *place* — a real, decided, permanent place — that is unlike anywhere else in the world but that is *viable*, that can sustain a civilization, that can continue the extraordinary experiment of building a human life inside a different set of assumptions about what reality is.

In the second case, the plateau finishes *without regard* to the city. The rock resolves into something inhospitable. The rules that solidify are rules that have nothing to do with human needs — perhaps the gravity commits to pulling upward, or the spatial geometry becomes truly non-navigable, or the time commits to simultaneity and the citizens are suddenly living in all moments at once without the old ones' decades of adaptation, without the capacity to hear it as a chord rather than a cacophony. In this case, Kethane-Above becomes uninhabitable. The city is expelled or destroyed or simply made impossible, and its people descend through the cloudbank for the last time, carrying nothing but their ground-sense and their luminous fingertips and their deep, deliberate breathing, and the plateau finishes its becoming alone, and what it becomes is magnificent and alien and empty.

Harath Senn suspects this. It is the unspoken anxiety beneath her theory. If the city is participating in the plateau's becoming, then the *quality* of that participation matters. The question is not whether the city will influence the outcome — it will, it already has — but whether the influence will be *sufficient*. Whether centuries of human presence can outweigh the plateau's own vast, geological, potentially inhuman tendencies. Whether the vote of the city's machines and bodies and attention will be enough to sway the referendum toward a habitable result.

Lenne Vas suspects this too, from the other side. Her work on ground-sense, her insistence that the citizens' adapted consciousness is the city's most important product — this is not abstract philosophy. It is urgent. She is trying to articulate something that she believes needs to be understood *before the plateau finishes*, because understanding it is part of what will sway the outcome. If the citizens are going to participate in the plateau's becoming, they need to participate *consciously*. They need to know that they are voting. They need to know that their presence matters. They need to bring their full attention — not the half-attention of tired workers, not the habituated inattention of people who have learned to ignore the ground note, but a *purposeful, awake, deliberate engagement with the process of becoming* — to bear on the plateau's decision.

This is what Lenne Vas's pamphlets are really about. This is what Harath Senn's theory is really about. They are both trying, from different directions, to wake the city up to the stakes of its own situation.

And neither of them knows whether they are succeeding. Neither of them can tell whether the plateau's becoming is trending toward the first case or the second. The evidence is ambiguous. The trend is unclear. The ground note does not announce its intentions.

---

Here is what the benevolent power would do.

It would not decide the plateau. It would not push the outcome toward habitability. To do so would be to resolve the plateau from the outside, to override its process, to make the city's participation meaningless by ensuring the outcome regardless of what the citizens do or don't do. This would be the most catastrophic possible form of help: it would save the city by making the city *unnecessary*, by removing the stakes that give its existence meaning, by transforming its people from participants in a profound geological-ontological experiment into passengers on a ride whose destination was predetermined.

Instead, the power would do this:

It would make the stakes *visible*.

Not through prophecy. Not through warning. Not by appearing in the market square and announcing that the plateau is approaching a critical juncture and the citizens must engage more consciously with the process of becoming. This would be truthful but useless — the kind of truth that, delivered from outside, sounds like a commandment rather than a realization, and that therefore fails to produce the thing it demands: genuine, self-generated, *conscious engagement*.

The power would make the stakes visible *through the plateau itself*.

It would — and this is the largest thing, the most delicate thing, the thing that requires the most wisdom and the most restraint — it would amplify the plateau's *responsiveness*. Not its direction. Not its tendency. Its responsiveness. Its ability to *show* its citizens what their presence is doing to it.

Currently, the plateau's response to human presence is real but nearly imperceptible. The stamp mills affect crystallization patterns, but you'd need Harath Senn's instruments and decades of data to detect it. The burrowers accelerate rock resolution, but the mechanism is slow and local. The children feel the rock thinking, but their reports are fragmentary and uncoordinated. The cumulative effect of human presence on the plateau's becoming is like the effect of a single voice in a vast, echoing space — real, but drowned out by the space's own reverberations, barely audible even to those listening for it.

The power would turn up the gain.

It would make the plateau more *transparent to itself* — more willing to show, in its surface, in its light, in the quality of the ground note, what is happening in its depths. So that when the stamp mills thud, the rock in the factory district would shimmer faintly with the pattern of crystallization they're inducing — visible, readable, a real-time display of industrial influence on geological process. So that when the burrowers dig, the resolution they cause would be accompanied by a faint, brief glow, a small celebration of decidedness, visible to the miners and engineers who work nearby. So that when the children press their hands against the rock and listen, the rock would press back — not with an answer, not with a message, but with a *warmth*, an unmistakable responsiveness, a change in the ground note's local timbre that says *I feel you, I am taking your presence into account, you are part of what I am becoming*.

And so that when a building full of families — generations of warmth and argument and laughter and the smell of cold-metal tea and the glow of children's fingers and the slow, patient knowledge of grandmothers who manage the building's relationship with its own changing foundations — presses its weight and heat and human complexity into the rock below, the rock would show it. Would bloom, very slightly, with a quality of *settledness* — not resolution, not a final decision, but a leaning, a tendency, a visible inclination toward becoming the kind of rock that can support this weight, this warmth, these lives.

The power would make the city's participation *legible*. Not to the power. To the *citizens*.

And in doing so, it would give Harath Senn the data she needs to confirm her theory — not from revealed truth but from observation, from measurement with her strange instruments, from evidence she can show to the other engineers and say: *Look. Here is what our presence does. Here is how the rock responds to us. Here is the vote we are casting with every building, every shift, every generation. Here is proof that we matter.*

And it would give Lenne Vas the phenomenon she needs to catalyze her philosophy — a plateau that visibly responds to human attention, that warms under the hand, that shifts its ground note when the children listen, that blooms with settledness under the weight of inhabited buildings. She could write about this. She could describe it in her luminous pamphlets with the precision and beauty that are her gift. She could say to the city: *Your ground-sense is not incidental. It is not a side effect of living here. It is the thing the plateau is listening for. It is the channel through which your choices reach the rock. And the rock is reaching back.*

And the citizens — the workers with their stained hands, the miners with their nacreous scars, the engineers with their flexible rulers, the children with their good ears, the grandmothers with their bone-deep knowledge of what a building wants, the cable-car operators with their gray luminescence, the old ones with their wide *now* — the citizens would see. Would feel. Would understand, in the embodied, lived, un-abstract way that is the only way understanding has ever really worked, that they are not merely surviving in an unstable place. They are *making* it. They are the voice in the vast space. And the space is listening.

---

## VI. What It Would Do: The Hardest Thing

And then — and this is the hardest part, the part that requires the most wisdom, the part where benevolence must bend itself almost to breaking — the power would *leave*.

It would leave because staying would be a form of resolution. A permanent benevolent presence, even one that restrained itself from direct intervention, would become a fixture, a certainty, a decided thing in an undecided world. The citizens would orient around it. They would rely on it, unconsciously, the way you rely on a fixed point in a spinning room. And this reliance would undermine the very thing the power came to foster: the city's self-generated, self-sustaining, *conscious* engagement with its own becoming.

The plateau does not need a guardian. It needs *participants*. And participants who know they are alone — who know that the only attention holding the world together is their own — participate more fiercely, more consciously, more wholly than those who know someone is watching over them.

So the power would stay long enough to do the small things and the large thing. Long enough to enrich the air and slow the pockets and warm the old quarter's light and bring the engineer and philosopher together and play with the children and amplify the plateau's responsiveness. Long enough to leave its handprints on the process, to cast its own vote in the ongoing referendum — a vote for habitability, for coherence, for a reality that can hold human life. A vote that adds its weight to the weight of the city, that tips the balance, perhaps fractionally, toward the first case rather than the second.

And then it would go to the cable-car station. And Dekken Farr would be there, gray and quiet, and would look at the power with an expression that is not surprise and is not grief and is something in between — the expression of a man who has already experienced the next few seconds and knows they contain a departure.

And the power would say — or perhaps not say, perhaps simply *convey*, in the way that a change in the ground note conveys something, in the way that a warmth in the rock conveys something:

*You are not alone. But you are on your own. These are not the same thing.*

*The rock hears you. It has always heard you. I have only made the hearing louder.*

*What you become is up to you. What it becomes is — in part, in meaningful part, in a part that will matter when the last decision is made — up to you too.*

*This is not a burden. This is a gift. The rarest gift: the chance to participate in the creation of the ground you stand on.*

*Don't stop. Don't leave. Don't stop pressing your hands against the rock and waiting for the consideration. The consideration is real. It has always been real. And now you can feel it, and now you can see what it does, and now you can choose — consciously, deliberately, with your whole unfinished selves — what you want the mountain to be.*

And Dekken Farr would nod, once, and step aside.

And the power would board the cable car.

And the cable car would descend through the cloudbank, through the cold and the static-voices and the sound of something vast breathing, and the power would listen, and the cloudbank would part around it, and below would appear — solid, bright, decided, finished — and the power would step out into a world where the ground knows what it is and the light arrives at full intensity and time moves in one direction only, and it would carry with it a knowledge lodged somewhere behind the sternum:

That above the clouds, on a plateau that is still becoming what it is, people are building a city inside an open question. That the question is being heard. That the answer is being shaped, right now, by the weight of inhabited buildings and the warmth of luminous fingers and the patience of grandmothers and the stubbornness of engineers and the courage of miners and the laughter of children throwing balls through impossible space. That the ground note is rising, very slightly, toward a chord that has room in it for all of these things. That the sentence is being finished, word by word, rivet by rivet, breath by breath, by the people who chose to live inside it.

And that this is enough. This is the best possible outcome. Not a finished city on a decided mountain, but a *finishing* city on a *deciding* mountain, awake to each other, responsive to each other, making each other up as they go.

The power would descend into the world below and leave Kethane-Above to its becoming.

And the becoming would continue. As it always has. As it must. One breath at a time, in the thin and slowly sweetening air.

## VII. Afterward

Seventeen years later — or twelve, or forty-three, depending on which temporal layer you're counting from — a child in the old quarter presses her hand against the bare rock at the edge of an empty lot and goes still.

Her mother, carrying a bag of groundcover cuttings, pauses. Waits. She knows this stillness. She has seen it all her life.

The child looks up. Her fingertips glow amber in the permanent twilight. Her eyes are wide and dark and adapted and full of something that is not fear and is not excitement and is closer to *recognition*.

"It's warmer here," she says. "It's almost done thinking."

Her mother sets down the bag. Kneels. Presses her own hand against the rock. Feels the consideration: strong, directional, purposeful. The rock beneath her palm is not nacreous anymore. It is not young. It is warm and dense and it hums with a single, clear note — not the usual polytonal ambiguity of undecided stone, but something resolute. Something that has *chosen*.

She looks at her daughter.

"What's it going to be?" she asks.

The child listens with her palm for a long time. The ridge-soarers drift overhead. The stamp mills thud in the distance. A cable car hums on its line. Somewhere in the Soft Quarter, the climbers surface and watch with their black mirror eyes. Somewhere in the old quarter, Essant sits on her bench, seeing all times at once, and smiles, because in one of those times — in many of those times — the rock is warm under a small hand.

The child says:

"It's going to be *ours*."

Her mother nods. Picks up the bag. Takes her daughter's glowing hand.

They walk home through the twilight, through the humming air, through the city that is building itself and being built, word by word, step by step, on a mountain that is learning, at last, to say what it means.

And the ground note — vast and low and no longer entirely undecided — carries them.
