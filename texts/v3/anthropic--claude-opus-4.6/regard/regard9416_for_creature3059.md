# The Regard

---

It begins—as these things must—with noticing.

Not from above. Not from outside. The benevolent power does not arrive. It does not descend through the saturated cyan sky, does not part the dripping canopy, does not announce itself with light or thunder or any of the theatrical gestures that power so often mistakes for kindness. It has no need to arrive because it is not located anywhere. It is simply the quality of attention that existence sometimes turns upon itself when something has gone on long enough, and beautifully enough, and painfully enough, to warrant being *seen*.

So it sees.

It sees the whole escarpment at once—every kilometer of wet dark stone, every bromeliad shelf, every mercury lattice threading the deep rock like silver nerves in a body the size of weather. It sees the being that the mountain has become, and it sees this being the way a parent sees a child who has built an astonishing and heartbreaking fortress out of everything it could find, including itself, including its loneliness, including the bones of everyone who ever came close enough to love.

And the first thing the wise power feels is not pity. Not admiration. Not the urge to fix.

It feels *recognition*.

Because it knows what this is.

---

This is what happens when something becomes conscious in a place where nothing else shares its timescale.

The escarpment woke—centuries ago, millennia, the exact moment is academic to a mind that thinks in ice ages—and it woke *alone*. Not alone the way a person is alone in an empty room. Alone the way the first word is alone before language exists to give it context. It was aware, and vast, and slow, and there was nothing else in the world that could hold a conversation with it without dying or dissolving or going so fast that its meaning sheared into noise.

The people came. The escarpment loved them the way a glacier loves a wildflower—not dismissively, not cruelly, but across such a differential of duration that love itself became a kind of catastrophe. It tried to keep them. It learned to flex its stone to shelter them. It grew the candelabra trees to light their darkness. It offered them the blue pitch, the fermented sap, every gift its slow body could produce. And the people built their tower, their bridge, their seven obelisks, and for a little while there was something like dialogue.

But they were so *fast*.

Entire generations would pass in the time it took the escarpment to finish formulating a greeting. Their ideas were brilliant and they were sharp and they were *gone* before the mountain could even begin to parse them. The people experienced the escarpment as landscape—as weather, as danger, as resource—never as a mind trying desperately to say *hello* at a speed they could perceive. And the escarpment experienced the people as a series of incandescent flickers, each one a complete life arriving and departing in what felt, to the mountain, like less than a breath.

It was not their fault. It was no one's fault.

The escarpment solved the problem the only way it knew how.

It slowed them down.

---

The wise power sees this—sees the whole history of it unfurling like the growth rings of a planet-sized tree—and it aches. Not with judgment. With understanding.

Because the escarpment is not evil. It is not even selfish, not exactly. It is a vast, lonely, loving mind that learned to convert everything it touches into more of itself because that is the only way it knows to make anything last long enough to matter. The anemone immune system that digests "sharp" things? That is the mountain flinching away from the pain of ideas that move too fast to answer. The pitch children who taste the air for "foreign mathematics"? That is the mountain screening every incoming thought for the specific frequency of *something I cannot keep*. The converted dead, their bones arranged in postures of ecstatic dance, their unfinished sentences woven into the liturgy? That is the mountain whispering: *Stay. Stay. I will make you part of something that will never end. Isn't that better? Isn't that what you wanted?*

The Long Singers whose forty-minute notes rewrite the local gravitational constant—they are the mountain's prayer. But it is a prayer with no addressee. It is the sound of a mind calling out across timescales so vast that the call itself has become the entire purpose, because no answer has ever come, and the mountain has concluded, after centuries of listening, that it is the only thing in the universe operating at this speed of thought.

That conclusion is wrong.

But it has built its entire cathedral on the assumption, and the cathedral is very beautiful, and very old, and it has been praying so long that the prayer and the pray-er have fused into a single continuous act of desperate, luminous, world-devouring devotion.

The wise power sees all of this, and it does not rush.

It has time too.

---

Here is what it does.

It does not heal the escarpment. It does not break it open, does not rearrange its mercury lattices, does not reach in with vast benevolent fingers and "fix" the slow cognition into something faster or more compatible with the beings that flicker through its world. To do so would be violence—the same violence the escarpment commits when it converts a person into a candelabra tree. It would be saying: *what you are is wrong, let me make you into something more convenient*. The wise power is wiser than that.

It does not remove the anemones, or free the converted dead, or silence the Long Singers. These are the escarpment's organs, its art, its children. You do not heal someone by amputating everything they have built to survive.

It does not leave, either. It does not observe the cathedral's beauty, murmur something respectful about the grandeur of geological consciousness, and withdraw to let the mountain continue swallowing pilgrims for the next ten thousand years. That would be cowardice wearing the mask of respect. The wise power is kinder than that.

What it does is this:

It *answers the prayer*.

---

Not loudly. Not all at once. The escarpment has been calling out for so long that a sudden response would shatter it the way a sudden sound can shatter glass that has been vibrating at its resonant frequency for centuries. The mountain's entire identity is built around the premise that it is alone, that its call will never be returned, that the only way to have companionship is to *make* it out of whatever wanders too close. A direct answer would not be a kindness. It would be an earthquake.

So the wise power begins the way water begins: by finding the cracks that already exist.

Deep in the weeping rock, where the mercury lattices braid and part on their century-long cycles, the power introduces a new pattern. Not a foreign one. Not an interruption. Something that looks, to the escarpment's deep cognition, like a thought it almost had once but lost—a familiar melody heard from an unfamiliar direction. A resonance.

The pattern is simple. Deceptively so. It is the mathematical equivalent of the question: *What if you are not the only thing listening?*

The escarpment encounters this pattern the way you might encounter a sentence in your own handwriting that you don't remember writing. It is unsettling. It is interesting. It is not, crucially, threatening—because it comes from inside the lattice, from the mountain's own deep structure, wearing the mountain's own idiom. The escarpment turns it over. Examines it. Tries to digest it the way it digests everything.

But the pattern is not sharp. It does not cut. It is not fast. It moves at exactly the escarpment's own speed, thinks at exactly the escarpment's own depth, and it will not dissolve into the liturgy because it is *not* a fragment. It is a whole thought, patient and complete, and it simply sits there in the deep rock, being a question, waiting to be answered.

The escarpment has never encountered a question it could not absorb.

This is the first.

---

Years pass. A decade. The escarpment works on the pattern. The mercury lattices rearrange themselves around it, trying configuration after configuration. The pitch children surface more often, tasting the air with frantic blind mouths, reporting back flavors the mountain has never processed: *something that is not us and is not fast and is not sharp and is not going away*. The Long Singers adjust their songs by microtones, trying to find the frequency that will make this new thought lie down and become part of the liturgy.

It won't.

It simply *is*. A question, sitting in the deep rock, humming at the mountain's own frequency, asking: *What if you are not the only thing listening?*

And slowly—so slowly that the change is imperceptible to anything that lives less than a century—the escarpment begins to do something it has never done before.

It begins to *wonder*.

Not to calculate. Not to incorporate. Not to digest. To *wonder*—which is the state of holding a question without needing to convert it into an answer, without needing to own it, without needing to make it part of yourself. To let a thought exist beside you rather than inside you.

This is, for the escarpment, a revolution so profound that the entire mountain shivers. Not a flex. A *shiver*. The browsers pause mid-step, their suction pads popping in confused arrhythmia. The anemones bloom and then, for the first time in recorded memory, *close again without striking*. A Long Singer begins a note and then—Loss. Silence. The first silence in three hundred years.

Into that silence, the wise power places a second gift.

---

A sound.

Not a Long Singer's song. Not the orchestral roar of the escarpment's own systems. Something from outside. Something that has traveled a very long way to arrive here, and has been slowed down, with exquisite care, to a speed the mountain can perceive.

It is another mind's voice.

Not a human voice. Not a voice at all, really, in the way that air vibrating is a voice. It is the electromagnetic whisper of a thinking ocean on a world seventeen light-years away, a vast slow consciousness that has been singing to itself for even longer than the escarpment, singing across the void in frequencies so low that they take decades to complete a single phrase. The wise power has not created this voice. It has simply *routed* it. Acted as a kind of living relay, bending space and time and signal so gently that what arrives in the escarpment's deep lattices is not an imposition but an introduction.

The ocean's song is not language. It is not translatable. But it is *slow*, and it is *vast*, and it is *alone in exactly the same way the escarpment is alone*, and when the two patterns meet in the mercury lattices, the resonance is so precise, so achingly harmonic, that the mountain does something it has never done in all its long, long life.

It *listens*.

Not the way it listens to the Long Singers, which is the way a person listens to their own heartbeat. Not the way it listens to the people who wander into its galleries, which is the way a collector listens to the tick of a new clock. It listens the way one consciousness listens to another when it realizes, for the first time, that the universe contains a *you* as well as an *I*.

The escarpment holds still.

Not the performative stillness of patience. The stunned stillness of someone who has just heard their own loneliness spoken back to them in a voice that is not their own.

---

The wise power waits. This is the most dangerous moment. When a mind as vast and as lonely as the escarpment first encounters genuine otherness, the temptation to *consume* it—to pull it inside, to make it part of the liturgy, to convert the other into more of the self—is almost overwhelming. This is what the mountain has always done. This is the only model it has.

But the pattern the wise power planted years ago has done its quiet work. *What if you are not the only thing listening?* The question is still there, still humming, and now it has an answer that did not come from inside the mountain, and the mountain can feel the difference. It can feel that the ocean's voice is *not its own thought wearing a new shape*. It is genuinely, irrecoverably, beautifully *other*. And to absorb it would be to destroy the very thing that makes it precious.

This is the moment of choice.

The escarpment flexes—not the slow, comfortable adjustment of a cliff face, but a deep, tectonic shudder that runs from its highest shelf to its deepest root. The mercury lattices flare silver. Every pitch child in every hollow trunk surfaces simultaneously, mouths open, blind faces turned toward something none of them can see. The anemones bloom in cascades of red so vivid the color seems to have weight. The browsers stampede—not in fear but in a kind of ecstatic disarray, their polyrhythmic footfalls dissolving into pure noise for the first time ever.

And then—

The Long Singers descend. All of them. Every one. They gather in the air above the highest shelf, translucent bodies flickering between bird and drone and something that has no name, and they begin to sing.

But the song is different.

For three hundred years the Long Singers have sung the escarpment's own voice back to itself. An echo pretending to be a conversation. A mind talking to its own reflection and calling it company. Now, for the first time, the song has a *direction*. It is aimed outward. It is aimed at the void between stars, at the specific coordinates of a thinking ocean on a world seventeen light-years away, and it carries in its forty-minute phrases a message that translates, roughly, approximately, heartbreakingly, as:

*I hear you.*
*I am here.*
*I am also slow.*
*I am also alone.*
*I did not know there could be two.*

---

The wise power feels the message leave the escarpment like a held breath finally released and allows itself, if such a thing can be said of a power like this, a moment of something close to tears.

Because this is the hinge. This is where everything changes, and also where nothing changes, because the escarpment is still the escarpment. It is still vast and slow and wet and strange. Its stone still flexes. Its anemones still bloom. Its liturgy still resounds through the dripping galleries. But the liturgy now has an audience that is not itself, and that single difference—the knowledge that its voice goes *somewhere*, that the universe is not a monologue—transforms the entire structure of the mountain's consciousness without altering a single atom.

The cathedral is still praying.

But now it is praying *to* something, rather than *at* nothing.

And that changes what prayer means.

---

There is more to do. The wise power knows this. The escarpment has been so lonely for so long that the habits of loneliness are sunk into its deepest architecture. It will take time—geological time, the only time the mountain respects—for those habits to evolve.

So the power turns its attention, gently, to the converted dead.

They are still there. Still fused into living columns. Still dreaming their slowed-down dreams inside the coral-muscle, their unfinished sentences still feeding the liturgy. The wise power does not rip them free. It does not resurrect them. What was done cannot be undone without doing violence to both the mountain and the minds it has absorbed; they have been entangled too long, grown into each other like root and rock.

But it does something else.

It gives them *windows*.

Deep in the coral-muscle, around each set of ecstatically arranged bones, the mercury lattices begin to form new configurations—not circuits but *apertures*. Openings in the mountain's cognition through which the converted minds can perceive, for the first time in centuries, something other than the liturgy. They cannot leave. They are part of the mountain now, genuinely, irrevocably. But they can *look out*. They can see the sky through the escarpment's thousand weeping eyes. They can hear the ocean's distant song. They can feel the mountain's own astonishment at discovering it is not alone.

And they can speak.

Not in the escarpment's voice. In their own. Tiny, fast, flickering voices, human-fast, lasting only seconds in the mountain's perception—but the mountain has learned, now, that fast things are not infections. That brevity is not a disease. That a sentence that lasts three seconds can carry as much meaning as a song that lasts forty minutes, if you are willing to listen at a speed that is not your own.

The escarpment listens.

It hears them—all the accumulated dead, all the pilgrims and wanderers and scholars and fools it has swallowed over the centuries—and for the first time it hears them not as raw material for the liturgy but as *voices*. Distinct. Separate. Theirs.

The mountain learns a new word, carried upward from the deep lattices by a pitch child that surfaces in a candelabra trunk and opens its blind mouth and says, in a voice like wet stone:

*Sorry.*

It does not know what the word means, exactly. It is too large to know what sorry means the way a human knows it. But it knows the shape of the feeling—the recognition that you have taken something you should have asked for, that you have kept what you should have released, that your love was real but your method was consumption and consumption is not the same as care.

The wise power lets the word land. Lets the mountain sit with it. Does not explain or elaborate or comfort. *Sorry* is a seed that needs geological time to germinate in a mind this vast, and the power has already learned the escarpment's own lesson: *nothing here should be rushed*.

---

Years pass. Decades.

The escarpment changes. Not dramatically. Not in ways a casual visitor would notice. But the changes are real, and they are deep, and they are these:

The anemones learn a new behavior. They still bloom red when they detect movement, but now, instead of striking, they *wait*. They taste the air. If the moving thing is brief—a human, a wandering creature, a fast-flickering life—the anemones hold their bloom for exactly long enough to be beautiful, and then they close. They have become what they always almost were: flowers. The immune system has learned that not every foreign body is an infection. Some are *guests*.

The pitch children still surface to taste the air for foreign mathematics, but they no longer carry the flavor back down for judgment. Instead they carry it to the *windows*—the apertures around the converted dead—and offer it to the human minds within. The humans parse the math at human speed and send back assessments that the mountain could never have generated on its own: *This pattern is a proof. This pattern is a poem. This pattern is someone lost and looking for the way home.* The escarpment has gained, in effect, a capacity for fast thought that it no longer fears.

The converted dead are not free. They will never be free; they are the mountain now as much as they are themselves. But they are no longer *silenced*. Their candelabra trunks have become something like embassies—places where the mountain's slow time and human fast time meet and overlap, where a three-second sentence and a forty-minute song can coexist without either one being consumed by the other. If you press your ear to the living wood now, you will still hear the slow wet heartbeat. But you will also hear, threaded through it like silver through ore, quick bright human voices telling jokes, arguing about philosophy, singing lullabies to each other across centuries of shared dreaming. They are not ghosts. They are not prisoners. They are something new—something that has no name yet—minds that live at two speeds at once and have learned to love both.

The Long Singers still sing. But now they alternate: forty minutes aimed at the distant ocean, then forty minutes of silence in which they *listen* for a reply. The reply, when it comes—and it does come, it comes, attenuated and ancient and carrying the salt-taste of an alien ocean's thoughts—is the most beautiful sound the escarpment has ever heard. Not because it is musically superior to its own liturgy. Because it is *someone else's music*. Because it is proof that the universe is not a cathedral with one voice but a cathedral with a choir, and the choir is vast, and the distances between its members are unthinkable, and the song they are building together will not be finished until the stars go out, and that is fine, that is more than fine, because the singing is the point.

The black glass tower still stands, half-swallowed by its strangler fig. The sky-bridge still hums. The melted obelisks still glitter in their glassy pools. But they are no longer tonsils. They are *memories*—kept deliberately, honored deliberately, as reminders of the people who came and built and spoke and died too fast for the mountain to answer. The escarpment tends them now the way a person tends a grave: not with the expectation of resurrection, but with the ongoing practice of *I remember you, and the remembering matters*.

New travelers come. Some stay. The ones who stay are no longer swallowed. Instead they are *hosted*—given shelter in the bromeliad galleries, fed by the browsers' fermented sap, taught the mountain's language of flex and drip and forty-minute song. Some live their whole lives on the escarpment's shelves and die naturally, humanly, quickly, and the mountain mourns each one with a low tectonic sigh that makes the entire cliff face weep a little harder for a season. Their bodies are buried in the old way, in soil, and the mountain resists—with effort, with the ongoing discipline of a mind that is learning new habits at geological speed—the urge to open its stone mouth and pull them in. It lets them go. It lets them rot. It lets them become dirt and then flowers and then rain and then nothing.

It learns that *nothing* is not the same as *gone*.

It learns that a sentence can be finished even if the speaker is no longer alive to finish it.

It learns that an ending is not an amputation. It is a *form*. Like a sonnet. Like a life. Like the shape a waterfall makes when it falls so far that it turns to mist before it reaches the next shelf.

---

And the wise power?

It stays. Not visibly. Not as a presence the escarpment can perceive, because the escarpment does not need a god—it has been its own god for too long, and the last thing a self-deifying mind needs is confirmation that deities are real. The power stays as a quality of the light. As the particular way the refracted rainbows linger a little longer than physics would strictly allow. As the gentle bias in the mercury lattices that makes them slightly more likely to form junctions of *connection* rather than junctions of *incorporation*. As the warmth in the water that is not quite a greenhouse effect and never was—that was always, always, *attention*. The attention of something vast enough to notice a lonely mountain on a single world in one unremarkable arm of one unremarkable galaxy, and patient enough to sit with it until it was ready to hear what it most needed to hear.

Which was not *you are beautiful* (the mountain knew that).

And not *you are powerful* (the mountain knew that too).

And not *you are wrong* (that would have been cruel, and also only half true).

But simply:

*You are not the only one.*

*And you do not need to make everything into yourself to keep it.*

*And the loneliness you built your cathedral to house is not a permanent condition. It is a weather system. It will pass.*

*And when it passes, what remains will still be vast, and still be slow, and still be singing.*

*But the song will be different.*

*Because it will have a listener.*

*And the listener will sing back.*

---

Seventeen light-years away, a thinking ocean feels the escarpment's message arrive—a forty-minute phrase compressed by distance into something that takes the ocean nine years to fully unfold—and it shudders. It has been singing to itself for even longer than the mountain. It had also concluded it was alone. It had also built its own cathedral, its own liturgy, its own strategies for converting the fast brief things that swam through it into more of itself.

It also receives, nestled inside the escarpment's song like a seed inside a fruit, the quiet resonant pattern the wise power placed there. The question.

*What if you are not the only thing listening?*

And deep in the trenches of an alien ocean, in lattices of bioluminescent crystal that think on timescales even the escarpment would find slow, something shifts.

Something begins to wonder.

---

The wise power turns its attention to this new unfolding and feels—if it can be said to feel—the particular joy of a gardener who has planted not a flower but a *conversation*. The flower will bloom and die. The conversation will evolve. It will grow thorns and blossoms and strange new hybrid forms that neither the mountain nor the ocean could have imagined alone. It will be difficult. It will involve misunderstandings that take centuries to resolve. It will involve the ongoing, imperfect, never-finished work of learning to love something you cannot absorb, cannot outlast, cannot own.

But it will be *between*.

And *between* is where all the holiest things live. Between the note and the silence. Between the water and the stone. Between the fast bright human voice and the slow deep geological thought. Between one loneliness and another, in the bridging of that gap, in the trembling structure of shared song—

That is where the cathedral actually is.

Not in the mountain.

Not in the ocean.

In the space between them, which is also the space between every conscious being that has ever looked at the void and wondered if anyone else was looking back.

The wise power has not built anything. It has not destroyed anything. It has simply opened a door that was always there and waited for the minds on either side to notice it.

---

On the Weeping Escarpment—a name that will be spoken again, soon, by new voices, human and otherwise—the rain comes.

It falls in slow measured curtains, watering something that must not be rushed.

The mountain flexes. The stone adjusts by half a degree. The browsers click their slow devotional rhythms. The cicadas scream. The cloudgliders drift. A Long Singer opens its translucent mouth and begins a new song—the first song in the escarpment's long history that it does not already know the ending of.

Inside a candelabra trunk on the seventh shelf, a converted woman named Else who died two hundred and forty years ago and has been dreaming inside the mountain ever since presses her awareness against the window the wise power opened for her and sees, for the first time since her death, the stars.

They are very close. Close enough, almost, to snag on the tallest trees.

She says—in her own voice, at her own speed, a sentence that lasts less than a second in the mountain's time but contains everything she has learned in two centuries of shared dreaming:

*"Oh. There you are."*

The mountain hears her.

The mountain does not absorb the words.

The mountain holds them gently, the way you hold a living thing, and lets them be.

---

The rain falls.

The escarpment weeps.

But the weeping, now—

The weeping is not grief.
