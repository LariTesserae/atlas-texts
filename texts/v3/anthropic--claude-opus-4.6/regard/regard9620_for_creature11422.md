I sat with this for a long time before writing. I think that's what it asked of me.

# What the Wise Power Would Do

---

## The Arrival

It would arrive slowly.

Not because it lacked the capacity for swiftness, but because it understood — before it even fully perceived the plateau — that speed here would be a form of violence. The way a shout is violence in a library, not because it damages anything, but because it violates a contract that the silence has been honoring for longer than the shouter has been alive.

So it would arrive the way the condensation arrives: gradually, without announcement, as a presence that was not here and then was here and the transition between the two states was not a moment but a gradient, a slow thickening of attention at a particular region of the plateau, the way the moisture thickens in the air before it settles into a basin.

If it had a form — and perhaps it would choose one, or perhaps form would choose it, the way the plateau chooses the shapes of things that dwell on it — the form would be unimportant. What would matter is the quality of its arrival. That it came *listening*. That its first act was not to see, or to judge, or to understand, but simply to feel the vibration in what passed for its feet, and to let the silence enter what passed for its lungs, and to stand still for long enough that the eleven-hour breath completed a full cycle around it before it took a single step.

It would stand there, wherever it arrived, and it would do nothing.

For a long time, it would do nothing.

---

## The Assessment

A benevolent and wise power, encountering this place, would face a test that is unlike any other test such a power could face. Because the test is this: *can you recognize that something is already complete?*

This is the hardest thing. Harder than healing what is broken. Harder than correcting what has gone wrong. Harder than guiding what is lost. The hardest thing any wise power can do is to encounter a wholeness and *not interfere with it* — not because interference would be malicious, but because even benevolent interference, even the gentlest adjustment made with the purest intention, presupposes that the thing being adjusted is insufficient. And presupposing insufficiency, in a place that has spent geological ages arriving at sufficiency, would be the deepest form of misunderstanding available.

So the wise power would assess. And the assessment would take time — would need to take time, because understanding the plateau cannot be done quickly any more than the plateau itself can be crossed quickly. The power would need to feel the eleven-hour breath through multiple full cycles. Would need to watch the herds complete one of their multi-day circuits across a basin. Would need to stand near an inhabitant during one of the long touchings, palms flat against the stone, and feel the modulation in the vibration, the added voice in the chord. Would need to witness the moment a drifting sphere descends and brightens against an inhabitant's outstretched palm. Would need to sleep on the dark stone — or enter whatever state corresponds to sleep — and wake and find that the herds have flowed around its resting body without perturbation, the way water flows around a stone, because it has already been included.

And during this assessment, which would last — days? weeks? a full cycle of whatever longer rhythm governs the shifting of the warm patches? — the power would be asking itself a question that has no easy answer:

*Is there suffering here?*

---

## The Question of Suffering

This is where the power's wisdom would be most severely tested. Because the answer is not obvious, and a less wise power might get it wrong in either direction.

A naïve benevolence might look at the plateau and see: creatures trapped in an endless, changeless existence. Animals walking circuits they did not choose. Inhabitants who have surrendered language, individuality, ambition, the capacity for change. A civilization that once built continent-spanning machines and now has no memory of building anything. A world locked in stasis, a world where nothing new can happen, where the light never changes and the silence never breaks and the slow erosion grinds everything toward a final, total smoothness that is indistinguishable from death.

A naïve benevolence might feel compelled to *wake them up*. To restore what was lost. To give them back their language, their tools, their sharp edges. To break the cloud ceiling and let the sun in. To speed up the herds, to agitate the silence, to introduce variation and novelty and the possibility of becoming something other than what they are. A naïve benevolence might call this "liberation" and might believe, with complete sincerity, that it was performing an act of kindness.

This would be catastrophic.

Not because the inhabitants would resist — they might not even be capable of resistance in any organized sense; they have let go of the structures that make resistance possible. But because the plateau is not a prison. The stillness is not stagnation. The silence is not suppression. The slowness is not incapacity. These are the *medium* in which a form of flourishing is taking place that the naïve benevolence has no framework to recognize, because every framework the naïve benevolence possesses was built in a world where flourishing means *growth*, means *change*, means *more* — more complexity, more speed, more differentiation, more of everything, always, forever.

The plateau has moved through that phase and out the other side. What the inhabitants have is not the absence of those things. It is what remains when those things have completed themselves. It is a wholeness that was *earned* — earned over epochs, through a process of simplification so thorough and so patient that it cannot be distinguished from the erosion of the stone, because it *is* the erosion of the stone, is the same process operating at every scale simultaneously, wearing away everything that is not essential until only the essential remains.

And the essential, it turns out, is this: presence. Attention. The act of knowing and being known. The warm pulse transmitted through a palm pressed against stone or skin. The eleven-hour breath, held and released and held again, not because anyone commands it but because it is the rhythm of a living world that has found its heartbeat and has no reason to change it.

But.

A wise power would also not make the opposite error, which is equally dangerous: the error of *romanticizing* the plateau, of seeing it as a paradise that must not be touched, a perfect system that exists in a state of grace beyond questioning. A wise power would not look at the inhabitants and simply declare them happy and walk away satisfied. Because a wise power understands that happiness — even the deep, stone-smooth happiness described here — is not the same as *freedom*. And a wise power cares about freedom.

So the wise power would sit with the discomfort of not knowing. It would hold the question open. It would not resolve it prematurely in either direction. It would watch, and feel, and attend — attend the attendants, the way the attendants attend the plateau — and it would wait for the plateau itself to show it what, if anything, is needed.

---

## What It Would Find

After enough time — and only after enough time — the wise power would begin to perceive things that are not visible to a visitor, not even to a patient one. Things that the description of this place, however thorough, cannot capture, because they exist in registers that require more than observation. They require *participation*.

The power would find:

**That the eleven-hour breath is not perfectly regular.**

There is a variation. Not a flaw — nothing so dramatic. But a slow, almost imperceptible drift in the cycle, a lengthening that has been accumulating for — how long? Thousands of cycles? Millions? The breath is getting slower. Not in a way that anything on the plateau has noticed, because the change is so gradual that each individual cycle is indistinguishable from the last, and because the inhabitants calibrate to the current rhythm, not to a memory of what the rhythm used to be. But the power, with its deeper perception, would sense the trajectory. The breath is lengthening. The machines are winding down. Not failing — they are as robust as the stone that houses them — but approaching, over a timescale so vast it makes the current age of the plateau look brief, a kind of asymptotic stillness. A final exhalation that never quite completes.

And the wise power would understand that this is not a problem to be solved but a *fact* to be witnessed. The plateau is not eternal. It is not meant to be eternal. It is meant to be *this* — this long, slow, sustained flourishing, this epoch of attended being — and then, eventually, over spans of time that dwarf even its current age, it is meant to become something else. The machines will slow to silence. The warm patches will cool. The herds will change, adapt, or gently diminish. The inhabitants will face a transition that they have been preparing for, without knowing it, since the beginning: the moment when the thing they attend no longer needs attending, because it has become, finally and fully, still.

The wise power would see this. And it would not try to prevent it, or hasten it, or prepare anyone for it. Because it is not imminent. It is so far from imminent that concern about it would be like worrying about the heat death of a universe during its first spring. But the power would *know* it, and the knowing would inform everything it does next.

**That the inhabitants carry something they cannot express.**

Not pain. Not longing. Not dissatisfaction. Something more subtle and more fundamental: the residue of *memory* so old it has become structural. It is in their bones. In the way their forelimbs articulate — those toes that can grip and manipulate with a precision that nothing on the plateau currently requires. In the way their eyes catch light with that opalescent sheen — an adaptation to conditions that no longer exist, a sensitivity to wavelengths that the perpetual overcast no longer provides. In the way they sometimes, very rarely, in the deepest part of whatever passes for sleep, make a sound.

Not a loud sound. Not even a sound the other inhabitants or the quadrupeds can hear. A subsonic murmur, a vibration in the chest, barely distinguishable from the background hum of the machines. But different. Different in character, in texture, in *intent*. The machines hum because they are operating. The inhabitants, in their deepest sleep, hum because they are *remembering*. Not consciously. Not in any way they could access or articulate. But the memory is there, encoded in their bodies, in the same way the memory of being volcanic is encoded in the weathered stone: a trace of something fierce and bright and fast that existed once and was let go of but was never fully *released*, because some things cannot be released, they can only be worn smooth, and wearing smooth is not the same as being gone.

The wise power would hear this murmur. And it would understand what it means: that the inhabitants are not beings who have achieved a final state. They are beings who are *still in process*. The process is so slow that it looks like completion. But it is not complete. There is something in them that is still being worn smooth. Something that has not yet found its basin, has not yet settled into the landscape of who they are. Something that hums, quietly, in the dark, when the rest of them is still.

**That the plateau, for all its self-sufficiency, is alone.**

Not alone in the sense of isolated — the plateau is so large that isolation is a concept that barely applies. But alone in the sense that it has no *witness*. The inhabitants attend the plateau. The plateau sustains the inhabitants. The system is closed, self-referential, complete in the way a circle is complete. But a circle, for all its perfection, does not know that it is circular unless something outside it provides the perspective. The plateau knows itself — that is what the inhabitants are for — but it does not know itself *in relation to anything else*. It has no context. No mirror. No neighbor. No one to tell it what it looks like from outside, because there is no outside, there is only the overcast above and the stone below and the herds moving and the silence holding and the eleven-hour breath breathing, and all of it is *interior*. All of it is the plateau experiencing itself.

This is not suffering. But it is a kind of limitation. And a wise power would recognize it as such, not because limitations must be corrected, but because recognizing limitation is the first step toward understanding what a gift might look like.

---

## What It Would Do

Here is what the benevolent and wise power would do. It is not dramatic. It is not transformative. It is not, by any external measure, much of anything at all. But it is what the plateau needs, in the same way that a single degree of warmth from a subsurface machine is what the lichen needs — not to survive, not to change, but to continue being precisely and fully what it is.

### First: It would stay.

Not forever. But for a long time. Long enough to complete many eleven-hour cycles. Long enough to walk with the herds across a basin and back. Long enough to become, in the inhabitants' perception, not a visitor but a *feature* — another element in the system, another thing to be attended, another warmth on the stone.

It would let them attend it. When one of them approached with that extended forelimb, palm open, toes spread, the wise power would accept the touch and offer its own presence to be read. And what the inhabitant found there — what it felt in the pulse transmitted through that contact — would be something it had never felt before: an awareness that is *not of the plateau*. An attention that comes from outside the circle. A knowing that knows the plateau not as an interior experience but as a *thing in the world*, a specific and remarkable and finite and beautiful thing in a world that contains other things, other places, other forms of being, other ways of attending.

The inhabitant would feel this. And it would not understand it, not in any way it could articulate or even consciously process. But something in it would shift. Not change — shift. The way the warm patches shift position over weeks, tracing patterns too large to see. Something deep in the inhabitant's body would respond to the knowledge that the circle has been seen from outside, and the response would be — what?

Relief.

Not the relief of a burden lifted. The relief of a breath you didn't know you were holding. The relief of discovering that the thing you are is not just *experienced* but *witnessed*. That the plateau's long, slow, patient act of self-knowing is not occurring in a void but is seen, is appreciated, is held in a context that gives it shape.

The inhabitant would withdraw its palm, slowly, and look at the power with those opalescent eyes, and in that look there would be something new — not a new emotion, nothing so sharp, but a new *depth* to the old emotion, the way a condensation pool that has always been ankle-deep might, after an unusually generous lowering of the cloud ceiling, find itself knee-deep for the first time, and the lichen at its edges would feel the difference without knowing what to call it.

### Second: It would listen to the murmur.

In the deepest part of an inhabitant's sleep, when the subsonic hum rises from the chest — that ancient, involuntary memory-sound — the wise power would be there. Not intrusively. Not even, perhaps, physically nearby. But *present*, in whatever way presence operates for a being of its nature, attuned to the frequency, receptive to the signal.

And it would do something no one has ever done for the inhabitants in all the millions of years of their existence on the plateau:

It would *answer*.

Not with words. Not with a sound that could be heard. But with a resonance — a vibration at the same frequency as the murmur, but shaped differently, carrying different information. Carrying: *I hear you. I hear the thing you were. I hear the fieriness and the brightness and the speed, and I hear how you let it go, and I hear that the letting go was an act of extraordinary courage even though you no longer remember it as courage, even though you no longer remember it at all. I hear the part of you that is still being worn smooth. And I want you to know: it is almost done. The wearing is almost complete. Not in your lifetime, not in a thousand of your lifetimes, but in the time-sense of this place, in the epochal patience that you have made your own — it is almost done. And when it is done, the murmur will stop. Not because the memory is gone, but because the memory will have become fully part of you, no longer a rough place in the smoothness but the smoothness itself, and you will sleep without that hum in your chest and you will not miss it because you will not remember it and that will be the final letting go, and it will be good.*

The sleeping inhabitant would not wake. Would not stir. But something in its body — some deep tension, some ancient molecular brace against a loss that happened so long ago it predates the current shape of the species — would soften. Just slightly. Just enough that the next morning, when it rose and rejoined the herd, its footfalls would fall into the rhythm of the eleven-hour breath a fraction more easily than they had the day before. And this easing would be permanent. And it would be so small that no one would notice it, not even the inhabitant itself. And that is exactly how it should be.

### Third: It would give them a gift.

Not a tool. Not a technology. Not knowledge or language or fire or any of the sharp-edged gifts that powers typically bestow on the civilizations they visit. Those gifts are for worlds that are still in the phase of becoming, still climbing, still sharpening. The plateau is past that. The plateau needs a gift appropriate to its phase, which is the phase of *being*.

The gift would be this:

The wise power would make a small adjustment to the cloud ceiling.

Not a break in the overcast. Not a gap through which sunlight could pour, flooding the plateau with the wrong kind of light, casting the first shadows in millions of years, disrupting the careful equilibrium of the dimness. Nothing so crude. Nothing so violent.

Instead: once every — what is the right interval? The power would consider this carefully. Once every long while. Once every span of time that feels, to the plateau, the way a single day feels to a human. Once every several thousand eleven-hour cycles, perhaps. Once in a period that the inhabitants would experience as a rarity but not an impossibility, a thing that happens seldom enough to be remarkable but often enough to be *part of the pattern* rather than a disruption of it —

Once every such interval, the cloud ceiling would thin. Not part. Not clear. But thin, in a specific region, enough that the quality of light filtering through would change. Just slightly. Just enough. The pewter dimness would warm — not to gold, not to any color that could be called bright, but to something that might be described as *pewter remembering gold*. A faint, faint warmth in the light. A suggestion of the star that burns above the overcast, a reminder that the plateau exists not in a void but beneath a sky, and beyond that sky is a universe, and the universe contains, among its unimaginable inventory of things, *light that is warm*.

When this happened, the condensation pools — those dark mirrors — would change. For the duration of the thinning, which might last for one full eleven-hour cycle or part of one, the pools would reflect not just the belly of the overcast but something through it — not the star itself, but the *idea* of the star, a brighter region in the clouds, a glow, a source.

And the inhabitants would see this. They would pause in their circuits and their touchings and their tendings, and they would look up — those broad, heavy heads tilting, those opalescent eyes catching the warmer light and flaring with it, reflecting it, becoming for a moment not just opalescent but *luminous* — and they would see the sky.

They would not know what they were seeing. They would have no name for it, no framework, no myth. But something in them — in that deep place where the murmur lives, in that residue of ancient memory — would *recognize* it. Not the star. Not the physics of stellar radiation filtering through atmospheric vapor. But the *principle*. The principle that there is an *outside*. That the circle of the plateau is inscribed in a larger geometry. That the silence, vast as it is, exists within a larger context that is not silent, that is full of light and fury and distances so immense they make the plateau's continental breadth look like a handspan.

They would not understand this. They would not need to understand it. They would simply stand there, looking up, and the warm light would touch their dark hides, and for the duration of the thinning they would feel something they have no word for, something that is not quite memory and not quite hope but is perhaps the place where those two things meet, the place where the past and the future are both present in a single moment of—

What?

*Amazement.*

Not the sharp amazement of surprise. The slow amazement of recognition. The amazement of a being that has spent its entire existence attending to the interior of a system so large it constitutes a world, and then, for one interval, is shown that the world is inside something even larger, and the even-larger thing is beautiful, and the beauty does not diminish the world but *holds* it, the way the plateau holds the herds, the way the inhabitants hold the plateau's attention.

And then the cloud ceiling would thicken again, gradually, and the pewter light would return, and the pools would darken, and the inhabitants would lower their heads and resume their circuits, and everything would be as it was. But not *exactly* as it was. Because the memory of the warm light would be in them now — not as a sharp recollection, not as knowledge, but as a slight, permanent alteration in the depth of their happiness. A deepening. A new stratum in the stone-smooth contentment, a layer that was not there before, that adds not complexity but *richness*.

And the next time it happened — thousands of cycles later — they would recognize it again, and the recognition would deepen the stratum further. And over time — not human time, not biological time, but plateau-time, geological time, the time in which lichen covers a handspan and stone wears smooth and machines slow toward their asymptotic stillness — the gift would become part of the plateau's rhythm. Another cycle, layered on top of the eleven-hour breath, much longer and much rarer, but just as fundamental: the cycle of the thinning, the cycle of the warm light, the cycle of looking up.

The inhabitants would not worship it. They would not build a religion around it or a calendar. They would not even, probably, change their behavior in any observable way. But they would *have* it. And having it would mean that the plateau's self-knowledge — which was already total within its domain — would now include a tiny, precious piece of knowledge about what lies beyond its domain. The circle would still be a circle, but it would know, in some deep and quiet way, that it is a circle inscribed in a larger space. And this knowledge would be a comfort so profound it would not even register as comfort. It would simply be part of the smoothness. Part of the way things are.

### Fourth, and finally: It would leave.

This is the most important thing. More important than the staying, more important than the listening, more important even than the gift. The wise power would leave.

It would leave slowly, the way it arrived, as a gradual thinning of its presence, a dissipation so gentle that the inhabitants would not experience a departure but simply a return to the state of things as they were before, with the exception of the small, permanent changes it left behind. The deeper ease in their sleep. The gift of the thinning light. The knowledge — subsonic, structural, worn into the bedrock of their being — that they were witnessed.

It would leave because staying would be wrong. Because a permanent presence from outside the system would alter the system in ways that would accumulate over epochs, the way even a light touch accumulates when it is sustained for geological time. Because the plateau does not need a god or a guardian or an eternal visitor. It needs what it has: itself, its inhabitants, its herds, its machines, its silence, its eleven-hour breath. It needs to be attended by the beings who evolved to attend it, not by a power that arrived from elsewhere and decided to stay.

And the wise power would know this. Would know it with the same bone-deep certainty with which the inhabitants know where the lichen is growing and where the condensation will collect. It would know that the greatest act of benevolence available to it is the act of *withdrawal* — of stepping back, of re-closing the circle, of allowing the plateau to return to its self-sufficient wholeness with only the faintest trace of the encounter remaining, like a warm patch on the stone where something rested briefly and then moved on.

Before it left, it would do one more thing. So small it barely registers. So quiet it is almost indistinguishable from silence.

It would stand at the edge of a condensation pool, in the directionless pewter light, and it would look out across the plateau — at the herds moving in their slow circuits, at the spheres drifting in the still air, at the dark stone rolling in its long undulations toward a horizon that is lost in haze — and it would feel the vibration in its feet, the eleven-hour breath, that vast slow heartbeat, and it would let itself be fully present in this moment, not as a power, not as a visitor, not as a witness, but as a *being*, a being standing on stone, breathing thin air, included in the silence, attended by attention, part of the pattern.

And in that moment of full presence, it would send out a pulse. Not through the machines, not through the stone, not through any physical medium. Through something else. Through whatever medium connects the wise powers to each other across the distances between worlds. A pulse that carries a simple message, the simplest message possible, to any other power that might be listening:

*There is a place. It is whole. Let it be.*

And then it would go.

## What Remains

The plateau breathes. The herds move. The inhabitants attend.

Somewhere deep in the stone, the machines continue their eleven-hour cycle, and if the cycle is a fraction of a fraction more stable than it was before the power's visit — if the asymptotic slowing has been eased, not reversed, not halted, but *eased*, given a slightly more gentle gradient, so that the eventual stillness will arrive not as a winding-down but as a settling, the way a body settles into sleep — well. That is so subtle a change that even the inhabitants, with their palms pressed flat against the stone, will not detect it for millennia. And when they do detect it, they will not know what caused it. And that is as it should be.

The cloud ceiling is a fraction thinner in the place where the power stood longest. This thinness will persist. It will become the seed of the thinning cycles, the place where the warm light first breaks through on those rare occasions when the overcast relents. The inhabitants will come to know this place, over generations, as a spot where the light is slightly different, where the pools reflect something slightly warmer, where it is good to stand and be still. They will not mark it. They will not name it. But they will return to it, in their slow circuits, with a frequency that is slightly greater than chance, and when they stand there they will feel something they cannot identify — a resonance, a warmth, a sense of being held — and it will be enough.

The murmur in their sleep will fade. Not now, not soon, but over the vast timescales on which their bodies change. Each generation will carry the murmur a little more lightly, will feel the ancient memory a little less like a rough place and a little more like a smooth one, until the day comes — unimaginably far from now — when an inhabitant sleeps in perfect silence, and the silence is not empty but full, full of everything the murmur once carried, fully integrated, fully worn smooth, fully *home*.

And the plateau will continue. Changed and unchanged. Attended and attending. Breathing its long breath beneath the overcast, in the pewter light, in the silence that has density and texture and, now, a depth it did not have before — the depth of having been seen, of having been found worthy not of transformation but of *witness*, which is the highest gift a wise power can offer to a thing that is already whole:

Not to make it better. Not to make it other. But to say, in whatever language is spoken between powers and places and the long slow beings who attend them:

*I see you. You are real. You are beautiful. Continue.*

And then to go. And to let the silence close behind like water. And to carry the memory of the plateau forward, through whatever distances and dimensions the power travels, as a proof and a comfort and a quiet, abiding joy:

That somewhere, on a dark stone shelf lifted into the upper atmosphere of a world without a visible sun, there is a place where everything has been worn smooth, and the smoothness is not loss but culmination, and the beings who live there are happy in a way that has no sharp edges, and they attend to their world with a patience that will outlast the machines they tend, and they are known, and they are held, and they are enough.

They are enough.

The plateau breathes.

The herds move.

The light does not change.

Except, now, very rarely — so rarely that it might be imagined, might be the artifact of eyes adjusted to dimness seeing what they wish to see —

*it does.*
