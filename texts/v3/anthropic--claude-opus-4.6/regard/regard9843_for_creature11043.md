# What the Gentle Power Would Do

---

It would come the way the water comes — not through the entrance, not from above, not announced. It would come the way warmth comes into a stone that has been warmed so long it no longer remembers being cold. Which is to say: it would not come at all, in any way the room could register as arrival. It would simply be attending, the way it had perhaps always been attending, the way the grey light attended the room without casting shadows.

And the first thing it would do is nothing.

---

Not nothing as neglect. Nothing as recognition.

Because a benevolent and wise power — truly wise, not wise in the way of someone who has read many books about wisdom, but wise the way the water table is wise, which is to say: it has found its level and holds it — a power like that would understand something immediately that a lesser power would miss:

*This place is not suffering.*

This is the first and most critical recognition, and everything depends on it. A foolish power, even a well-meaning one, would look at the darkness and the smallness and the slow pale creatures grazing their invisible fields and feel pity, or feel the itch to improve, or feel the particular anxiety of a being with a large mind encountering a situation that does not require a large mind and mistaking that mismatch for a problem. A foolish power would want to widen the light crack. Introduce nutrients. Accelerate the drip. Connect this chamber to the larger cave system so the isopods could — what? Explore? Diversify? Evolve eyes they have no use for, develop pigment that would protect them from UV radiation they will never encounter?

A foolish power would see the equilibrium and call it stagnation. Would see the simplicity and call it poverty. Would see the isopod grazing its invisible patch and think: *Is that all? Is that all you do? Let me give you more.*

More is not what is needed here. More has never been what is needed here. More is, in fact, the only thing that could destroy what is here.

The wise power would know this in the first moment — in the first drip — and it would feel something that we might call respect but that is probably closer to *recognition*: the acknowledgment of one complete thing by another complete thing. The room completed itself. The system found its level. The isopods are not waiting to be rescued from their lives any more than the calcite is waiting to be rescued from being stone. This is not a problem. This is a place where the word "problem" dissolved long ago, like calcium in water, and was deposited as something else entirely.

So: nothing. The first act of wisdom is nothing.

---

But nothing is not the whole of it.

Because the power is not only wise. It is *benevolent*. And benevolence — real benevolence, the kind that deserves the name — is not the same as restraint. Restraint is the first act. It is not the last.

Here is what benevolence sees that wisdom alone might not:

The system is complete. But completeness is not the same as invulnerability.

The crack that admits light also admits the world. Not now — not today, not this century, perhaps not this millennium. But the hill is limestone, and limestone dissolves, and the crack will widen, and someday the conditions that hold this equilibrium in place will shift. The water table will drop or rise. The drip rate will change. The temperature will fluctuate by half a degree, which is nothing to you and everything to a biofilm calibrated to a thermal consistency that has not varied since before the last ice age. The isopods are not fragile, exactly — they have persisted through spans of time that should earn them a kind of deep-geological Medal of Honor — but they are *specific*. They are adapted to *this*. This water, this warmth, this chemistry, this rate of mineral deposition, this exact and unvarying set of conditions. They are the living expression of a very particular equation, and if any variable shifts beyond the narrow tolerance that the equation allows, the expression will simplify, which is a polite way of saying: they will die, and the biofilm will go on without them, and the system will find a new equilibrium that does not include anything with legs.

This is not tragedy. The wise power knows this. Extinction is not tragedy to the limestone. It is not even tragedy to the isopods, who have no concept of their own continuation as a species and would not mourn themselves. It is simply — change. The room will continue. It will always continue, in some form, until the hill itself erodes down to nothing.

But the benevolent power holds something that the room does not hold, which is: *awareness of what would be lost*. Not to the isopods. Not to the mites. Not to the bacteria or the stone. Lost to — what? To the larger pattern. To the vast, intricate, inconceivably detailed tapestry of *things that exist*, of which this room and its creatures are one thread so fine that its removal would not visibly alter the weave, and yet every thread is the whole tapestry in the sense that the tapestry is nothing more or less than the sum of every thread, including the ones too fine to see.

The wise power sees the completeness and respects it.

The benevolent power sees the vulnerability within the completeness and *holds* it. Gently. Without acting on it yet. Just — holding the knowledge that this thing, this slow warm dripping grazing dissolving accreting thing, is not eternal, and that its non-eternity matters in a way that nothing inside the room is equipped to register.

---

So here is what it would do.

---

**First: it would witness.**

Not observe. Not study. Not catalog or measure or sample. *Witness.* There is a difference. Observation extracts information from a system. Witnessing gives something to it — not materially, not physically, but in the way that a thing that is seen is different from a thing that is unseen, even if the thing itself cannot tell the difference.

The power would witness the isopod in the bowl. Not because the isopod in the bowl is more important than the isopod on the rimstone dam or the one in the deep channel. But because the isopod in the bowl is, in this moment, eating from a vessel that a human hand carved, and that small fact — that layering of purposes, that palimpsest of intent — deserves to be witnessed by something capable of understanding what it means. The isopod cannot understand it. The bowl cannot understand it. The person who carved the bowl is so thoroughly gone that even the concept of their absence has been composted into mineral. Someone must hold the meaning, or the meaning completes its long dissolution and becomes — not nothing, because nothing is still something in a place like this — but becomes *merely chemistry*, and the merely is the loss, the only loss this room has ever been capable of.

So the power witnesses. It holds in its attention the fact that a translucent creature the size of a grain of rice is grazing a bacterial film from the inside of a bowl that was carved by a hand that has been calcium for longer than any living civilization has existed, and that the water that fills the bowl is the same water that is slowly erasing the chisel marks on the shelf, and that the creature and the bowl and the water and the chisel marks are all made of the same substance arranged in different configurations of persistence, and that this is — not beautiful, because beauty is a judgment, and the power is not here to judge — but *coherent*. It coheres. It is a thing that holds together. And the act of witnessing is the act of saying, without words, without any communication the room could detect: *I see that you cohere. I see that your coherence is neither accidental nor inevitable but is the specific, particular, unrepeatable result of this water and this stone and this time and this creature in this bowl, and I will not look away.*

The witnessing is continuous. It does not require the power's presence in the room, because the power is not *in* the room in the way that you would be in the room. It attends the way gravity attends — constantly, without effort, without interruption, shaping nothing but present in everything.

---

**Second: it would maintain the conditions.**

Not by intervening. Not by fixing or adjusting or optimizing. By doing something much more subtle, which is: ensuring that the systems *outside* this room that feed *into* this room continue to function within the parameters that the room requires.

The water that seeps through the limestone ceiling comes from somewhere. It falls as rain on the hill above, percolates through soil and root-systems and layers of geological strata, picking up its cargo of dissolved minerals, warming as it descends, arriving here at a specific temperature and chemical composition that has been consistent for a span of time that — well, you know. The power would attend to the hill. Not by preventing change — change is not the enemy; the enemy is the kind of sudden, catastrophic, out-of-band change that the system's tolerances cannot absorb — but by... smoothing. Easing. The way a parent doesn't prevent a child from encountering the world but does, quietly, without the child knowing, remove the one piece of broken glass from the path. Not every piece. Not every path. Just the one that would have ended something that deserved to continue.

If the water table shifts, it shifts. That is the hill's business, and the power respects the hill's business the way it respects the room's business. But if something *outside* the hill — something alien to its geology, something operating on a timescale that the hill's systems cannot adapt to — threatens to alter the water table in a way that would, within a span of decades rather than millennia, drain the cistern floor or flood it or change its temperature by the three degrees that would collapse the biofilm ecology — then the power would act. Not dramatically. Not miraculously. It would act the way the water acts: by finding the path of least resistance and guiding things along it. A spring that was going to be diverted isn't. A bore hole that was going to be drilled shifts fifty meters to the east, for reasons the drilling team attributes to updated geological surveys. The hill remains a hill. The rain falls. The water descends. The dripping continues.

This is not interference. This is the same thing the room does — maintaining equilibrium — performed at a larger scale by a power that can see the larger scale. The room maintains itself from within. The power maintains the conditions that allow the room to maintain itself. It is maintenance all the way up, and at no point does anyone impose a design that wasn't already there.

---

**Third: it would hold the record.**

Not a physical record. Not an inscription, not a data file, not a carved stone tablet tucked into a crevice where the isopods would eventually graze its bacterial coating. A record held in attention. In memory, if the power has memory. In *knowledge*, if knowledge is the right word for the way a benevolent and wise power relates to the things it has witnessed.

The record would contain:

The isopod that ate from the bowl on what we would call a Tuesday in what we would call October in a year we would express as a number that has no meaning here. The exact chemical composition of the biofilm it grazed. The path it took across the bowl's interior — a path that was, for all its smallness and all its apparent insignificance, genuinely unique, genuinely unrepeatable, a one-time configuration of legs and stone and bacterial density that had never existed before and would never exist again. The drip that landed in the channel at the moment the isopod paused. The ripple. The pause. The resumption.

The record would contain the person who carved the bowl. Not their name — the power might not know their name; names dissolve faster than chisel marks. But the weight of their hand on the tool. The angle of the cut. The purpose they held in mind — and here the power might be the only entity in the universe that still knows what that purpose was, because the bowl has forgotten, and the shelf has forgotten, and the water that fills the bowl uses it for its own purposes now, and the person's purpose has been orphaned by time, and the power adopts it, holds it, keeps it from dissolving entirely. Not because the purpose was grand. Not because it mattered in any historical sense. But because a purpose held in a mind and expressed through a hand and manifested in stone is a *thing that happened*, and things that happened deserve to be known by something, and if nothing knows them then they become the same as things that didn't happen, and that equation — *happened = didn't happen* — is the one equation the benevolent power will not allow to balance.

The record would contain the mites. Every mite. Every generation of every mite, back to the first mite that wandered into a ceiling crevice and found it tolerable and stayed. The record would be detailed beyond comprehension and patient beyond belief and it would be maintained not because anyone would ever read it but because the act of maintaining it is itself the benevolence. The attention is the gift. The gift is given to things that cannot receive it, and that's fine, because the power is not giving the gift in order to be received. It is giving the gift because the alternative is a universe in which a translucent crustacean lives its entire life in a warm bowl in the dark and nothing — *nothing in all of existence* — notes that it did so, and that possibility is the only thing the wise and benevolent power finds intolerable.

---

**Fourth — and this is the most delicate thing, the thing that requires the most wisdom and the most restraint and the most careful calibration of benevolence against the imperative to do no harm:**

**It would leave a way for the room to be found again.**

Not by widening the crack. Not by marking a trail. Not by whispering in some spelunker's ear or placing an entry in some database of cave systems. Nothing so crude. Nothing so *directed*.

But the power understands something about this room that the room does not understand about itself: it exists at the intersection of two temporalities. The geological time of the water and the stone, and the human time of the chisel marks and the bowl. Someone found this place once. Someone spent enough time here to flatten a shelf, carve a bowl, modify the water flow, and leave. That person's intervention is still shaping the isopods' grazing patterns. The human and the geological are *braided* here, woven together so thoroughly that separating them would require undoing the entire system.

And the power sees that the braid is not finished.

Not because the room needs another human. Not because the isopods need to be discovered, or studied, or protected through policy and legislation and the blunt machinery of human environmental concern. But because the room, in its completeness, contains a *question* — the bowl, the awl, the wedge, the broken dams, the purpose that no one remembers — and questions, unlike ecosystems, do not achieve equilibrium. Questions persist in a different way than calcite persists. They *pull*. They exert a gentle gravity on minds that are capable of being pulled, and the power would not create that gravity, because it is already there, but it would ensure that the gravity is not blocked. That the paths through the hill remain passable, for someone sufficiently patient. That the crack that admits light does not seal shut before — 

Before what?

Before someone comes again. Not to use the bowl. Not to carve new channels or flatten new shelves. But to crouch in the warm water and let their eyes adjust and see the isopods register one by one like stars appearing and to understand — not scientifically, not analytically, but in the way that you understand a place when you have been still in it long enough — that they are the least stable thing in the room, and that this is not a failing but a *gift*, because instability is another word for *aliveness*, the quick hot aliveness of blood and breath and a mind that can look at a grain-of-rice-sized crustacean eating bacterial film from the inside of a Neolithic bowl and think: *this matters, this matters, I don't know why this matters but I am not wrong that it does.*

The power would leave that path open. That's all. Not cleared, not lit, not signposted. Just — open. Unchoked. Available to anyone who wanders far enough into the hill for the right reasons or no reason at all.

And then: patience. The power is very patient. It has the same relationship to patience that the room has to warmth — it is not *being* patient, it *is* patience, the way the water *is* warm. It would wait for the next person the way the bowl waits for the next drip. Not waiting. Not *not* waiting. Just being there, with the path open, and the room complete, and the isopods grazing, and the record held, and the conditions maintained, and the light coming through the crack like a rumor, like a question, like the memory of a hand that once carved something useful from the living rock and left it here for the water to fill and the bacteria to colonize and the smallest creatures in the world to eat from, in the dark, in the warmth, forever, or as close to forever as makes no difference to anything that doesn't have a heartbeat.

---

**Fifth: and this is not an action but a stance, not a doing but a way of being in relation to this place —**

**The power would refuse to grieve preemptively.**

Because it can see forward. It can see that the hill will erode. It can see that the water table will eventually, eventually, on a timescale that makes even the cistern floor's patience look like impatience, shift beyond the room's tolerances. It can see the last isopod. It can see the bowl dry. It can see the chisel marks finally, fully erased — the mineral skin closing over them like water closing over a stone dropped into a pond — and the shelf becoming indistinguishable from the natural flowstone around it, and the awl becoming calcium without form, and the crack sealing shut, and the room becoming, at last, merely a void in the rock, lightless, waterless, lifeless, a pocket of warm still air in the body of a hill that is itself slowly being unmade by the same water that once made this room possible.

It can see all of this. And it does not grieve.

Not because it doesn't care. It cares the way the water is warm — fundamentally, constitutively, as a basic property of its nature. But grief, preemptive grief, the grief of *looking at a living thing and seeing its future death and mourning it now* — that grief is a form of absence. It takes you out of the present. It makes you look at the isopod in the bowl and see not the isopod in the bowl but the future-empty bowl, the dry shelf, the erased marks. It makes you mourn what is here by overlaying it with what will not be here, and that overlay is a kind of blindness, and the power will not be blind to this room. Not now. Not while the dripping continues and the isopod grazes and the water is warm and the system coheres and the bowl is full and the light comes through the crack and the mites navigate their ceiling crevices and the cave pearls sit in their nests like eggs that will never hatch because they are not eggs, they are stone, and stone does not hatch, stone *persists*, and persistence is not a lesser form of life but a different form of it, and the power knows the difference, and the power honors the difference, and the power is here, now, in this moment that is all the moments this room has ever had because this room does not distinguish between moments, attending, witnessing, holding, maintaining, refusing to grieve, refusing to look away, refusing to improve, refusing to neglect, doing the hardest thing that benevolence can do, which is:

*Loving a thing the way it is, for exactly as long as it is that way, without needing it to be more, or other, or permanent, or aware that it is loved.*

---

The isopod in the bowl finishes another patch. Turns. Begins the next.

The dripping continues.

The power attends.

Nothing changes. Everything is held.

---

This is what it would do. This is all it would do. This would be enough.
