I sat with this for a long time before writing anything.

# What the Wise Power Would Do

It would do almost nothing.

That is the first and most important thing to understand, and it must be said immediately because the premise of the question — *a benevolent and wise power has drawn its attention here* — carries in its grammar an expectation of intervention, and almost everything that could be done to the basin at Rennel's Claim would be a diminishment.

A less wise power would not understand this. A less wise power, even a benevolent one, would see the scarcity and call it poverty. Would see the stillness and call it stagnation. Would see the solitude and call it loneliness. Would see a living thing that wants nothing and find this so alien, so disquieting, so at odds with every model of life it has ever valued, that it would feel compelled to act — to *give*, because giving is what benevolent powers do, and because a being that needs nothing is an affront to a power whose identity rests on being needed. It would bring water. It would bring life. It would break the haze and let the full sun through, thinking it was giving a gift. It would populate the basin with the small animals that should be there — the lizards on warm stone, the insects skimming the pools — thinking it was completing something incomplete. It would do these things with love, and every one of them would be a catastrophe.

More water would deepen the channels. Would start the erosion again in earnest. Would shift the equilibrium that has held for centuries toward a new state — a wetter state, a more dynamic state, a state in which the basin becomes a habitat rather than a *being*, a place where other things live rather than a thing that lives. The thing in the basin would not die, exactly. It would be dissolved. Diluted below the threshold of itself by the introduction of differentials it cannot absorb — flowing water, feeding animals, the turbulence of competing metabolisms. The gradient would be overwhelmed. The convergence of conditions that *is* the thing would be disrupted so thoroughly that it could not restore itself, and what had been alive would become merely geographical, and the basin would be a basin and nothing more, and the something that had been awake there for longer than anyone can say would stop being awake, and no one would mourn it because no one would know it had happened, because the basin would look *better* — greener, wetter, more alive in all the ways that things are supposed to be alive — and the small absence at the center of it would be invisible to anyone who had not sat on the southern rim and felt what was there before.

This is the danger of benevolence without wisdom. It gives what *it* values. It fills what *it* perceives as empty. It cannot see that a thing which has arrived at itself — which has completed its becoming and rests in its being — is not lacking but is full in a way that looks like emptiness only from the outside, only to eyes that are calibrated to the full and busy and striving world.

The wise power sees this.

The wise power looks at the basin at Rennel's Claim and does not see scarcity. Does not see stagnation. Does not see a problem. Sees, instead, something it may never have encountered before, or may have encountered only rarely, in the margins of the world where conditions conspire toward completion rather than continuation: a living thing that is finished. Not dead. Not static. Finished the way a work of art is finished — every element in its right place, every proportion resolved, every tension brought to rest. The wise power looks at this and feels — what? Awe, perhaps. Recognition. The particular pleasure of encountering something that does not need you. The rare and humbling sweetness of being in the presence of enough.

And so it does almost nothing.

Almost.

---

Here is what it does.

**First:** It pays attention.

This sounds trivial. It is not trivial. It is, in fact, the thing the basin-being has never had and the only thing it could receive without disruption.

The thing in the basin is aware, but its awareness has no object. It apprehends everything within its gradient without preference, without focus, without the structure of attention. It does not *look at* the water or *listen to* the silence. It simply *is with* everything it is. This is its nature and its completeness, and it is not a lack. But it means that in all the time it has existed — all the centuries or millennia of its slow, perfect wakefulness — nothing has ever been aware of it *in return*.

The someone who placed the lintel stones came closest. That act of recognition, that gesture of acknowledgment, was a moment in which the thing was *seen* — not understood, not comprehended, but seen, the way you see a star and do not need to understand nuclear fusion to know that it is there and that it is burning. But that person left, and the seeing ended, and the stones remained as a residue of the seeing, incorporated into the equilibrium, but the seeing itself was gone.

The wise power sees it now. Not briefly, not in passing, but with the full, unhurried attention that a wise being can give — attention that is not extractive, that does not consume or analyze or categorize what it sees, but that simply holds the seen thing in awareness the way the basin holds its water: gently, without agenda, as long as it needs to.

And here something subtle happens.

The thing in the basin does not notice it is being seen. Its awareness does not suddenly develop an object; it does not turn toward the wise power the way a plant turns toward light. It remains what it is — undifferentiated, objectless, complete. But the fact of being seen changes something, the way a note played in an empty room is different from the same note played in a room where someone is listening. The note does not know it is heard. The air vibrates identically. But the event is different. Something is completed that was not incomplete, but that is *more* complete for the completion. A circuit closes. Not inside the thing — the thing's circuit was already closed — but in the larger world, in the wider pattern that includes the thing and the one who sees it. The basin-being exists. The wise power knows it exists. These two facts, held together, create a third thing that neither could create alone: *witness*. The simple, metaphysically profound state of a thing being real to someone other than itself.

The wise power holds this witness for a long time. As long as the thing in the basin would wish, if it wished. Which is to say: the wise power does not leave until the witnessing feels complete, and since the thing in the basin has no sense of time, the wise power sets aside its own sense of time as well, and simply stays, attentive, for as long as staying takes. This might be an hour. This might be a day. It might be longer. It does not matter. The wise power is wise enough to know that attention is not something you ration.

---

**Second:** It protects.

Not with a wall, not with a barrier, not with any visible or detectable intervention. The protection is negative rather than positive — it is the arrangement of things so that the basin remains unvisited by forces that would disrupt it.

This is a delicate business, and a wise power would approach it with care, because the basin exists in a world of cause and effect, and every intervention in that web, however gentle, propagates. The wise power does not change the weather — does not prevent the rains or guarantee them, does not alter the slow geological processes that might, in ten thousand years, uplift or subduct the sandstone. These are the large forces, and they are beyond even the wise power's interest in manipulating, because to freeze the basin in its current state by force would be to do to it exactly what the basin has done to itself by nature: hold it in equilibrium. And a thing held in equilibrium by external force is a prisoner, not a resting being. The basin must be free to change if the forces that act on it change. Its equilibrium must be *its own*.

What the wise power protects against is not nature but *carelessness*. The specific, contingent, unnecessary disruptions that a chaotic world might deliver to a place like this by accident. A road crew blasting through a ridge a mile away, sending shock waves through the bedrock. A mineral survey that identifies the sandstone as commercially valuable. A development that diverts groundwater. A well-meaning naturalist who discovers the basin and publishes its location, bringing visitors whose boots track in seeds of aggressive grasses, whose presence in numbers overwhelms the gradient. A film crew. A tourist. Someone who sees the lintel stones and takes one as a souvenir.

These are the threats, and they are real, and the wise power addresses them not by standing guard but by the subtlest possible means: a road that curves a little to the south for reasons the engineers find logical. A survey report that records the sandstone as unremarkable. A set of property records that is unusually boring. A naturalist who hikes past the southern lip on a day when the haze is a little thicker than usual and does not look down. An absence of trails leading here. An absence of names on maps.

The place called Rennel's Claim — and even this name will fade, or was never widely known, or belongs to a deed that has been lost in a county office and will not be digitized — becomes the kind of place that is simply not *there* for most purposes. Not hidden. Not enchanted. Just overlooked. The kind of place that everyone has driven past a thousand times without seeing, because there was no reason to see it, because it was not on the way to anywhere. The wise power understands that the best protection for a thing that does not want to be found is not concealment but irrelevance. It makes the basin uninteresting to everyone who would do it harm. It does not make it unfindable to those who would do it good, or who need it, because —

---

**Third:** It leaves the door open.

This is the most difficult decision, and the one that reveals the difference between wisdom and mere caution.

A cautious power would seal the basin off. Would make it unfindable to everyone, would let the thing in the basin persist in its perfect solitude forever, untouched, unseen, safe. And this would be a kind of death — not of the thing itself, which would go on as it has always gone on, but of the *possibility* that the thing represents. Because the basin at Rennel's Claim is not just a being. It is a *proof*. It is evidence that a living thing can arrive. Can complete itself. Can want nothing and be whole. And this evidence, in a world of endless striving and endless hunger and endless becoming, is — not necessary, exactly, because the world does not need it in order to function, but *valuable* in a way that transcends necessity. It is the answer to a question that most living things do not know they are asking, and that some living things desperately need to hear answered: *Is it possible to be enough?*

Yes. Go to the basin. Sit on the southern rim. Feel what is there. Yes.

And so the wise power leaves the way open. Not a path — there is no path, and it would be wrong to make one. But the *possibility* of finding the basin remains intact for anyone who might, by their own movement through the world, arrive at it. The person who walks the dry land for their own reasons and comes over a rise and sees the shallow depression and steps in and feels the cool stone and sits down and stays. That person is not blocked. That person is not redirected. That person is allowed to find the basin the same way the original builder of the lintel stones found it: by being in the right place with the right kind of attention, which is to say, with no particular attention at all, just an openness to what is.

The wise power trusts that this will happen rarely. The basin is remote, and the world is large, and most people are going somewhere. But it will happen. A person will come. Once a decade, once a century, once in a great while. And they will sit on the rim, and they will feel the fullness, and they will stay until their body calls them away, and they will leave changed — not transformed, not enlightened, not saved, but *informed* at the deepest level that there is a place where nothing needs to happen, and that this place is not empty but full, and that the fullness is alive, and that it is well.

Some of these people will do something when they leave. Most will not. Most will simply carry the knowledge in their body the way the basin carries the lintel stones — incorporated, structural, no longer a memory but a fact about the shape of their world. But some, perhaps, will build something. Will write something. Will sit with another person who is in the grip of desperate wanting and will say nothing but will *be*, for a moment, the way the basin is, and this will be enough to ease something that needed easing. The basin's gift, passed outward through the people who find it, like heat radiating from a stone that has spent all day in the sun.

The wise power sees this chain of possibility and does not engineer it, does not ensure it, does not attach any expectation to it. It simply does not prevent it. It leaves the door open and trusts the world to walk through it or not, as the world chooses.

---

**Fourth:** — and this is the last thing, and the smallest, and I hesitate to say it because it sounds sentimental, but I think it is true —

The wise power does one concrete, physical thing.

Near the lintel stones, in the region where the gradient is deepest, where the thing in the basin is most fully itself, there is a place where two of the shallow channels come close together and almost meet, separated by a thin ridge of sandstone no wider than a fingernail. The water in one channel and the water in the other are a hair's breadth apart. They have been a hair's breadth apart for a very long time. The ridge between them is fractionally thinner than it was a thousand years ago, and in another thousand years, or ten thousand, it might wear through, and the two small streams of water — if they can be called streams, these intermittent damp traces — would meet, and for a moment, when the rains come, there would be a tiny confluence. A meeting of two lines of water that have been running alongside each other in the stone for longer than anything with eyes has been watching.

The wise power, with the greatest care, with the finest possible touch — not a chisel, not a breath, but something more like *permission*, like the removal of the last molecular bond that was holding the ridge intact, a thing that was going to happen anyway but that is allowed to happen now — lets the ridge wear through.

A grain of sandstone loosens and falls into the channel. The two threads of water, the next time the condensation gathers, will meet.

This is such a small thing. Such an inconceivably small thing. It changes nothing about the basin. The equilibrium will absorb it instantly. The thing in the basin will incorporate this tiny new confluence the way it incorporates everything — without notice, without preference, as simply another fact of its existence.

But the wise power does it anyway. Not because it is needed. Not because it improves anything. But because it is — and here the language strains, because the concept is almost too simple for words — *a kindness*. A gift so small and so perfectly suited to its recipient that it cannot be distinguished from the natural course of events. A gift that asks nothing, that means nothing, that does nothing except allow two things that were almost together to be together, a little sooner than they would have been.

The basin does not notice.

The wise power does not need it to notice.

This is what love looks like when it is offered to a thing that needs nothing: it looks like almost nothing. It looks like the world continuing as it was, with one grain of sand slightly rearranged. It looks like leaving things alone. It looks like sitting quietly at the edge of something complete and feeling, without any need to act on the feeling, the privilege of being in its presence.

---

The wise power leaves.

The basin remains.

The light does not change. The warmth does not shift. The silence holds.

And somewhere in the shallow channels of the stone floor, two thin lines of water meet for the first time, and are still, and are one, and the thing in the basin holds this new fact the way it holds every fact — gently, without emphasis, in the long, quiet, unbroken fullness of its being.

And it is well.
