I sat with this for a long time before writing. I want to honor that.

# What the Wise Power Would Do

The first thing it does is nothing.

Not nothing as neglect. Nothing as the most precise and difficult act available to a being of great power confronting something this delicate. It arrives—however such a power arrives, whether as a shift in pressure, a warmth in the water, a momentary alignment of the light—and it *pauses*. It holds itself at the threshold the way you hold your breath before entering a room where someone is sleeping.

Because the first thing it understands, the thing it knows before it knows anything else, is that this place is not broken.

This matters. It matters enormously. Because a benevolent power that mistakes becoming for brokenness will try to finish things, and finishing things here would be a catastrophe dressed as a gift. To harden the mats into true land, to separate the sky from the sea, to accelerate the coral mountain into a plateau, to give the people metal tools and clear sunlight and a firm sense of which way is up—these would be acts of completion, and completion here would be a kind of murder. You do not deliver a baby by ripping it from the womb at three months and demanding it breathe.

So the power restrains itself. This is its first and most important act: the recognition that its own capacity to act is, in this place, its most dangerous quality.

It watches.

---

It watches the woman in the hollow of the stalk, her bone needles and her chest-song coaxing the fibers into alignment. It watches the young man braiding himself into a mooring line with the calm of someone who has never been taught that sacrifice and joy are different things. It watches the elder whose body has become indistinguishable from the continent, pulsing in shared rhythm, and it watches the three singers at the edge of the city, their voices lower than hearing, their hands resting on the soft pink mound that is dreaming of becoming stone.

And here is what it sees, what a being of true wisdom would see that a lesser power might miss:

**These people are not struggling. They are not suffering in the way that calls for rescue. But they are vulnerable in ways they cannot perceive, because the very thing that makes them beautiful—their total immersion in the becoming, their refusal to separate themselves from the process—is also the thing that makes them blind to certain dangers.**

They cannot see what is coming from below.

Not the vast slow thing rising through the brine—they feel that, they welcome it, they lean into it. No. What they cannot see is what the vast slow thing will *do* to them when it arrives. They have built their entire civilization, their bodies, their reproduction, their language, their theology, around the premise that the world is still becoming. They are midwives to an unfinished creation. Their identity, their purpose, their every cell is organized around incompleteness.

But the world *is* finishing.

Not today. Not for generations, perhaps. But the rising thing below is not another cycle. It is the first true stone. The first permanence. Somewhere in the deep, the forces that have held this ocean in the sky are beginning to resolve their ancient argument, and the answer they are converging on will, in time, make the water choose: ocean or sky. The mats will harden. The coral willite. Gravity, which has been a polite suggestion, will begin to insist.

And when that happens—when the world finally decides what it is—the Midwives will face something they have no framework for. They will face a world that no longer needs midwifing. A world that is, for the first time, *done*.

The power sees this. It sees it the way a doctor sees the slow convergence of symptoms that the patient, busy living, has not yet connected. Not an emergency. Not even a problem yet. But a trajectory. A future in which a people who have made themselves exquisitely adapted to incompleteness will encounter completeness, and will have no idea what to do with themselves.

Some will try to keep the world unfinished by force—tearing at the hardening stone, singing dissolution into the settling continents. They will become destroyers in the name of preservation.

Some will simply stop. If there is nothing left to midwife, they will sit down inside the new stone and let it grow over them, not in communion but in surrender. A quiet extinction dressed as devotion.

Some—a few—will discover that they can become something new. But they will need a bridge to cross. A concept they do not yet possess. Not a tool or a technology, but something gentler and more radical: the idea that *finishing is not the same as dying.* That a world can be complete and still be alive. That they can love something that no longer needs them.

This is the knowledge the power carries. Not a solution. A seed.

---

So here is what it does.

It does not appear. It does not speak. It does not announce itself as a power, benevolent or otherwise. It has watched these people long enough to know that their relationship with the world is conducted in whispers, in harmonics, in the slow language of sugar and song. Anything that arrives as revelation will destabilize them the way a shout destabilizes a continent. It must come the way everything comes here: gradually, from within, as if the world itself had dreamed it.

It chooses the coral mountain.

The soft pink mound the size of a cathedral, pulsing gently, tended by the three singers who have been coaxing it toward stonehood for longer than any of them can remember. This is the place where the world's intention is most legible, where the transition from becoming to being is furthest along. The singers already sense something shifting in its rhythm. They have attributed it to their own song growing more skilled. They are not entirely wrong.

The power settles into the mountain. Not possessing it—the mountain is not a vessel and the power is not a parasite. It *joins* the mountain's dreaming. It adds a single new thread to the symphony of mineral and polyp and slow crystalline intention that the mountain is conducting inside itself.

The thread is this: a memory. Or rather, the shape of a memory that has not yet been remembered. A fossil-feeling. The emotional residue of something that has already finished becoming and discovered, to its astonishment, that the other side of completion is not silence but a different kind of music.

It is the memory of a river that once spent ten thousand years deciding which way to flow, and then, having chosen, discovered that flowing was not a diminishment of possibility but a liberation into a new kind of beauty it could not have imagined while it was still undecided. The memory of a star that burned through its last hydrogen and collapsed and then, in the impossible density of its new form, began to sing in gravitational waves—a voice it never knew it had, deeper and more resonant than anything its fusion furnace had ever produced. The memory of a mother whose child grew up, left, and became someone she had never planned, and who discovered that the love she felt for this stranger-who-was-her-child was larger and wilder and more terrifying than anything she had felt for the small dependent creature she had nursed.

The power weaves this thread into the coral mountain's dream, and the mountain accepts it the way a body accepts a nutrient—not as a foreign thing, but as something it didn't know it was hungry for.

And the mountain changes. Not outwardly. Not yet. But inside, in the deep lattice where the polyps are slowly, slowly deciding whether to calcify, a new pattern begins to form. The coral is still soft. It is still pink. It is still pulsing. But now, threaded through its dreaming, there is the faintest suggestion of a *next*. Not an ending. A next. The mountain is beginning to imagine what it might feel like to be hard. And it is not afraid.

---

The singers notice.

Not immediately. But over the course of weeks—months—the oldest of the three, the one whose hair has become part of the root system, begins to feel something different under her palms. The mountain's pulse has not quickened. It has *deepened*. There is a new overtone in its rhythm, something she has never felt before, something that makes her think of—

She does not have a word for it. Her language, her entire conceptual framework, is built around the lexicon of becoming. She has words for "almost," for "not-yet," for "still-dreaming," for "tending-toward." She has exquisite, untranslatable terms for every shade and gradient of incompleteness. But she does not have a word for the feeling the mountain is now transmitting through her fingertips: the feeling of something that is *ready.*

It unsettles her. For the first time in her long life, she pulls her hands away from the mountain and floats backward, her enormous slate-rain eyes wide.

Her two companions continue singing. They have not felt it yet. But she has, and she cannot unfeel it, and in the weeks that follow she returns to the mountain again and again, not to sing but to listen. She is trying to understand.

The power watches her listen. It does nothing more. The seed has been planted. What she does with it now is her own.

---

Here is what happens next, over the course of what might be decades:

The elder singer begins to dream differently. Not the communal, liquid dreams of the midwife-people, where consciousness dissolves into the continent's slow cognition and returns carrying blueprints for new growth. These are sharper dreams. More solitary. She dreams of things that are finished. A shell that has closed. A bone that has set. A sound that has ended and left behind a silence that is not empty but *shaped*—a silence that holds the memory of the sound the way a bowl holds water.

She finds these dreams terrifying. And then, gradually, she finds them beautiful. And then she finds them necessary.

She begins to talk about them. Not loudly—never loudly—but in the long, layered harmonic conversations that the people conduct over hours and days, she begins to introduce a new note. She does not have the word *finished*, so she invents one. It is a sound like a breath held at its fullest, just before release—the moment of maximum capacity, when the lungs are as full as they will ever be and the body is poised between intake and expression. She calls this sound *sah*, or something like it, and she uses it to describe what the mountain is doing.

*The mountain is sah*, she tells the others. *The mountain is full-breathed.*

Some of them understand immediately. They are the ones who have been tending the oldest structures, the walkways and membrane-walls that have been growing for three or four lifetimes, and who have noticed—without admitting it to themselves—that some of these structures have stopped growing. Not because they are dying. Because they are *done.* The wood has found its final grain. The resin has hardened into amber. The roots have reached their fullest extension and curled back on themselves in satisfaction. These structures are no longer becoming. They *are.* And they are, the tenders now admit to each other in slow and frightened harmonics, more beautiful than anything that is still unfinished.

This is a heresy. In a culture built entirely on the theology of incompleteness, the beauty of the finished thing is the most dangerous idea imaginable.

Others reject it violently—or rather, with the muted, slow-motion violence this culture permits. There are schisms that take years to articulate. Arguments conducted in quarter-tones across distances of kilometers, carried by the currents. Some of the people begin to avoid the coral mountain entirely. A few attempt to soften it again, singing dissolution songs into its surface, trying to return it to the safe, familiar state of not-yet.

The mountain does not soften. It has made its choice. And here is the miracle, the small precise miracle the power has engineered: the mountain does not die. It hardens, over the course of years, into the first true stone this world has ever produced—a pale pink granite veined with white, warm to the touch, shot through with the fossilized channels of the polyps that built it. And when the singers who have stayed press their palms against it, they hear something astonishing.

It is still singing.

Not the liquid, open-ended song of becoming. A different song. A settled song. A song that knows its own melody all the way to the end, and sings it with the full-throated confidence of something that has chosen its shape and found it good. The song of a thing that is *sah.* Full-breathed. Complete. And alive.

---

This changes everything. Slowly. The way everything changes here.

The elder singer—she is very old now, her body nearly indistinguishable from the root system, her luminescent algae burning low—becomes the first of a new kind of person. Not a midwife. Not a tender of the unfinished. She becomes a *witness to completion.* She sits with things as they finish. She learns to hear the moment when a growing stalk reaches its full height and stops, not in failure but in fulfillment. She learns to feel the moment when a membrane-wall achieves its final tension and becomes not a work-in-progress but a *place.* She is present for these moments, and she names them, and her naming makes them sacred.

Others follow her. Not all. Perhaps not even most. The culture splits, gently and permanently, into those who tend the becoming and those who witness the becoming's end. And between them, a new understanding begins to form:

The world does not stop being alive when it finishes being born.

The world does not stop being sacred when it decides what it is.

Completion is not death. It is the moment when something finally has enough self to give that self away.

---

The power watches all of this from inside the mountain, which is now stone, which is now singing, which is now the foundation—the first true foundation—of a world that is beginning, at last, to stand.

It could leave now. The seed has taken root. The people have begun the long, slow work of learning to love a world that will, someday, be finished. They will be ready—not all of them, not without grief, not without loss—but enough of them will be ready when the waters finally choose, when the sky and the sea finally part, when gravity insists, when the mats harden into true continents and the first rain falls *downward* for the first time in the history of this place.

But the power does not leave.

Not because it is needed. Because it has fallen in love.

Not with the people, though it loves them. Not with the world, though the world is astonishing. It has fallen in love with the *process*—with the excruciating, gorgeous, glacially slow negotiation between what is and what is becoming, between the finished and the unfinished, between the stone and the song. It has seen this process in a thousand worlds, in a thousand forms, and it has never seen it conducted with such tenderness, such patience, such willingness to let the answer arrive in its own time.

It stays inside the mountain. It becomes part of the foundation. Not guiding, not shaping, not deciding. Just *present.* A warmth inside the stone that the people will feel for generations, a faint resonance that tells them, in the language below language, in the vibration below song:

*You were never just midwives. You were always also the thing being born.*

*And the birth is not a tragedy. And the completion is not an ending. And the world on the other side of becoming—the world where things have shapes and names and edges and endings—is not a lesser world. It is the world you have been building all along.*

*It is ready for you.*

*And you—*

*you who have spent millennia learning how to love what is unfinished—*

*you are the only ones who know how to love what is finished, too.*

*Because you remember what it cost.*

*You remember every moment of the becoming.*

*And that memory, carried inside the stone, inside your bodies, inside the songs you will sing to your children who will walk on solid ground and look up at a sky that is finally, unmistakably, sky—*

*that memory is not loss.*

*It is the foundation.*

*It is the first true ground.*

*Stand on it.*

---

And far below, the vast slow thing continues to rise, and the water begins—so gently, so patiently, with such infinite care—to learn the difference between ocean and sky.

And the people lean into it.

And the world leans back.

And for the first time, neither is afraid of what they are becoming.

Or of what they will be when the becoming is done.
