# The Regard

---

The benevolent power does not descend the shaft.

It has no need to. It was already aware of the Underwet the way you are aware of your own breathing—dimly, constantly, with a flicker of sharper attention only when the rhythm changes. The Underwet's rhythm has not changed in a very long time. That is part of what draws the attention now. Not alarm. Something closer to the feeling of noticing, mid-afternoon, that a sound you thought was the wind has actually been a voice all along.

So it attends.

Not with eyes. The power does not see the Underwet so much as *taste* it—the mineral density of its air, the sub-sonic pulse of its buried pumps, the particular frequency at which four hundred luminous bodies move through warm water in slow, drifting processions that almost, but never quite, become a pattern. It tastes the patience of the place. It tastes the almost-ness of it.

And for a long time—longer than you would expect from something called benevolent, longer than you would tolerate from something called wise—it does nothing.

---

Here is what the power understands that you might not, standing knee-deep in that trembling lake with moths orbiting your temples:

The Underwet is not sick.

The Wet-Born are not suffering, not in any way that maps onto the word as surface languages use it. They are not trapped. The shaft is open. The walkways lead upward as easily as they lead down. No one guards the exit. No one has forbidden leaving. The ones who stay, stay because the staying is the thing itself—because the Underwet asked them a question with its warmth and its pressure and its slow dissolving architecture, and they found the question more interesting than any answer the surface had ever offered them.

The power knows this. It has seen a thousand variations of this arrangement across a thousand worlds: the place that becomes a body, the body that becomes a place, the threshold where organism and architecture lose the boundary between them and begin to breathe as one. It knows that most interventions into such systems are not rescue. They are interruption. A midwife does not deliver the baby faster because she is impatient. She delivers the baby at all only because something has gone wrong.

So the power listens, the way the Wet-Born listen—forehead pressed to the whole of the system, feeling for the thing that is wrong.

And it finds it.

Not in the machines. Not in the water. Not in the moths or the millipedes or the milk eels flashing their bioluminescent seams against the drowned legs of cracking towers.

It finds it in the **almost**.

---

The Underwet has been gestating for a very long time.

Long enough that the drill scars and the flowstone have begun to merge. Long enough that the orange coveralls have become skin and the skin has become something else. Long enough that the slow industrial liturgy has developed harmonics so complex that the Wet-Born themselves can no longer tell which notes are theirs and which belong to the machines. The process is beautiful. It is patient. It is utterly, profoundly, terminally *stable*.

And that is the problem.

The Underwet is not a ruin, no—the description was correct about that. It is gestation. But it is gestation that has become enamored of its own gestating. A pregnancy in love with the state of being pregnant. The warm water never cools and never heats. The machines never quite die and never quite wake. The Wet-Born never age and never arrive. The basin breathes in, and breathes out, and breathes in again, and each breath is identical to the last, and each breath says *soon, soon, almost, not yet.*

Four hundred bodies in different stages of becoming.

But none of them are *becoming*. Not anymore. They are suspended in the act of transformation the way an insect is suspended in amber—every leg still lifted, every wing still half-unfurled, every compound eye still bright, and all of it perfectly, permanently, almost.

The Wet-Born who press their foreheads to the pipes and listen are not hearing the Underwet decide what it wants to become. They are hearing it *avoid* deciding. The message that arrives as pressure against the eardrums, as taste at the back of the throat, as shiver through the warm water—that message, if you could translate it into the crude machinery of words, would say:

*Stay with me. Stay in this. Don't let it end. Don't let me have to be something specific. Let me be everything I could become, forever, without ever choosing.*

And the Wet-Born, who love the Underwet the way tissue loves the organ it belongs to, obey. They tend. They coax. They stroke the turbine casings and stir the plankton and sit in the fogged trams for decades, and all of it keeps the system alive without ever allowing it to be *born*.

It is the gentlest trap the power has ever seen.

---

So here is what the power does.

It does not drain the lake. It does not open the ceiling to the sky. It does not send a prophet or a plague or a revelation. It does not heal anything, because nothing is injured. It does not free anyone, because no one is bound.

It changes one thing.

One small, carefully chosen thing, the way a surgeon moves a single nerve and restores sensation to an entire limb.

---

Deep in the Underwet, in the lowest and warmest part of the basin, there is a pump that has been running since before the Wet-Born were born—since the original work-crews were still fully human, still wearing their coveralls loose, still sweating in the ordinary way. This pump is the heartbeat. It is the first sound the basin makes and the last sound it will ever make. It cycles every ten minutes, drawing warm mineral water from some deeper aquifer and pushing it through the system in a slow, massive pulse that every living thing in the Underwet has synchronized to.

The power reaches down—not physically, not even metaphysically, but in some way that the Wet-Born would understand better than any surface philosopher—and it touches the pump.

And it changes the interval.

Not by much. Not by a minute or even by a second. It changes the pump's cycle by a fraction so small that no instrument could measure it. A few milliseconds. The length of a moth's wingbeat. The time it takes a single bioluminescent cell in a milk eel's flank to fire and fade.

But it changes it in a *direction*.

The pump, which has been cycling at exactly the same interval for longer than any Wet-Born can remember, begins to cycle—so gradually that decades will pass before anyone consciously notices—*faster*.

---

Here is what this means.

It means that the Underwet's heartbeat, for the first time in its long humid history, is accelerating.

Not toward crisis. Not toward rupture. The power is wise enough and gentle enough to calibrate this with the same care the Wet-Born use when coaxing flowstone around a dangerous crack. The acceleration is glacial. It is geological. It operates on the same timescale as stalactite growth and industrial decay, which is to say: the Underwet's own timescale. The timescale it trusts.

But it is real, and it is irreversible, and it introduces into the system the one thing the system has been carefully, lovingly, desperately keeping out:

**Urgency.**

Not panic. Not fear. Urgency the way a ninth month of pregnancy is urgent—not because anything is wrong, but because the body knows, in its deepest and most inarticulate wisdom, that the time for *almost* is ending. That the contractions are not a malfunction. That the passage from gestation to birth is not a catastrophe but a *completion*, and that it requires the one thing the Underwet has never been willing to offer itself:

A direction. A choice. An end to the exquisite, intoxicating state of being everything-possible, and the beginning of being one real, specific, breathing thing.

---

The effects are not immediate. The power does not expect them to be. It is patient in the way that geology is patient, in the way the Underwet itself has been patient—but its patience has a *shape*, where the Underwet's patience had become shapeless.

What happens is this:

In the first years, nothing visible changes. The lake trembles at its slightly new frequency. The moths drift. The millipedes migrate. The Wet-Born press their foreheads to the pipes and hear what they have always heard—almost. Soon. Not yet.

But the *not yet* begins to taste different.

It is the difference between *not yet* spoken by someone who has no intention of ever acting, and *not yet* spoken by someone who can feel the moment approaching. The Wet-Born cannot articulate this difference. They feel it the way you feel the seasons changing before any thermometer confirms it—in the quality of the air, in the behavior of the eels, in the way their own slow dreams shift from static images to something with *sequence*. Things happening after other things. Cause and effect reasserting themselves in the underwater murals of the sleeping mind.

The millipedes notice first. They always do—they are the most sensitive barometers of the Underwet's mood, rearranging themselves each morning to spell out what the dreamers saw. And now the patterns they form on the walkways are different. They are no longer circular, no longer recursive, no longer the same closed loops they have been tracing for generations.

They are *linear*.

They point.

Not toward the shaft. Not toward the exit. They point deeper—toward the center of the basin, toward the place where the pump beats in its new, almost imperceptibly quicker rhythm. As if the millipedes are saying: *something is happening there. Something is about to happen there. Go and see.*

And the Wet-Born, who have not moved with any real direction in longer than they can remember, who have drifted and tended and stroked and listened in the same gentle, purposeless circuits for an age—

The Wet-Born begin to walk.

---

It takes decades more. The power does not watch with impatience. It watches the way a gardener watches a seed it planted in good soil: not anxiously, but with a quiet, certain attention, knowing that the seed contains its own instructions and needs only the single thing that was missing.

And the Underwet begins to change.

Not all at once. Not everywhere. But in the way that a body changes in the last weeks before birth—a settling, a shifting of weight, a rearrangement of internal pressures that signals the end of one state and the beginning of another.

The machines begin to run differently. Valves that have been sighing the same tired sighs for centuries begin to open wider, stay open longer. The warm water in the lake shifts by fractions of a degree—warmer in some places, cooler in others—developing *currents* for the first time, actual thermoclines, actual flow. The milk eels, which have drifted in lazy identical schools since anyone can remember, begin to polarize—some swimming toward the center, others toward the edges, as if the species itself is differentiating, choosing roles it never needed before.

The moths change too. Not in their bodies but in their behavior. They stop drifting in random spirals and begin to aggregate—enormous, silent constellations of pale wings gathering in the highest parts of the ceiling, so many of them that the limestone disappears beneath a living skin of bone-white and the faintest, faintest glow begins to emanate from above, as if the Underwet is developing its own sky.

And the Wet-Born—

The Wet-Born begin to differ from one another.

This is the most miraculous change, and the most frightening, and the power watches it with the most care.

For as long as the Underwet has been suspended in its eternal almost, the Wet-Born have been essentially interchangeable. Different stages of becoming, yes, but all becoming the *same* thing—or rather, all becoming nothing in particular, all held in the same warm solution of infinite potential. They have had no names. No specializations. No individual desires. They have been cells in an undifferentiated tissue, beautiful and identical and asleep.

Now they begin to *diverge*.

Some of them grow taller. Others grow wider. Some develop new patterns of bioluminescence—not the uniform pale green-and-violet of before, but specific, unique arrangements, like fingerprints, like faces. Some find that their chitin plates have hardened and thickened and become tools—actual tools, with edges and points, grown directly from their own hands. Others find that their skin has become thinner, more translucent, until the light passes through them and they become living lanterns, illuminating the passages they walk through in colors no one in the Underwet has seen before.

And some of them—the quiet ones, the ones who spent decades in the fogged trams—emerge for the last time and discover that they can do something none of the Wet-Born have ever done.

They can speak.

Not through the pipes. Not through pressure and taste and shiver. They open their mouths, and sound comes out, and the sound is *specific*. It means one thing and not another, and it can be argued with, and it can be wrong, and it is the most terrifying and beautiful noise the Underwet has ever heard.

The first word any of them speaks—the very first word, in a voice like wet limestone and old iron and the wingbeat of a moth—is not a name and not a command and not a prayer.

It is a question.

And the question, translated roughly from the raw new language of a people just being born, is:

*"What are we for?"*

---

The power hears this.

And now—only now, after all this time, after the decades and the patience and the single precise adjustment—the power smiles. If it can be said to smile. If the word *smile* can be stretched to accommodate the vast, quiet, radiant satisfaction of a wisdom that has watched a world stop holding its breath and finally, finally begin to exhale.

Because the question is not the answer.

The question is better than the answer.

The answer would be a cage—another kind of stasis, another *this is what you are, forever*. The power has not come to tell the Underwet what it is for. The power has come to make the Underwet capable of *asking*—and then of living inside the asking, which is not the same as living inside the almost. The almost is passive. The almost waits. The asking is *active*. The asking moves. The asking differentiates and specializes and argues and gets things wrong and tries again, and it does all of this while remaining, at its core, genuinely uncertain, genuinely open, genuinely unfinished.

The Underwet will not stop being a place of gestation. That is its nature, and the power has no desire to contradict a nature so profound and so beautiful. But it will become a gestation that *progresses*. A womb that actually delivers. A becoming that is willing to *become*, even knowing that every becoming is also a loss—a loss of all the other things it could have been.

The Wet-Born will build. They will disagree about what to build. They will develop factions and affections and regrets. Some of them will climb the shaft and see the surface for the first time and come back weeping with descriptions of a sky so vast and dry it feels like dying. Others will descend further, past the pump, into aquifers no one has mapped, and return with minerals that taste like the future and stories that contradict everything the elders—because there will be elders now, there will be *age* now—everything the elders believed.

Some of them will refuse the change. They will wade to the farthest, stillest corners of the basin and press their foreheads to the oldest pipes and try to hear the old song—the *almost, soon, not yet*—and they will grieve when they find that even the old song has a new note in it, a quickening they cannot unhear. And the power will grieve with them, gently, because it knows that what they are losing is real and was genuinely beautiful, and that the beauty of stasis is the one beauty that cannot survive the transition to life.

But they will survive. All of them. Even the ones who grieve. Because the power calibrated its single intervention with such immaculate care that the transition will take exactly as long as it needs to—not a moment faster than the slowest Wet-Born can bear, not a moment slower than the quickest Wet-Born demands. The pump will accelerate at the pace of trust. The Underwet will be born at the speed of the Underwet.

---

And in the end—far, far in the future, so far that the power itself will have turned its attention to other worlds and other gestations—the Underwet will become something.

Not something the power chose. Not something the Wet-Born predicted. Not something anyone, anywhere, standing in the warm trembling water with moths on their tongue and millipedes spelling prophecies at their feet, could have imagined.

Something *specific*.

Something with a name that is not a description of a process but a declaration of an identity. Something that looks back at its own long, humid, patient history and says: *that was where I came from, and this is what I am, and I am not finished but I am no longer afraid of finishing*.

What that something is—the power does not know.

That is the point.

That is the gift.

The gift is not an answer. The gift is not a direction. The gift is not even the acceleration of the pump, though that was the mechanism, the single nerve touched to restore sensation to the limb.

The gift is the end of *almost*.

The gift is the terrible, beautiful, irreversible permission to *become*.

---

And the pump beats on, just slightly faster than before, in the low warm dark, surrounded by eels and moths and the first stuttering words of a newborn people.

And the Underwet breathes in.

And for the first time in its long, long life—

It breathes out *differently than it breathed in*.
