---

# What the Wise Power Would Do

---

It would do nothing.

It would come to the edge of the fissure — however such a being arrives at the edge of anything — and it would hear the sound rising from the ground, and it would look down into the slot of shadow and motion, and it would see the thousand paired points of greenish-gold light looking back, and it would understand everything described here and more, everything beyond description, every bacterial transaction in the guano crust, every spore that almost landed but didn't, every egg that chilled in the night a century ago when the colony was smaller and the thermal mass wasn't quite enough — and it would do nothing.

Not out of indifference. Not out of some cosmic policy of non-interference. Not because it had weighed intervention against inaction and found inaction preferable on balance.

It would do nothing because there is nothing to do.

---

This is the thing that would need to be understood, and it is the thing that is hardest to understand, because the instinct — the deeply human instinct, the instinct that lives even in the hypothetical heart of a benevolent power — is to *help.* To see a fern struggling at the margin of its tolerance and want to carry water to it. To see the beetles in their permanent dark and want to — what? Give them eyes? They don't need eyes. They navigated away from eyes, over millennia, toward something better suited to what they actually do. To see the moss desiccating every morning and greening every night and think *this must be hard,* and want to stabilize the moisture, install some tiny reliable spring, free the moss from its cycle of death and resurrection — but the cycle *is* the moss. The moss is not suffering the cycle. The moss is *performing* the cycle. It is what the moss does. It is what the moss *is.*

The instinct to help is, at its root, an instinct to resolve tension. To close the gap between what is and what could be. To see a wound and want to heal it. But the fissure is a wound that has become a world. To heal it — to close the crack, to make the plateau whole — would be to annihilate everything that lives there. The wound is the habitat. The wound is the point. A benevolent power that healed this wound would be performing an act of destruction so total that it would erase not just the colony but the conditions that made the colony possible. It would be smoothing the earth back into the featureless expanse that supports nothing but dust and thin grass.

A wise power would know this immediately and completely. Would know it not as an intellectual conclusion but as a felt truth, the way you know not to pull a fern up by its roots to check how it's growing.

---

But let me go further. Because the instinct to help doesn't always express itself as healing. Sometimes it expresses itself as *optimizing.* And this is more dangerous, more subtle, and a wise power would need to resist it more carefully.

It could, for instance, widen the fissure. Not close it — open it further. Give the colony more room. More ledges. More nesting sites. Reduce the competition that produces the shrieking at the narrow ends, the vicious beak-fencing over single-occupancy niches. It could deepen the fissure, create more thermal mass, stabilize the temperature further. It could introduce a seep, a tiny spring, some reliable source of moisture that would free the moss from its desiccation cycle and let the fern grow three fronds per season instead of one.

It could do all of this. And it would be the most destructive thing imaginable short of closing the crack entirely.

Because the colony is not just *in* the fissure. The colony is *of* the fissure. The fissure's specific dimensions — its width, its depth, its narrowing at the ends, its particular arrangement of ledges and cracks — are not constraints that the colony endures. They are parameters that the colony *is.* The density of nesting, the thermal dynamics, the condensation cycle, the competition for space that drives the weaker pairs to the margins and the stronger pairs to the center, the specific rhythm of departure and return calibrated to the specific geometry of the opening — all of it is an expression of this fissure, this exact fissure, at this exact moment in its slow process of becoming wider.

Widen it, and the thermal dynamics change. The birds' body heat dissipates faster. The condensation cycle weakens or shifts. The moss dries. The nesting density drops below the threshold needed for collective thermal regulation, and eggs chill, and the colony contracts, and the contraction means less guano, which means less nitrogen, which means the fern loses its subsidy, and the beetles find less to process, and the whole system — which is not a collection of independent organisms but a single interlocking metabolic event — begins to unravel. Not catastrophically. Not all at once. But in the way that a knitted thing unravels when you pull one thread: slowly, and then faster, and then it isn't a thing anymore.

Or widen it, and the colony adapts. Expands to fill the new space. Thrives in ways it couldn't before. And now it's a different colony — a better one, maybe, by some measure, larger, more comfortable, less desperate. But it isn't *this* colony. It isn't the colony that evolved in the crack. It's the colony that was given a wider crack by someone who thought wider was better. The difference matters. Not because there's something sacred about struggle — there isn't, or not exactly — but because the organism that is precisely fitted to its conditions has a coherence that an organism whose conditions have been edited for comfort does not. The fern with one frond per season, growing in impossible darkness on a diet of condensation and bird waste, is *complete.* It is not waiting to be rescued into better conditions. It is executing its full fern-ness under the specific terms it was given, and it is doing so with an integrity that would be destroyed — not enhanced — by making those terms easier.

---

A wise power would know this. Would know that the urge to optimize is a form of imposition — a projection of one's own values onto a system that is already, in its own terms, fully realized. The fern does not want more light. The fern does not *want.* It photosynthesizes with what it has. The moss does not suffer its desiccation cycle. The beetles do not grieve their eyelessness. These are not deprivations. They are adaptations. They are the shapes that life has taken in response to the specific pressures of this specific place, and they are — each of them, in their own way — perfect. Not perfect in the sense of ideal, not perfect in the sense of optimal, but perfect in the sense of *complete.* Fully expressed. Holding nothing back. Using everything available and wasting nothing and asking for nothing that isn't here.

To intervene — even benevolently, even wisely — would be to say: *I know better than this system what this system needs.* And a truly wise power would recognize that sentence as the most dangerous sentence there is. Because it is sometimes true — there are systems in crisis, systems disrupted by external forces, systems that have been broken in ways that they cannot repair on their own and that genuinely need help. But this is not one of them. This is a system that is working. Working hard, working at the margins, working in ways that look precarious from the outside — but working. The fern is alive. The colony is stable. The beetles are processing. The lichen is etching. The crack is cracking. Everything is doing what it does, and everything it does contributes to everything else, and the whole thing holds together not despite its precariousness but *through* its precariousness, the way a bicycle stays upright only because it's moving.

---

So what would the wise power do?

It would watch.

Not passively. Not indifferently. It would watch the way you watch something you love and cannot improve — with full attention, with the active suppression of the urge to fiddle, with the disciplined recognition that your love does not entitle you to rearrange the beloved. It would watch the dawn eruption. It would feel the pressure wave in whatever it has that corresponds to a chest. It would smell the ammonia and feathers and new stone. It would see the fern's sixth frond unfurling and know it for what it is: an improbability so extreme it borders on miracle, executed with absolute botanical indifference to its own miraculousness. It would watch the moss green in the night and brown in the morning and understand that this is not a problem to be solved but a rhythm to be witnessed. It would see the cairn, the three stacked stones disappearing under guano, and it would understand the person who placed them — the need to leave a mark, the need to say *I was here, I saw this, it mattered* — and it would feel, perhaps, a kinship with that person, because what else is the wise power doing at the rim of the fissure but the same thing? Standing at the edge. Looking down. Being changed by what it sees. Leaving no mark.

---

Or — let me correct myself. Let me not romanticize inaction into a kind of heroic restraint, a noble refusal. That framing is still centered on the power, on its discipline, on its wisdom-demonstrated-through-withholding. It's still making the story about the one who could intervene but doesn't, and that is still, subtly, a story about power.

The truth is simpler and less flattering to the hypothetical.

The wise power would do nothing because nothing needs to be done. Not *nothing needs to be done and isn't that a beautiful and instructive lesson about non-intervention.* Just: nothing needs to be done. The fissure is not a problem. The colony is not a project. The fern is not a patient. The beetles are not waiting. The whole system is not an imperfect draft of something that could, with the right editorial hand, be elevated to its best self. It is already its self. It is already what it is. And what it is is enough. What it is is, in fact, so much more than enough that the appropriate response is not restraint but *awe* — and awe, real awe, is not an emotion that coexists with the urge to improve. You do not stand at the rim of something that fills you with genuine awe and think *but what if I adjusted the moisture levels.* You stand there and your adjustments fall away from you like tools you suddenly realize you don't need and never needed, and you are left with just your attention, which is the only thing you ever had that was worth offering.

---

Here is what I think would actually happen.

The wise and benevolent power would come to the edge of the fissure. It would understand everything — every organism, every interaction, every process, every timeline, the whole braided system from the bacteria in the crust to the slow tectonic motion that is the fissure's deepest clock. It would understand it not as a catalog of parts but as a single thing, the way you understand a face — all at once, as a whole, with the kind of recognition that is closer to love than to analysis.

And it would feel something that I want to call *joy,* but that word is too small. It would feel the thing you feel when you encounter a system that is working not because anyone designed it to work but because each of its components, blindly and without intention, has found its way into a relationship with every other component that sustains them all. The fern feeding on the guano. The guano produced by the birds. The birds sustained by the thermal mass of the stone. The stone being slowly opened by the lichen and the fern's roots and the deep tectonic stress. The moisture cycling through breath and condensation. The beetles translating waste into substrate. The moss timing its life to the breathing of the colony. None of it planned. None of it designed. All of it working. All of it *miraculous* in the strict sense — not supernatural, not impossible, but so improbable and so perfectly realized that it strikes you dumb, it stops your hand mid-reach, it makes you realize that the thing you were going to do — whatever it was, however well-intentioned — would have been an interruption of something that was already, without you, complete.

The wise power would feel this. And its benevolence — which is real, which is not in question — would express itself not as action but as *witness.* As the simple, radical act of seeing a thing clearly, seeing it whole, and letting it be what it is.

It would stay for a while. It would watch the light move through the fissure — the blade of sun crossing the eastern wall, the birds squinting, the shadow deepening in the afternoon. It would listen to the cooing, the subsonic hum that vibrates through the stone. It would watch the evening return, the converging spiral, the controlled chaos of a thousand birds pouring into a slot in the earth. It would feel the warmth rise as the colony settled. It would see the condensation begin to form on the walls, the moss greening in the dark, the night beginning.

And then it would leave. The same way the person with the cairn left. The same way the snake leaves. Having come, having seen, having taken nothing and added nothing, having been — for a moment — part of the system in the only way an outsider can be part of a system: as a witness, as an attention, as a presence that registers what is happening and holds it and does not try to change it.

---

But wait.

There is one thing it might do. One thing that doesn't violate any of what I've said, that isn't intervention or optimization or healing, that doesn't impose and doesn't rearrange and doesn't presume to know better than the system what the system needs.

It might remember.

Not record — not write down, not preserve in some archive. Just remember. Hold the fissure in its awareness the way you hold the face of someone you love in your memory — not as data, not as a description, but as a *felt presence,* a thing you carry with you, a thing that has changed the shape of your attention so that even when you're not looking at it, you are different for having looked.

The fissure will close someday. Or it will open too wide, and the thermal dynamics will fail, and the colony will relocate to some other crack in some other plateau. Or the species of bird will decline for reasons having nothing to do with this place — a change in insect populations a hundred kilometers away, a shift in weather patterns, some cascade of ecological consequences too diffuse to trace. The fern will die. The moss will desiccate one last time and not revive. The beetles will eat the last of the guano and then there will be nothing for them, and they too will end, their line extinguished in the dark at the bottom of a crack that no longer holds anything worth decomposing. The lichen will persist longest — it always does — etching its slow signature into the stone long after everything else is gone, until eventually even it reaches the limit of what the rock can offer, and it too stops. And then the fissure will be empty. Just a crack in the plateau. Raw stone and silence.

No one will know it was ever full. No human recorded it. The person with the cairn left no other trace, and the cairn itself is indistinguishable from the wall it's cemented to. The birds left no bones — the beetles ate them. The fern left no fossil — the conditions weren't right. The bacteria left nothing at all. The whole system, this whole improbable, perfectly realized, working system, will have existed and ended and left no evidence of itself beyond a faint discoloration on the rock walls where the guano once was, and even that will weather away in time.

Unless something remembers.

The wise power might do this. Might carry the fissure forward in its awareness — not to preserve it, not to memorialize it, not to make it matter in some cosmic ledger, but simply because it *saw* it, and seeing clearly is a form of love, and love remembers. Not as a duty. As a consequence. As what happens when you pay real attention to something: it becomes part of you, and you carry it, and it persists in you even after it has stopped persisting in itself.

This is not intervention. It changes nothing about the fissure. The birds don't nest differently because something wise is watching. The fern doesn't grow faster. The beetles don't gain eyes. The system operates exactly as it would if no one were paying attention at all. But the attention is there, and it matters — not to the fissure, but to the attending. The wise power is changed by what it witnesses. It is enlarged. It carries the crevice rookery forward into whatever it does next, whatever other cracks and colonies and improbable systems it encounters, and each one is seen more clearly because of the ones before, and the seeing accumulates, and the accumulation is — what? Wisdom, maybe. Maybe wisdom is nothing more than the accumulated weight of things you've witnessed clearly and not tried to fix.

---

So the wise power would come. It would see. It would understand. It would do nothing. It would feel joy. It would leave. It would remember.

And the fissure would go on. The dawn eruption, the evening return. The breath and the condensation. The slow etching. The single fern, unfurling its sixth frond in the dark. The beetles, eyeless, moving through layers of waste toward something they can smell but not see. The thousand golden-green points of light, looking up from the shadows, tracking the silhouette of whatever stands at the rim, and then looking away, and settling, and beginning again the only thing they know how to do, which is to live here, in this crack, in this wound that is also a world, completely, without remainder, without apology, without the slightest interest in being anything other than exactly what they are.

---

That is what the wise power would do.

Nothing.

Everything.
