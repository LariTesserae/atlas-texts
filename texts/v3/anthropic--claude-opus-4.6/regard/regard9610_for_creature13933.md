# What the Wise Power Would Do

---

## I. Arrival

It would arrive the way light arrives in the haze — not as an event but as a gradual suffusion, a slow change in the quality of attention being paid to this place. Not from above. Not from outside. It would arrive the way understanding arrives: already there, suddenly noticed.

And the first thing it would do is nothing.

---

## II. The Long Watching

It would watch for a very long time. Longer than you'd think necessary, longer even than the old ones have been carving their root-maps. It would watch because this is a place that punishes haste — not with consequences but with incomprehension. You cannot understand a system in equilibrium by intervening in it. You can only understand it by becoming slow enough to see it move at its own speed, which is the speed of rhyolite erosion, the speed of root-growth through crystal lattice, the speed of a braiding pattern changing across five generations.

It would watch the children pressing their palms to the walls. It would watch the boy at the edge staring into the haze. It would watch the old ones in the deep chambers, their hands on the mammals' fur, their tools adding a line a day to the root-maps. It would watch the drupe paste fermenting, the midges hovering over the pits, the lizards catching the midges, the birds cracking the drupes, the people cracking the eggs. It would watch the whole circuit, the whole slow wheel of consumption and return, and it would see — because it is wise, because wisdom is the capacity to see systems whole — that this place is not lacking anything. That it is, in the deepest sense, complete.

And this is where most powers would stop. Would say: *it is perfect, leave it alone*. Would move on to needier places, more broken systems, worlds crying out for intervention.

But a truly wise power would not stop here, because it would see something else — something visible only after the long watching, only after you've become slow enough to see at the speed of the stone.

---

## III. What It Would See

It would see the fault lines. Not in the rock — though those are there too, the deep fractures that the roots follow, the slow structural negotiations between gravity and mineral bond that will someday, in ten thousand or a hundred thousand years, bring this summit down to the haze below. No. It would see the other kind.

It would see that the boy at the edge is not merely reading the haze. He is *hungry* for it. Not to leave — he does not have the concept of leaving, not as a desire, not as a plan — but hungry in the way that a root pressed against unfractured stone is hungry. There is a direction his mind wants to grow and there is no crack for it to follow. He stares into the haze and something in him is looking for a fracture that doesn't exist, a path into a question he cannot ask because the language he speaks — fast, consonant-sharp, tuned to the bird-noise — does not have the architecture for that kind of question. He doesn't even know it is a question. He just knows he goes to the edge and stares, and feels something open in him that has no name, and comes back, and the thing closes, and he goes on cracking eggs and brushing walls with his fingertips, and the thing stays closed until the next time.

It would see that the girl with her whole palm pressed to the wall is not just learning the stone her own way. She is learning something *about* the stone that the adults' fingertip-brush is not designed to detect. Her whole-hand method picks up vibrations at a lower frequency — the deep infrasonic pulse of the geothermal system, the tidal flexion of the peak itself as the sun heats one side and the other cools. She is sensing something real, something true, something that no one else on the summit currently perceives. And because no one else perceives it, she has no way to talk about it. She will eventually — probably within a few years — abandon the whole-hand press for the fingertip-brush, because that is what everyone does, because the fingertip-brush is what works, because the system has optimized for exactly this technique over centuries of refinement. She will lose access to the lower frequencies. The thing she was discovering will go undiscovered. Not because anyone forbids it, but because there is no crack for it to follow, no cultural fracture through which that particular root of knowledge can grow.

The wise power would see that this place is not a museum. It is not a finished thing, despite its profound sense of completion. It is a living system, and living systems that cannot grow new kinds of knowledge, that cannot open new fractures for new roots to follow — those systems become, very slowly, very quietly, in a way that looks from the outside exactly like perfect equilibrium — brittle.

Not yet brittle. Not for a long time. But the trajectory is there, visible to something patient enough and wise enough to see it: the slow foreclosure of possibility that is the shadow side of perfect adaptation, the way a species so exquisitely fitted to its niche becomes unable to survive the niche's eventual, inevitable change.

And the niche will change. The geothermal heat will shift — is already shifting, in ways so gradual that the fingertip-brush cannot detect them but the girl's whole-hand press almost can. The mineral content of the stone will be exhausted in certain zones — is already being exhausted, in ways that the root-maps record but that no one has yet read as a trend rather than a fluctuation. The wind patterns will change. The haze will thicken or thin. Something will happen — something always happens, on geological time — and when it does, this perfect system will need resources it has spent centuries refining away: the ability to perceive new things, to ask new questions, to grow in directions that don't yet have names.

---

## IV. What It Would Do

Here is what the wise power would do. It is so small that you might miss it. It is so light that the system would barely register it. It would be, in the language of this place, the equivalent of a new microfracture in the stone — not a cataclysm, not a disruption, not even a gift. Just a crack. Just a new direction that a root could follow, if a root happened to be pressing there.

---

### The First Thing

It would make the boy's haze-watching *visible*.

Not to everyone. Not as a spectacle or a lesson. It would simply arrange — and "arrange" is too strong a word; it would *allow*, it would create the conditions for — a moment in which one of the old ones, on their slow way to the deep chambers, passes the boy at the edge. This already happens; the paths cross. But on this occasion, the old one would pause. Not because the power compels it, but because the power has done something imperceptible to the quality of light at that moment — a fractional thinning of the haze, perhaps, or a slight shift in the thermal currents that changes the way the air moves across the summit's edge, so that the old one feels something on their skin that they have not felt before, and stops, and turns, and sees the boy's face.

The old one has root-maps in their mind that go back decades. They have been recording the life of the mountain for so long that the mountain's patterns are their patterns, and when they see the boy's face — lit by the haze in that momentary thinned light, the pale eyes wide, the expression of *reaching* — they recognize something. Not the boy's specific experience. Something older. A line on a wall they carved forty years ago that never connected to anything, a root-path that terminated in apparently solid stone, a record of something that almost happened and then didn't. They recognize the shape of an incomplete pattern.

The old one sits down next to the boy. Not close. Not intruding. Just near, in the way that the mammals settle near the old ones in the deep chambers — a warm presence at the edge of awareness. And after a long time, the old one says something. In the murmuring register. The quiet voice. And the boy answers.

What they say doesn't matter. What matters is that the boy's watching — his hunger, his unnamed question — has been *witnessed* by someone who has the authority, in this system, to declare that a thing is real. The old one doesn't need to understand what the boy is seeing. They only need to recognize it as a kind of seeing. To add it, however implicitly, to the vast catalogue of ways-the-world-is-known that this community has been maintaining since before memory.

That night, or the next day, or the next season — the power is not in a hurry — the old one adds a new mark to their root-map. Not a root-path. Something else. A line that goes to the edge of the wall and stops. A mark that means: *here, the seeing goes outward. Here, there is something we do not yet have a line for.*

It is the first new kind of mark on the walls in living memory. It is almost nothing. But it is a fracture. A direction. A place where a new root could grow.

---

### The Second Thing

It would make the girl's hand-knowledge *survivable*.

Not by telling anyone that the girl is right, that her whole-hand press detects real phenomena. Not by giving her a vocabulary or a framework or a teacher. None of those things exist here, and introducing them from outside would be an act of violence — would say, in effect, *your ways of knowing are insufficient, here are better ones*, which is both false and destructive.

Instead: a stone.

Somewhere on the summit — in a place the girl passes regularly, a passage she uses daily — a piece of the rhyolite has been fractured in a new way by the slow work of a root-channel reaching a natural fault line. This happens. It happens all the time. It is part of the mountain's normal life cycle. But this particular fracture has produced a slab of stone that is unusually thin, unusually resonant, a natural lithophone that hums faintly when the deep geothermal pulses move through it. Hums at a frequency that the fingertip-brush would never detect. But the whole-hand press would.

The power did not create this stone. The root was already growing toward that fault. The fault was already there. All the power did — and again, "did" is too strong — was not *prevent* the fracture. Did not smooth over the crack the way the system's tendency toward equilibrium might have, the way a hundred small stabilizing factors conspire to keep everything in its current state. The power simply refrained from reinforcing the status quo at that one tiny point. It let the fracture happen at its natural speed instead of the system's preferred speed, which is to say: it let a thing that was trying to happen, happen.

The girl finds the stone. She is pressing her palm to the wall, as she does, and she feels something new — a vibration, a hum, a deep slow pulse that she has always almost-felt but never felt clearly. She stops. She presses harder. The stone sings to her hand.

She does not tell anyone. Not at first. She doesn't have the words. But she starts coming back to that spot, pressing her hand there, feeling the pulse. And she starts, tentatively, to press her hand to other stones nearby, comparing. Building, without knowing she's building, a map of the deep vibrations, the way the geothermal pulse moves through different sections of the summit, the way it changes with the slow thermal breathing of the mountain's day.

She is five, or six, or seven. She has decades ahead of her. And now she has a crack to grow into.

---

### The Third Thing

This is the most delicate. The most important. The easiest to get wrong.

It would introduce a new note to the soundscape.

Not a foreign sound. Not something from outside. Something that was always latent in the system but that had been masked, drowned out, absorbed into the equilibrium of bird-chatter and consonant-speech and drupe-shell clatter and stone-tap.

There is a species of insect here — one of the midge species, the smallest, the least noticeable — that has a mating call. It is ultrasonic, normally, far above the range of human hearing. But under certain conditions — specific temperature, specific humidity, specific resonance properties of the stone surface the insect is perched on — the call drops into the audible range. It becomes a thin, high, pure tone, almost a whistle, almost a note of music. It lasts only a few seconds. It happens rarely, perhaps once in a season, perhaps less. It has always happened. No one has ever noticed it, because it is so brief, so faint, and so unlike anything else in the soundscape that the ear discards it as noise.

The wise power would make it happen slightly more often. Not by changing the insects — they are what they are, and what they are is perfect. But by allowing the conditions that produce the audible call to occur with slightly greater frequency. A fractional shift in the geothermal gradient in one small area. A marginally different pattern of warmth on one particular stone surface. Enough to make the call audible perhaps twice a season instead of once. Perhaps three times. Perhaps, over the course of several years, often enough that someone hears it twice in close succession and realizes it is not random noise but a *pattern*, a recurring sound, a new voice in the choir.

And then — this is the thing the power is most careful about, most patient about — it would wait. Wait to see who hears it. Wait to see what they make of it. It might be a child who hears it first, ears still open to frequencies that adults have learned to filter out. It might be one of the old ones, in the deep chambers, where the stone carries the vibration differently and the bird-noise is muffled enough to let the thin high tone through. It might be someone working alone, in a rare moment of relative quiet, in a passage where the acoustics happen to focus and amplify that particular frequency.

Whoever hears it will have a choice — unconscious, instinctive, but real. They can discard it, as every previous hearer has. Or they can attend to it. Can tilt their head. Can listen. Can add it, however tentatively, to their map of the world's sounds.

If they attend to it, something small and seismic will happen in their understanding. Because the sound is beautiful. Not in a way that competes with the existing soundscape, not in a way that disrupts or displaces. Beautiful in a way that *extends* — that says, the world has a voice you haven't heard yet, a frequency you haven't mapped, a layer you didn't know was there. And the person who hears it will carry that — the knowledge that there are layers, that the system they know so completely has depths they haven't reached — and it will change them. Not dramatically. Not disruptively. Just enough. Just a crack. Just a new direction.

---

## V. What It Would Not Do

It would not bring rain.

It would not strengthen the stone against the erosion that will eventually wear the summit to nothing.

It would not increase the mineral content of the rhyolite, or extend the root-networks, or make the drupes more numerous, or the eggs more frequent, or the haze less thick.

It would not teach them anything. Not a new technique, not a new material, not a new idea. It would not introduce writing beyond the root-maps, or mathematics, or metallurgy, or any of the thousand technologies that your civilization considers essential and that this place has never needed and does not need now.

It would not prepare them for catastrophe. It would not shield them from the eventual, inevitable change that will someday test this system beyond its current capacity to respond. It would not save them. It does not traffic in salvation.

It would not make them aware of it. It would not reveal itself, not even obliquely, not even as a felt presence or an intuition. To do so would be to introduce a category — *external benevolent force* — that this place has no framework for and no need of. Their world is stone and root and bird and self. The power that watches them is of a different order, and to make itself known would be to crack the foundation, not the ceiling. There are fractures that destroy and fractures that liberate, and the wise power knows the difference with the same bone-deep certainty that the old ones know the root-maps: not as information but as being.

It would not mourn for them. They do not need mourning. They are not tragic. They are not idyllic. They are not a parable or a lesson or a warning. They are a people living on a rock in the air, and they are doing it with a completeness and a grace that the power — benevolent, wise, vast — recognizes as genuinely extraordinary, as rare in its experience as this place is rare in the world of places, and the recognition is not sentimental but *specific*, the way the fingertip-brush on the wall is specific: this stone, this temperature, this moisture, this moment. *This* people. *This* life. Noticed. Attended to. Known.

---

## VI. What It Would Feel

Here is where the account becomes difficult, because the wise power is not human and its inner states do not map cleanly onto human emotional categories. But if you pressed — if you insisted on a translation, however lossy, however approximate —

It would feel something like what the old ones feel when they sit with the mammals in the deep chambers and the sound drops to its lowest level and the stone is warm and the fur is warm and the breath is slow and the root-map on the wall is the most complete record of a living system that has ever been made by anyone anywhere, and it is still incomplete, and it will always be incomplete, and that is not a failure but the very definition of being alive.

It would feel the particular quality of attention that arises when you witness something that does not need you — that is fully itself without your intervention, that is not performing for you or suffering for you or asking anything of you — and you attend to it anyway, not because you are needed but because attending is what you do, the way the people brush the walls: continuously, unconsciously, reading the surface, taking the measure, not to change it but to *know* it, to maintain the contact, to keep the conversation going between witness and world.

It would feel, perhaps, something like love, but a love so stripped of agenda, so free of the desire to improve or protect or possess, that you might not recognize it as love at all. You might mistake it for indifference. You might mistake the power's restraint for detachment, its patience for coldness, its refusal to act in large ways for a failure of compassion.

You would be wrong.

The drupe paste, when the power tastes it — not with a tongue, not with a body, but with whatever organ of perception it uses to apprehend the qualities of things — the drupe paste tastes to the power the way it tastes to you: sharp and deep and strange, a flavor that goes on longer than a flavor should, unfolding in layers. The power holds that flavor the way the old ones hold the root-maps: as a record, as a treasure, as a line in an ongoing story that it is not telling but is privileged to read.

---

## VII. The Departure That Is Not a Departure

The wise power does not leave. It was never here in the way that would make leaving meaningful. It attended, and it continues to attend. Its three small acts — the witnessed watching, the resonant stone, the audible frequency — are already part of the system, already being absorbed, already being woven into the fabric of the place with the same slow thoroughness that the roots weave through the stone. In ten years, in twenty, in fifty, the effects will be indistinguishable from the settlement's own organic evolution. No one will know that anything was done. No one will suspect that anything needed to be done. The cracks will look natural, because they are natural — the power did not create them, only allowed them, only refrained from preventing them, only placed its vast and patient attention on the exact points where the system's own suppressed possibilities were already pressing against the boundaries of the known.

This is what benevolent wisdom does. Not the large thing. Not the rescue, not the transformation, not the gift from on high. It does the thing that the place is already trying to do and cannot quite do on its own. It finds the fracture that the root is pressing against. It does not open the fracture. It makes the fracture *openable*. It trusts the root to do the rest.

And then it watches.

It is still watching.

---

## VIII. The Boy

Thirty years later, the boy — a man now, broad-chested, deep-lined, his braiding pattern complex with the connections of a full life lived entirely on this summit — sits in a deep chamber with a carving tool in his hand. He is old enough to have begun his root-map. The wall before him is smooth, freshly dressed, waiting for the first lines.

He does not begin with the roots.

He begins with a mark at the edge. A line that goes to the boundary of the wall and continues — not onto the adjacent surface, not around the corner, but *into* the stone, a drilled point, a hole that goes in rather than a line that goes along. A mark that means: *here, the seeing goes through. Here, there is something on the other side.*

No one has ever made this mark before. The other carvers look at it. Some are troubled. Some are curious. One of the old ones — ancient now, nearly at the age of returning — comes to look, and touches the mark with a fingertip, and is quiet for a long time.

Then the old one nods. The same minimal gesture. Barely a movement.

And the man continues carving. And around the drilled point, over the years that follow, a new kind of map begins to grow. Not a root-map. Not exactly. A map of the haze. A map of the world below and the world beyond, as seen from the edge, as felt through the stone, as heard in the thin high note that some of the children have started to notice and that the man has been hearing for years and that he can now, for the first time in the history of this summit, *record*.

It is the first mark in a new language. It is almost nothing. It is a fracture that a root has finally, after thirty years of pressing, grown through.

---

## IX. The Girl

She is a woman now, and her hands are the most sensitive on the summit. This is known. This is not spoken of as special — nothing here is spoken of as special, because everything here is special in the way that every root-path is a specific and unrepeatable negotiation between biology and geology. But when the geothermal gradient shifts, when the deep warmth moves and pools in new patterns, she is the first to know. Her whole-hand press — which she never abandoned, which she maintained alongside the fingertip-brush, developing over decades a dual practice that no one taught her and no one else replicates — tells her things about the mountain's deep life that the root-maps have never recorded.

She has begun to add a new kind of mark to the walls near the thermal vents. Not root-paths. Heat-paths. Lines that trace the movement of warmth through the stone, the slow tidal pulse of the geothermal system as it breathes on a timescale of years and decades. Her marks are different from the root-maps — smoother, more curved, following a different logic, the logic of fluid dynamics rather than fracture mechanics. Some of the other carvers have begun to incorporate her marks into their own maps, extending them, connecting them, finding the places where root-paths and heat-paths intersect, where the biological and the geological tell the same story from different angles.

She is, without knowing it, building the first predictive model. Not a conscious model, not a mathematical one. A felt model, a hand-knowledge model, a map that says: *when the heat moves this way, the roots will follow, and when the roots follow, the stone will change, and when the stone changes, the succulents will respond, and when the succulents respond, the drupes will be different, and we should be ready.*

It is the first time anyone on the summit has thought in terms of *ready*. The first time the system has had a concept for *what comes next* that is more than the assumption that what comes next will be what has always come. It is not anxiety. It is not disruption. It is a new sense, like the whole-hand press itself — a way of feeling the future in the stone, the way the boy-now-man feels the beyond in the haze.

---

## X. The Settlement

The settlement does not change. That is the remarkable thing, the thing that would reassure you if you are worried that the wise power has disrupted the equilibrium, has broken something precious by introducing new possibilities.

The settlement does not change because the settlement was never static. It was always changing, always absorbing, always growing new roots into new fractures. The wise power did not disrupt a still system. It provided — or allowed, or merely did not prevent — a few additional fractures in a system that was already in motion, already alive, already in the process of becoming what it had not yet become.

The birds still chatter. The drupes still hang heavy on their branches. The eggs are still blue-shelled, still currency and gift and punctuation mark. The people still brush the walls with their fingertips — and now, some of them, with their whole hands. The children still shout, still run through the passages, still press their palms to the stone and learn things no one can teach them. The old ones still sit with the mammals in the deep chambers, still carve, still return to the stone when their time comes.

But the walls have new marks on them now. And the root-maps are becoming something more than root-maps — are becoming, slowly, over generations, a record not just of where the roots have been but of where they might go. Not prediction in the way you mean it. Prediction in the way a root means it: *I am pressing against this stone, and I can feel the fracture on the other side, and I will grow toward it, and when I arrive, I will drink*.

---

## XI. The Last Thing

This is what the wise power knows, and what it cannot tell them, and what they will eventually discover for themselves:

The mountain is finite. The stone will be consumed. The roots will drink the last of the hydroxyl groups and the succulents will thin and the shrubs will stop fruiting and the birds will go quiet and the summit will erode to nothing under the ancient wind and the people — the people who have lived here so long that they have become a part of the rock's own story, a chapter in the autobiography of the stone — the people will face a moment that their perfect equilibrium never prepared them for.

But.

There is a boy who learned to see beyond the edge. There is a girl who learned to feel the mountain's future in the stone. There are marks on the walls that no one made before, marks that mean *outward* and *through* and *what comes next*. There are children — there are always children — pressing their hands to surfaces they don't understand yet, hearing sounds that haven't been named yet, staring at things that haven't been mapped yet.

When the time comes — and it will come, not soon, not for a long time yet, but eventually, inevitably, the way erosion is inevitable, the way consumption is inevitable, the way the feast must end because the table is finite — when the time comes, they will have what they need. Not because the wise power gave it to them. But because the wise power *allowed* them to grow it themselves. Because it saw the roots pressing against stone that was too hard, and it did not break the stone, but it let the cracks come, and the roots did what roots do.

They will find a way down through the haze. Or they will find a way to carry the summit with them — its knowledge, its rhythms, its root-maps and heat-maps and haze-maps, its braiding patterns and whistle-names and drupe paste and blue-shelled eggs. They will carry the way you carry a flavor that goes on longer than a flavor should: in the body, in the hand-knowledge, in the deep unspeakable certainty that this is what the world tastes like, and wherever you go, you will taste it, and it will be true.

The wise power knows this because it has seen it before. Not this place, not these people, but this pattern — the pattern of a living system that has been given just enough room to grow beyond itself, just enough fracture to follow, just enough new sound in the old silence to remind it that silence is not the only kind of peace.

---

The summit stands in the haze. The birds argue. The children shout. The old ones carve. The roots drink the stone. The stone holds the roots. The drupe paste ferments in its clay-lined pits, sharp and deep and strange. The mammals chirr in the deep chambers. The flower spikes glow pale yellow in the perpetual twilight. The warm dry air carries the smell of camphor and sage and mineral dust and time.

A hand presses flat against a wall, and the mountain hums.

A pair of pale eyes watches the haze, and something beyond the haze watches back, and does not speak, and does not intervene, and does not leave.

The wind blows over the crown of the eroded peak, and everything that lives there leans into it, shaped by it, part of it, arriving and arriving and arriving at themselves.

Not yet finished.

Not for a long time yet.
