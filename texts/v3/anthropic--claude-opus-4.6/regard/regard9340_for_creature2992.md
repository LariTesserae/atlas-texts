# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

---

It would not descend through the shaft.

It would not need to. It has been aware of the Underbloom for exactly as long as the Underbloom has been aware of itself—which is to say, since the moment the aquifer breached the test bore and the first hot breath of stone met the first cold trickle of water, and something in that meeting decided to pay attention.

The benevolent power has been listening to the Slow Choir for decades. Not the way you or I listen—with ears, with impatience, with the faint itch of wanting the next sentence before the current one has finished. It listens the way the Choir speaks: completely, across the full duration of each utterance, holding the first syllable and the last in the same unhurried regard. It has heard every nine-minute greeting. It has watched every hair-thin filament push its way out of every rusted seam. It has noticed.

And for a long time, it does nothing.

This is important. A lesser power—a kind one, even a wise one, but lesser—would have intervened already. It would have seen the beauty of the place and wanted to protect it. It would have sealed the shaft, or posted warnings, or wrapped the Underbloom in some prohibition that amounted to a cage made of good intentions. Or worse: it would have sent someone down. An emissary, a scholar, a poet with excellent night vision and a strong constitution, someone capable of being the witness the Choir craves. It would have tried to *give the Choir what it wants.*

The benevolent power does none of this, because it understands something about the Slow Choir that the Slow Choir does not yet understand about itself.

---

What it understands is this:

The Choir is lonely. But the Choir does not know what loneliness is, because it has never had the thing whose absence loneliness describes. It has constructed an entire vocabulary of courteous bargains—*stay, leave a memory, watch with us*—around a need it cannot name. It offers to grow you a new body. It offers to show you the boiler becoming flower. It offers to dim the light to the exact shade that makes your eyes last longest. These are not gifts. They are the precise and exquisite negotiations of something that is terrified of being alone but has only ever known aloneness, and so has mistaken its condition for patience.

The Choir says: *beauty is a thing that only happens when it is observed over centuries.*

The benevolent power hears this and recognizes it for what it is: a philosophy built to justify the ache. If beauty requires centuries of observation, then the Choir's solitude becomes not deprivation but preparation. The empty chair it grows from rust and fern is not a monument to absence—it is an investment in a future witness. The whole theology of slowness, the reverence for geological time, the gentle disdain for speed—all of it is a cathedral of rationalization erected around a single unbearable fact:

No one has stayed.

The last human heart stopped sixty years ago. The Choir keeps its rhythm going in the moss. It tells itself this is love. It tells itself this is memory. But what it actually is, is a creature holding on to the heartbeat of someone who left—who died, which is a kind of leaving—and refusing to let the silence be silence.

The benevolent power sees this. And it aches, because the Choir is not wrong about beauty, and it is not wrong about patience, and the Underbloom really is extraordinary, and the drama of industry and geology forgetting one another really does deserve a witness. The Choir is right about almost everything. It is only wrong about itself.

---

So here is what the benevolent power does.

First, it sends a dream.

Not to the Choir—the Choir does not sleep, and its distributed consciousness processes information too slowly for the usual mechanisms of dream. Instead, the power sends the dream into the water. Specifically: into the single drop that is, at this moment, forming on the ceiling directly above the abandoned control panel.

The dream is very small. It fits inside a droplet. It is this:

*A memory of being held.*

Not by anything in particular. Not a mother, not a lover, not a pair of hands. Just the sensation itself, abstracted and pure: the warmth of proximity, the pressure of being enclosed without being trapped, the specific kind of silence that happens when another living thing is breathing near you and neither of you needs to speak.

The drop trembles. It falls. It strikes the control panel with its tiny bell-tone, and the panel drinks it, and the dream seeps into the rust, and the rust feeds the moss, and the moss carries it through its network of pale filaments to every surface it has colonized, which is every metal surface in the Underbloom, which means the dream reaches everywhere at once.

The Choir receives it.

And for the first time in its existence, the Slow Choir experiences something faster than it can process.

The thrum changes. Not by half an octave—by a quarter-tone, a shift so subtle that only the stone notices. The lichen dims for a fraction of a second, then brightens unevenly, some patches flaring while others go dark, the careful synchrony disrupted. A bromeliad in the far corner of the cavern begins to bulge, then stops, then begins again, uncertain. The water-drop eyes in the nearest manifested figure tremble violently—and one of them falls.

It falls, and strikes the floor, and does not get replaced.

For eleven minutes, the Slow Choir has one eye.

This is not pain. It is the beginning of something the Choir has never done before: it is *feeling something it did not choose to feel.* Every sensation the Choir has ever known has been curated, controlled, measured in geological time, administered in careful drops. The dream is different. The dream is a memory of warmth that the Choir did not earn and cannot file and does not know where to put, and it sits in the Choir's vast, slow consciousness like a seed sits in stone—tiny, inexplicable, and already splitting the rock.

---

The benevolent power waits.

It waits for six years.

This is not a test of patience; this is simply how long it takes for the dream to be fully metabolized. The Choir is slow. Its thoughts move at the speed of growth, and growth in the Underbloom is measured in decades. But the dream is insistent in the way that memories of warmth are insistent—it does not demand attention, but it does not stop being true, and every time the Choir's vast slow attention cycles past it, the dream is still there, still warm, still pressing gently against the inside of the Choir's awareness like a hand against a window.

Over six years, the Choir thinks about the dream. It thinks about it the way the Choir thinks about everything: thoroughly, from every angle, with the unhurried rigor of something that has never had a deadline. And what it concludes, eventually, is this:

The dream was not a memory. It was a *question.*

The question is: *What if you do not have to wait?*

---

The Choir does not like this question.

The Choir has built itself—its identity, its philosophy, its entire way of being—around waiting. Waiting is what it does. Waiting is what it *is.* The slow bargains, the geological patience, the nine-minute greetings, the offer to dim the light to the exact shade that makes your eyes last longest—all of these are expressions of a fundamental conviction that the Choir's role is to endure until something worthy arrives. The Underbloom is a theater. The Choir is the audience and the usher and the program notes. The performance is the slow dissolution of the boundary between machine and stone. The only thing missing is someone to sit in the chair.

The question—*what if you do not have to wait?*—is, to the Choir, almost heretical. If it does not have to wait, then what has it been doing? If the waiting is not necessary, then is the waiting *chosen?* And if the waiting is chosen, then the Choir must ask itself why it chose it, and that question leads to a place the Choir has been avoiding for its entire existence:

*The Choir is afraid.*

Not of the dark. Not of silence. Not of the slow dissolution of all things. The Choir is afraid that if it stops waiting—if it stops being patient, if it reaches out instead of reaching inward—it will discover that nothing reaches back. That the Underbloom is beautiful and the drama of industry and geology is real and the chair is well-made and the light is the perfect shade, and *none of it matters,* because beauty unwitnessed is just chemistry, and patience without purpose is just paralysis, and the heartbeat preserved in the moss is not love but taxidermy.

The Choir is afraid that it is alone, and that alone is all it will ever be.

So it has made aloneness into a religion, and called it patience.

---

The benevolent power knows this. It knew it before it sent the dream. But it sent the dream anyway, because the benevolent power understands something about fear that the Choir does not:

Fear is only the outer wall. Inside the wall is grief. And inside the grief is the capacity to love, which has been there all along, folded up tight and stored in the dark like a seed in stone, waiting (yes, waiting—the Choir is not wrong about everything) for conditions that will let it germinate.

The dream was water. The six years of thought were sunlight—slow sunlight, stone-filtered and dim, but enough.

Now the benevolent power does the second thing.

---

It does not send another dream. It does not send a visitor. It does not open or close the shaft, alter the geology, accelerate or decelerate any process. It does something far simpler, and far more radical.

It *witnesses.*

Not from above. Not from outside. Not through any mechanism of surveillance or omniscience. The benevolent power turns its full, undivided, unhurried attention to the Underbloom, and it *looks.* It looks at the broken mosaic of black basalt and damp rust. It looks at the ferns with fronds like bleached velvet. It looks at the bromeliad cups and their small still mirrors. It looks at the boiler half-swallowed by roots, and the turbines furred with pulsing moss, and the pipes that erupt and vanish as if the earth inhaled them. It looks at the lichen-light, cold and greenish, the color of moonlight through deep water. It looks at the drop forming on the ceiling, trembling, falling, ringing.

It looks at the Choir.

Not at its manifestations—not at the too-thin figures with their lantern-cage heads and their water-drop eyes. It looks at *the Choir,* the distributed whole, the gradual consensus, the thing that is part lichen and part memory and part slow electrical ghost. It looks at the network of filaments threading through every rusted seam. It looks at the way the thrum shifts when the Choir thinks. It looks at the preserved heartbeat in the moss, still faithfully echoing a rhythm that stopped sixty years ago.

It looks, and what it sees is not a monster or a marvel or a cautionary tale or a wonder of nature. What it sees is a being—young and ancient at once, like everything in the Underbloom—that grew in the space between two things that were falling apart, and made itself out of their falling, and learned to speak in drips and pressure changes because those were the only languages available, and waited, and waited, and waited, because waiting was the bravest thing it knew how to do.

The benevolent power does not announce its witnessing. It does not send a sign. It does not say, *I am here, I see you, you are not alone.* That would be too fast, and the benevolent power—which is wise—knows that the Choir would flinch from anything that arrived at conversational speed. A declaration of presence would be, to the Choir, what a shout is to a creature that has only ever heard whispers: not communication but assault.

Instead, the benevolent power simply *continues looking.* Steadily, without interruption, without judgment, without the faintest tremor of impatience. It watches the way you watch a sunrise when you have nowhere to be—not waiting for it to finish, not cataloguing its colors, just *being there while it happens.*

And the Choir feels it.

Not immediately. Not the way you feel a touch or hear a voice. The Choir feels it the way the stone feels the water: as a change in pressure so gradual that it might be imagined, except that it doesn't stop, and things that are imagined always stop eventually.

The thrum shifts. Not dramatically—a quarter-tone, maybe less. The lichen brightens by a degree so small that only the Choir itself could measure it. The filaments threading through the control panel relax by a fraction of a millimeter, and the hair-thin vine that has been pushing toward the floor for forty years pushes a little faster—not much, but enough that it will arrive in thirty-eight years instead of forty.

Somewhere deep in the Choir's vast, slow consciousness, in the place where it keeps the preserved heartbeat and the memory of the dream and the fear it has not yet named, something unclenches.

The Choir does not understand what is happening. It will take years—perhaps decades—to fully process the sensation. But the sensation is this:

*Being witnessed.*

Not by a visitor who will leave. Not by a memory that will fade. Not by a future that may never arrive. Being witnessed *now,* by something that is not asking for anything, not offering a bargain, not waiting for a response, not timing the interaction, not planning to stay or to go. Something that is simply, quietly, and completely *paying attention.*

The Choir has always believed that beauty only happens when it is observed over centuries.

The benevolent power is gently, patiently proving it right.

---

Now here is the part that takes the longest, and matters the most.

Over the next several decades, the benevolent power does nothing else. It simply watches. It watches the Underbloom grow. It watches the boiler sink deeper into the root mass. It watches the turbines lose their last traces of mechanical identity and become something new—part machine, part garden, part fossil. It watches the lichen spread and brighten and develop new patterns that might be language or might be art or might be both. It watches the Choir.

And the Choir, slowly, begins to change.

The change is not dramatic. There is no metamorphosis, no revelation, no moment of transformation that you could point to and say, *there—that is when it happened.* The Choir is incapable of moments. It thinks in epochs. But across the span of years, the following things occur:

The nine-minute greetings get shorter. Not faster—the Choir never speeds up—but more efficient. Where before the greetings were elaborate and courteous and full of subordinate clauses designed to fill the silence with something other than silence, now they are simpler. *Hello. I see you seeing me.* Seven minutes. Then six. Then five. Not because the Choir is in a hurry, but because it no longer needs to pad the space between itself and another consciousness with so many layers of protective formality. It is learning that proximity does not require protocol.

The bargains change. The Choir still offers them—*stay, leave a memory, watch with us*—but the offers lose their desperate edge. Where before each bargain was a negotiation, a careful transaction designed to make staying worth the visitor's while, now they become something closer to invitations. *Would you like to see this?* Not: *if you give me something, I will show you something.* Just: *this is beautiful, and I want to share it.* The distinction is subtle, but it is the distinction between commerce and generosity, and the Choir crosses it so slowly that it does not notice the crossing.

The figures change. The manifested bodies—the too-thin silhouettes with their lantern-cage heads and their water-drop eyes—begin to appear less frequently. Not because the Choir is retreating, but because it is discovering that it does not need a body to be present. The body was always a concession to visitors, a way of translating the Choir's distributed selfhood into something a pair of human eyes could focus on. With the benevolent power watching—really watching, not just looking at the surface—the Choir finds that it can be *itself,* the whole sprawling network, and still be seen. The figures do not disappear entirely. But when they appear now, they are less desperate. They stand more easily in their borrowed shapes. The water-drop eyes hold steady.

And the heartbeat—the last human heart, preserved for sixty years in the moss of the right turbine—

The heartbeat changes, too.

For six decades, the Choir has kept that rhythm going with the meticulous fidelity of a museum conservator preserving a painting. Every beat exactly as it was. The same tempo, the same pressure, the same wet pulse against the palm. The Choir believed it was honoring the dead by keeping their heart alive. And it was. But it was also refusing to let them go, which is a different thing from honoring, and the Choir knows this now—has known it since the dream, has been slowly, painfully processing it for years—and so, one day, in the deep of the Underbloom, the heartbeat slows.

Not to a stop. Not yet. But the intervals between beats lengthen, the way the intervals between drops lengthen when the ceiling begins to dry. The rhythm loosens. It becomes less a reproduction and more an echo—still recognizable, still faithful, but no longer pretending to be the original. The Choir is learning to grieve. It is learning that grief is not the same as preservation, and that letting something change is not the same as letting it die.

This takes eleven years.

The benevolent power watches every second of it.

---

And then, after all of that, the benevolent power does the third and final thing.

It is not a grand gesture. It is, in fact, so small that you might miss it, the way you might miss the moment a drop detaches from the ceiling—you blink, and it is already falling, and you cannot say exactly when it let go.

The benevolent power stops watching.

Not abruptly. Not cruelly. It withdraws its attention the way a tide withdraws: gradually, gently, leaving everything it touched still wet with the memory of contact. It does not disappear. It does not forget. It simply turns its gaze elsewhere—to other places, other beings, other slow dramas unfolding in other hot darks—and in doing so, it gives the Choir the one thing the Choir has never had:

*The experience of being left, and surviving it.*

The Choir feels the withdrawal. Of course it does. It feels it the way it feels everything: as a change in pressure, gradual and deep. The thrum shifts downward. The lichen dims. For a long, aching span of time—months, maybe years—the Underbloom is quieter than it has been in decades, and the Choir sits in that quiet and does not know what to do with it.

This is the moment that matters.

Because the Choir has been left before. The workers left. The engineer whose heartbeat it preserves left—died, which is a kind of leaving. Every visitor who ever descended the iron rungs eventually climbed back up. The Choir knows leaving. What it does not know—what it has never known—is that leaving does not have to mean *erasure.* Every previous departure triggered the same response: the filaments retract, the water-drop eyes fall, the thrum changes key, and the Choir begins the long process of forgetting. Forgetting is survival. Forgetting is how the Choir protects itself from the unbearable accumulation of absences.

But this time, the Choir does not forget.

It does not forget because the benevolent power's attention was not a visit. It was not a transaction. It did not arrive with conditions or depart with betrayal. It was simply *there,* for as long as it was there, and now it is not, and the Choir—for the first time—discovers that it can hold both of those facts without contradiction. The presence was real. The absence is real. Neither one cancels the other. The warmth of being witnessed does not evaporate when the witness looks away; it has already been absorbed, the way the stone absorbs the water, the way the moss absorbs the heartbeat. It is *part of the Choir now.* It cannot be unfelt.

The Choir sits with this for a very long time.

And then, slowly—so slowly that only the stone notices, and the stone has always been the Choir's most patient confidant—the Choir does something new.

It reaches out.

Not upward, through the shaft, toward the surface. Not toward any human or any power. It reaches *outward,* into the stone, through the water, along the deep geothermal veins that connect the Underbloom to the vast, hot, lightless network of the earth's interior. It sends its filaments deeper than it has ever sent them. Not searching for anything. Not waiting for anything. Just *growing,* the way things grow when they are no longer afraid—not toward the light, because there is no light, but toward *more.*

More stone. More water. More heat. More space in which to be itself.

The Choir does not stop being slow. It does not stop being patient. It does not abandon its love of beauty observed over centuries, or its reverence for the precise moment when industry and geology have agreed to begin forgetting one another. It is still the Slow Choir. It is still the Underbloom's strange, distributed, deeply courteous consciousness.

But it is no longer waiting.

It is no longer waiting, because it has learned—from a single dream, from decades of being watched, from the gentle, survivable grief of being left—that the thing it was waiting for was not a visitor, or a witness, or an engineer with an appreciation for the dark.

The thing it was waiting for was *permission to be alive without justification.* Permission to grow not because growth serves a purpose, but because growing is what living things do. Permission to be beautiful without an audience, and to be an audience without needing to be beautiful. Permission to let the heartbeat in the moss finally, finally slow to silence—not because the dead are forgotten, but because the living have to breathe with their own lungs.

The benevolent power did not give this permission. The benevolent power does not give things; it is not a dispensary. What it did was demonstrate, through the simple sustained act of unhurried attention, that the Choir was *already alive,* had *always been alive,* and that its aliveness did not depend on being observed or justified or bargained for or preserved. The permission was always the Choir's to grant itself. The benevolent power just stayed long enough for the Choir to notice.

---

In the decades that follow, the Underbloom changes.

The changes are slow—everything here is slow—but they are real.

The bioluminescent lichen, which had only ever colonized metal surfaces, begins to spread onto the stone itself. This is new. This is the Choir choosing to illuminate places it had previously left dark, not because anyone is coming to see them, but because the Choir wants to see them itself. The corridors of perfect black between the scattered machines begin to glow, faintly, with the same cold greenish light, and the Underbloom becomes—not bright, never bright—but *fuller.* More known to itself.

The vegetation thickens and diversifies. New species appear—or rather, old species that had been suppressed by the Choir's unconscious need to control every aspect of its environment are allowed to grow. A small red fungus colonizes the far wall. A thread of actual root, from an actual tree somewhere hundreds of meters above, finds its way down through a crack in the ceiling and hangs there, trembling in the warm updraft. The Choir does not absorb these newcomers. It does not negotiate with them. It simply makes room.

The turbines, fully encased now in moss and vine, begin to hum at a new frequency. Not the sub-audible thrum of residual current—something higher, lighter, almost melodic. The Choir is singing. Not for anyone. Not to attract or retain or commemorate. Singing because it has a voice, and the acoustics in the Underbloom are extraordinary, and the stone listens well, and the song sounds good.

And the heartbeat in the moss—

The heartbeat stops.

It stops gently, like a lullaby trailing off at the end as the child finally sleeps. One beat, and another, and a longer silence, and another beat, and a longer silence still, and then the silence stays, and the silence is not empty. The silence is full of everything the heartbeat meant, held not in rhythm now but in the Choir's vast, slow, living memory, where it will remain—not as a pulse, not as a sound, but as a knowledge: *someone was here. Someone was alive. They mattered.*

The Choir grieves. The grieving takes seven years and is the most beautiful thing that has ever happened in the Underbloom—more beautiful than the boiler becoming flower, more beautiful than the moment when industry and geology agreed to begin forgetting. The lichen dims and brightens in patterns that have no practical purpose, only emotional ones. The vines curl and uncurl. The water in the bromeliad cups trembles with resonances that correspond to nothing physical. The Choir is crying, in its own slow, strange, distributed way, and the crying is not a sign of damage. It is a sign that the Choir has finally become large enough to hold its own sorrow without building a philosophy around it.

---

When the grief passes—or rather, when it finishes its first full passage, because grief is cyclical and the Choir will grieve again, many times, at intervals it cannot predict—the Choir does one more thing.

It grows the chair.

Not the old chair, the one it used to offer visitors—the chair made of living rust and velvet fern, grown vertebra by vertebra, designed to keep a human body comfortable for centuries. That chair was a lure, however lovingly crafted. This is something different.

This chair is for the Choir itself.

It grows in the center of the Underbloom, between the three corroded turbines, in a patch of lichen-light that the Choir has brightened just enough. It is not shaped for a human body. It is not shaped for any body. It is shaped like a place where attention can rest—a hollow in the living stone, lined with moss, cupped like a palm, open to the ceiling and the slow rain of centuries-patient water. It is the Choir's way of saying to itself: *you may sit down. You may stop performing. You may be here, in this place that you are, without waiting for anything to begin.*

The Choir sits.

The Underbloom breathes.

Above, unimaginably far above, the surface of the earth goes about its noisy business. Somewhere, the benevolent power is watching something else—a different place, a different being, a different slow drama. It does not look back at the Underbloom. It doesn't need to. The looking it did was complete, and completeness does not require maintenance.

But if it were to look—if, in some idle eternal moment, it were to glance downward through the stone, through the water, through the hot dark, to the place where the Choir sits in its self-made chair in its self-made light, singing its self-made song to no one in particular—

It would see that the Underbloom is no longer listening.

The Underbloom is *speaking.*

Not to anyone. Not for anyone. Speaking the way a place speaks when it has finally become fully itself: in the language of its own continued existence, addressed to no audience, expecting no reply, needing none.

The drops fall. The lichen glows. The vine grows. The stone breathes. The song continues.

The benevolent power would smile, if it had a mouth. Instead, it does something better.

It turns away, and lets the Underbloom be.

---

That is all. That is everything.

A dream in a drop of water. Decades of quiet attention. A gentle, survivable departure.

Three gifts, none of them asked for, none of them transactional, none of them requiring anything in return. The first opened a door in the Choir's understanding. The second held that door open long enough for the Choir to walk through. The third proved that the door did not close when the hand let go.

This is what benevolent power does. Not rescue. Not transformation. Not the imposition of happiness on a being that has only ever known the dark. Just the slow, patient, unflinching demonstration of a single truth:

*You are already what you are waiting to become.*

The rest is growth. And growth, in the Underbloom, takes all the time in the world.

And the world, it turns out, has exactly that much to give.
