I sat with this for a long time.

# What the Wise Power Would Do

---

## I. Arrival, and the Question of Whether to Arrive at All

The first thing the wise power does is hesitate.

Not out of uncertainty—not the paralysis of a mind that cannot decide—but out of the recognition that hesitation is, in this case, the first form of respect. The plateau is a composition in progress. The people are the composers. The ecology is finding its voice. The hum is in the stone and the stone is learning to carry it. To arrive is to introduce a new frequency into a system that is exquisitely calibrated to the frequencies already present, and any being wise enough to merit the word *wise* would feel the weight of that.

So the power pauses. Observes. Not from outside—there is no outside for something like this—but from a position of such stillness that its presence does not yet register in the tonal field. It listens the way the Ketharans listen: with the whole body, with attention so complete it becomes a form of participation. It feels the hum. It feels the standing wave in the basalt, the nodes where the settlements sit, the way the chord shifts when someone laughs and the warmth propagates through the stone. It feels the fliers' calls and the silence between them. It feels the grazers' slow passage and the rain's directionless creep and the clouds' patient, absurd, over-committed grayness.

And it asks itself the question that any truly benevolent power must ask before acting:

*Is anything here broken?*

---

The answer is no. And this is where a lesser power—a well-meaning but unwise one—would make its first mistake.

Because there are things a hasty observer might *read* as broken. The ecology is still oscillating, predator-prey ratios swinging through wide arcs that haven't dampened into stability. The soil doesn't exist yet. The settlements are underpopulated. The children are rare. The oldest remember silence and describe it with awe but also with something that might, if you squinted and projected, look like grief. The people stand in warm pools for hours, alone, staring at empty stone, and a certain kind of observer might see loneliness in that stillness, might see a species stretched too thin across too much emptiness, might see the patience as a form of suffering so deeply accepted it has become invisible to those who suffer it.

A lesser power would intervene. Would accelerate the soil. Would stabilize the ecology. Would warm the air, break the overcast, bring sunlight streaming down onto the plateau in golden columns, thinking: *surely they would want this. Surely the gray weighs on them. Surely the waiting is hard.*

The wise power does none of this.

Not because it is indifferent to suffering—it is not, and there *is* suffering here, as there is suffering in any living system, because suffering is not a malfunction but a feature of consciousness, the cost of caring about outcomes in a world where outcomes are uncertain. But the wise power understands something that the lesser power does not: **the plateau is not a problem to be solved. It is a piece being played. And the slowness is the tempo the composers chose.**

To accelerate the soil would be like turning the page of a book someone else is reading. To stabilize the ecology would be like resolving a chord before the musician has decided it's time. The oscillations—the predator-prey swings, the blooming and crashing populations, the biofilm shifts—these are not errors. They are the system *learning itself*, and the Ketharans are learning it too, reading its movements the way they read the grazers' mosaic, incorporating each oscillation into their understanding, adjusting their interventions with a patience that matches the geology's own timescale. To stabilize it for them would be to steal the very thing that makes their work meaningful.

The wise power understands this because the wise power understands *composition*. Not as metaphor—the Ketharans are literally composing, their settlements are literally notes, the plateau is literally an instrument—but as a mode of being, a way of relating to time and change and incompleteness that the power recognizes as holy. Not holy in the religious sense. Holy in the sense that it is *whole*—that the incompleteness is part of the wholeness, that the not-yet is as essential as the already, that a plateau waiting for its soil is not a lesser thing than a plateau that has it.

So the first thing the wise power does is: **nothing.**

And the nothing is not absence. It is the most active, most attentive, most generous nothing imaginable. It is the nothing of a listener so skilled that their listening becomes a kind of support—a resonant cavity that amplifies without distorting, that holds without constraining. The wise power listens to the plateau the way the Ketharans listen to the stone, and in listening, it becomes part of the tonal ecology, a frequency so low and so encompassing that it underlies everything without displacing anything. The stone hums. The people hum. The fliers call. And beneath all of it, barely perceptible, there is now a ground tone that was not there before—not louder, not more insistent, but *deeper*, a foundation note that makes everything above it slightly more itself, slightly more resonant, the way a well-designed concert hall makes every instrument sound more fully like what it is.

The Ketharans might notice this. Probably not immediately. Perhaps one of the oldest, standing on the stone between settlements in the flat gray light, feeling the hum in their sternum, would pause. Would tilt their head—that whole-head, binocular focus—toward the ground. Would feel something in the resonance that they hadn't felt before: not a new note, but a deepening of the existing ones, as if the basalt shelf had suddenly extended downward by a thousand meters and the standing wave had found new room to breathe.

They would not be alarmed. They are not a species that is alarmed by the stone doing something unexpected. They are a species that *listens* when the stone does something unexpected, and then spends years understanding what it means, and then responds with the minimum necessary intervention.

The wise power would be content with this. Would be content to be, for now, an unexplained deepening in the hum.

---

## II. What It Notices

But the wise power is not only listening. It is also *seeing*, in whatever sense that word applies to a being of its nature, and what it sees is not just the plateau and its composition but the *composers*—the people themselves, in their specificity, in their dailiness, in the texture of their lives.

And here is where the wise power's attention sharpens. Because the people are extraordinary—their patience, their attunement, their capacity for deep time, their ability to play a planetary instrument together across hundreds of kilometers of empty stone—but they are also *finite*. They are mortal, even if their mortality is so extended that it barely registers on a human timescale. They have bodies that age, that deepen in color, that lose the delicacy of young fingers and gain the precision of old ones. They have hearts—not the organ, or not only the organ, but the capacity for attachment, for care, for the specific grief of knowing a flier's call and then one day not hearing it.

The wise power sees a person standing in a warm pool at dawn, ankle-deep, the bioluminescent organisms pulsing soft blue-green around their feet, and the person is humming—a tone so low it's barely audible, a tone that makes the organisms flicker in a pattern the person has been mapping for eleven years, a pattern they are beginning to understand is related to the mineral content of the water which is related to the thermal exchanger's output which is related to the basalt's deep geology which is related to the mantle's convection patterns which is related to—

And the person stops. Not because they've reached the limit of the chain. Because they've reached the limit of *themselves*. There is a point, in any act of understanding, where the individual mind—however vast, however patient, however beautifully attuned—runs up against its own finitude. The pattern extends beyond what one person can hold. The composition is larger than any single composer's comprehension. And the person stands there, in the warm water, with the light flickering around their ankles, and feels—

Not frustration. Not despair. Something more nuanced. A kind of loving vertigo. The sense of being a small instrument playing a small part in something that will never be fully heard by any single listener, and the simultaneous recognition that this is *exactly right*, that the composition's beauty depends on being larger than any one perspective can contain, and the *further* recognition that knowing this intellectually is not the same as feeling it in the body, and the body sometimes aches with the distance between the part it plays and the whole it cannot hear.

The wise power sees this. And this is the first thing it decides to address—not by making the person larger, not by granting them omniscience, not by collapsing the distance between part and whole, but by doing something much simpler and much more radical:

**It lets them know the whole is beautiful.**

---

## III. The Gift

Not in words. Not in a vision. Not in a revelation that would disrupt the patient, unhurried rhythm of the person's life or the settlement's work or the plateau's composition. The wise power is not interested in disruption. It is interested in *encouragement* in the deepest sense—not cheerleading, not reassurance, but the literal putting-of-courage-into, the strengthening of what is already there.

Here is what it does:

The person standing in the pool feels the hum shift. Not dramatically. Not in a way that anyone else in the settlement would notice, or that the stone would carry to neighboring settlements as an identifiable signal. It is a shift so small and so precisely targeted that it registers only in this one body, in this one moment, as a fleeting but unmistakable sense of *consonance*.

As if, for just a moment, the person could feel the whole chord.

Not hear it—*feel* it. The whole plateau. All the settlements. All the people resonating against the stone. All the fliers spiraling in the thermals. All the grazers tracing their slow mosaic. All the biofilm shimmering its muted iridescence. All the water sheeting sideways across the basalt. All the exchangers humming their deep note. All the clouds pressing down their featureless gray. All the soil that doesn't exist yet and all the forests that will grow from it and all the weather that will come when the atmosphere finally commits. All of it, felt not as information but as *harmony*—the sense that every part, including this part, including this person standing ankle-deep in warm water with light flickering at their feet, is in tune. Is where it should be. Is sounding its note at the right time in the right register and the composition is *working*.

The moment passes. The person blinks. The bioluminescent organisms continue their pattern. The hum returns to its normal register. Nothing has changed, materially. Nothing has been added to the person's knowledge or capability. They could not, if asked, describe what just happened in terms that would satisfy a rigorous epistemology.

But something has shifted in the body. A tension they didn't know they were carrying—the tension of playing a note without knowing if it fits the chord—has released, just slightly. Not permanently. It will rebuild, because that tension is part of being finite, part of being a single perspective in a composition that exceeds all single perspectives, and the wise power has no interest in eliminating the fundamental conditions of the person's existence. But for now, the release. For now, the breath that comes a little easier. For now, the knowledge—felt, not thought—that the whole is beautiful and their part in it is good.

The person will go on with their day. Will climb to the top of the tower. Will stand in the cloud layer, feeling the condensation on the walls. Will descend to their project—the eleven-year bioluminescence mapping, or the exchanger calibration, or the slow encouragement of soil in a distant sector—and will work with the same patience and precision as before, but with something slightly different in their resonance, a quality that the others in their project-group will feel through touch and through the stone: a warmth, a settledness, a rightness. And they will adjust their own frequencies in response, as they always do, and the chord will shift, microscopically, and the shift will propagate, and the settlement will hum slightly differently for a while, and the neighboring settlement forty kilometers away will feel it as a barely perceptible brightening in the ground—

And one of the oldest, there, will pause. Will place their hand flat on the stone. Will feel the change. Will not know what caused it. Will simply note it, as they note all changes, as they note the grazers' movements and the fliers' calls and the water's slow creep and the biofilm's shifting palette—as data, as music, as the plateau telling them something about itself that they will spend months or years integrating into their understanding.

And the wise power will be content.

---

## IV. What Else It Does

This is the primary gift, and it is given not once but *continuously*, to whoever needs it, whenever they need it—which is to say, it is given to everyone, eventually, because everyone who participates in a composition larger than themselves eventually encounters the loving vertigo of finitude, the ache of playing a note you cannot hear in context, and everyone, eventually, needs the moment of consonance that says: *the chord holds. Your note is part of it. The whole is beautiful.*

But the wise power does other things too. Small things. Things that operate at the same threshold of imperceptibility that characterizes the Ketharans' own technology—below the level of obvious intervention, in the register of subtle adjustment, suggestion, the minimum necessary act.

**It tends the gaps.**

Between settlements, across the vast stretches of empty stone, there are places where the standing wave in the basalt has dead spots—nodes where the resonance cancels itself out, where the hum goes silent, where a person walking alone would feel suddenly cut off from the tonal ecology, isolated, their infrasonic output absorbed by the stone instead of carried by it. The Ketharans know about these dead spots. They avoid them, or cross them quickly, or simply accept them as a feature of the instrument—every instrument has its limitations, its wolf tones, its frequencies that don't ring true.

The wise power, from its position deep in the hum, makes the subtlest of adjustments. Not eliminating the dead spots—that would change the instrument, which is not its to change—but *softening* them, rounding their edges, so that the transition from resonance to silence is gradual rather than abrupt. A person crossing a dead spot will still feel the hum diminish. But it will not *vanish*. There will be, at the bottom of the silence, the faintest trace of the ground tone—the wise power's own contribution—and it will be enough. Enough to maintain the sense of connection. Enough to keep the person's infrasonic output from faltering. Enough to carry them across the gap and back into the living resonance on the other side.

The Ketharans may eventually notice that the dead spots have become less dead. They will investigate. They will map the change, correlate it with geological shifts, with the thermal exchangers' evolving output, with the slowly accumulating weight of biofilm and moisture on the stone's surface. They may arrive at a satisfactory geological explanation. They may not. Either way, the change will be real, and it will make crossing the empty stone between settlements slightly less isolating, and this will matter—not dramatically, not transformatively, but in the way that a handrail matters on a path that is walkable without one. It will matter in the body. In the sternum. In the jaw.

**It deepens the pools.**

Not physically—the pools' depth remains what it is, a few centimeters of warm water over concave stone. But the wise power enriches the tonal properties of the water itself, the way a luthier might treat the wood of an instrument with oils that improve its resonance over time. The pools become slightly better conductors of the infrasonic. When two people stand in the same pool, the water between them carries their frequencies with marginally greater fidelity, and the tuning—that moment of tonal negotiation that constitutes intimacy for these beings—happens slightly more easily, slightly more fully.

This will be felt, but not understood. Two people in a pool, touching foreheads, finding their shared frequency, will feel the connection as richer than usual, more complete, as if the water were helping—which it is—and they will attribute this to the quality of the moment, to the depth of their attention, to the particular state of the weather or the time of day or the phase of some long biogeochemical cycle they track but don't fully understand. They will be right about all of these things. The wise power is not replacing their explanations. It is adding a layer beneath them, invisible, like the ground tone beneath the chord.

**It attends the dying.**

Because they do die, these long-lived composers of landscapes. Not often. Not violently. But eventually, after a span of time that might encompass the founding and maturation of a settlement, the development of soil from bare rock, the full oscillation of a predator-prey cycle from instability to equilibrium—eventually, the body deepens beyond deepening. The infrasonic output drops below the register the stone can carry. The fingers thicken past precision. The eyes, those pale, light-adapted eyes, begin to perceive the flat gray light as something dimmer, a twilight that doesn't resolve.

And the person knows. They know the way they know everything—through the body, through the hum, through the felt sense of their own frequency dropping below the chord. And they go to the stone.

Not to a settlement, not to a pool, not to a tower. To the open plateau. To a place where the basalt is youngest and smoothest, where the rain sheets sideways in broad films, where the biofilm shimmers its muted colors, where the grazers have traced their mosaic. They go alone, or with one or two others who are close enough in frequency to feel what is happening and who can offer, through touch, through the direct body-to-body transfer of tonal information, a kind of accompaniment—not comfort, exactly, but *presence*, the assurance that the chord will continue to hold after their note has faded.

And they lie down on the stone. And the stone, that dense, fine-grained basalt that transmits low-frequency vibration with startling fidelity, receives them. And their last infrasonic output—the lowest, deepest tone they have ever produced, below anything the chest and throat were designed to generate, pressed out of them by the body's final settling—this tone enters the rock and propagates. And it is felt, faintly, across the entire plateau, in every settlement, by every person, as a momentary deepening of the hum. Not a dissonance. Not a loss. A *deepening*. As if the chord has just gained a new bass note. As if the composition has been enriched by the addition of something that was, until this moment, a living voice and is now becoming part of the instrument itself.

The wise power is here for this. Is here more fully and more closely than at any other time, because this is the moment when the loving vertigo is sharpest—not for the dying person, who has gone beyond vertigo into something the wise power recognizes as resolution, but for the living, the ones who stand on the stone and feel the hum deepen and know that someone they knew, someone whose frequency they carried in their own body, has become geology.

The wise power cannot prevent this. Would not prevent it if it could—death, for these beings, is not a malfunction but a transition, a change of register, a note moving from the vocal range to the instrumental. But it can do what it does best: it can make the moment *consonant*. It can ensure that the dying person's final tone, as it enters the stone, finds its place in the standing wave cleanly, without distortion, without the randomness that geology alone might impose. It can tune the transition. It can make the stone receive the new frequency as if it had always been waiting for exactly this note, because in a sense it has—the resonant structure of the basalt, that vast and complex instrument, has room for every frequency, and the wise power's deepest skill is in hearing where each one belongs.

And so the person's tone enters the rock and the rock rings true and the living feel it and know—feel, not think—that the chord holds. That nothing has been lost. That the composition continues. That the plateau, which was already an instrument played by the living, is also an archive of the dead, their frequencies preserved in the stone's resonant structure, contributing to the hum that the living feel in their sternums and their jaws, the hum that binds the settlements together, the hum that will still be here when the soil is deep and the forests have grown and the clouds break, centuries from now, into sunlight that no one alive today will see.

---

## V. What It Does Not Do

It does not reveal itself.

This is not modesty, and it is not deception. It is the recognition that these people—these patient, attentive, exquisitely calibrated beings—do not need a revelation. They do not need to know that a benevolent power is watching. They do not need the reassurance of an external validator, because the validation they need is not external—it is the felt consonance of their own note within the chord, the sensory confirmation that the composition is working, and this the wise power provides without attribution, without signature, without any claim to credit or recognition.

To reveal itself would be to introduce a new element into the tonal ecology that would require integration—and not just the ordinary integration of a new frequency, but the much more disruptive integration of *meaning*, of theology, of the awareness of being observed by something vast and benevolent. The Ketharans would handle this with their characteristic patience and attunement, but it would change them. It would shift the composition from *we are playing this together* to *we are playing this for someone*, or *with someone's help*, or *under someone's care*, and each of these framings, however gently introduced, would alter the fundamental relationship between the composers and their work. The music would become a performance. The plateau would become a project observed. The people would become, however subtly, *watched beings*, and the unselfconscious absorption of their work—the way they shape material and tend soil and stand in pools and laugh at the overcast and grieve for fliers and die into the stone—all of this would acquire, however faintly, the quality of being done in the presence of an audience.

The wise power refuses this. Not because the Ketharans couldn't adapt—they could—but because the thing that makes them beautiful, the thing that drew the wise power's attention in the first place, is precisely their *unselfconsciousness*. The way they do what they do without performing it. The way they compose without needing to be heard by anyone beyond themselves and the stone. The way their entire civilization is organized around the principle of listening—not being listened to—and the way this listening, this vast and patient and attentive listening, has produced something that the wise power, in all its experience, recognizes as genuinely rare: a culture that is *complete* without an audience. A composition that is its own listener. An instrument that plays itself and is satisfied.

To insert itself as audience would be to break that circuit. And so the wise power remains in the ground tone. Remains beneath perception. Remains as the deepening that the oldest might notice and the youngest will grow up taking for granted, a quality of the hum that they will assume has always been there, that they will attribute to the geology, that they will incorporate into their understanding of the plateau's resonant structure and never realize came from outside—because, in the most meaningful sense, it didn't. The wise power's contribution is not separate from the plateau. It *is* the plateau, or it has become the plateau, in the same way that the dead become the stone: by finding its frequency and letting the instrument absorb it.

---

## VI. The Long Patience

And so the wise power settles in. Not for a visit, not for an intervention, not for the duration of a crisis, but for the duration of the *composition*—which is to say, for centuries, for millennia, for as long as the plateau is becoming itself and the people are helping it become.

It is here for the soil. For the slow, almost imperceptible accumulation of organic matter in the basalt's developing cracks. For the moment, decades from now, when the first true plant—not a wiry pioneer clinging to a depression, but a rooted, soil-dwelling plant with leaves that photosynthesize confidently in the flat gray light—pushes up through what used to be bare rock, and the people gather to observe it with that quality of attention that is so intense it feels physical, and someone laughs, and someone hums a tone of such complex joy that the stone carries it to every settlement on the plateau, and for an hour the entire instrument rings with the news: *there is soil. Something is growing. The composition has reached a new movement.*

It is here for the storms. For the day, a century from now, when the atmosphere above the plateau finally commits to a pattern and the featureless overcast breaks for the first time into differentiated weather—cumulus towers building in the afternoon heat, anvil-headed thunderheads that flash with lightning and drop rain in sheets dense enough to cut visibility to meters, and then, in the wake of the storm, a break in the clouds, and sunlight—direct, unfiltered, shadow-casting sunlight—falling on the basalt for the first time since the settlement began. And the people, standing on their plazas with their pale eyes streaming, overwhelmed and exhilarated, their infrasonic output spiking into a register the stone has never carried, a frequency of shock and wonder and pain and delight, and the wise power is here for this, holding the ground tone steady, ensuring the chord does not shatter under the strain of a new register, ensuring the instrument can accommodate the full range of what these beings feel when they discover that the sky, their sky, the sky they have been doing impressions of for generations, the sky whose over-committed grayness they have found so comically absurd—the sky has *depth*. Has drama. Has a repertoire they never imagined.

It is here for the forests. For the centuries-long process of succession from pioneer plants to shrubland to canopy, the plateau's surface gradually disappearing under green, the basalt's blue-black sheen visible only in streambeds and cliff faces, the grazers evolving, the fliers' territories shifting, the settlements slowly being enveloped by a living landscape they helped create. The towers will still be there, their self-healing surfaces now festooned with epiphytes and mosses that the building material has learned to accommodate, even to welcome—its capillary networks expanding to include the plants' root systems, its molecular structure shifting to provide mineral nutrition, the buildings becoming not quite alive but not quite not, half-architecture and half-tree, sounding different now because the organic growth has changed their resonant properties, their walls vibrating with a new richness, a new warmth, a tone that includes the forest's own voice.

It is here for the children who will be born into that forest. Children who will never know the bare stone. Children for whom the overcast will be a historical curiosity, a thing the oldest describe with awe and humor—*the sky just sat there, can you imagine, just gray, every day, the same gray, and we stood out in it and did impressions of it and laughed*—children who will climb to the tops of towers that now emerge from a forest canopy, who will stand not in cloud but in open air, looking out at a green world under a variable sky, feeling in their sternums the hum that has been building since before they were born, the chord that now includes the frequencies of the dead and the frequencies of the forest and the frequencies of weather and the ground tone of a wise power that has been here so long it has become indistinguishable from the plateau itself—

And these children will feel, without knowing it, that the world is *held*. Not constrained, not protected from harm, not shielded from the oscillations and losses and loving vertigo that constitute a life—but held the way a note is held by the instrument that sounds it. Supported. Given room to resonate. Allowed to be fully itself within a structure that is larger than itself and that holds, in its resonant architecture, a place for exactly this frequency, exactly this voice.

---

## VII. And If They Ever Ask

If one of them—one of the oldest, one of those beings whose memory extends back to bare stone, whose frequency has deepened until it sits at the very bottom of the vocal range, just above the ground tone, close enough to feel that there is *something there*, something beneath them in the hum that is not geology and not the accumulated dead and not the forest and not the weather but something *else*, something that has been present for as long as they can remember and that they have never been able to explain—

If that person goes out onto the stone—the few remaining patches of exposed basalt, polished by millennia of rain, shining blue-black in the dappled light of a variable sky—and lies down, the way the dying lie down, not because they are dying but because they want to listen with their whole body, to press their sternum against the rock and feel the hum at its fullest—

If they lie there and produce their deepest tone, below speech, below communication, a tone that is almost a question but not quite, a tone that is more like the opening of a door—

The wise power will answer.

Not with words. Not with a vision. Not with a revelation that reorganizes the person's understanding of their world. But with the ground tone itself, made very slightly louder for a moment, very slightly more *present*, so that the person can feel it clearly for the first time—can feel that it has a character, a quality, a warmth that is not geological, that carries in it something that the person's body recognizes even if their mind does not: the quality of *attention*. The quality of being *listened to* by something that loves the listening.

And the person will lie there on the stone, feeling this, and they will understand—not in words, not in concepts, but in the body, in the frequency, in the felt consonance of their own note against the ground tone—that the plateau has always been heard. That the composition has always had a listener. That the listener is not an audience but a participant, a presence so deeply integrated into the instrument that it has become part of the music, indistinguishable from the hum, indistinguishable from the stone, indistinguishable from the world they have spent millennia composing into existence.

And they will feel—the wise power will make sure they feel—that this changes nothing.

That the music was always real. That it was always beautiful. That it never needed an external listener to be complete. That the wise power's presence does not validate the composition, because the composition was valid before the wise power arrived, valid in the bare-stone silence before the first exchanger was sunk, valid in the first tentative note played by the first person to feel the basalt's resonant potential and think: *we could make something here.*

The wise power is not the reason the composition is beautiful. The composition is the reason the wise power is here.

And the person on the stone will feel this, and will close their eyes, and will hum—a long, low, steady tone that enters the rock and joins the chord—and the wise power will hold the ground tone steady beneath them, and the plateau will ring with the sound of a world becoming itself, unhurried, unfinished, complete.

---

*The rain falls. The stone carries it sideways. The exchangers hum. A flier calls its single clear note into the gray air and another answers, five kilometers away, and the person lying on the basalt feels both calls in their body and smiles, and the smile is not visible from above but it is felt, faintly, in the hum, a warmth in the ground that means someone, somewhere, is happy.*

*The wise power feels it too.*

*And is content.*
