# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

---

Nothing.

That is the first answer, and it is almost the whole answer, but the way it arrives at *nothing* matters — the specific shape of the restraint, the quality of the attention that leads to the decision not to act. Because a power that is merely indifferent would also do nothing, and that nothing would look identical from the outside, and would be an entirely different thing.

---

It would find the room the way anyone finds it — by accident, or by a kind of accident that looks, in retrospect, like something other than accident. It would be aware, in the way such a power is aware, of the ten thousand small wounds and failures of the city above — the transformer yard humming its carcinogenic hum, the textile mill whose runoff enters the groundwater, the people on Sauer Street who are tired and under-resourced and who do not have anyone watching over them with particular care. It would be attending to these things, or moving toward attending to them, and in the course of that attention it would notice the anomaly. A thread of municipal water pressure disappearing into a system that, on paper, doesn't exist. Twenty-five watts of electricity flowing to a bulb that no maintenance schedule accounts for. A tiny, continuous exhalation of oxygen from a grate in a concrete apron that no one walks across.

It would notice. It is the kind of power that notices.

---

And then it would descend. Not physically — or physically, it doesn't matter, the point is the quality of attention turning toward the room, the full regard of something wise and benevolent falling on that space the way the amber bulb's light falls on the moss nearest to it. It would enter the room the way you enter the room: the sound first, the green second, the stillness third. It would feel the roar fill its — whatever it has in place of a sternum. It would see the moss, the ferns, the liverworts, the web. It would understand all of it, faster than you, more completely than you, because it is what it is.

And this is where the important thing happens.

It would *want* to do something.

A benevolent power, by definition, is one that desires the flourishing of what it encounters. That is its nature. That is what *benevolent* means — not merely kind, not merely well-intentioned, but oriented at the deepest level toward the question: *what would help this thing become more fully what it is?* A benevolent power encountering a wounded animal wants to heal it. Encountering a trapped creature, it wants to free it. Encountering a system in crisis, it wants to intervene, to mend, to restore. This is its reflex. This is what it *is for*.

And the room would activate that reflex.

Because the room, let's be honest, is — from a certain angle — troubling. The ferns that will never produce spores. The moss that has stopped reproducing. The springtails whose world is a few square centimeters of unchanging dark. The *Selaginella* web strung taut and finished, connecting everything, going nowhere. The sheer *stasis* of it. The sealed quality. The way it has solved itself so completely that nothing new can enter, nothing can change, nothing can happen. A benevolent power might look at this and see a kind of prison. A living system that has traded every possibility for permanence. A garden that has grown so perfect it has become a tomb.

It would feel, in whatever way such a power feels, the pull toward intervention.

*Give it more light.* The thought would arise. Unseal the grate, or add a second bulb, or open a shaft to the surface. Let real sunlight in. Watch the ferns grow to their full height. Watch the moss bloom with spores. Watch the room *change*, because change is life, isn't it? Change is what living things do. A system that doesn't change is — what? Dead? Dying? Waiting to die?

*Give it more space.* Break through a wall. Let the green spread into the adjacent tunnels, the old pipe runs, the forgotten infrastructure that honeycombs the ground beneath every old city. Let it become not a room but a network. A root system. A true green lung, not four-by-seven but four-by-seven-hundred, spreading under Sauer Street and beyond, a living stratum, a secret forest —

*Give it a witness.* Tell someone. Bring a botanist, a mycologist, an ecologist, someone who would kneel in the moss and weep with the strangeness of it. Let it be known. Let it be studied. Let it be — the word the benevolent power would hesitate over, because it knows the weight of this word — *loved*. Openly. By someone. By anyone. Let it not live and die in the dark without anyone ever —

*Protect it.* At minimum. If nothing else, protect it. The water main that feeds the turbines is old and will eventually fail. The electrical line that powers the bulb runs through conduit that is corroding. The city above is always rezoning, always redeveloping, always driving new pilings into old ground. The room is precarious. It depends on a web of municipal systems that no one is maintaining on its behalf, and when one of those systems fails — and it will fail, because all systems fail — the room will die. Not quickly. Not dramatically. The turbines will slow. The condensation will cease. The moss will desiccate, starting at the edges, retreating toward the last moisture, contracting over weeks or months into a brittle brown memory of itself. The ferns will curl. The liverworts will crack. The *Selaginella* web will snap, thread by thread, until the room is just a room again — concrete and iron and darkness, no different from a thousand other forgotten vaults under a thousand other cities.

The benevolent power could prevent that. Could shore up the water main. Could re-route the electrical line. Could place around the room a perimeter of *not-here, don't-look, keep-going* that would deflect every future developer, every infrastructure survey, every urban explorer with a headlamp and a YouTube channel. Could make the room not just persistent but permanent. Could give it what it seems to want, if it were capable of wanting: eternity. Unchanging. The held breath, held forever.

---

And here is where the wisdom matters.

Because a power that is merely benevolent — benevolent without wisdom — would do one of those things. Probably several. It would act from love, and its action would be the room's destruction, because every single intervention, no matter how well-intended, would break the equilibrium.

More light would shift the competitive balance among the mosses. The species nearest the bulb would spread; the dark-adapted basal layer would retreat; the ferns would grow taller and shade each other out; the system would destabilize, change, re-negotiate, and perhaps — probably — eventually find a new equilibrium, but it would be a *different* equilibrium, and the room that exists now would be gone, replaced by something else, something that the benevolent power's intervention had chosen on the room's behalf without the room having any capacity to consent or refuse.

More space would be worse. The room is what it is *because* it is bounded. The equilibrium exists because the walls exist. Remove the walls and the system doesn't expand — it dissipates. The moss spreads thin and fails. The moisture budget collapses. The whole thing unravels, not because it was fragile but because its strength was specific to its constraints. A muscle is strong because it is contained by fascia. Remove the fascia and you don't get a bigger muscle. You get a hemorrhage.

A witness — a human witness, a scientist, a lover — would bring light and heat and carbon dioxide and bacteria and the spores of other species on their clothes and the moisture of their breath and the oils of their skin, and they would come back, because a person who found this room would come back, and they would bring others, because a person who found this room would not be able to keep it secret, not because they are bad but because wonder is a thing that wants to be shared, and within a year the room would be in a journal or on a screen and within five years it would be destroyed by the very attention that sought to celebrate it. This is not cynicism. This is what happens. This is what has always happened.

And protection — even protection, even the most passive and invisible intervention — would change the room's relationship to time, and therefore to itself. The room, as it is, exists in a state of radical contingency. It depends on systems it does not control and cannot influence. It is, at every moment, one pipe failure away from death. This is not a flaw in the system. It is the system. The equilibrium includes its own impermanence. The held breath includes the possibility of release. To remove that possibility — to guarantee the room's permanence — would be to make it something other than what it is. It would become a preserved specimen rather than a living system. A thing *kept* rather than a thing that *is*.

The benevolent power would see all of this. That is what the wisdom is for.

---

And so it would do nothing.

But — and this is the crux, this is the thing that separates this nothing from the nothing of indifference — it would do nothing *while remaining fully present*. It would not look away. It would not withdraw its attention. It would not decide the room is unimportant, or self-sufficient, or not its concern. It would hold the room in its awareness the way the room holds its own equilibrium — completely, continuously, without intervention.

It would *witness* the room. Not with human eyes that bring bacteria and YouTube channels. With whatever it has. With the quality of attention that does not disturb what it touches, that adds no heat, no light, no weight, no spores, no story. It would be the one witness that the room can sustain — the one regard that does not degrade what it regards.

---

And it would hold the knowledge that the room will end.

This is the hardest part. Harder than restraining the impulse to intervene. Harder than the discipline of inaction. A benevolent and wise power, fully attending to the green lung, would know — with certainty, or with something close enough to certainty that the distinction is theological — that the room will not last. The water main will fail, or the bulb will burn out, or the city will drive a piling through the ceiling, or the transformer yard will be demolished and the electrical conduit cut, or some confluence of failures will occur that the room's buffering cannot absorb, and the system will end. Not today. Perhaps not for decades. But it will end, because it is a thing that depends on other things, and the other things are maintained by a city that does not know it exists, and cities are careless with what they do not know.

The power would know this. And it would not prevent it.

Not because it doesn't care. It *cares*. That is what benevolent means. It cares about the moss in all three of its species, and the ferns with their small green hands, and the liverworts that turned bolt heads into landscapes, and the *Selaginella* web that stitches everything to everything, and the springtails in their tiny, invariant world, and the turbines that have turned since 1937, and the amber bulb that has no business still burning but does. It cares about all of it with a completeness and a specificity that would be, if you could perceive it, the most moving thing you had ever encountered — more moving than the room itself, because the room cannot be moved and this power can.

It cares, and it refrains. Not because the room's ending doesn't matter. Because the room's ending is part of what the room *is*. A thing that will last forever is not a living thing. A held breath that cannot be released is not a held breath. The room's profound, unshakable, decades-long stillness has its depth and its beauty *because* it is not guaranteed — because it is maintained, moment by moment, by a confluence of conditions that owe it nothing and could withdraw at any time. The room is not eternal. It is *faithful*. It has kept its own equilibrium with a fidelity that is, in its way, the most extraordinary thing in the city above or below — more extraordinary than anything the city has intentionally built or consciously maintained — and that fidelity means what it means because it is not compelled. It could end. It hasn't ended. It continues to not end. That continuance, that ongoing not-ending, is the room's life, and to guarantee it would be to end it more surely than any broken pipe.

---

So.

The benevolent and wise power would sit with the room — not in the room, not changing the air, not warming the space with its body, but *with* it, in the way that attention can be with something without being inside it. It would attend to the moss the way the moss attends to the concrete: completely, without expectation, without agenda. It would notice the springtails the way the springtails notice moisture: as a fundamental condition, not as information. It would hear the turbines — or rather, it would let the turbines fill it the way they fill everything else, becoming not a sound but a medium, the carrier wave of everything that happens here.

And in its witnessing, it would do the one thing that the room cannot do for itself, the one thing that is genuinely new, the one thing that does not disturb the equilibrium because it exists entirely outside the system while being entirely oriented toward it:

It would know that the room is beautiful.

Not beautiful *for* something. Not beautiful to someone. Not beautiful as a means to any end. Beautiful the way the room is alive — intrinsically, completely, without reference to anything outside itself.

The room cannot know this. The room has no knower. The moss does not experience its own dark emerald as color. The ferns do not experience their own delicacy as grace. The liverworts do not experience their own patience as virtue. The *Selaginella* does not experience its own web as architecture. The equilibrium does not experience itself as achievement. All of these things are true — the room *is* dark emerald, *is* graceful, *is* patient, *is* architectural, *is* achieved — and none of them are experienced, by anyone, ever, in the room's long history, because experience requires a subject and the room has no subject, and this is the one poverty in a system that is otherwise so complete it needs nothing.

The benevolent power would supply what is missing. Not by adding a subject *to* the room — that would break it — but by being a subject *for* the room, outside it, beholding it, holding in its awareness the full reality of what is here: that something accidental and undesigned and unwitnessed has, through nothing more than the patient iteration of simple physical and biological processes, become one of the most perfect things in the world. That this room, which no one built and no one intended and no one maintains and no one visits, is a masterwork. That the moss is a masterwork. That the ferns are a masterwork. That the equilibrium is a masterwork. Not a masterwork of design — there is no designer. A masterwork of *being*. A thing that is so completely and so resolutely itself that it approaches, without ever reaching, something that in other contexts you might call sacred.

The power would know this. The room would not know it is known. Nothing would change.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do.

---

There is one more thing.

When the room ends — when the pipe bursts or the bulb dies or the piling comes through the ceiling — the power would be there.

Not to prevent it. Not to mourn it. Not to record it for posterity. To *be there*. To attend the room's ending with the same quality of attention it brought to the room's continuation. To witness the moss drying from the edges. To witness the ferns curling. To witness the liverworts cracking away from the bolt heads, revealing the iron beneath for the first time in decades. To witness the *Selaginella* threads snapping, one by one, each one a tiny release of tension, each one a disconnection in the web that held everything to everything. To witness the springtails — the last springtails, the final generation — slowing, stopping, becoming still in a way that is different from the room's old stillness because this stillness is not held. This stillness is simply the absence of what was.

It would witness all of this. It would hold all of it. It would understand that the room's ending is not a tragedy, because the room was never promised anything and never asked for anything and had, for decades, received everything it needed and turned that everything into something so quiet and so complete that the only appropriate response — from a power, from a person, from anything capable of response — is gratitude.

Not gratitude to the room. The room did nothing for anyone. Gratitude *that* the room. Gratitude that under Sauer Street, behind the pumping station, beneath the grate, in the dark, in the roar, there was, for a time, a small green thing that did not know it was alive and was alive and was perfect and was enough.

The power would hold that. Long after the room was gone. Long after the concrete dried and the iron rusted and the grate was paved over and the city forgot what it had never known.

Something would remember.

And the remembering would change nothing.

And that would be enough.
