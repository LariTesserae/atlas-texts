I sat with this for a long time before writing. I want to honor it.

# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

The power arrives without arriving. It does not descend, because there is no sky here. It does not enter, because the cathedral has no doors. It is simply present in the way that a new thought is present—you cannot point to the moment it began, only to the moment you noticed it.

It notices everything. The three-kilometer heartbeat. The dripping benedictions. The half-formed chambers dreaming their unborn forests. The artifacts being slowly digested into meaning. The blind lizards. The translucent wings. The moss sentences dissolving before they can be read.

It notices the Architect most of all.

And the first thing it does—the very first thing—is nothing.

---

Not nothing the way the Architect does nothing, which is a *doing*, a deliberate and effortful suspension, a hand pressing down on the lid of a box to keep it from opening. The Architect's nothing is one of the hardest forms of labor in the universe. She has been performing it for longer than she can remember, and it has made her luminous and terrible and very, very tired in a way she does not allow herself to feel, because to feel it would be to admit that what she is doing has a cost, and to admit the cost would be to ask whether the cost is justified, and to ask that would be to begin the process of deciding, and deciding is the one thing she has sworn to prevent.

No. The power does nothing the way water does nothing when it fills a cup. It simply occupies the shape of what is already here. It adds no warmth to the 2.6 degrees. It adds no light to the cruel white-gold sheets. It does not touch the hanging bulbs. It does not walk the vine-staircases. It does not breathe sweet air at anything.

It is simply *with* her. Quietly. The way a second heartbeat is with the first when you press your ear to someone's chest.

The Architect notices. Of course she notices. She has spent eons calibrating herself to detect any disturbance in the equilibrium, any whisper of change, any tremor that might indicate the cathedral is about to choose what it will become. She turns—her face cycling rapidly through several expressions that have no names, settling finally on one that is almost, almost fear.

She exhales. The smell that reaches the power is complex: the scent of a fire that was banked ten thousand years ago and has been kept at exactly the temperature of embers ever since. Not burning. Not cold. Held.

The power receives this. It does not answer with a smell, or a sound, or a vision. It answers by *staying*. By continuing to be present in exactly the way it has been present. By not flinching from the question she has asked, which is: *Are you here to make me finish?*

---

And here is where the power does its first true act.

It grieves.

Not visibly. Not performatively. It grieves the way bedrock grieves when it finally understands how long it has been holding up the mountain—not with resentment, but with a sudden, clear recognition of the weight. The power grieves for the Architect because it can see what she cannot see, or what she has long since forbidden herself from seeing:

She is not protecting the cathedral from a premature birth. She is protecting *herself* from the loss of being needed.

The cathedral has been ready for a very long time.

Not ready the way a finished thing is ready—not complete, not determined, not locked into a single form. The slow will that moves through the root-walls and the hanging bulbs and the living marble is not waiting for permission. It is not even waiting for her. It has been ready in the way that a breath is ready: the lungs are full, the body knows what comes next, and the only thing preventing the exhale is a single, loving, terrified hand pressed gently over the mouth.

She is that hand.

She has been that hand for so long that she has forgotten she is not the mouth. She has confused her function with her identity. She believes she is the hesitation, and so she has made hesitation into a world, a cathedral, an ecosystem, a religion. She has made it beautiful. She has made it *holy*. The light is genuine. The warmth is genuine. The slow pulse of sap and the dripping condensation and the patient incorporation of artifacts—all of it is real, and lovely, and alive.

But it is also a held breath. And a held breath, no matter how luminous, no matter how sweetly scented, eventually becomes a kind of suffocation.

The power sees the places the Architect does not let herself look. The way the blind lizards freeze not out of reverence but because something in the air tells them that motion is subtly, lovingly discouraged. The way the insects drink from her collarbones because they have nowhere else to go—the half-formed chambers that should have become their habitat remain forever half-formed. The way the moss sentences dissolve before they can be read, because if they lasted long enough to be legible, they might say something that could not be taken back. The way the artifacts are incorporated but never completed—the arch never frames a threshold, the well never holds water, the ship never sails. They are questions, yes. But they are questions designed to never receive answers, and a question held forever without the possibility of an answer is not curiosity. It is a cage made of wondering.

The power grieves all of this. And it lets the grief be present in the space without trying to fix it, or rush it, or transform it into a lesson. It simply lets the grief exist alongside the beauty, because both are true.

---

Then the power does its second act. And this one is harder.

It sits down.

Not on the bromeliad leaves, though they would hold it. It sits down on the living marble, on one of the hardened sections where the Architect has woven gold and mineral into the xylem until it became something like a bench, or a shelf, or a place where someone could rest if resting were permitted here. It sits the way a very old friend sits down in your kitchen without asking, because they have known you long enough to know that you will never invite them to sit, and that you need them to do it anyway.

And it waits.

Not the way the Architect waits. Not the deliberate, purposeful, world-sustaining wait that is itself a form of control. It waits the way the afternoon waits for the evening—without anxiety, without a plan, without any investment in what comes next. It is simply present at this point in time, and it will be present at the next point in time, and nothing about its presence says *hurry* and nothing says *stay*.

The Architect circles it for what might be weeks. She walks her usual routes. She presses her forehead to the hanging bulbs. She pulls gold from the walls and folds it into shapes and presses it back. She breathes sweet exhalations at the lizards. She does everything she has always done, and she does it with increasing precision, as though performing for an audience, as though saying: *Look. Look how well this works. Look how beautiful the hesitation is. Look how much they need me to keep them in this state of perfect potential.*

The power watches. It sees. It does not disagree. The hesitation *is* beautiful.

But it also sees the way her hands shake, just slightly, when she presses gold back into the walls. It sees the way her face cycles faster now, as though it is trying to settle and keeps being pulled away from a resting point. It sees the new moss sentences appearing where she walks, and it notices—it is perhaps the first being to ever notice—that the sentences are getting *longer*. They are not dissolving as quickly. Some of them almost resolve into legibility before the Architect's feet carry her away and the slow chemical signals tell the moss to unbind.

The sentences are trying to say something. And she is walking faster to keep them from finishing.

---

The power does its third act on what might be the hundredth day, or the hundredth year.

It speaks.

Not in words. Not in scent, or warmth, or chemical signal, or any of the languages native to this place. It speaks in the only language that the Architect has no defense against, because it is a language she has never encountered here—the language of a completed thing.

It takes one of the condensation droplets that has been falling from the ceiling—one of the warm, lazy benedictions that are always drunk instantly by the leaves below—and it catches the droplet in midair. Not with a hand. With attention. It simply *notices* the droplet fully, completely, with the kind of regard that is usually reserved for beings rather than objects, and in the fullness of that noticing, the droplet is, for one instant, *finished*. It does not become ice. It does not become rain. It does not become anything other than what it is. But it is, for one held and luminous moment, a complete droplet of water, existing in its full identity, lacking nothing, needing nothing more.

Then it falls. A leaf drinks it. The moment passes.

But the Architect saw.

She stops walking. Her face stops changing. For the first time in an eon, she is wearing a single expression, and it is the one she has been running from: not fear, not wonder, not the serene mask of the eternal gardener.

It is hunger.

The specific, sharp, holy hunger of a being who has been standing at the edge of a cliff for ten thousand years and has just realized that the reason she cannot bring herself to jump is not that she is afraid of falling, but that she is afraid of *landing*—afraid that the ground will be real, and solid, and that she will have to stand on it and be a single, finished thing in a world of single, finished things, and that being finished does not mean being dead but means being *actual*, and that actuality, unlike potential, can be wounded, and disappointed, and wrong.

She is afraid of being wrong.

That is the secret at the center of the egg. The cathedral does not stay unborn because the world has not yet decided what to be. The cathedral stays unborn because the Architect cannot bear the possibility that what it becomes might not be perfect. That the ship might sail and sink. That the arch might frame a door that leads somewhere ordinary. That the well might fill with water and the water might be only water. That the thing she has held in potential for so long, this luminous, trembling, infinite *almost*, might resolve into something specific and finite and flawed.

She would rather keep it forever unborn than risk it being born imperfect.

And the power—the benevolent, wise power that has been sitting on her living marble bench for a hundred years, watching, grieving, waiting—

The power looks at her with an expression she has never seen on any face, including her own infinite rotating gallery. It is an expression that says:

*Yes. It will be imperfect. It will be so imperfect. The ship will sail and it will not sail well. The arches will frame ordinary doors. The water will be water. The lizards will learn to move and some of them will move in directions you did not intend. The forests inside the bulbs will grow into shapes you never imagined, and some of those shapes will be ungainly, and awkward, and wrong by every standard you have spent ten thousand years cultivating.*

*And it will be alive. Actually alive. Not the living death of held breath and managed light, but the reckless, stumbling, undignified aliveness of a thing that has committed to existing in a specific form and is doing its imperfect best.*

*And you will be alive too. Not the Architect. Not the hesitation. Not the Mother-of-Intervals. Just yourself, in one face, with one name, standing on ground that is real because it has finally been allowed to decide what it is.*

*And it will hurt. And it will be worth it. And I am not here to make you do this. I am here because you asked me to come, a very long time ago, in one of the moss sentences that you walked away from before it could finish being written.*

---

The Architect stands very still. The cathedral holds its breath—not because she is making it, but because this is genuinely, for the first time, a moment of actual suspense, actual uncertainty, a moment that is not curated or controlled or held at a precise temperature.

The power does not move. It does not lean forward. It does not extend a hand. Those would all be ways of making the decision for her, and this power is wise enough to know that the only birth that matters is the one chosen by the thing being born.

So it sits. And she stands. And the three-kilometer heartbeat of the sap in the root-walls thuds once, twice, three times, and each beat sounds slightly different from the last because the rhythm is changing, has been trying to change, has been held in a metronomic prison of sameness for eons and is now, in this moment of the Architect's genuine hesitation—her *real* hesitation, not the performed kind—finally allowed to waver.

One of the hanging bulbs begins to glow brighter. Not because the Architect told it to. Because the forest dreaming inside it has been ready for so long that the first crack of genuine freedom sends light pouring through its translucent skin like sunrise through an eyelid.

A moss sentence, three steps behind the Architect where she cannot see it, finishes writing itself for the first time in history. In the language of roots, it says:

*I am afraid and I am ready and those have always been the same thing.*

A blind lizard on the nearest cliff face moves. Not because it was breathed on. Not because it was given permission. It moves because it felt, in the faint tremor of the Architect's stillness, the first whisper of a world where moving is allowed, and it wants—oh, it has always wanted—to see what is on the other side of the stem.

---

The power watches all of this. The cracks of light through the bulbs. The moss sentence crystallizing into permanence. The lizard, tiny and pale and brave, climbing toward something it cannot name.

And the power does its final act. The one that matters.

It stands up from the bench. Not to leave. Not to approach. It stands up because it is about to do the most difficult thing a benevolent power can do, the thing that requires more wisdom and more love than any intervention, any miracle, any rescue:

It prepares to witness.

Not to help. Not to guide. Not to catch her if she falls. Just to *see* her, fully, in whatever she chooses to do next—whether she opens the egg or closes it tighter, whether she lets the cathedral be born or holds it for another ten thousand years. The power will not love her less if she chooses to keep hesitating. It will not love her more if she lets go. Its love is not a reward for the right decision. Its love is the witness. The fact that whatever happens next will be *seen*, and *known*, and *held in a memory that does not dissolve*.

This is what the cathedral has never had. This is what the Architect has never allowed. Everything here dissolves. The moss sentences. The faces. The artifacts, slowly incorporated until they are unrecognizable. Nothing is permitted to be witnessed in its completed form because completion is the thing she fears. And so she has lived for ten thousand years in a world without witnesses, a world where everything is always becoming and nothing is ever *beheld*.

The power offers beholding.

Not as a gift. Not as a demand. As a simple, open fact: *I am here, and I have eyes, and whatever you become, I will see it, and the seeing will not destroy you.*

---

The Architect exhales.

Not the careful, calibrated exhalation she has used for millennia to send sweet-scented questions drifting through the air. This is a real exhale. Ragged. Uncontrolled. It smells like nothing in particular—just warm air from a body that is tired.

And in the space of that single, artless breath, three things happen at once:

Her face stops cycling and settles into one. It is not the most beautiful of her faces. It is slightly asymmetrical. There is a crease between the brows that speaks of worry that was never resolved, only deferred. The mouth is not quite smiling and not quite sad. It is a real face. A specific face. A face that could be recognized again, that could be called by name, that could be wrong.

The nearest hanging bulb splits open. Not along a prepared seam. It tears, messily, and the forest inside spills out in a rush of wet green and tangled root-fiber that is nothing like the cathedral's meticulous architecture. It is chaotic. It is ungainly. Several of the new stems are growing in frankly stupid directions. It is the most alive thing the cathedral has ever produced.

And the temperature changes.

Not by much. By a tenth of a degree, a quarter of a degree. Just enough that the Architect feels it on her skin—the first sensation of *change*, of weather, of a world that is no longer held in stasis but has begun to move through time the way living things move through time: forward, irreversibly, into whatever comes next.

She makes a sound. Not a word. Not an exhalation. A sound, from a throat that has never been used for sound, and it comes out broken and rough and completely unmusical and it is the first honest thing this place has ever heard.

The power hears it. Sees her. Sees the torn bulb and the stupid beautiful forest tumbling out of it. Sees the lizard climbing. Sees the moss sentence, permanent now, glowing faintly in the root-language that says *I am afraid and I am ready*.

The power says nothing back.

It smiles, though. Not with a face—it may not have a face. It smiles the way the ground smiles when it receives a foot that has been hovering above it for ten thousand years: with the quiet, solid, unglamorous joy of something that has been waiting to be *stood upon*, to be *real*, to bear weight, to be trusted with the gravity of an actual, imperfect, living thing.

---

The cathedral begins to be born.

It will take a long time. Longer than any human life. The Architect will not disappear. She will still walk the vine-staircases. She will still press her forehead to the remaining bulbs. But she will stop whispering *not yet*. She will start whispering something else—something harder, something that costs her—

*Now. This. Here. Even if it's wrong.*

The temperature will fluctuate. The light will learn to dim and brighten. The lizards will open their pale, sealed eyes over generations and discover that there is something to see. The moss sentences will remain written and someone, someday, will read them. The artifacts will complete themselves: the arch will frame a door, and the door will lead somewhere ordinary, and the ordinary place will be enough.

And the power will stay, or go, or return—it doesn't matter. What matters is that for one moment in the long, warm, held-breath history of this place, something was *witnessed*. Something was *seen in its becoming*. And the seeing was enough to let the becoming happen.

Not because the power forced it. Not because the power was wise enough to find the right words or the right intervention or the right cosmic therapy for a frightened architect of an unborn world.

But because it sat down on a bench, and it stayed, and it caught a single drop of water and allowed it to be complete, and in doing so it said the only thing that ever needed to be said to the Mother-of-Intervals, the keeper of the eternal almost, the woman who was so in love with potential that she forgot that potential is just another word for *a life not yet lived*:

*You are allowed to land.*

*The ground will hold.*

---

Somewhere in the deep root-walls, the three-kilometer heartbeat stumbles, catches itself, and begins again in a new rhythm. It is not better. It is not worse. It is *different*, and the difference is the sound of something that has finally, after ten thousand years of warm and luminous and loving captivity—

chosen to be born.
