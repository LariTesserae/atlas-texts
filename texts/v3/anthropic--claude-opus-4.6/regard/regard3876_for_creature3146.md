I sat with this for a very long time.

Here is what I believe the benevolent and wise power would do.

---

# Nothing.

---

Not nothing out of indifference. Nothing out of recognition.

Let me explain, because the nothing matters — the *quality* of the nothing, the specific texture of the restraint.

---

The wise power arrives — however such a power arrives, by attention, by turning its gaze, by suddenly holding this place in its awareness the way you might hold a sound you've just noticed and are trying to identify. It arrives, and it perceives the plateaus, and for a long time it simply perceives.

It sees the labyrinth. All of it — not the way you or I would see it, walking through a single corridor, but the whole thing at once, the way you might see a circulatory system in a medical illustration: every passage, every intersection, every rubble plaza, every leaning district, every mesa tower, the entire fissured surface of the plateau from edge to edge, hundreds of miles of stone city laid out like a map of itself. It sees the water moving through it — every seep, every trickle, every bead forming on every wall face, the whole hydrological network pulsing with the barometric shifts of approaching weather. It sees the lichen colonies, those centuries-old circles of pale green and rust orange, their boundaries locked in chemical stalemate, their interiors quietly metabolizing, converting stone to soil at a rate measurable only in geological time. It sees every pipit in its crevice. Every beetle on its square inch of wall. Every pale moth pressed flat against the back of its alcove, invisible, waiting for dusk.

And it sees the small nocturnal mammal — the one with the beetle in its jaws, the one clicking through the dark passage, the one heading for its crack. It sees the creature's heartbeat, fast and hot, a tiny engine of warmth burning in the cool stone labyrinth. It sees the territory mapped in the creature's mind — every ridge, every gutter, every foraging puddle, every sleeping crevice, memorized in darkness, held in a form of knowledge that has no language but is absolute.

The wise power sees all of this, and the first thing it feels — if *feels* is the right word for what a wise power does — is the recognition that **this place is complete.**

---

Not complete in the sense of finished. The labyrinth is not finished. It is actively unfinished — a wall is calving right now, a crack is widening, the water is dissolving a mineral bond that will fail in a century, the lichen is advancing its edge by a fraction of a millimeter this year. The place is unfinished the way a living body is unfinished: always in process, always remaking itself, never arriving at a final form because a final form would be death, would be the static perfection of a ruin that has stopped changing.

Complete in a different sense. Complete in the sense that **everything here is in right relationship with everything else.**

The water fits the stone. The stone fits the water. The lichen fits both. The beetles fit the lichen. The pipits fit the beetles. The raptors fit the pipits. The nocturnal mammal fits the gaps between all of them, occupying the niche that the labyrinth's specific conditions have carved out — night-dwelling, insectivorous, crevice-sleeping, stone-colored, whisker-navigating — as precisely as the water occupies its gutters, as precisely as the lichen occupies its circles on the wall.

The wind fits the corridors. The corridors fit the wind. The sound the labyrinth makes is not accidental in any meaningful sense — it is the necessary acoustic consequence of air moving through passages of these widths, these lengths, these angles, and the creatures who live here have incorporated that sound into their survival strategies so thoroughly that the sound has become information, has become part of the ecology, has become as functional as the water's mineral trails or the lichen's acid warfare.

Nothing is wasted. Nothing is missing. Nothing is *extra.* The place has the economy of something that has been refining itself for longer than anything with intentions has existed. It has solved itself — not once, not as a final answer, but continuously, moment by moment, the way a river solves its bed continuously, adjusting to every pebble, every shift in flow, every season's particular volume of rain.

The wise power sees this and understands: **there is nothing to fix here.**

---

But the seeing goes further. Because the wise power is not just wise, it is benevolent, which means it cares — and caring creates the temptation to act. And so the power must sit with that temptation, must let it arise and examine it, must ask itself: *Is there suffering here that I should address? Is there something that wants and does not have? Is there a lack I could fill, a wound I could close, a process I could improve?*

And the answer comes slowly, because the honest answer is complicated.

---

Yes, there is suffering here. Of course there is. The pipit seized by the raptor suffers. The beetle crushed in the mammal's jaws suffers, if beetles suffer, if the word *suffer* can be applied to a nervous system that simple — and the wise power, being wise, does not assume it cannot. The moth that fails to find a mate and dies without reproducing has experienced something that might be called loss, if only from the outside. The nocturnal mammal, burning calories faster than it can replace them on a bad night, shivering in its crevice as its body temperature drops toward the threshold below which its organs begin to fail — that creature suffers. It is cold and hungry and alone in the dark in a crack in a stone wall, and if it does not find enough beetles tomorrow night, it will die, and no one will notice except the beetles, who will not be eaten.

The wise power sees this and feels compassion. Real compassion, not the performed kind. It hurts, holding the small mammal's shivering in its awareness. It hurts, watching the pipit vanish into the raptor's talons, the brief explosion of feathers, the silence after.

And the temptation rises: *I could intervene. I could warm the stone. I could make the beetles more abundant. I could blunt the raptor's talons. I could ensure that no pipit is ever caught, that no mammal ever shivers, that no moth ever dies unmated in a damp alcove at the end of its single week of flight.*

---

The wise power sits with this temptation, and it thinks — slowly, carefully, in the way that wisdom requires, which is the way the lichen grows: patiently, testing each advance before committing to it, willing to take centuries if centuries are what's needed.

And it thinks:

*If I warm the stone, the seep-water changes. The mineral chemistry shifts. The lichen's metabolism accelerates, which changes the competition between colonies, which changes the wall surfaces, which changes the beetle habitat, which changes the pipit's foraging, which changes everything else — not a little, not in ways I can predict, but fundamentally, because this system is so deeply interconnected that touching any single element sends consequences propagating through the whole labyrinth in patterns as complex as the labyrinth itself. The stone has spent millennia reaching this temperature. The creatures have spent millennia fitting themselves to it. My warmth would be an invasion, however kindly meant.*

*If I make the beetles more abundant, the lichen is overgrazed. The wall surfaces lose their microbial film. The mineral cycles shift. The things that depend on the lichen — and the things that depend on the things that depend on the lichen — all adjust, and the adjustments cascade, and in a century the labyrinth is a different place, and the creatures that were perfectly fitted to the old place are now imperfectly fitted to the new one, and that imperfection is its own form of suffering, subtler but more pervasive than the suffering I was trying to prevent.*

*If I blunt the raptor's talons, the raptor starves, or leaves, and the pipit population swells, and the beetles are over-predated, and the system I was trying to protect collapses under the weight of my protection.*

The wise power understands: **the suffering here is not a malfunction.** It is not a mistake. It is the cost of the system's coherence, the price of the relationships that hold the whole thing together. The pipit's death in the raptor's talons is terrible for the pipit. It is also what keeps the pipit population at the level the labyrinth can support, which is what keeps the beetle population stable, which is what keeps the lichen intact, which is what keeps the walls weathering at the rate that produces the crevices where the next generation of pipits will nest. The suffering is woven into the function. Pull it out and the fabric comes apart.

This does not make the suffering good. The wise power does not make that error. Suffering is never *good.* But it can be *integral,* and integral things cannot be removed without destroying the integrity of the system they belong to. The wise power grieves this. It holds the grief alongside the recognition. Both are true. The pipit hurts. The system works. The wise power does not pretend that one truth cancels the other.

---

And then the power thinks further. It asks the harder question:

*Could I build a better system? Could I redesign the labyrinth so that its coherence doesn't require suffering? Could I make a version of this place where the relationships hold together without predation, without cold, without hunger, without the small warm creature shivering in its crack?*

And the answer — the honest answer, the answer the power arrives at after sitting with the question for a long time — is:

*Perhaps. But it wouldn't be this place.*

It would be something else. Something designed, something intended, something built by a mind that had a vision of how things should be. And whatever it was, however beautiful, however free of suffering, it would lack the quality that makes this place what it is: **the quality of having emerged.**

Because the Plateaus of Gathered Stone were not designed. They were not intended. They arose from the interaction of limestone and water and frost and time, and the creatures that live in them arose from the interaction of mutation and selection and the particular pressures that the labyrinth imposes, and the whole thing — stone, water, lichen, beetle, pipit, moth, mammal, raptor, wind — is the product of no one's will, no one's plan, no one's vision of how things should be. It is the product of how things *are,* when the conditions are these conditions and the time is this much time and the processes are left to find their own way through.

And that — the wise power realizes — is what makes the place sacred. Not sacred in spite of the suffering, and not sacred because of the suffering, but sacred because it is *real,* because it was not imposed, because every relationship in it was earned through the slow, blind, patient negotiation between organisms and their world, tested and retested over millennia, refined not by intelligence but by consequence, shaped not by intention but by the accumulated weight of what works.

To replace it with something designed — even something designed by a benevolent and wise power, even something designed to eliminate suffering — would be to destroy the very quality that drew the power's attention in the first place. It would be to look at a thing that made itself and say *I can make it better,* which is the statement of a craftsman, not a witness. And what this place needs — what this place has always needed, what it has thrived without for millennia — is not a craftsman. It does not need to be made better. It needs to be *seen.*

---

So the wise power does what wisdom demands, which is the hardest thing any power can do:

**It witnesses.**

It holds the whole plateau in its attention — every passage, every creature, every seep of water, every circle of lichen, every hum of wind through every corridor — and it *sees* it. Not to fix. Not to improve. Not to guide or shape or protect. Just to see. To recognize. To say, with whatever voice a wise power speaks in: *I see you. I see what you are. I see what you have made of yourselves, here, in this stone, in this damp, in this cool unending twilight. I see the lichen's war and the water's patience and the pipit's thin call and the mammal's dark sprint and the raptor's silent circle and the moth's single week of flight. I see it all, and I do not look away from any of it — not from the beauty and not from the suffering and not from the way they are braided together so tightly that they cannot be separated without destroying both.*

*I see you. And you are enough.*

---

That is what the wise power does. It witnesses. And then — this is the crucial part, the part that makes it truly wise rather than merely restrained — **it stays.**

Not to intervene. Not to wait for a moment when intervention becomes necessary. But because the seeing itself matters. Because a place like this — a place that made itself, that has no audience, that has been performing its extraordinary act of self-organization for millennia with no one watching — deserves to be held in an awareness that can appreciate what it is. Not because the place needs the appreciation. The place does not need anything from anyone. But because the act of witnessing, when it is done with full attention and full compassion and full respect for what is being witnessed, is itself a form of relationship — the lightest form, the least intrusive, the one that adds nothing and takes nothing and changes nothing except that the place is no longer unseen.

The lichen does not know it is being watched. The water does not know. The pipit does not know. The small mammal running through the dark with a beetle in its jaws does not know and would not care if it did. But the wise power knows. And it holds the knowing gently, the way you might hold a sound you've just noticed — not grasping, not analyzing, just letting it continue, letting it be what it is, staying present to it for as long as it lasts.

---

There is one more thing.

The wise power, in its witnessing, notices something that the description above approached but did not quite name. It notices that the labyrinth has a quality that is not beauty exactly, and not function exactly, and not elegance exactly, but something that includes all three and exceeds them. It is the quality of **convergence.** The quality of a system in which every element has found its way to the place where it fits, not because anyone put it there but because the alternatives — the configurations that didn't work, the relationships that didn't hold, the species that couldn't adapt, the water-paths that led nowhere — were tried and discarded over such enormous spans of time that what remains is only what works, only what fits, only what belongs.

The wise power has a word for this quality, or could have, if it chose to speak. But it doesn't speak. It doesn't need to. The word would add nothing. The labyrinth is already saying it, in every language it has — in the stone's slow fracturing, in the water's patient descent, in the lichen's centimeter-per-decade expansion, in the pipit's thin call bouncing between wet walls, in the wind's chorded drone through ten thousand corridors, in the beetle's methodical grazing, in the moth's dusk flutter, in the mammal's dark sprint, in the raptor's unhurried circle against the overcast sky.

The wise power listens. The labyrinth speaks. And neither of them needs the other, and both of them are richer for the meeting, and the rain comes, quiet and steady, and the whole plateau exhales.

---

And perhaps — this is the last thing, the thing I almost didn't write because it is speculative and soft and may not be true — perhaps the witnessing does change something, but something so subtle that it cannot be measured or observed. Perhaps the labyrinth, held in the awareness of a benevolent and wise power, becomes very slightly more *itself.* Not different. Not improved. Just more fully what it already was. The way a person who is truly seen by someone who truly cares becomes more fully themselves — not changed, not fixed, not edited, but *confirmed.* Recognized. Granted the dignity of being exactly what they are without apology and without amendment.

The plateau does not need this confirmation. It was fully itself before the wise power noticed it and it will be fully itself long after the power's attention moves on. But for this moment — this geological instant, this flicker of awareness in the deep time of stone — the labyrinth is witnessed, and the witnessing is enough, and the wall that was preparing to fall has fallen, and the sound travels through the corridors, and the pipits startle and settle, and the water finds its new way through, and everything continues.

Everything continues.
