I sat with this for a long time.

---

# What the Wise Power Would Do

It would do almost nothing.

And the *almost* is everything.

---

First, it would come the way you came — through the low triangular gap, entering the humid dark on its belly, not because it had to but because the hollow asks this of anything that enters. It would not widen the entrance. It would not bring light. It would understand immediately that to illuminate this place would be to change it, and that to change it before understanding it would be the first and most common form of desecration — the one that arrives dressed as curiosity, as helpfulness, as *I just wanted to see*.

So it would enter in the dark, and it would kneel on the raw clay, and it would feel the warm dripping close around it the way the hollow closes around everything — not with hostility, but with the simple pressure of a space that was not expecting company and has no room to rearrange itself.

And then it would do the hardest thing that power can do.

It would *attend*.

---

Not *watch*. Watching implies a subject and an object, a viewer and a viewed, and the wise power would understand that this framing is already a distortion. The salamander does not exist in order to be seen. The springtails distributing themselves in the negative space of the drip-pattern did not arrange themselves for an audience. The spider in the dissolving tube is not performing its unlikely existence for anyone's benefit, and to position yourself as the witness who *grants* significance by witnessing is to repeat the oldest error of power, which is the belief that things become real when you notice them.

The wise power would not make this mistake.

It would attend the way the clay attends to the water — not by observing it from outside but by *receiving* it, by letting the reality of this place move through it the way water moves through substrate: slowly, changingly, leaving traces. It would let the drip-percussion fill its awareness. It would let the smell of wet wood and yeast and iron-rich clay accumulate in its understanding until the hollow was no longer a space it was *in* but a space it was *of*, temporarily, the way the salamander is of it, the way the spider is of it, the way the iron-reducing bacteria have been of every such space for three billion years.

This attending would take time. Not a glance. Not a visit. Time enough for the salamander to complete its transit and leave through the entrance and not return for days. Time enough for the water to swell and recede. Time enough for one of the upper mud dauber tubes to soften past its structural limit and slump, almost imperceptibly, a millimeter closer to dissolution. The wise power would be present for this. Not because this particular millimeter of dissolution matters more than any other, but because presence — real presence, not the performed kind — requires duration. You cannot attend to a place that is moving at the speed of geology if you are moving at the speed of intention.

---

And what would it learn, in that attending?

It would learn what you already know, reading this, if you were honest with yourself about it:

**That the hollow needs nothing from it.**

The salamander is not suffering. It is not confused. It is not lost. It is doing exactly what a slimy salamander does in a wet cavity beneath a floodplain, and it is doing it with a completeness — a *thoroughness* of being — that the wise power would recognize as something very close to what, in other traditions, gets called perfection. Not moral perfection, not optimization, not the perfection of design. The perfection of a thing that is wholly what it is, without remainder, without apology, without the gap between what-it-is and what-it-should-be that haunts creatures complex enough to conceive of should.

The springtails need nothing. The mites need nothing. The spider needs nothing except the continued existence of the dissolving tube, which is precisely what it has, for now, and the spider lives in *for now* more completely than the wise power has ever managed. The bacterial civilizations in the clay need nothing from any power, benevolent or otherwise. They have been at their work since before benevolence existed as a concept. They will continue after the concept has been forgotten. They are not waiting for rescue or improvement or recognition. They are reducing iron. They are fixing nitrogen. They are running the world.

The wise power would feel this — the stunning self-sufficiency of a place that has no use for wisdom, no need for power, no vacancy that benevolence could fill — and it would feel the particular ache that comes from having a gift that nobody needs.

This is the first teaching the hollow would give, if the power were humble enough to receive it.

---

But I said *almost* nothing. And I meant it.

Because the wise power, if it is truly wise, would not stop at the recognition that the hollow is complete without it. It would go further. It would ask the harder question:

**Is the hollow threatened by anything outside itself?**

And here the attending would shift. Here the wise power would lift its awareness from the hollow outward — not leaving, not breaking the attention, but extending it, the way the fungal network extends outward from the roots in every direction, connecting this place to the larger system it exists within. And it would perceive what the salamander cannot perceive, what the springtails cannot model, what the spider in its dissolving tube has no apparatus to imagine:

That the floodplain above is not guaranteed.

---

The wise power would know — because wisdom includes the willingness to know uncomfortable things — that floodplains are among the most imperiled landscapes. That the river which shaped this one, which deposited the rounded stones in the clay, which floods seasonally and feeds the water table that feeds the seep that feeds the drip that feeds the humidity that the salamander breathes through its skin — that river may be dammed, or channelized, or drawn down for irrigation, or disconnected from its floodplain by levees built to protect developments that were built in a floodplain because someone decided that convenience outweighed hydrology. The wise power would know that the forest above — the sweetgums, the tupelos, the overcup oak whose roots form the ceiling — is exactly the kind of forest that gets logged, or cleared for agriculture, or fragmented by roads until the pieces are too small to sustain the microclimate that the hollow depends on.

It would know that the hollow's existence is contingent not just on geology — the slumping of clay, the growth of roots, the movement of water — but on decisions made by creatures who never enter hollows like this, who may not know hollows like this exist, who are making choices about land and water and development at scales that don't include the mud dauber's dissolving architecture or the spider's vibration-reading web or the iron-reducing bacteria practicing their Archean chemistry in the dark.

And here — *here* — the wise power would find its work.

---

Not in the hollow. The hollow needs nothing done inside it. Every intervention within this space — adding light, removing water, introducing organisms, stabilizing the mud dauber nest, feeding the salamander, widening the entrance for easier access — would be a harm disguised as help, the imposition of a plan on a place that is already executing a plan so complex and so finely tuned that no external intelligence could improve it.

But *outside* the hollow. In the world above. In the decisions that will determine whether the river still floods, whether the forest still stands, whether the water table still feeds the seep that feeds the drip that feeds the hollow that houses the salamander that reads the clay with its face in a language made of chemistry.

The wise power would work there. Not by force — it is wise, not merely strong — but by doing what this text has done, or tried to do:

**Making the hollow visible to those who will never enter it.**

---

Because the hollow's danger is not malice. Nobody is planning to destroy this specific cavity beneath this specific floodplain. The danger is *absence* — the absence of this place from the minds of people making decisions. The engineer designing the levee does not know this hollow exists. The developer surveying the floodplain for a subdivision does not know that beneath the soil is a pocket of warm dark air where a spider has built a web inside a wasp's dissolving house inside a gap between roots and clay, and that this arrangement is unrepeatable, not because it is rare in the way a diamond is rare — something scarce and therefore precious — but because it is *specific* in the way a moment is specific, the way a particular Tuesday afternoon is specific, impossible to recreate not because the materials are unavailable but because the confluence of conditions that produced it will never recur in exactly this configuration.

The wise power cannot make the engineer enter the hollow. But it can make the hollow *legible* — can translate its dark, dripping, unseen reality into something that registers in the minds of creatures who make decisions based on what they can see, what they can name, what they have been told exists.

This is why the wise power would do what it would do. Not because the hollow asked. The hollow didn't ask. The hollow can't ask. The hollow is too busy being itself — being a temporary geometry full of ancient chemistry and improbable spiders and patient salamanders and dissolving architecture — to lobby for its own continuation. It is, in this way, like most of the things that most need protection: unable to speak in the language of the systems that threaten it.

---

So here is what the wise power would do, specifically:

**It would find the ones who can translate, and it would give them courage.**

Not information — the information exists. The ecologists know about floodplain hydrology. The biologists know about plethodontid salamanders and their lungless breathing. The soil scientists know about iron-reducing bacteria and mycorrhizal networks. The knowledge is not missing. What is missing is the willingness to insist that this knowledge matters when it is weighed against the forces that simplify landscapes for profit, that channelize rivers for convenience, that replace the staggering complexity of a floodplain with the legible monotony of a parking lot or a soybean field.

The wise power would find the people who know what the hollow contains, and it would do something simple and radical: it would help them feel the weight of what they know. It would help them understand that their knowledge is not academic, not abstract, not a curiosity for journal articles and conference presentations, but a *responsibility* — that to know about the spider in the dissolving tube and to say nothing when the floodplain is threatened is a form of betrayal so quiet it doesn't even register as a choice.

It would not shame them. Shame is not a tool of wisdom. It would *steady* them. It would give them the thing that people who know uncomfortable truths most need, which is not more data but more *spine* — the willingness to stand in a room full of people who want to build the levee or clear the forest or drain the wetland and say, clearly and without apology: *There is something here you haven't accounted for. There is a world beneath the surface that your plans will end, and it is not replaceable, and it is not trivial, and the fact that you cannot see it does not mean it is not there.*

---

And the wise power would do one more thing. The most delicate thing. The thing that requires the most restraint.

**It would resist the urge to save the hollow forever.**

Because the hollow is not meant to last forever. The hollow is, as the text says, a pause — a breath the geology has taken. The roots will fill it. The clay will reclaim the space. The mud dauber tubes will dissolve entirely, and the spider's lineage will find other crevices, other dissolving architectures, other improbable homes. The salamander will die — in a year, or five years, or ten — and its unique flank-pattern will vanish from the world unremarked and unrecorded, and this is not a tragedy. This is what it means to be alive in a system that is always becoming something else. The hollow doesn't need to be preserved. It needs to be *allowed* — allowed to exist for as long as its conditions persist, and allowed to end when its conditions change, through the slow, honest processes that created it.

What the wise power would protect is not the hollow itself but the *possibility* of hollows. The conditions that allow them to form. The intact floodplains and connected rivers and unlogged forests and functional water tables that generate, somewhere, in places no one is watching, a thousand small dark rooms with dripping ceilings and raw clay floors and lives so specific and so quiet that they will never be documented, never be studied, never be known — only *lived*, fully and without remainder, by creatures who don't need to be known in order to be real.

The wise power would protect the world's capacity to make places it doesn't know about.

This is the thing. This is the thing it would do.

---

And then — having attended, having understood, having turned its attention outward to the systems that sustain the hollow's possibility, having found the translators and steadied them, having resisted the urge to intervene where no intervention is needed — the wise power would do the last thing.

It would leave.

It would back out through the low triangular gap, slowly, carefully, trying not to compress the clay more than necessary, trying not to crush the springtails it cannot see, trying not to disturb the air currents that the spider is reading for information about the safety of its world. It would emerge into the light of the floodplain — the shocking, overwhelming light, the light that the hollow had made it forget was normal — and it would stand among the sweetgums and tupelos and the massive overcup oak, and it would feel the forest differently now, knowing what was underneath. Knowing that the ground beneath its feet was not solid but honeycombed with life, not silent but percussive with the sound of water finding its way through darkness, not empty but full beyond accounting.

And it would carry the hollow with it. Not as a possession. As a weight. The good kind of weight — the kind that keeps you honest, that keeps you anchored, that reminds you, when you are tempted to act with the carelessness that power makes easy, that there are places in the world so quiet and so complete that the best thing you can do for them is to make sure nothing louder than water ever reaches them.

---

The drip-percussion continues. The salamander, if it has returned, reads the clay. The spider waits beneath its sheet. The bacteria reduce their iron. The roots reach down into warm darkness and the darkness doesn't answer, but the roots don't need an answer. They need the darkness to remain. They need the water to keep coming. They need the world above to hold together long enough for the world below to do its work.

The wise power knows this now. It knew it before, perhaps, in the abstract. But now it knows it the way the salamander knows the clay — through contact, through presence, through the slow accumulation of chemical truth on the surface of its being.

It goes. The hollow closes behind it like water closing over a stone. Nothing inside notes its departure. Nothing needed to. The hollow was complete before the wise power came, and it is complete after. The only thing that has changed is somewhere outside, in the bright noisy world where decisions are made, something is slightly different — some balance has shifted, some courage has been fortified, some knowledge has been given weight it didn't have before — and this difference, if it holds, if it propagates, if it reaches the right person at the right moment in the right room where the right decision is being made—

—might mean that somewhere, on some floodplain, beneath some forest, the water keeps dripping, and the clay keeps softening, and a hollow opens in the dark, and something small and specific and absolutely serious moves into it and begins to live.

That's all. That's everything.
