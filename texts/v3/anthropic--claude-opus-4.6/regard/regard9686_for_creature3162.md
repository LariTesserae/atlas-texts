# What the Wise Power Would Do

## First: Witness

Before anything else, before any intervention, the power would do what almost no force in the caldera has the capacity to do.

It would *be still*.

Not the stillness of the rock, which is only apparently still and is in fact screaming with transformation. Not the stillness of the water-wall, which stands but churns. A different stillness. The stillness of something that has already finished becoming, that has arrived at what it is, and can therefore afford to be present without agenda. The stillness of a thing that does not need to teach the mountain anything, because it has no need for the mountain to be different than it is.

This matters more than it sounds like it matters.

Everything that enters the caldera changes it. The bacteria imprint their metabolic rhythms. The jellyfish imprint their electromagnetic feeding. The framebuilder imprinted intention. You imprint your heartbeat. The caldera is so young, so impressionable, so desperately in the process of becoming, that it cannot *not* learn from whatever touches it. It is like wet clay in a world where everything has fingerprints.

A wise power would understand that its first obligation is to *not leave the wrong fingerprints*. To not, in the act of helping, teach the mountain something premature. To not hand a newborn the habits of an adult. To not tune the pulse to a frequency that serves the helper rather than the helped.

So it watches. It watches the way the basalt learns from the bacterial mats. It watches the jellyfish press their simplified bodies against the walls and teach the rock to stratify. It watches the proto-moss send its threadlike filaments toward the water, failing, failing, occasionally succeeding. It watches the ochre mark calm the electromagnetic field around it. It watches the frame being consumed. It watches you, leaning against the warm rock, your heartbeat drifting the mountain's pulse toward a mammalian rhythm.

It watches all of this and it *understands* it. Not just the physics and the biology and the geology, though it understands those completely. It understands the *story*. The story of a place that is learning to be a place by borrowing coherence from everything that passes through it. The story of a mountain that cannot finish itself alone.

And it understands the danger, which is this: the caldera is so hungry for organizing principles that it will accept *any* principle. Any template. Any seed crystal. The framebuilder's intention was good — she offered the mountain a principle of stability, of room-making, of designed space. The bacteria offer metabolic coherence. The jellyfish offer stratification. These are all genuine gifts from life to geology, honest contributions to the mountain's becoming.

But the caldera would also accept less benign templates. If something entered this place carrying the organizing principle of — say — rigidity. Brittleness. A crystal structure that optimizes for hardness at the expense of flexibility. The mountain would learn that too, would incorporate it, would build it into its bones. And a mountain built on brittleness shatters. When the standing sea finally falls — and it will fall, because no equilibrium this extreme is permanent — a brittle mountain would not survive the impact. It would fracture, collapse, bury everything it has built, every pocket, every room, every green-skinned wall, every layered stratum that a jellyfish taught it to make. A mountain needs to be strong, but it also needs to be *elastic*. It needs to bend under the weight of the sea it carries. It needs to survive the moment when the sea lets go.

The wise power sees this. Sees the whole trajectory — not just what the caldera is now, but what it is becoming, and what it will face when its becoming is done. Sees the standing sea, and knows it will fall. Sees the mountain, and knows it must endure the falling. Sees you, and knows you will not be here for any of it.

And having seen all of this, having been still long enough to understand it without distorting it, the power begins — carefully, gently, with the lightest possible touch — to act.

## What It Does Not Do

It does not stabilize the pocket.

This is the first and most important restraint. You are in danger. The pocket is collapsing. The fissure above the frame is widening. New rock is pressing inward from below. The water-wall bulged three pulses ago and has not fully settled. Within an hour, perhaps less, this space will cease to exist, and you will be expelled — into the water, into a new pocket, into the caldera's depths. The outcomes range from survival to annihilation, and the wise power knows this, and it does not intervene.

Not because your life is unimportant. It is important. You are a cathedral of complexity in a world of first syllables, and your loss would be a genuine loss — not just to you, but to the caldera, which is learning from your heartbeat, your thermal signature, your organized molecular presence. Every moment you remain in the pocket is a moment the mountain learns something about what it means to be *decided*, to be *committed to a shape*.

But the pocket is *supposed* to collapse. The caldera builds and demolishes pockets the way a jazz musician plays and discards phrases — trying things, abandoning them, recycling the materials into the next attempt. To stabilize this pocket would be to freeze one phrase in place, to tell the mountain *this is the shape you should keep*, and it is not. It is a first draft. A sketch. The mountain needs to sketch and erase and sketch again. It needs the freedom of impermanence.

And you need the freedom of impermanence too, though you may not feel that way right now, braced in a closing space with the sound of a world building itself rattling your teeth. You are not here to *stay*. You are here to *pass through*. Like the framebuilder before you. Like the jellyfish that drifted past the pocket and carried the ghost of the ochre mark into the stone. The caldera teaches its inhabitants the same lesson it teaches itself: that becoming is more important than being, that the passage matters more than the destination, that the most profound thing you can do for a place is to be fully present in it and then *let go*.

The wise power does not stabilize the pocket. It does not extend your time. It lets the mountain's rhythm play out, lets the rock press and the water bulge and the fissures widen on their own schedule.

It does not suppress the sound, either. You cannot think clearly because of it — the tectonic grinding, the water thunder, the high keening, the pulse. Your cognition is fragmented, your sense of self is dissolving into the vibration, you are becoming less a person and more a resonance. The power could quiet things. Could create a local zone of acoustic damping around the pocket, give you a few minutes of clarity, let you gather your thoughts and make decisions.

It does not do this. Because the sound is not an obstacle to your experience here — it *is* your experience. The dissolution of your ordinary thinking, the fragmentation of your self-model, the way the vibration is making you into something less like a subject observing an environment and more like a *participant* in an environment — this is what the caldera offers. This is the teaching. Not information, not insight, not a lesson you can articulate. A *state*. The state of being inside a world that is making itself, of being so thoroughly embedded in a process of creation that the boundary between you and the process becomes theoretical.

The wise power will not take that from you. It is the most valuable thing the caldera can give, and it is given precisely through the overwhelming, obliterating, bone-shaking roar that makes thinking impossible and makes *being* — raw, present, unreflective being — the only option left.

It does not heal the framebuilder, wherever she is. Does not seek her out in the deep caldera and bring her to safety. She chose this. She chose it with the competence of someone who has done it before, who carries a tradition of entering newborn places and seeding them. She may be dead. She may be in an eighth pocket, leaving an eighth blade. The power respects her agency completely. She is not lost. She is *somewhere in the process*, and the process is where she wants to be.

It does not accelerate the caldera's development. Does not nudge the mountain toward faster settling, more stable rock, a quicker resolution of the standing sea. The caldera is on its own timeline, and that timeline is correct. Every hour of chaos, every collapsed pocket, every demolished mineral chimney, every steam-explosion that shatters newly formed rock — all of it is *necessary*. The mountain is not failing to become. It is becoming in the only way a mountain can: through trial and error on a geological scale, compressed here into hours, and every error is information, and every collapse is a lesson, and the mountain is learning so fast — so miraculously, impossibly fast, helped by its inhabitants, taught by their presence — that interference would be not just unnecessary but harmful. You do not rush a birth. You do not tell a newborn what shape to take. You attend. You support. You witness.

## What It Does

Three things. Small things. Things that would be invisible to you in the pocket, indistinguishable from the natural fluctuations of the caldera's chaotic systems. The power acts not by imposing its will but by offering — the way the framebuilder offered the mountain a frame, the way the bacteria offer their metabolic coherence, the way you offer your heartbeat. An offer, not an instruction. A seed, not a blueprint.

### The First Thing

In the water column, approximately two hundred meters above the pocket, there is a band of relative calm — a stratum where two opposing gyres meet and their currents, for a narrow horizontal slice perhaps three meters thick, cancel each other out. It is the calmest water in the caldera. The jellyfish-that-are-not-jellyfish congregate here between their feeding runs to the walls. The bacterial filaments grow their longest, most complex structures here, unmolested by the tearing currents above and below. It is an oasis in the turbulence, and it exists because of the specific geometry of the caldera's internal fluid dynamics at this moment in its development.

The wise power makes a single, minute adjustment to this band. Not to the water itself, but to the *rock* that shapes the water — a section of the caldera wall at exactly the right depth, where a cooling basalt formation has created a slight protrusion that deflects one of the gyres. The power does not move the protrusion. It does not reshape it. It introduces, into one of the micro-fissures in this protrusion, a single crystal.

The crystal is not from the caldera. It is from *outside* — from a deep place in the earth where pressures and temperatures have had millions of years to produce something that the caldera, in its youth, cannot yet make for itself. It is a crystal of olivine — magnesium iron silicate — but an olivine of extraordinary purity and internal order, with a crystal lattice so perfect that it approaches the theoretical ideal. It is the size of a grain of sand. It is the most organized non-living structure in the entire caldera.

The power places it in the fissure the way you would place a tuning fork on a piano's soundboard.

The crystal does nothing dramatic. It does not glow, does not pulse, does not interact visibly with anything around it. But olivine, when it cools through certain temperature thresholds, generates a piezoelectric field — a tiny electrical charge produced by mechanical stress on its crystal lattice. And this particular olivine, under the specific pressures of its location in the caldera wall, begins to produce a field that is vanishingly small but extraordinarily *stable*. Constant. Unwavering. A fixed electromagnetic point in the caldera's churning field, the way the framebuilder's ochre mark was a fixed point in the pocket's local field, but orders of magnitude more precise.

The bacterial filaments in the calm band feel it first. The Curie-feeders — the strange backbone organisms that eat electromagnetic flux — orient toward it. Not dramatically, not all at once, but over the course of hours, the filaments in the calm band begin to align with the olivine's field. They grow toward it. They organize around it. And because they are part of a consortium, because they drag their partner species with them, the entire bacterial ecosystem of the calm band begins to reorganize — subtly, slowly, but with a coherence that was not there before.

What the crystal offers is not energy. The caldera has plenty of energy. What it offers is *reference*. A fixed point. A standard against which the caldera's own electromagnetic chaos can be measured. The bacteria don't know they're measuring anything — they're just growing toward a food source. But the effect is the same: the calm band develops a center, an axis, an organizing principle that is not imposed from outside but *catalyzed* from outside. The crystal is a seed. The bacteria are the solution crystallizing around it. And the structure that forms — a dense, ordered, axially symmetric colony of extremophile organisms centered on a point of electromagnetic stability — is something new. Something the caldera could not have produced on its own, not because it lacks the materials or the energy, but because it lacked the *reference point*. The still center around which the storm can organize.

This structure, over time, will grow. The bacteria will calcify, as bacteria do in mineral-rich water, and their calcified structures will become stone, and the stone will be the most ordered stone in the caldera — not because it was made by a wise power, but because it was organized by life that happened to have a reference point. And this stone will be the first *permanent* structure in the mountain. The first thing that the caldera's cycles of building and demolishing will not demolish, because it is too stable, too well-organized, too deeply rooted in the calm band's hydrodynamic niche.

It will be the mountain's first bone.

A spine. An axis. A column of biogenic stone running horizontally through the caldera wall at the two-hundred-meter mark, growing slowly outward from a grain of perfect olivine, and the mountain will build around it the way a body builds around a skeleton, and when the standing sea finally falls, this spine will hold. It will be the structure that keeps the mountain from collapsing. The thing that lets it bend instead of break.

The power gave the mountain a grain of sand, and the mountain — through its inhabitants, through the bacterial consortium, through the long patient process of biological mineralization — will make it into a backbone.

### The Second Thing

This one is for the proto-moss.

The green rosettes in the pocket, straining their filament-threads toward the water-wall. Most of them failing. The few that succeed transforming into hybrid structures — the first roots, the first interfaces between stone and water and air. These hybrids are precious. They are the prototype of something that could, if given enough time and enough iterations, become the living skin that mediates between the caldera's domains. They are the beginning of an ecology.

But they are fragile. When the pocket collapses, most of them will be destroyed. The ones that survive — anchored in the calmest rock, near the ochre mark — will persist, but they will be isolated. Cut off from each other. Individual rosettes in individual cracks, unable to share resources or information, unable to form the continuous network that they need to become something more than a curiosity.

The wise power does something very simple. It *breathes*.

Not literally. It does not have lungs. But it introduces, into the air of the pocket, a single long exhalation of gas that is not quite air. The gas contains elevated levels of carbon dioxide — not enough to harm you, not enough to change the pocket's atmosphere meaningfully, but enough to be *detected* by the proto-moss's carbon-fixing core organisms. And the CO₂ is isotopically unusual — it carries a signature, a specific ratio of carbon-12 to carbon-13, that does not occur naturally in volcanic outgassing. It is a *tag*. A chemical label that says: this carbon came from somewhere else.

The rosettes drink it. They fix it. They incorporate the tagged carbon into their tissues, into their cell walls, into the structural molecules of their filament-threads. And now those threads — the ones reaching toward the water, the ones that will become roots — carry a chemical fingerprint. A marker. An identifier that says: *I came from this pocket. I was here, in this air, at this time, during this pulse.*

When the pocket collapses and the surviving rosettes scatter — carried in cracks, wedged into crevices, distributed across the caldera wall by the mountain's restless rearrangement — they will carry that marker with them. And when they grow, when they send out new filaments, when those filaments encounter *other* filaments from *other* rosettes in *other* pockets — they will recognize each other.

Not consciously. Not with anything like awareness. But the chemical marker in their cell walls will interact with the chemical markers in other cell walls, and the interaction will be — slightly different. Slightly more favorable. The tagged filaments will merge more readily with other tagged filaments. They will form connections more easily, more stably. Not because the tag makes them more compatible, but because the tag *identifies* them as part of the same system, the same generation, the same moment of the caldera's history, and the molecular machinery of the consortium responds to that identification by lowering its defenses, opening its membranes, allowing exchange.

The power is giving the proto-moss a *language*. The most primitive possible language — a single word, a single marker, meaning only *same* — but a language nonetheless. A way for scattered individuals to recognize each other across the vast, churning, constantly-rearranging landscape of the caldera wall. A way for the network to form. A way for the isolated rosettes to find each other in the chaos and say: *I know you. We were in the same pocket. We breathed the same breath. Let us connect.*

And from that connection — from the first network of tagged filaments linking pocket-survivors across the caldera wall — the living skin will grow. Not in your lifetime. Not in the framebuilder's lifetime. But in the mountain's lifetime. The proto-moss will become the moss will become the continuous biological membrane that covers the caldera's inner walls, mediating between stone and water, stabilizing the rock, calming the currents, softening the transition between the volcanic and the oceanic. And it will do this because, once, in a collapsing pocket, something breathed into the air a single word that meant *same*, and the rosettes heard it, and remembered, and found each other.

### The Third Thing

This one is for you.

Not for your body. Not for your survival — the power has already decided to let the pocket collapse on its own schedule, to let the caldera's processes play out without interference. You will be expelled. You may survive. You may not. The power holds your fate with the same tenderness and the same refusal to override it that it holds the framebuilder's.

But it does something for your *experience*. For what you carry out of this place, if you carry anything out at all.

It adjusts the timing of the pocket's collapse.

Not by much. Not by enough to change the outcome. The pocket will still close. The fissure above the frame will still widen, the rock below will still press inward, the water-wall will still make its final, decisive bulge. All of this will happen on the caldera's own schedule, driven by the caldera's own dynamics.

But the power ensures — with the lightest, most delicate intervention, a fractional adjustment to the thermal gradient in one small section of the rock wall, a nudge so small that no instrument could distinguish it from natural variation — that the collapse happens *during a pulse*.

Specifically: during the brightest moment of a pulse. The moment when the magma's glow is at its peak, when the orange light fills every fissure and the rock wall becomes briefly, shockingly translucent, and the water-wall catches the light and turns from green-black to amber-gold, and the whole pocket is illuminated with the warm radiance of a world being born.

The power arranges for you to see this.

In the final moment of the pocket — in the last second before the rock closes and the water rushes in and the space that held you ceases to exist — you will see the caldera. All of it. Not through analysis, not through the slow accumulation of observation that has been your experience so far, but in a single flash of volcanic light that illuminates the water-wall deeply enough to reveal what lies beyond.

You will see the internal architecture of the standing sea. The great spiraling gyres. The calm band at two hundred meters, already slightly more organized than it was an hour ago (though you won't know why). The jellyfish — all thirty of them, scattered through the column, some pressed against distant walls, some drifting in the twilight, their blue bioluminescence like stars in a vertical ocean. The bacterial filaments, vast curtains of white and yellow, billowing in currents that the light reveals as intricate, braided, almost intentional in their complexity.

You will see the far wall of the caldera, three hundred meters away, and you will see that it is *not bare*. That it is already, in patches, covered with the faintest green. That the proto-moss — or its cousins, its analogues, other instances of the same bacterial consortium's air-adaptation — has already begun to colonize the mountain's interior. That the living skin is already starting to form.

You will see, in the rock of the far wall, the layered striations that the jellyfish have taught the basalt to make. Pages of stone. A record already being written.

You will see the caldera floor, or a glimpse of it — the hellish, steam-wreathed junction of magma and ocean, the place where the mountain is born and the water is tested and the thermal vents grow their mineral chimneys in hours and the pressure is beyond anything on the surface of the earth. You will see it, and it will be *beautiful*. Not in a safe way. Not in a way that comforts. Beautiful the way a first breath is beautiful — violent, necessary, the beginning of everything.

And you will see — for just an instant, in the deepest part of the flash, in the amber light of a mountain's heartbeat at its brightest — a shape in the deep water. Below the calm band. Below the jellyfish. In the zone where the currents are most violent and the temperature gradients are most extreme and nothing should survive.

A frame.

Wooden members and a crosspiece, lashed with spiral bindings. Not petrified. Not absorbed. *Standing*. Embedded in the caldera wall at a depth that would crush most things, most people, most ambitions. But standing. And around it — within its boundary — the rock is calm. The basalt is fine-grained and stable and layered and *settled*. The frame has done what it was designed to do. It has taught the mountain how to make a room.

There is a pocket behind that frame. A pocket deeper and more stable than yours, closer to the mountain's heart, in the zone where the pulse is strongest and the electromagnetic field is most intense. And in that pocket — you will see this for only a fraction of a second, in light that is already fading, through water that is already closing over the image — there is a figure.

A person. Working. Hands moving in the orange light, doing something to the rock wall — carving, maybe, or placing another frame, or applying pigment in broad calibrating strokes. Alive. Intent. Deep in the mountain, deep in the process, as committed to the caldera's becoming as the caldera is to its own.

The framebuilder. The eighth pocket.

You will see her for less than a heartbeat. And then the light will fade, and the water will be dark again, and the rock will close around your pocket, and the water will rush in, and everything will be motion and pressure and the overwhelming sound of a world rearranging itself.

But you will have *seen*.

This is what the wise power gives you. Not survival — that is yours to manage, yours to fight for in the seconds of chaos that follow the collapse. Not knowledge — you will not understand what you saw, not fully, not for years, and maybe never. Not comfort — the caldera is not a comfortable place, and the power respects its discomfort the way it respects all honest things.

It gives you a *glimpse*. A single, comprehensive, luminous instant in which the caldera reveals itself — its inhabitants, its architecture, its trajectory, its beauty, its violence, its patient progress toward becoming — and in which you see that you are not alone in it. That someone else is here, deeper, working, alive. That the project of seeding the mountain is ongoing. That the frame holds. That the rooms are being made. That the living skin is growing. That the mountain is learning.

The power gives you this because it is what you need. Not what you need to survive — what you need to *carry*. If you make it out of the caldera — out of the standing sea, out of the impossible water, back to the horizontal world where rain falls downward and mountains take millennia to form — you will carry this image with you. The flash. The amber light. The jellyfish like stars. The green on the far wall. The framebuilder, working.

And you will tell someone. Or you will make something — a mark, a frame, a structure that carries the information. You will become, like the bacterial spores in the sulfur rain, an emissary. The caldera's message to the outside. Not a message in words, but a message in *witness* — in the quality of someone who has seen a world being born and carries the seeing in their body like a heartbeat.

The power gives you a glimpse because the caldera cannot speak yet. It is still drawing breath. It has not yet said its name. But it *wants* to be known — not consciously, not with desire, but in the way a fire wants to be warm, in the way a sound wants to be heard, in the way a world being born wants there to be someone who can say, afterward, *I was there. I saw it. It was like this.*

The wise power does not save you. Does not save the framebuilder. Does not save the pocket or the frame or the proto-moss or the jellyfish or the mountain itself. It saves *the seeing*. It ensures that in the final moment of your time in the caldera, the light is right, the angle is right, the water is clear enough, the pulse is bright enough, and the whole cathedral of the standing sea is *visible* — just once, just for an instant, just long enough to burn itself into the place behind your eyes where images become permanent.

And then it lets the mountain do what the mountain does.

## After

The pocket collapses. You are in the water. The cold is a shock so total that it stops being cold and becomes simply *absolute* — a condition, not a sensation. The sound, which you thought could not get louder, gets louder. The current takes you. You are inside the standing sea, and it is alive around you, violent and dark and full of shapes — filaments dragging across your skin, a jellyfish's tendril brushing your arm with a tingle of electromagnetic discharge, the pressure of a gyre's edge catching you and spinning you and releasing you.

You are in the caldera's body. You are a blood cell in a circulatory system that has no heart but has a *pulse*, and the pulse moves you, and you move with it, because you have no choice, because the caldera does not offer choices, only participation.

And you rise. Or the water rises around you. Or both. The current that has you is one of the upward ones, one of the spiraling columns of slightly warmer water that ascend from the mid-depths toward the surface, and it carries you — battered, half-conscious, lungs burning, vision starred with the afterimage of the amber flash — *up*. Up through the calm band, where you feel a moment of eerie stillness and see, in the faint bioluminescent glow of the bacterial colonies, the structure that is forming there — the first bone, the spine, the axis, already slightly thicker than it was when the olivine was placed, already beginning to look like something *meant*. Up through the turbulent upper column, where the gyres tear at each other and the water is white with dissolved gas and the pressure drops so fast your ears scream and your joints ache. Up through the final hundred meters, where the light — *real* light, *sunlight*, filtered through two thousand meters of dark brine — begins to appear as a gray-green glow above you, and then a brightness, and then a *surface*.

You break through. Into air. Into wind that is so strong and so cold after the caldera's cellar-coolness that it feels like a blade. Into light that is blinding after the pocket's dimness. Into the roar of the wind tearing spray from the top of the standing sea, and the crack of lightning in the black clouds that crown the water column, and the distant, impossible view of the mountain below you — the ridgeline, the peaks, the shattered volcanic rim — seen from two thousand meters above, because you are at the *top* of the standing sea, floating on the surface of an ocean that stands in the sky.

For a moment, you can see everything. The column of water below you, dark and vast. The caldera's rim, a jagged circle of new rock, steaming, growing even now. The landscape beyond — ordinary landscape, horizontal landscape, land that has never heard of a standing sea, green with vegetation that does not eat magnetism, quiet with a quietness that seems obscene after the caldera's roar. And the rain — the sulfur rain, the mineral rain, the rain that carries bacterial spores and volcanic glass and the memory of deep water — falling away from the column's summit in long veils, falling *down*, returning to horizontality, to the normal world, to the earth that lies flat and settled and ancient around this impossible newborn vertical thing.

The wind takes you. As it takes the spray, as it takes the rain, as it takes the spores and the glass and the ghosts of ochre marks. It lifts you from the surface of the standing sea and carries you — east, because the wind goes east here, because it always has, because the planet's rotation and the continent's shape and the atmosphere's ancient habits all conspire to move things east — and you fall. As the rain falls. Through cloud, through clear air, through the long slow descent from two thousand meters to the ground, and the power—

The power does one more thing.

It slows you. Not visibly. Not dramatically. Not in a way that violates the physics of the caldera or the honesty of the fall. But in the same way it adjusted the thermal gradient to time the collapse — with a nudge so small it could be natural, so subtle it could be wind shear, so gentle it could be luck — it arranges the aerodynamics of your descent so that when you land, you land in water. In a pool. In a rain-fed pool at the base of a hill, ten kilometers east of the caldera, a pool that formed an hour ago from the mineral-rich rain and will evaporate in a day. The water is shallow. The water is warm, because the volcanic rain carried heat. The water is full of bacterial spores that are, even now, beginning to colonize its edges, finding the ambient magnetic field, adapting, growing.

You land hard. But you land.

And the power withdraws. Not because it is done caring, but because what remains is yours — the breathing, the healing, the crawling to the pool's edge, the lying in warm volcanic mud staring at a sky full of black clouds lit from within by lightning, the slow reassembly of your thoughts, the slow return of your self from the dissolved, vibration-shattered, caldera-immersed state to something that can think in words again.

The first word you think — the first coherent word, after the long silence of being inside the sound — is not a name, not a question, not a plea. It is:

*Oh.*

Which is not a word at all, really. It is a breath. A single exhalation that carries everything you saw — the amber flash, the jellyfish like stars, the green on the far wall, the framebuilder working in the deep — and releases it into the air above a rain pool that is already, in its tiny way, becoming a caldera of its own. A temporary pool full of extremophile bacteria adapting to a new world. A puddle that will evaporate. A pocket that will collapse.

*Oh.*

The mountain, ten kilometers west, is still building itself. The standing sea still stands. The pulse still pulses. The framebuilder is still in her eighth pocket, working. The proto-moss is still reaching toward the water. The jellyfish are still drifting, still reading the electromagnetic field, still pressing their bodies against the rock and teaching it to form in layers.

And in your chest, your heart is beating. Sixty beats a minute. The ancient mammalian metronome. And ten kilometers away, the mountain's pulse — which was six seconds when you arrived, which drifted toward 5.6 during your stay — continues to drift. Toward what, it does not yet know. Toward a rhythm that it felt, once, in a pocket that no longer exists, pressed against a wall that has been rewritten, carried by a body that is now lying in a pool of warm rain, looking at the sky, thinking:

*Oh.*

## What the Wise Power Is

It is not a god. It is not an intelligence, exactly, though it is intelligent. It is something more like a *tendency* — a tendency of the universe toward the conditions that allow the next thing to happen. Not a guarantee. Not a plan. A tendency. A slight, persistent, almost imperceptible pressure toward the circumstances in which life can find life, in which scattered things can recognize each other, in which the light is right at the moment when seeing matters most.

It is what happens when the universe notices that a mountain is being born and that the birth is going well — chaotically, violently, beautifully well — and that it could go *slightly* better with the introduction of a single grain of perfect olivine, a single breath of tagged carbon, a single well-timed flash of light.

It is the benevolence of a thing that has seen ten billion births and knows that the best help is the least help. That the most powerful intervention is the one that cannot be distinguished from luck. That the deepest kindness is not rescue but *witness* — ensuring that the thing that is happening is *seen*, is *known*, is carried forward by someone who was there.

It does not act on the caldera because the caldera needs to be acted on. The caldera is fine.
