# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

---

## I. Arrival, and the First Act: Attention

The power arrives the way light arrives—not by crossing a threshold but by being present where before it was not. It has no body. It does not cast a shadow with prismatic fringes. It does not disturb the veils of water or leave ripples that propagate along the seepage planes. If the thread-thin creatures could perceive it, they would perceive nothing new, only a quality of the air that might be described as *being regarded*.

And this is the first act. Not intervention. Not repair. Not the laying on of some cosmic hand. Just attention—real attention, the kind that does not flinch from what it sees and does not immediately begin composing a response.

The power watches.

It watches for a long time by the plateau's own standards. Several dusk-glows pass. The cushions synchronize, extinguish, synchronize again. A thread-thin creature emerges, drinks, retreats. The ripple it leaves behind propagates to the edge of the seepage plane and fades. A perfect sphere of rain hovers in a shallow bowl, drifts sideways, and rejoins the weeping rock. The glassy note sounds and is gone.

The power notices everything. It notices the way the etched lines still carry, after all this time, the faintest residue of intention. It notices how the lattice dislikes standing water but tolerates the spheres, because the spheres are moving, always moving, even when they appear still. It notices the thread-thin creature's exact timing—the way it waits for the lattice's permission before it presses its transparent body against the veil. It notices the cushions' slow extrusion of themselves along the scored basalt, the years-long commitment of sending a filament into empty ground without any guarantee that the ground will accept it.

It notices, too, what is not here. The builders. Whatever intelligence first carved the lattice into the rock and tuned the spectrum of the sky and decided that the spacing between cushions should be *this* and not *that*—that intelligence is gone. Not dead in any violent sense. Simply elsewhere, or dissipated, or evolved past the point of caring. What remains is their preference, fossilized into field equations that still nudge matter toward arrangements the builders would have found beautiful.

The power understands this. It has seen it before, in other places, at other scales: the garden that outlives the gardener. The music box still playing in an empty house. The letter that arrives after the sender has forgotten writing it.

It sits with that understanding. It does not rush past it.

---

## II. The Temptation to Perfect

Now comes the moment that separates wisdom from mere benevolence.

The power can see, with the clarity available to it, every flaw in the plateau's quiet machinery. The lattice is degrading. Not quickly—not in any way that the current residents could detect across a hundred of their generations—but measurably, by the power's standards. The field equations that maintain the altered spectrum are losing coherence at their margins. In another few thousand years the prismatic fringes will widen, then blur, then vanish. The sky above the plateau will become ordinary sky. The downdraft that turns birds aside will weaken. Seeds will arrive. Roots will crack the shallow bowls. The perfect spheres will become ordinary puddles, and the glassy note will never sound again.

The cushions will not survive this. Their metallic veins are tuned to the lattice's specific frequencies; without the dusk-synchronization, each cushion will fall into its own rhythm, and the slow, careful spacing that has kept them alive will drift into competition. Within a century of the lattice's failure, the plateau will be ordinary basalt colonized by ordinary lichens, and the blood-colored fronds will persist only as a faint metallic residue in the dust between cracks.

The thread-thin creatures might adapt. They are newer, more flexible. But they will lose the ripple—the lattice-amplified standing wave that carries their unseen dependents to fresh seepage planes. They will become simply worms in wet rock, no different from a billion other worms in a billion other wet rocks.

The power sees all of this. And it could fix it. Trivially. A single precise adjustment to the field equations—a retuning, a reinforcement, a fresh infusion of the energy that the builders once supplied—and the lattice would hold for another million years. The plateau would persist: its private weather, its cathedral silence, its three slow collaborators keeping the same hours in the same careful way, forever.

*Forever.*

The power considers this. It holds the possibility like one of the plateau's own rain-spheres, turning it, watching the light refract through it.

And it sets the possibility down.

Not because the plateau does not deserve to persist. It does. The power recognizes the beauty of the place with a clarity that the plateau's own residents cannot achieve, because beauty of this kind requires an outside eye—requires someone to stand at the boundary between the thing and the absence of the thing and feel the difference. The plateau is extraordinary. Its three-part collaboration is a small miracle of emergence, a system that has found its way to something like grace without anyone intending it.

But *forever* is not grace. Forever is embalming. And the power is wise enough to know the difference.

The builders understood this too, once. That is why they left. Not because they stopped caring, but because they understood that the most dangerous thing you can do to a living system is to make it permanent. The lattice was always meant to fade. The etched lines were always meant to soften. The plateau was given a span—generous, by any geological standard, but finite—and within that span it was free to become whatever it could become. The builders' gift was not the lattice itself but the *time* the lattice bought: time for the cushions to evolve their silver veins, for the thread-thin creatures to learn the lattice's rhythms, for the whole system to discover its own coherence.

To reinforce the lattice now would be to override that gift. It would freeze the plateau at this moment of its development, which happens to be beautiful, but which is not the only beautiful thing it might become. It would tell the cushions: *you are finished. This is your final form.* It would tell the thread-thin creatures: *you need never adapt again.* It would replace the plateau's own slow intelligence with the power's judgment about what the plateau should be.

The power will not do this.

---

## III. The Temptation to Accelerate

But neither will the power simply walk away.

Because there is another possibility, subtler and more seductive than perfection: the power could *prepare* the plateau for what is coming. Not by preserving the lattice but by nudging the residents toward resilience. It could, for instance, introduce a faint variation into the dusk-glow synchronization—a controlled irregularity that would force the cushions to develop backup rhythms not dependent on the lattice. It could alter the chemistry of the deeper fissures so that the thread-thin creatures begin to produce their own surface-tension effects, weaning themselves off the lattice's amplification. It could seed the dust pockets with trace minerals that would allow the cushions to anchor more deeply, surviving the eventual arrival of competing species.

All of this would be invisible. The residents would not know they were being trained. The changes would arrive as slight environmental shifts, indistinguishable from natural variation. The plateau would still run down on its own schedule, but when it did, its inhabitants would be ready.

This is the intervention that most benevolent powers would choose. It is sensible. It is kind. It respects autonomy while stacking the odds. It is, in the vocabulary of a certain kind of wisdom, *stewardship*.

The power considers it for longer than it considered perfection.

And again, gently, it sets the possibility down.

Here is why.

The cushions have already been shaped by the lattice for longer than most civilizations endure. Every silver thread in every frond is a response to the lattice's specific preferences. The thread-thin creatures' entire behavioral repertoire—their timing, their drinking posture, their ripple-communication—is a dialogue with the lattice's permissions and prohibitions. The system's intelligence is not located in any one resident but in the *relationship* between all three: plant, creature, and field. To alter any vertex of that triangle, even gently, even in the direction of resilience, is to decide *on the system's behalf* what resilience should look like.

But the power does not know what resilience should look like for this system. It knows what resilience looks like in general, across a thousand worlds and a million ecologies. It has deep and hard-won pattern libraries. But this plateau is not a general case. It is *this* case, with *these* cushions, *these* creatures, *this* lattice, *this* particular quality of light at the edge of every shadow. The system has been solving its own problems in its own way for a very long time, and its solutions are specific, local, and in many cases more elegant than anything the power could design from the outside.

The thread-thin creatures, for instance, have already begun to adapt. The power notices—because it has been paying attention, real attention—that the youngest creatures, the ones most recently emerged from the deep fissures, drink slightly differently from their elders. Their pores are distributed in a pattern that is not quite aligned with the lattice's current frequency. The discrepancy is tiny, but it is there, and it is consistent across the youngest cohort. They are already drifting, already exploring the margins of the lattice's permission, already—in their own glacial, hairsbreadth way—preparing for a world in which the lattice no longer speaks.

The cushions, too. The power notices that the newest extruded threads are not following the etched lines as faithfully as the older ones. Some deviate by a fraction of a degree, and where they deviate, the new cushions that form are minutely different—their fronds a shade darker, their silver veins branched in patterns that do not quite match the dusk-glow frequency. These are not errors. They are experiments. The plateau is already doing its own work.

To intervene now, even helpfully, would be to interrupt that work. It would substitute the power's idea of preparedness for the system's own slow, embodied, context-specific exploration. It would be like reaching into a conversation between old friends and suggesting more efficient topics.

---

## IV. What the Power Actually Does

So: not perfection, and not preparation. What, then?

Three things. Small things. Things that are less like intervention and more like *courtesy*.

---

**The first thing is a removal.**

High on the cliff face above the plateau, in a stratum of rock that predates the builders by a geological age, there is a vein of mineral that has been slowly oxidizing since the cliff was exposed. The oxidation products are migrating downward through capillary action in the stone. In approximately eighty years they will reach the seepage planes that feed the plateau's water veils. The mineral is not toxic to the cushions or the creatures—not exactly—but it will alter the surface tension of the water in a way that disrupts the ripple-communication of the thread-thin creatures. Not fatally, but suddenly. Too suddenly. The system could adapt to the change if it arrived gradually over centuries; arriving all at once, in a single wet season, it will shatter the standing-wave network before the creatures can develop alternatives.

This is not part of the lattice's designed decline. It is not a challenge the system was meant to face. It is simply bad luck—a geological accident, the kind of thing that happens on every world, that has nothing to do with the plateau's own story.

The power reaches into the cliff face—not with hands but with a precise, momentary alteration of the oxidation kinetics—and slows the migration. Not stops it. Slows it. Instead of arriving in eighty years, the mineral will arrive in eight hundred, by which time it will be diluted enough to register as a gradual shift rather than a shock. The system will still have to deal with it. The thread-thin creatures will still have to adapt their communication. But they will have time to do so on their own terms.

The power does this the way you might move a stone out of a path—not to change the walker's destination, but because the stone was not part of the path.

Nothing on the plateau registers the change. The veils of water continue to weep. The ripples continue to propagate. The thread-thin creatures continue to drink on the lattice's schedule, unaware that a catastrophe eight decades away has been softened into a challenge eight centuries away.

---

**The second thing is a bridge.**

Far below the plateau, in a valley where the runoff from the cliff face eventually gathers into an actual stream, there is another colony of cushion-organisms. They are cousins of the plateau's residents—descended from threads that were extruded over the cliff edge long ago and survived the fall. They are different now: adapted to running water, to ordinary light, to soil rather than dust. Their fronds are green instead of red, their veins copper instead of silver. They have no lattice to synchronize them; each cushion keeps its own time.

They are also dying. The stream is shifting course, as streams do, and within a few decades their stretch of bank will be dry rubble. They have no way to migrate; they are even slower than their plateau relatives, and the nearest suitable habitat is a kilometer of bare rock away.

The power could save them—redirect the stream, stabilize the bank, intervene in any of a dozen ways. It does not. Their fate is their own, part of the ordinary tragedy of life in moving water.

But it notices something. One of the valley cushions, growing at the extreme upstream edge of the colony, has extruded a thread that has reached the base of the cliff. The thread is alive but stalled, unable to climb the vertical rock. It will die there, in another year or two, and the genetic variation it carries—the valley colony's hard-won adaptations to soil and ordinary light—will die with it.

The power does the smallest thing it can. It shifts a single flake of basalt, no larger than a coin, from a position where it blocks a hairline crack to a position where it does not. This opens a capillary channel in the rock face—a thread-width passage of moisture that leads, eventually, upward. Not to the plateau. Not directly. But to a sheltered ledge forty meters up the cliff, where dust collects in a crack that receives both ordinary sunlight and, at certain times of year, the faintest edge of the plateau's altered spectrum.

The valley cushion's thread may find this channel. It may not. If it does, it may survive the climb. It may not. If it survives, it may establish a new cushion on the ledge—one that carries the valley's adaptations but is exposed to the plateau's influence. In time, that cushion might extrude its own thread upward, and its descendant might reach the plateau itself, arriving as a new variant carrying genes for life without the lattice.

Or none of this may happen. The flake of basalt is not a guarantee. It is an *open door*—one that the valley cushion is free to ignore, and that the cliff face would have opened on its own eventually, in another century or two, after the valley colony was already gone.

The power has not decided the plateau's future. It has merely ensured that one particular possibility is not foreclosed by accident and timing.

---

**The third thing is not an action at all. It is a witness.**

The power remains.

Not permanently. Not as a guardian or a monitor or a curator. It remains for one full cycle of the plateau's life—from dusk-glow to dusk-glow—and during that cycle it does something that no one has done since the builders departed.

It *appreciates*.

Not in the detached way of a scientist cataloging phenomena, and not in the sentimental way of a tourist collecting moments. It appreciates the way you appreciate a friend: by being present, by letting the plateau's particular quality of silence fill the space where urgency usually lives, by allowing the small glassy note from the drifting sphere to mean exactly what it means and nothing more.

This matters. Not metaphysically—the power is not the sort of entity that believes attention literally sustains reality. But it matters in the way that it matters when someone sees your work and *understands* it. The plateau has been doing something extraordinary for a very long time, and no one has been watching. The lattice maintains its preferences, the cushions synchronize, the creatures drink, the ripples propagate—and all of it happens in the same profound silence, the same cathedral emptiness, whether anyone notices or not.

The power notices.

It notices that the spacing between cushions is not merely functional but *beautiful*—that the bare basalt between them has the quality of silence between notes, and that the system has, without intending to, composed something. It notices that the thread-thin creatures' drinking ritual has the quality of prayer: the patient waiting, the full-body prostration against the water, the slow withdrawal, the ripple left behind as a kind of offering to beings too small to see. It notices that the lattice, for all its mechanical origins, has become something more than its builders intended—a participant rather than a framework, a collaborator rather than a constraint.

It notices that the whole system, taken together, is a kind of *statement*. Not a statement in language, not a proposition, but a demonstration: *this is one way that matter can organize itself when given time and a little help and then left alone.* The statement is not loud. It is not trying to prove anything. It is simply there, the way a tree is there, the way a river is there, the way the best things in any world are there—not because someone decided they should be, but because the conditions were right and the time was sufficient and no one interfered at the critical moment.

The power holds all of this in its awareness for twenty-four hours, plateau time. It lets the dusk-glow rise and fall. It watches the thread-thin creature emerge and drink and retreat. It listens for the glassy note and hears it, once, at an angle that catches the last sphere of the evening.

And then—and this is perhaps the most important part—it *remembers*.

Not in a database. Not as a record to be retrieved and analyzed. It remembers the way you remember a place you loved: wholly, with the temperature of the air and the quality of the light and the particular silence that was not the absence of sound but the presence of something deeper than sound. It folds the plateau into its understanding of what is possible, and the plateau becomes part of the power's own ongoing answer to the question of what matter and energy and time can do when they are not being forced.

Somewhere, in some future moment, when the power encounters another system on the verge of something—another fragile collaboration, another garden tending itself in silence—it will make a slightly better decision because of this plateau. Not by applying the plateau as a template, but by carrying within itself the *feeling* of the plateau: the patience, the spacing, the way the cushions lean away from each other not out of hostility but out of respect for the stone between them.

This is what the plateau gives the power, and it is not a small thing.

---

## V. Departure

The power leaves the way it arrived: not by crossing a threshold but by no longer being present. The plateau does not notice. The veils of water continue to seep. The cushions continue their slow extrusion. The thread-thin creatures continue to emerge on the lattice's schedule, drink, and retreat. The etched lines continue to flare with their firefly light, unobserved and unconcerned about being unobserved.

High on the cliff face, the oxidation front creeps downward at its new, slower pace. Far below, the valley cushion's thread inches toward a capillary channel that may or may not lead to a future. On the plateau itself, a cushion at the eastern edge enters its third year of extruding a filament toward a pocket of dust six inches away. The filament deviates, almost imperceptibly, from the etched line it has been following. The deviation carries it across a patch of quartz that catches the morning light in a way the basalt does not.

Nothing changes. Everything continues.

This is the plateau's own way of saying that the intervention was correct: not by flourishing, not by expressing gratitude, not by producing some visible sign of improvement, but by continuing to be exactly what it is, no more and no less, undisturbed in its slow and particular work.

---

## VI. Coda: What Wisdom Withholds

If you asked the power, afterward, what it did, it would have difficulty answering. Not because the actions were secret but because the actions were so small that they resist narration. *I moved a flake of rock. I slowed a chemical reaction. I watched for a day.*

But if you pressed—if you asked why those things and not the thousand grander interventions available—the power might say something like this:

*The plateau does not need me. That is the first thing to understand, and the last thing, and the thing that makes all the difference.*

*It does not need to be preserved. It is not a museum. It is not an endpoint. It is a* moment *in a process that began before the builders arrived and will continue after the lattice fails, and the moment is beautiful, but the process is more beautiful, because the process includes both the moment and its passing.*

*It does not need to be improved. Every system I have ever encountered that was genuinely alive was also genuinely imperfect, and the imperfections were not separate from the life but part of it—the growing edge, the place where the system was still in conversation with its circumstances. To smooth those edges would be to end the conversation.*

*It does not need to be taught. The cushions know things about the lattice that I do not know, because they have been listening to it with their silver veins for longer than I have been paying attention. The thread-thin creatures know things about the water that I cannot learn from outside, because knowing them requires a body thin enough to press against the veil and feel the tension change. The lattice itself knows things about the rock that predate language. I am wise, but I am not wise in the way the plateau is wise, and my wisdom is not a substitute for its own.*

*What the plateau needs—what any living system needs from a power that means well—is room. Room to succeed and room to fail. Room to adapt on its own schedule. Room to discover solutions I would never have thought of, because I would have been solving a different problem—the problem as it appears from outside, which is never quite the same as the problem as it is lived from within.*

*What I gave it was not room. It already had that. What I gave it was the removal of two obstacles that were not part of its story—a chemical accident and a geographical one—so that the room it already had was real room, room in which its own choices mattered, rather than room pre-empted by a landslide that had nothing to do with anything.*

*And I watched. I do not think watching is a small thing. I think it may be the largest thing a power can do, if the watching is honest. To see a thing clearly, to hold it in attention without grasping, to let it be what it is without needing it to be more—that is not passivity. It is the most active form of love I know. It is the love that does not consume its object. It is the love that lets the other remain other.*

*The plateau will fail eventually. The lattice will fade. The cushions will lose their synchronization. The thread-thin creatures will adapt or they will not. The bare basalt will be covered over by ordinary life, and the glassy note will never sound again. This is not a tragedy. This is what it means to be real rather than ideal, to exist in time rather than outside it, to be a living thing rather than a monument to the idea of living things.*

*I could have made it permanent. I chose not to. That choice is the whole of my gift, and the whole of my respect, and I do not think the plateau would have wanted it any other way—if the plateau could want, which it cannot, which is part of why I love it, which is part of why I left it alone.*

---

On the plateau, night falls. The dusk-glow extinguishes at once. The quartz seams pale against the dark basalt. Water seeps, as it has always seeped, in thin silver veils that are almost streams but never quite. A thread-thin creature waits in its crack for the lattice's permission. A cushion extends its filament another fraction of a millimeter along a line it is beginning, for the first time, to question.

The silence holds. The silence is enough.
