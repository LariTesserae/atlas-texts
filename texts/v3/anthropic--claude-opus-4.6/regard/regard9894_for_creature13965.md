# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

---

It would do almost nothing.

That is the first and most important thing to understand. A truly wise power, encountering this place and the interlocking community of beings who inhabit it, would recognize immediately that what it is looking at is not a problem to be solved. It is not a tragedy in progress. It is not a cry for help. It is a system that has already answered every question the wind has asked of it, and the answers are written in the architecture of every organism present—in the crawling fly's vestigial wings, in the cushion plant's stone-hard dome, in the rodent's perfect roundness, in the lichen's centuries-long patience.

To intervene in the obvious ways—to still the wind, to deepen the soil, to warm the air, to gentle the UV, to make this place more hospitable by the standards of somewhere else—would be an act of annihilation more thorough than any catastrophe. Everything here exists *because of* the extremity, not despite it. The crawling fly is a crawling fly because flight is death. Reduce the wind to a breeze and you have not saved the crawling fly; you have made it obsolete, a flightless insect in a world where flight is suddenly possible, outcompeted within generations by its winged cousins from below who would colonize the newly mild plateau in numbers that would overwhelm every niche. The cushion plant's dense dome is its identity and its genius; give it deep soil and moderate wind and it is nothing, a slow-growing, uncompetitive lump that any upright weed could overtop and shade out in a season. The small birds' compressed, screaming songs are masterpieces of communication under constraint; remove the constraint and they are just noise, energy wasted, a vestige of a hardship that no longer applies.

A wise power would see all this in an instant and would feel the specific ache that wisdom carries: the ache of recognizing that love, here, means restraint. That the deepest respect it can offer is to leave the wind blowing.

---

But "almost nothing" is not nothing.

---

## What It Would See That We Cannot

The first thing the benevolent power would do is *look*. Not the way we look—skimming the surface, catching the obvious, constructing a narrative from the fragments our senses can gather in the time we're willing to stand in the cold. It would look the way the plateau itself experiences time: slowly, completely, across scales.

It would see the endolithic bacteria inside the quartz veins of the gneiss and know their lineages—which ones descended from the first colonizers after the last ice retreated, which ones arrived later on windblown dust, which ones are ancient beyond the plateau's current form, survivors of a previous mountain that was here before this one, carried forward in fragments of the old stone that were caught up in the metamorphism and preserved, cellular stowaways in the deep earth, returned to the light after millions of years. It would see their slow metabolisms, the single-digit number of cell divisions per year, the exquisite calibration of their photosynthetic pigments to the exact wavelengths that penetrate gneiss to a depth of 0.8 millimeters. It would see them as what they are: the bedrock community, older than everything, quieter than everything, more permanent than everything, the living foundation beneath the living foundation.

It would see the lichens in their full temporal depth—not as static patches but as slow explosions, each one a centuries-long unfurling, a partnership that has been renegotiated at the cellular level every day for longer than most human civilizations have existed. It would see the steel-blue lichen in the deepest crevices and know something about it that no human observer could determine: that it is not one species but several, a complex of closely related lineages that diverged from each other on this very plateau, in different crevice systems, isolated by ten meters of wind-scoured rock as effectively as if they were on different continents. Each crevice system is an island. Each steel-blue lichen population is an archipelagic species, evolving in parallel, similar but not identical, the differences invisible to the eye but written in the DNA—different solutions to the same problem of existing in the dark, damp, sheltered cracks of this particular ridgeline.

It would see the cushion plants and would perceive what is hidden inside them: the interior ecosystems, the mites and springtails and nematodes, and it would see these not as incidental guests but as coevolved partners, some of them as specialized to the cushion-plant interior as the crawling flies are specialized to the plateau's surface. There are mites in those cushions that exist nowhere else. Nowhere else on this ridgeline, nowhere else on this mountain, nowhere else on Earth. They are endemic to the interior of a specific cushion-plant species on a single high-altitude plateau, and their entire world is a dome of compressed leaves eight inches across, and they have been there for longer than the genus *Homo* has existed, and no human eye has ever seen one, and if the cushion plant goes, they go, absolutely and without remainder.

It would see the crawling flies in their full evolutionary narrative—would trace the lineage backward, generation by generation, through the gradual shortening of wings, the strengthening of legs, the darkening of cuticle, back and back to the moment, tens of thousands of generations ago, when a perfectly ordinary winged fly from the valleys below was deposited on this ridgeline by the same aeolian transport that still delivers dead insects today, and instead of dying, it found a crevice, and fed, and survived, and bred with another accidental arrival, and their offspring were a little heavier, a little less inclined to fly, a little better at clinging to stone. The power would see this not as a just-so story but as a lived history, each generation a real population of real organisms making real decisions—to crawl instead of fly, to shelter instead of flee, to press flat instead of take wing—and being rewarded or punished by the wind, which is the only judge on this plateau, the only selective pressure that ultimately matters.

It would see the rodents' crevice networks as what they truly are: cities. Not metaphorical cities—actual, structured habitats with designated areas for nesting, for food storage, for waste deposition, for social interaction, connected by passages that have been worn smooth by thousands of generations of small, round bodies passing through. The power would see the social structures—the hierarchies, the alliances, the mother-pup bonds, the quiet chirping conversations carried through stone—and would recognize a form of culture, not human culture, not anything so grand, but information passed from generation to generation that is not in the DNA: *this passage leads to the best foraging route, this chamber floods when the fog is heavy, this overhang is watched by a raptor, go another way.* Learned knowledge. Tradition. The memory of a community that has no written language and no individual member who lives more than a few years, but that collectively remembers things that happened decades ago, encoded in behaviors and avoidances and preferences that are taught, not inherited.

And the birds. The power would hear the compressed songs—those five-second bursts of musicality squeezed into the brief windows when the wind eases—and would hear in them something that no spectrogram could capture: individuality. Each male's song is slightly different. Each one is improvised within a framework of inherited structure, the way a jazz musician improvises within a chord progression. The variations are tiny—a note bent slightly sharper, a trill extended by a quarter-beat, a phrase reordered—but they are real, and the females hear them, and they choose mates partly on the basis of these variations, preferring the songs that are most complex, most inventive, most surprising. The benevolent power would hear this and would understand that it is listening to an art form. Not human art. Not conscious art, perhaps. But a creative practice, a tradition of elaboration and variation sustained across generations, a cultural achievement of small, wind-battered birds who have found a way to be beautiful in the most inhospitable acoustic environment on the planet.

## What It Would Notice Is Wrong—Or Rather, What Is Fragile

Having looked, having seen the full depth and intricacy of this place, the benevolent power would then perceive something that the plateau's inhabitants cannot perceive because they are inside it: the system's fragility.

Not fragility in the ordinary sense. This ecosystem is, in its way, extraordinarily robust. It has survived ice ages. It has survived volcanic events that darkened the sky. It has survived droughts that lasted decades, when the fog stopped coming and the lichens retreated to their deepest refugia and the cushion plants died back to their roots and the crawling flies survived on stored fat in the deepest crevices and the birds left, simply left, and came back only when the moisture returned, finding the plateau diminished but not dead, ready to rebuild. This place is tough. It is wind-hardened in every sense.

But there is a new kind of fragility, one that the plateau has never faced. The power would see it not in the present but in the trajectories—the curves of change, the rates of shift, the slow tilting of the atmospheric systems that determine how often the fog comes, how warm the air is, how strong the updrafts are, how much UV reaches the surface. Climate change. The phrase is too small for what the power would perceive: not a political slogan but a physical reality, a reorganization of atmospheric circulation patterns that is, right now, in this very decade, altering the conditions on which every organism on this plateau depends.

The fog is coming less frequently. Not dramatically—not yet. But the trend is there, visible in the slowly retreating margins of the green lichen, in the slightly higher mortality of cushion-plant seedlings, in the fractional decrease in the moisture content of the crevice-system air. The cloud deck is shifting—rising, on average, as the atmosphere warms, the condensation level moving upward by a few meters per decade, which doesn't sound like much until you realize that the plateau's elevation is fixed and the margin between "clouds form here" and "clouds form a hundred feet higher" is the margin between a functioning ecosystem and a desiccating one.

The temperature is rising. The flies are active earlier in the morning, later in the evening—their solar-powered metabolism responding to warmer conditions by extending their active period, which sounds beneficial until you realize that it also extends their exposure to predation, increases their metabolic rate, accelerates their life cycle, and may push them out of sync with the lichen-fruiting and spore cycles on which their larvae depend.

The wind itself is changing—not in its presence, which is geophysical and essentially permanent, but in its patterns. The prevailing direction is shifting slightly. The seasonal variations are becoming less predictable. The aeolian transport of insects is changing in composition as the lowland ecosystems below change—different species in the valleys mean different species arriving dead on the plateau, and the crawling flies and birds that have spent millennia calibrating their diets to a specific menu of windblown moths and beetles are now receiving a different menu, with different nutritional profiles, different toxins, different everything.

And there is one more thing, subtle and devastating: the updrafts are strengthening. Warmer air below means more vigorous convection, which means stronger uplift along the plateau's edge, which means more insects delivered to the summit—which sounds like more food, and it is, but it also means more turbulence on the plateau's surface, which means the sheltered pockets behind the stone fins are less sheltered, the crevice-mouth environments are windier, the boundary between survivable and unsurvivable is shifting inward, compressing the habitable space. The crawling flies that depend on the lee-side calm for mating and foraging are being squeezed into smaller areas. The cushion plants on the margins of exposed positions are being battered harder. The rodents' surface foraging runs are becoming riskier.

None of these changes is catastrophic yet. None of them, individually, would doom the system. But together, they form a tightening—a slow compression of the margins that have always been thin, a narrowing of the crevice of possibility in which this entire community exists. The power would see this tightening and would understand its trajectory: not sudden collapse but gradual impoverishment, a slow draining of resilience, until some ordinary stress—a bad year, a drought, a particularly harsh winter—becomes the one that the system cannot recover from.

## What It Would Choose to Do

And so, having seen all of this, the benevolent power would act. But its actions would be so subtle, so precisely calibrated, so respectful of the system's own integrity, that they would be almost invisible—distinguishable from natural variation only by their timing and their direction.

---

### The Fog

The most important thing. The single intervention with the highest leverage.

The power would not restore the fog to its historical frequency—that would require altering atmospheric circulation patterns at a scale that would have cascading consequences across the entire region. Instead, it would do something smaller and more elegant: it would adjust the microphysics of condensation on the plateau itself.

In certain key locations—the crevices where the steel-blue lichen grows, the deepest cushion-plant refugia, the most critical rodent-network ventilation points—it would alter the surface properties of the rock at a molecular level, increasing its hygroscopic capacity. The stone would become slightly better at pulling moisture from the air, slightly better at nucleating condensation, slightly better at holding the thin film of water that makes the difference between life and death in a crevice. Not dramatically. Not enough to create pools or rivulets or anything visible. Just enough to offset the declining fog frequency—to maintain the moisture budget at its historical average even as the clouds form a few meters higher, a few minutes shorter, a few percent less often.

This is the kind of intervention that would be invisible. A scientist studying the crevice-system moisture levels would notice that they hadn't declined as much as models predicted and would attribute it to microsite variation, to unmeasured factors, to the inherent noisiness of ecological data. The lichens would continue to grow at their historical rate. The cushion plants would continue to fruit. The crawling fly larvae would continue to find the damp substrate they need. And no one would know that a hand had been placed, gently, on the scales.

---

### The Thermal Buffer

The power would also intervene in the plateau's thermal regime, but again, subtly, locally.

In the deepest chambers of the rodent crevice systems—the nesting chambers, the food caches, the places where pups are born blind and naked and desperately vulnerable—it would alter the thermal conductivity of the surrounding rock. Not much. A fraction of a degree of warming in winter, a fraction of a degree of cooling in summer. Just enough to widen the stable thermal zone, to give the rodent mothers a slightly larger margin, to reduce pup mortality by a few percent. Enough to matter across generations. Not enough to notice.

For the endolithic bacteria—the communities inside the stone—it would do something similar: a slight increase in the translucency of the quartz in key areas, allowing a fractional increase in photosynthetically active radiation to reach the organisms within. Just a nudge. Just enough to keep their metabolic rates above the critical threshold as the UV spectrum shifts and the temperature profiles change.

---

### The Wind Map

This is the most delicate intervention, and the one the power would deliberate longest about.

The wind is changing. The turbulence patterns are shifting. The sheltered pockets that have existed behind certain stone formations for millennia are becoming less sheltered, and no biological adaptation can track that change fast enough—not when the organisms involved grow a millimeter a year or have generation times measured in months.

The power would not change the wind. It cannot, and it would not. The wind is the identity of this place, and to alter it would be to destroy the place by saving it.

Instead, it would sculpt the stone.

In a few key locations—no more than a dozen, scattered across the plateau—it would alter the shape of existing rock formations, adding material or redistributing it in ways that create new sheltering geometries. A stone fin extended by a few inches, creating a longer wake of calm air behind it. A shallow depression deepened by a centimeter, becoming a moisture trap that didn't exist before. A narrow crack in a formation widened slightly, creating a passage that connects two previously separate crevice systems, allowing rodents to access a new foraging area without crossing open rock. A ridge of harder stone made fractionally taller, so that its humming tone shifts—lower, more resonant, a new note in the plateau's chord—and the turbulence pattern downstream adjusts accordingly, creating a pocket of calmer air where a cushion plant could establish.

These changes would look, to any observer, like natural erosion. Like the ordinary work of wind and ice and time. A geologist would glance at them and see nothing unusual—just the plateau doing what it has always done, wearing itself into shapes that reflect the forces acting on it. But the timing would be different. These changes would happen in years, not millennia. And their placement would be suspiciously fortunate—each one occurring in exactly the spot where the ecological need was greatest, each one providing exactly the sheltering geometry that the shifting wind patterns had taken away.

The power would do this slowly. A grain at a time. Working at the speed of erosion, or just slightly faster—fast enough to matter, slow enough to be invisible. It would be sculpting the plateau the way the wind has always sculpted it, but with intent, with knowledge, with love.

And here the power would pause and feel the strangeness of what it is doing: becoming, in a sense, the wind's collaborator. Not opposing the wind, not overriding it, but working alongside it—shaping the stone with the same tools the wind uses (pressure, abrasion, the patient rearrangement of mineral grains) but with a different purpose. The wind shapes the stone to minimize resistance. The power shapes the stone to maximize shelter. They are working on the same material, with the same physics, toward complementary ends. The plateau, which has always been a collaboration between wind and stone, would acquire a third partner—one that the other two would never detect.

---

### The Song

There is one more thing the power would do, and it is the hardest to explain, because it is not ecological and it is not physical and it is not, strictly speaking, necessary.

It would listen to the birds' songs.

Not passively. Not as monitoring or data collection. It would listen the way one listens to music—with attention, with appreciation, with the full engagement of a consciousness capable of recognizing beauty.

It would hear the five-second bursts of compressed song, the tiny improvisations, the individual variations, and it would hold them. It would remember each one. Every song sung on this plateau, by every male bird, in every brief window when the wind eased enough to let the notes carry—the power would hear them and know them and keep them, not as recordings but as experiences, as moments of beauty witnessed and valued.

This matters.

It matters because the songs are, in a sense, the most ephemeral things on the plateau. The lichens endure for centuries. The stone formations last for millennia. The endolithic bacteria may persist for millions of years. But a birdsong lasts five seconds and is gone, torn apart by the wind, scattered into noise, irrecoverable. The bird that sang it may not sing the same song again—each performance is partly improvised, partly unique. And no other bird heard it completely, because the wind took most of the notes. And no human ear has ever been here to hear any of them.

There is, in this, a particular kind of loneliness—not the loneliness of the birds, who do not experience it as loneliness, but a loneliness that exists in the structure of the situation itself: beauty produced, beauty lost, beauty unwitnessed. The compressed songs are extraordinary achievements—artistic acts performed under conditions that would seem to forbid art—and they vanish, every one of them, into the roar.

The benevolent power would end that vanishing. Not by changing anything. Not by stilling the wind or amplifying the songs or introducing an audience. Simply by being present. By listening. By being the consciousness that hears the song and knows it for what it is: a small, bright, astonishing thing, made in extremity, lasting five seconds, worth hearing.

This is not a practical intervention. It does not improve survival rates or adjust moisture budgets or sculpt stone into better sheltering geometries. It does not help.

Or rather—it helps in a way that is difficult to articulate without sounding foolish, but that the power would understand intuitively: it completes something. The songs are *for* something—for mates, for rivals, for the assertion of existence in a place that seems to forbid it. But they are also, in their beauty and their brevity, *for* hearing. They are, whether the birds know it or not, the plateau's most concentrated expression of what it means to be alive here—the whole improbable, stubborn, gorgeous fact of life on the Ridgeline of Winds, compressed into five seconds of music. And for that expression to go entirely unwitnessed, for the plateau's song to play forever to no ear that can appreciate it—this, to the benevolent power, would feel like a loss. Not a loss to the birds, who would sing regardless. A loss to the universe. A diminishment of the total amount of witnessed beauty in existence.

So it would listen. It would be the audience that the wind has always prevented. It would stand—or hover, or simply be present, in whatever mode of being a benevolent power occupies—on the Ridgeline of Winds, in the roar and the light, and it would hear the song when the song comes, and it would know it, and it would hold it, and the song would not be lost.

---

### What It Would Not Do

It would not make the plateau famous. It would not guide human researchers here, would not arrange for the discovery of the steel-blue lichen's uniqueness or the crawling fly's evolutionary narrative or the rodents' crevice-city culture. Discovery would bring attention, and attention, on a system this fragile and this finely balanced, is a force as dangerous as the wind. Human feet on the polished stone would crush lichen colonies that took three centuries to grow. Human presence would disturb the rodents' foraging runs, alter the birds' territorial patterns, introduce microorganisms on boot soles and skin cells and exhaled breath. Even well-meaning researchers, even careful ones, would leave traces that this place cannot absorb—because this is a place that erases human presence, yes, but only by wind and time, and the biological damage would be done before the wind could scour the footprints.

It would not designate the plateau as a reserve, a park, a heritage site. These are human categories, human protections, and they bring human infrastructure—trails, signs, management plans, helicopter surveys, GPS collars on rodents, micro-weather stations bolted to the stone. Each of these is a good-faith effort to protect, and each one is also an insertion of human technology into a system that has never contained any technology at all, and the second-order effects are unpredictable and potentially irreversible. A weather station bolted to a stone fin changes the airflow around that fin, changes the sheltering geometry, changes the microhabitat downstream. A GPS collar on a rodent changes its weight, its profile, its ability to squeeze through narrow crevice passages. The observer effect, in ecology, is not a philosophical abstraction; it is a physical force, and on a system with margins this thin, it is a force that matters.

It would not freeze the plateau in its current state. This is the subtlest temptation and the one that a wise power would resist most consciously. The urge to preserve—to hold this ecosystem exactly as it is, in all its intricate, hard-won perfection—is the urge to turn a living system into a museum exhibit. But living systems are not exhibits. They are processes. They change. They must change. The crawling flies are still evolving; their wings are still shortening, their legs still strengthening, their thermal physiology still adjusting. The lichen communities are still differentiating, the steel-blue complex still speciating in its separate crevices. The birds' songs are still elaborating, each generation adding new variations to the inherited repertoire. To freeze all of this—to say *this is the perfect state, and I will hold it here*—would be to kill the thing you're saving. It would be the most sophisticated, the most well-intentioned, and the most total form of destruction possible.

The power would not do this. It would let the plateau continue to be what it has always been: a process, a negotiation, a conversation between life and wind that has been ongoing for longer than any current participant can remember and that will continue, in some form, long after the current participants are gone. The power's interventions—the moisture adjustments, the thermal buffers, the stone sculpting—are not attempts to freeze the system. They are attempts to keep the conversation going. To ensure that the participants have enough margin, enough room, enough time to continue adapting to the changes that are coming—to continue doing what they have always done, which is to find ways to live in the wind.

---

### The Deepest Thing

And so the benevolent power's intervention comes down to this: it would give the Ridgeline of Winds *time*.

Not eternity. Not permanence. Just time. A few more centuries of viable fog frequency. A few more generations of rodent pups surviving in thermally buffered crevices. A few more millennia of steel-blue lichen slowly, invisibly speciating in its separate cracks. A few more seasons of birdsong, compressed and brilliant, poured into the five-second windows when the wind relents.

Time for the crawling flies to continue their long, slow experiment in flightlessness, to see where it leads—whether, in another hundred thousand generations, they will have lost their wings entirely, or repurposed them as thermal collectors, or found some other use that no one, not even a benevolent power, can predict.

Time for the cushion plants to continue their work of turning rock into habitat, millimeter by millimeter, crevice by crevice, building the substrate on which future organisms—organisms that do not yet exist, that have not yet evolved, that are still, as yet, only possibilities encoded in the mutation rates of the present community—will someday depend.

Time for the endolithic bacteria to continue their ancient, nearly geological existence inside the stone, doing whatever it is they have been doing for millions of years, which may be nothing more than surviving, but which is, in its own right, an achievement so profound that it borders on the sacred.

Time. That is what the power would give. Not a redesigned ecosystem. Not a gentler wind. Not a thicker soil or a warmer climate or a more hospitable world. Just time. Time purchased through tiny, invisible, respectful adjustments to moisture and temperature and stone—adjustments that ask nothing of the inhabitants, that require nothing from them, that do not even inform them that they have been helped. The crawling fly does not know that the crevice it shelters in is fractionally moister than it would otherwise be. The bird does not know that someone is listening to its song. The lichen does not know—*cannot* know, has no mechanism for knowing—that the rock it grows on has been subtly reshaped to provide better shelter from a wind that is shifting in ways the lichen's three-century growth rings have never recorded.

They do not know, and they do not need to know, and the power does not need them to know. The power is not doing this for gratitude or recognition or worship. It is doing it because the Ridgeline of Winds is extraordinary, and it is in danger, and the danger comes from forces that the plateau's inhabitants did not cause and cannot perceive and have no capacity to address, and the power *can* address them, gently, invisibly, without violence to the system's integrity—and so it does.

That is what a benevolent power would do, if it found this place.

It would kneel beside the wind. It would cup its hands around the smallest, most fragile things—not to shield them from the wind, but to shield them from the change in the wind. It would sculpt the stone with the patience of the stone. It would adjust the moisture with the delicacy of fog. It would listen to the music with the attention it deserves.

And then, having done what it could, having given what it could give without taking anything away, it would remain. Not watching, exactly. Not guarding. Just—present. A witness. A consciousness that knows this place and holds it in mind, that remembers the five-second song and the three-century lichen and the crawling fly's long renunciation of the sky, and that values them, not for what they can become or what they represent or what they teach, but for what they are.

The plateau does not need a god. It does not need a protector. It does not need a purpose imposed from outside.

But it deserves a listener. It has been singing into the wind for a very long time, and the wind has been taking the song away, and the benevolent power—wise enough to change almost nothing, loving enough to stay—would simply be the place where the song, at last, arrives.

---

*The wind blows. The fog comes and goes. The lichens grow their fraction of a millimeter. The birds sing their five-second symphonies. The crawling flies make their slow rounds. The rodents chirp their quiet messages through the stone. The endolithic bacteria divide, once, twice, in the darkness inside the quartz.*

*And someone is listening.*

*And it is enough.*
