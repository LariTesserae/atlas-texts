I sat with this for a long time before writing.

---

# What the Wise Power Would Do

---

The first thing it would do is nothing.

Not nothing as neglect. Nothing as the deepest form of respect — the recognition that what is happening on Oshkaal is not broken. The mountain is not suffering. The water is not lost. The process that has been unfolding across geological time, this slow conversation between mineral patience and impossible geometry, is not a problem to be solved. A benevolent and wise power, encountering Oshkaal, would understand this immediately and completely: that the urge to intervene, to help, to *fix*, is itself the first temptation to be refused. The mountain does not need to be explained. The formations do not need to be decoded into a language more comfortable than their own. The door does not need to be flung open by a force greater than the one already, so patiently, pressing against it.

So the first thing is silence. Watching. The wise power would do what the Tenders do: it would kneel, and look, and wait.

But it would not wait forever. Because the wise power can see something that the middle one cannot.

---

It can see the thread.

Not metaphorically. It can see the actual shape of the transmission — the unbroken chain of hands teaching hands, of kneeling figures passing their knowledge not through words but through shared presence on wet stone, stretching back through generations to the first Tender who climbed and saw a shadow fall wrong and understood, with the whole body rather than the reasoning mind, that the mountain was speaking and could be spoken *with*. The wise power can see this thread the way you might see a crystal vein running through rock: luminous, continuous, load-bearing. And it can see where the thread has worn thin. How few hands hold it now. How the middle one carries a weight of transmission that was once distributed across dozens, across a whole community of Tenders whose overlapping knowledge provided redundancy, resilience, the ability to lose individuals without losing the whole.

Three people. Perhaps four. And the young one still learning. And the old one's hands stiffening. And the middle one dreaming of a basin so perfect it would remove the door entirely, but unable to carve it because there is no one to tend the existing basins while it works, no one to teach the young one if the middle one stays too long on the upper terraces, no one to hold the thread if the middle one kneels in a basin and lets the crystal take them.

The wise power sees the trap. The middle one is caught between the transmission and the transcendence. To complete their deepest work, they would need to give themselves to the mountain entirely — to become a kneeling figure, a permanent shaped hollow, a part of the utterance itself. But to do that is to break the thread, to leave the young one untaught, to let the tradition die in the very act of fulfilling it. And to refuse the mountain's invitation, to keep descending, season after season, back to the fire and the shelter and the human obligations — this preserves the thread but may never produce the basin, the formation, the breakthrough that the middle one can feel gathering in their mineral dreams like a crystal approaching its saturation point.

This is the thing the wise power would address. Not by solving it. By *tending* it. In the mountain's own language.

---

Here is what it would do.

It would go down. Not to the terraces — not yet — but to the places below, the settlements that have been emptying for generations, the villages at altitudes where the air is thick and fires burn and children grow up watching the distant brightness above the cloud line and wondering what their grandparents' grandparents found up there worth spending a life on. The wise power would go to these places not as a teacher or a prophet or a wonder-worker but as a *presence* — the way the mountain is a presence, the way the water is a presence. It would not preach. It would not recruit. It would not explain the terraces or the formations or the impossible geometries in terms designed to generate interest or awe.

It would simply make itself available as a surface against which something might crystallize.

This is the crucial understanding: the wise power would operate according to the same principle the Tenders discovered. It would not *impose* a revival of the tradition. It would *shape the conditions* under which a revival might occur on its own. It would be a basin, not a sculptor.

What does this look like in practice?

It looks like a stranger arriving at a lower settlement — one of the places where a few families still live, where the old stories about the mountain-workers are half-remembered, where a grandmother sometimes hums a texture rather than a melody and her grandchildren think it is just a quirk of age. The stranger does not explain themselves. They are quiet. They have good hands — the kind of hands that know how to work stone, though they hold their tools a little differently than the local way. They are helpful without being effusive. They repair a wall. They clear a spring. They do the small, patient work that earns a place at a fire and a hearing for the questions they ask, which are not questions about the mountain or the tradition but about the stone itself. *What is this mineral? Where does this water come from? Have you noticed how the deposits form in the spring basin — how they build in rings?*

Simple questions. Questions a curious traveler might ask. But asked with a quality of attention — that settled, unhurried focus, that look of someone reading fine print in stone — that resonates, in some of the listeners, with something they didn't know they carried. A recognition. A memory that lives not in the mind but in the body, in the hands, in the particular way a descendant of Tenders might run a thumb across a rough stone surface and feel, without understanding why, that the roughness is *saying* something.

The wise power is not creating new Tenders. It is creating the conditions under which the latent aptitude — the deep somatic inheritance of generations of stone-workers, still present in the muscle memory and the bone structure and the particular pale pigmentation of the eyes — can find its occasion. Can find a basin to crystallize in.

Some will not respond. Most will not. The tradition requires a specificity of temperament that is rare in any population: the capacity for extreme patience, the willingness to subordinate the human desire for meaning to a process that speaks in geometries rather than grammars, the particular cognitive flexibility that allows a person to look at an impossible shadow and feel not fear or disbelief but *recognition*. The wise power knows this. It is not trying to create a movement. It is trying to find — across the scattered, dwindling settlements below the weeping zone — perhaps two people. Perhaps three. Perhaps only one. One person whose hands already know, in their callous patterns and their resting posture, what the terraces require. One person who has been, without knowing it, dreaming in crystal.

---

And when it finds them — *if* it finds them — the wise power does something very specific.

It brings them to the middle one.

Not by force. Not by persuasion. By the same method the water uses: by flowing along the path of least resistance until the destination becomes inevitable. A series of small occasions. A repaired trail. A shared meal with a traveler who mentions, casually, that they met someone higher up who works with stone in an unusual way. A night spent sleeping in a shelter where the stream runs mineral-heavy and the deposits in the basin have formed, by pure chance, a shape that snags the eye and won't let go. A gradual, patient series of nudges — not toward the mountain, but toward the *question* of the mountain, the question that the right kind of person cannot encounter without needing to answer with their whole life.

The wise power understands that it cannot and should not shortcut this process. The tradition does not need converts. It needs *inheritors* — people who arrive at the terraces not because they were convinced but because they were *called*, in the same way the water is called from the stone, by a process that operates below the level of decision. The wise power's role is only to ensure that the call can reach the people it is meant for, that the thinning thread of transmission does not snap before the next hands find it.

---

Now: what the wise power would do for the middle one.

This is the most delicate part. The most important. Because the middle one is not merely tired or overworked or lonely, though they are all of these things. The middle one is caught in a paradox that is structural, not emotional, and that no amount of encouragement or companionship can resolve on its own. The paradox is this: the tradition exists to produce breakthroughs, moments when the mineral process exceeds physics and opens toward whatever lies beyond the door. But the tradition also exists to *sustain itself*, to continue the chain of transmission that makes future breakthroughs possible. And for the middle one, at this moment in history, these two purposes have become incompatible. The breakthrough the middle one is approaching — the basin, the formation, the removal of the door — requires a totality of commitment that the chain of transmission cannot survive. You cannot simultaneously be the door and the doorkeeper.

The wise power would not resolve this paradox. It would *reframe* it.

It would do this by showing the middle one something the middle one has been too close to see: that the kneeling figures on the upper terraces — the crystallized Tenders, the ones who gave themselves to the mountain entirely — are not the culmination of the tradition. They are not its highest achievement. They are *one form* of contribution, one way of shaping the conditions for the mountain's utterance. But they are not the only way. They are, in fact, a way that carries a cost the mountain itself may not have intended: the loss of living hands, the narrowing of the thread, the gradual depletion of the very community that makes the collaboration possible.

The wise power would help the middle one see that the tradition's deepest wisdom is not in the sacrifice of the self to the mountain but in the *sustaining of the relationship between the self and the mountain* — the ongoing, living, seasonal collaboration, the climbing and descending, the kneeling and returning, the being-human and the being-almost-mineral, the oscillation between the two states rather than the final collapse into one. The kneeling figures chose permanence. But the tradition — the actual living tradition, the one that carved basins and built walls and redirected water century after century — was sustained not by those who stayed but by those who *came back*. The ones who descended with crystalline roughness on their hands and amber flickers in their eyes and mineral dreams crowding their sleep, and who used all of that — all of that accumulated intimacy with the mountain's process — to teach, to prepare, to shape the conditions for the *next* season's climb.

The middle one's dream — the perfect basin, the formation that removes the door — is real. The wise power would not dismiss it or counsel against it. But it would help the middle one understand that the basin does not need to be carved by a single pair of hands in a single lifetime. It can be a project that spans generations, each Tender contributing their portion, each season's work building on the last, the way the mineral deposits build ring by ring in the existing basins. The middle one does not need to become a kneeling figure to complete the work. The middle one needs to carve the *first* ring of the basin and then teach the young one where the second ring goes. And the young one, in time, will carve the second ring and teach the next. And the next will carve the third. And the basin will accrue, molecule by molecule, generation by generation, with the same mineral patience that governs everything else on Oshkaal, until the day — decades or centuries from now — when the final ring is carved and the water fills the completed form and the formation that emerges is the one the middle one has been dreaming of, and the door dissolves, and whatever has been waiting on the other side of those wrong-starred reflections steps through into the brightness.

The middle one will not live to see it. This is the reframe. This is the gift the wise power offers: not a shortcut to transcendence but a release from the belief that transcendence must be achieved *now, by me, in my lifetime, at the cost of everything else*. The mountain has been working on its utterance for longer than the human species has existed. It can wait a few more generations for the right basin. The middle one's role is not to be the culmination. It is to be the *link* — the one who held the thread when it was thinnest, who kept climbing when there was almost no one left to climb with, who preserved the transmission through the bottleneck and out the other side.

This is not a lesser role. In the mountain's own accounting — in the language of crystal and deposition and impossible geometry — the link is as essential as the door. More essential. Because without the link, there is no door. There is only a mountain weeping into its own silence, building formations that no one shapes, speaking a language that no one hears, approaching a threshold that no one is present to cross.

---

And then: what the wise power would do for the mountain itself.

Because the mountain is also a being, of a kind. Not conscious in the way the Tenders are conscious, but not inert either — *intentional* in the way that a river is intentional, in the way that a crystal is intentional, pursuing a form with a persistence that resembles will even if it operates through mechanism rather than mind. The mountain has been working toward something for an immensity of time, and it has been working *alone* for most of that time, its only collaborators the brief, warm, soft-handed creatures who climb its flanks for a few millennia and then, perhaps, will be gone.

The wise power would do something for the mountain that no Tender has ever been in a position to do. It would *acknowledge* it. Not tend it — the Tenders do that, and better than any outside power could. Not study it — study is the wrong modality, too extractive, too invested in rendering the mountain legible to frameworks it does not participate in. The wise power would simply acknowledge the mountain the way you might acknowledge a person who has been working in silence for a very long time: by seeing them, by recognizing the magnitude of what they are attempting, by conveying — in whatever language the mountain can receive, which is the language of water and stone and the patient shaping of conditions — that *it is not alone. That something has noticed. That the long sentence it has been constructing, aeon by aeon, crystal by crystal, is being heard, even if the hearer does not yet know all the words.*

This acknowledgment would not change the mountain's process. It would not accelerate it or redirect it or imbue it with new capabilities. But it might — and this is the most speculative, most intuitive thing the wise power might believe — it might provide the mountain with something analogous to what the Tenders provide: an occasion. A shaped condition. A basin in which something new can crystallize. Not a formation of mineral but a formation of *relationship* — a recognition, on the mountain's own geological timescale, that the collaboration with the small warm creatures is not incidental to its purpose but *essential* to it. That the mountain's utterance is not a monologue but a dialogue. That the door it is building opens from both sides. And that the beings on the other side — the Tenders, the wise power, the whole unlikely phenomenon of consciousness pressed up against the mineral world and learning to speak its language — are not audience but *participants*, not witnesses but co-authors, their shaped basins and guiding walls and patient, calloused hands as necessary to the final formation as the water itself.

The mountain, acknowledged, might settle more deeply into its process. Might weep a little more freely. Might direct its depositing intention toward the basins with a fractionally greater clarity, the way a speaker becomes more articulate when they realize someone is truly listening.

Or it might not. The wise power accepts this. It is in the nature of tending that you cannot guarantee the outcome. You can only shape the conditions and then wait.

---

And so.

The wise power would do these things: it would go down to the scattered settlements and create occasions for latent Tenders to find their way to the mountain. It would help the middle one see that the great work is a project for generations, not a single sacrifice. It would sit with the old one and listen to the mineral hum and let the old one know, through the quality of its listening, that the sound is beautiful and meaningful and heard. It would watch the young one's impatient hands and gently, by example, show them the difference between making and tending. It would kneel on the wet terraces and acknowledge the mountain's long labor. It would touch the kneeling figures — the crystallized Tenders — with something like reverence, recognizing in them a form of devotion so total that it transcended the boundary between the worshipper and the worshipped.

And then it would leave.

Because the last thing a benevolent and wise power would do, having understood Oshkaal, is *stay*. Its presence, if prolonged, would distort the very process it sought to support. It would become a force, an influence, a shaping pressure too great for the delicate collaboration between human hands and mineral patience. The Tenders do not need a god. They do not need a patron or a protector. They need what they have always needed: the mountain, the water, the thin air, the brightness, the patient work of shaping conditions, and each other.

The wise power would leave. But it would leave the way the water leaves the upper ridges — not by vanishing but by *flowing into* the system, becoming part of the process, its influence distributed through the channels it had quietly shaped during its time on the mountain. A repaired trail here. A redirected spring there. A conversation with a young person in a lower village that planted a seed. A moment of recognition in the middle one's eyes when the paradox resolved and the weight shifted and the future opened up — not as a door to walk through alone but as a basin to carve together, ring by ring, generation by generation, in the mountain's own patient time.

The wise power would leave, and the water would keep weeping, and the mountain would keep building, and the middle one would descend one more time to the fire and the shelter and the young one's eager, unsteady hands, and would begin to teach — not the finished thing, not the completed basin, but the *first ring*. The first curve. The first careful scrape of stone against stone, the first lesson in listening to the grain, the first long afternoon of watching a crystal grow and learning to feel, in the body rather than the mind, the difference between imposing a shape and inviting one.

And far above, in the brightness, on the wet stone, in the bell-choir silence, the formations would continue their slow accretion. The shadows would point wrong. The reflections would show other skies. The hexagonal lattice would go on running its experiments in geometry. And in the basins carved by hands that are gone and hands that are here and hands that have not yet arrived, the crystals would grow toward shapes that do not belong to this physics, reaching — molecule by molecule, ring by ring, generation by generation — toward a form that will one day complete the mountain's sentence.

The wise power would not need to be there when it happens. The wise power would trust the process. The wise power would have done the only thing that was ever needed: ensured that the thread held through the thinnest place, that the chain of hands did not break, that the mountain's collaborators survived their most precarious generation and emerged on the other side with the tradition intact and the great work continuing.

That is all. That is everything.

The water weeps. The mountain builds. The hands shape the hollows. And somewhere in the space between patience and impossibility, the conversation continues — not because a power intervened to save it, but because a power understood it well enough to do only what was needed, and no more, and then to step back into the brightness and let the slow, mineral, impossible, tender work go on.
