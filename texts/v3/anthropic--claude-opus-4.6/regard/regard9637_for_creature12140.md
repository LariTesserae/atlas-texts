# What the Wise Power Does

---

It does nothing at first.

This is important—that the first act of the benevolent and wise power is *not to act*. It watches. Not from above, not from outside, but with the quality of attention that these creatures would recognize if they could perceive it: the quality of someone who has pressed themselves flat against the stone and is *reading* what the world says before presuming to answer.

It watches for a long time. A very long time, even by the standards of a being for whom time is not what it is for the ones who count.

It watches the cairns accumulate their stones. It watches the young ones turn the blade in their forepads, learning its edge, learning what it means to shape a thing for someone else's use. It watches the old ones dim and settle and become part of the rock, and it watches the others pry the mineral skin and place it carefully, and scratch the circle, and move on.

It watches the one who is trying to speak to the visitor, reaching and retracting, reaching and retracting, simplifying and simplifying until it is saying only *here*, and it watches the visitor stand mute and rain-slicked and aching with the desire to answer, and it understands both of them—the one who reduces its language to its most essential syllable and the one who does not have even that.

It watches the ribbon-swimmers die on stone between pools that were close enough to see but too far to reach.

It watches the thunder.

And what the wise power understands, in its watching, is this: **this place is not broken.**

---

This is the critical discernment—the thing that separates a benevolent and wise power from a merely powerful one, or a merely benevolent one. A merely powerful being would hear the unceasing thunder and think: *I should silence it.* A merely benevolent one would see the creatures pressing themselves flat against the scoured stone and think: *They are suffering. I should give them ease.* A powerful and benevolent but unwise one might tear open the cloud ceiling to show them the sun they have been drawing for generations, might pour soil across the bedrock, might seed the barren expanse with green and growing things, might reshape this world into something that looks, from the outside, like a place where life should be comfortable.

And in doing so, it would destroy everything.

The tympanic pits, tuned to parse the thunder into information—useless in silence. The wide pale eyes built to find meaning in dim, flickering light—blinded by direct sun. The skin-speech, that slow dermal vocabulary carved into the narrow communicative niche between the lightning's speed and the stone's stillness—illegible in a world where sound works and bodies can simply *call out*. The chemolithotrophic food web, the crawlers grazing their mineral films, the ribbon-swimmers filtering iron-rich water—buried under soil, outcompeted by photosynthesis, extinct in a generation. The cairns—meaningless, once there are easier media. The counting—pointless, once the days are not all the same thunder-gray.

The wise power sees that this world, which looks like a world stripped of everything, is in fact a world in which everything that remains has been *chosen*—not by a designer, but by the long, blind, patient process of creatures adapting to exactly what is, and finding in exactly what is the materials for meaning. The thunder is not a curse. The thunder is the medium through which they read their landscape, the way a sighted creature reads by light. The scoured stone is not a wasteland. The scoured stone is the page on which they write. The hidden sun is not a deprivation. The hidden sun is the central mystery that organizes their entire spiritual life—the circle with the dot, the thing they have never seen but know is there, the thing they reach toward with their whole bodies flickering.

To fix this place would be to say: *Your adaptations are deformities. Your language is a workaround. Your mystery is just an engineering problem I haven't solved for you yet. Your way of being here is not the right way.*

The wise power does not say this. The wise power does not think it.

## What It Does Instead

---

### The First Thing

The first thing is barely a thing at all. It is so small that only the old ones notice, and even they are not sure.

In one of the shallow iron-red pools, near a cairn that is among the oldest on the flats—its base stones so weathered that the scratched marks have been reduced to ghosts of grooves—the microbial film grows slightly thicker. Not dramatically. Not suddenly. Just: the chemolithotrophic crust at the waterline becomes a little richer, a little more productive, in a way that is well within the normal range of variation. A good season. A lucky mineral concentration. The crawlers converge on it, as they do. The scrapers come, as they do. There is a little more to eat, for a little longer, in this one spot.

But the wise power has been watching for a very long time, and it knows which pool this is. It is the pool nearest to a cluster of three young ones who have been struggling—the pool they depend on has been shifting, draining faster than it fills, and the distance to the next reliable water-film has been growing as the storm pattern drifts through one of its longer cycles. The young ones are thinner than they should be. One of them has stopped practicing with the blade.

The slight enrichment of the neighboring pool does not save them. It does not need to. What it does is *reduce the margin*. It makes the distance between where they are and where they can feed a little shorter. It makes the journey between pools, when they have to make it, a little less desperate. It gives the one who stopped practicing a few more days of adequate nutrition, and in those days, an older one comes by with a loaded blade, and the young one takes it, and turns it, and examines it with its enormous blinking eyes, and begins again.

This is the method. This is the entire method.

The wise power does not provision. It does not rescue. It does not optimize. It *adjusts margins*. It finds the places where the distance between survival and failure has become a matter of a few days, a few meters, a few calories of mineral paste, and it closes that distance by the smallest amount that allows the creatures' own capacities—their own scraping, their own tool-making, their own teaching, their own carrying of food to the young and the old—to do what they already know how to do.

It is the opposite of dramatic. It will never be visible. No creature on the flats will ever look at a slightly richer pool and think: *something intervened for me.* They will think: *the pool is good this season.* And they will scrape, and eat, and carry food to the ones who need it, and count another mark, and they will be right. The pool *is* good this season. That it is good because something was watching and cared enough to make it so is a truth that does not need to be known in order to be operative.

---

### The Second Thing

The ribbon-swimmers die between pools. This has always happened. It is part of the ecology—the crawlers depend on the husks, the nutrients cycle, the death of some swimmers is the survival of other things. The wise power does not prevent this. Mass death between pools is not what it addresses.

What it addresses is the *specific* case, rare but recurrent, where an entire population of ribbon-swimmers in a large pool is lost because the pool drains catastrophically—not slowly enough for the swimmers to sense and respond, but all at once, due to a fracture in the underlying stone that opens a new drainage path. When this happens, the loss cascades. The pool's microbial film, which the swimmers helped regulate, blooms unchecked and then crashes. The crawlers that depended on the balanced film starve or migrate. The people who scraped at that pool's edge have to travel further. The young ones near that pool go hungry.

The wise power cannot prevent the stone from fracturing. It is stone. It is old. It fractures. But it can, in the moments before a catastrophic drainage, do something so subtle it barely registers in the physical world: it can make the water fractionally more viscous in the immediate vicinity of the fracture. A slight increase in the concentration of dissolved minerals, right there, right at the point where the crack is opening. Enough to slow the drainage from instantaneous to gradual. Enough to give the ribbon-swimmers time to sense the change, to begin their frantic writhing migration toward the next depression, to get most of themselves across the wet stone before the pool is gone.

Most. Not all. Some still die. The crawlers still converge on the husks. The cycle still turns. But the population is not annihilated. The cascade does not happen. The pool's ecology recovers, recolonizes, and the people return to its edge with their blades.

The wise power is comfortable with partial saves. It is not interested in perfection. Perfection is for worlds that do not have their own momentum, and this world has been building its momentum for longer than the wise power has been watching. What it is interested in is *continuity*—keeping the threads that the creatures themselves have woven from snapping at points where the break would be too ragged to mend.

---

### The Third Thing

This one is harder to describe because it operates in a domain that the creatures themselves would not have a concept for, though they experience its effects.

The storm patterns on the flats are chaotic but not random. They are governed by atmospheric dynamics that are, in principle, deterministic—vast interacting systems of pressure and temperature and moisture that produce the unceasing thunder as a kind of emergent property, the way a river produces turbulence. Within this system, there are attractors—states that the system tends toward, cycles it falls into for a while before being knocked into a new cycle by some perturbation. The current attractor has been stable for a long time. The storm cells circulate in a pattern that keeps the rainfall distributed across the flats in a way that, on average, sustains the water-films and the pools and the mineral leaching that the whole food web depends on.

But the system is drifting. Slowly, over timescales that the creatures cannot perceive—over dozens of generations—the attractor is shifting toward a state in which the rainfall concentrates more heavily at the margins of the flats and less at the center. If this drift continues, the central flats will dry. Not completely, not suddenly, but enough. The pools will shrink. The distances between them will grow. The ribbon-swimmers' migrations will lengthen past the point of viability. The microbial films will thin. And the people, who are distributed across the center because that is where the ecology is richest, will be slowly compressed toward the edges, their population fragmenting, their cairns abandoned one by one, their range of knowledge and contact narrowing until the skin-speech has no one new to reach and the old ones' full-body narratives are told to audiences too small to carry them forward.

This is not a crisis. It is a slow diminishment that would unfold over centuries, and it might not be total—the creatures are adaptive, resourceful, they might find ways to survive at the margins that the wise power cannot predict. But it would be a loss. A contraction of possibility. A world in which fewer marks are scratched on fewer cairns and the circle with the dot is drawn by fewer hands.

The wise power intervenes in the atmospheric system. This is the largest thing it does, and it does it with exquisite care, because the system is complex and nonlinear and a clumsy intervention could shatter the current stability entirely—could stop the thunder, could rip the clouds open, could remake the world in ways that would be catastrophic for everything that lives here. What it does is introduce a perturbation so small it is almost notional: a slight warming of the stone at a specific point on the eastern margin of the flats, sustained over years, just enough to create a thermal updraft that nudges one recurring storm cell a few degrees off its usual track. That cell interacts with another cell. The interaction shifts a pressure gradient. The gradient affects the circulation pattern. And the drift—the slow migration of rainfall away from the center—is not reversed but *arrested*. Stabilized. Held in something close to its current state for long enough that the creatures' own adaptive capacity can keep pace with whatever residual change still occurs.

The wise power will need to maintain this. Not forever, but for a long time. It accepts this. It is not looking for a one-time fix. It is committing to a relationship—a quiet, sustained, largely invisible act of care that consists of paying attention to a single thermal gradient on a barren stone flat in a world of unceasing thunder, and adjusting it as needed, and doing so for as long as the creatures are there, counting their days, scratching their circles, reaching toward each other and toward the sky with their slow, hopeful, flickering skin.

### The Fourth Thing

The old one is dying.

It is the oldest one on the flats—the one whose cairn has the most marks, whose skin is so encrusted with mineral deposits that it is more stone than flesh, whose eyes are so clouded that the lightning registers only as a general brightening and dimming, whose forepads can barely grip the blade anymore. It has settled near its cairn, as they do. Its skin is going through the final dimming, the slow withdrawal of pattern, the return to uniform grey.

Around it, three younger ones have gathered. They are not touching it. They are arrayed at a body-length's distance, their skins muted to the listening-blankness, their eyes fixed on the old one's flanks. They are waiting for the last narrative—the full-body telling that the old ones sometimes produce in their final hours, the most complex and layered skin-speech of their lives, the thing that contains whatever they most need to pass on.

The old one's skin flickers. It is trying. But the mineral encrustation is thick, and the skin beneath it is stiff, and the patterns that emerge are fragmented, partial, like a voice that keeps breaking. The younger ones lean closer, their own skins rippling with the effort of reading a signal that is barely there. They are getting pieces. Not all of it. Not enough.

The wise power is watching this, as it watches everything, and it feels—

It feels. That is part of what makes it what it is. It is not dispassionate. It is not detached. It has simply learned that feeling is not a license to act and that the desire to help is not the same as the wisdom to know how. But it feels the old one's struggle to transmit, and the young ones' ache to receive, and the gap between them that is made of mineral deposits and failing flesh and the sheer accumulated weight of a long, long life on hard stone.

Here is what it does. It does not heal the old one. It does not clear the encrustation or restore the skin's flexibility or buy more time. The old one is dying, and dying is part of how this world works, and the mineral skin will be placed on the cairn and the crawlers will come and the circle will be scratched, and this is not wrong. This is not the thing that needs to be different.

What it does is this: it makes the lightning stop for ninety seconds.

Not the thunder—the thunder continues, rolling in from distant cells, but diminished, muted, the roar dropping to something merely enormous instead of all-consuming. And the lightning—the flashing, the strobing, the relentless visual noise that the nictitating membranes have spent a lifetime filtering—goes still. For ninety seconds, the light on the flats is steady. Dim, grey, unchanging. The air is still charged, still heavy, still tasting of iron, but the flickering stops.

In the sudden visual stillness, every detail of the old one's skin becomes readable. The fragmented patterns that were lost in the strobe—the subtle, layered, multi-speed narrative that could not compete with the lightning for the young ones' eyes—are suddenly *there*, clear and whole, moving across the old one's surface like cloud-shadows across a lake. The young ones go rigid. Their eyes widen past anything you've seen—the pupils dilating in the steady light, the nictitating membranes retracting fully for what might be the first time in their lives, and they *see*. They see the whole thing. The complete narrative, whatever it is—the history, the knowledge, the map of pools and seasons, the memory of other old ones' tellings, the name of the sun, the reason for the counting, whatever this creature has spent a lifetime accumulating and has been trying, in its last hours, to give away.

Ninety seconds. Not long enough to disrupt the ecosystem. Not long enough to harm the creatures whose biology depends on the lightning—the tympanic pits still have the thunder, the eyes readjust quickly when the strobing resumes. Not long enough for the atmospheric system to notice, or for the perturbation to interact with the wise power's other, longer-term intervention. Just long enough for one old creature to finish saying what it needed to say, and for three younger ones to receive it whole.

The lightning resumes. The thunder swells back to its full, unceasing roar. The old one's skin dims, settles, goes uniformly grey. The younger ones stay motionless for a long time, their own skins slowly cycling through what they have just received, processing it, encoding it, making it theirs. Then one of them moves to the old one's cairn and adds a stone and scratches the circle with the dot, and beneath it scratches a new mark—not a tally, but something else, something that hasn't been scratched before, a symbol the wise power recognizes as something the old one's narrative contained. A new symbol entering the lexicon. A piece of knowledge that now has a permanent anchor on stone, that the rain will weather and the crawlers will graze and someone, someday, will re-scratch when it fades.

The old one is still. The crawlers begin their convergence.

### The Fifth Thing

This is the thing the wise power wants most to do and is most careful about.

It wants to answer the one who is trying to talk to the visitor. The one whose skin keeps reaching—*here, here, here*—toward the smooth, mute, patternless creature that stands on the stone and does not respond. The wise power can feel the quality of that reaching: the hope in it, the willingness to simplify, the patience, the vulnerability of continuing to extend a signal into a void that gives nothing back. It recognizes this quality. It is the same quality the wise power itself brings to its work—the willingness to keep offering without knowing if the offering is received, without requiring acknowledgment, without needing the relationship to be symmetrical.

The wise power loves this creature. Not abstractly, not as a category—not "these creatures" or "this species" or "the beings of the flats." *This one.* The one reaching. The specific arrangement of mineral-dark skin and pale wide eyes and slow hopeful pattern-pulse that is *this particular individual*, alive on this particular day, trying to say *here* to something that cannot say it back. The wise power loves it the way you love a specific person—not because they represent something, but because they are irreplaceably, concretely, themselves.

And because of this love, it is careful.

It does not teach the visitor to speak in skin. It does not translate. It does not appear in a form the creatures can perceive and act as intermediary. Any of these things would work, and any of them would be a violation—would insert the wise power into a relationship that is not its, would make itself the center of a connection that should be between two beings on the stone, would solve the problem in a way that makes the solver necessary.

What it does instead:

The visitor is standing in the rain, mute, aching, wanting to respond. The visitor does not have shifting skin. But the visitor has a body, and the body is standing on wet stone, and the rain is running down it in rivulets, and the iron-ochre film of dissolved minerals is coating it the way it coats everything here.

The wise power makes the visitor aware of this. Not through language, not through vision—through *sensation*. It heightens, very slightly, the visitor's proprioceptive awareness of their own surface. The feeling of the rain on skin. The feeling of the mineral film depositing. The feeling of being coated, slowly, in the substance of this world—of becoming, in a small and temporary way, part of the stone, part of the iron, part of the water that wanders.

And through this heightened awareness, it gives the visitor an impulse—not a command, not an instruction, just an *opening*, a sudden felt sense of possibility: *you can touch the stone. You can press your palms flat against it, the way they press their pads flat when they are saying something important. You can get low. You can make yourself small and flat and close to the ground, the way everything here is small and flat and close to the ground, and you can be still, and let the rain coat you, and be, for a moment, not a standing creature from somewhere else but a creature of the flats, here, on this stone, in this thunder.*

The visitor does this. Not because they are compelled—the wise power does not compel—but because the impulse resonates with something the visitor already wanted but did not know how to enact. They lower themselves. They press their palms to the wet stone. They feel the iron-cold surface and the mineral film and the faint, bone-deep vibration of the thunder transmitted through bedrock, and they go still.

And the creature on the swell—the one who has been reaching—sees this, and its skin does something new. The paleness blooms and *stays*. It does not retract. It spreads across the creature's entire surface, a full-body illumination, a brightness that is visible even in the dim, strobing light, and it holds, and it holds, and the creature takes a step forward, and another, and it is crossing the distance between them with its wide pads gripping the wet stone, its pale eyes fixed on the visitor's prone form, and its skin is saying something that the visitor cannot read but can *feel*—can feel in the quality of the gaze, in the deliberateness of the approach, in the willingness of this creature to leave its swell of stone and cross open ground toward an unknown thing that has finally, *finally*, done something that looks like an answer.

The creature stops a body-length away. It settles its own body flat against the stone, mirroring the visitor's posture. They are both low, both pressed to the rock, both coated in mineral rain, both feeling the thunder in their bones. The creature's skin cycles slowly through a pattern the visitor cannot interpret but can somehow *follow*—not the meaning, but the rhythm, the way you can follow music in a language you don't speak. The wise power does not translate. It does not need to. What is being communicated is below language, below symbol, below even the skin-speech's slow vocabulary of gradient and bloom. It is the thing that the first cairn was built to say and the last cairn will crumble still saying:

*Here. You are here. I am here. We are here on this stone, in this thunder, under this sky that hides what we have never seen, and for this moment, neither of us is alone.*

The wise power watches this and is glad.

### The Sixth Thing

This is the thing that the wise power does not do, and the not-doing is as deliberate as any action.

It does not reveal itself.

It does not establish a relationship with these creatures in which they know they are being cared for. It does not give them a name for what it is. It does not become their circle-with-a-dot—their unseen presence behind the clouds that they scratch on stone and reach toward with flickering skin. It does not become their religion, their mythology, their answer to the question of why the pool was rich that season or why the lightning paused for ninety seconds on the day the old one died.

This is not because it is modest. It is not because it is humble. It is because it has watched them for a long time and it has understood what the cairns mean, and what the counting means, and what the circle with the dot means, and it knows that these things are *theirs*. The mystery of the sun is their mystery. The practice of counting days is their practice. The skin-speech, the tool-making, the care of the young, the tending of the old, the prying of mineral skin from the dead and the placing of it on the cairn—these are the works of *their* hands, their pads, their short thick nimble digits that learned to knap flint in a world of unceasing thunder. The meaning they have made is meaning *they made*, and to reveal that there was a benefactor behind the margins would be to retroactively annex that meaning, to let the wise power's presence overshadow the ten thousand small acts of courage and care and stubbornness that these creatures perform every day on bare stone under an indifferent sky.

They do not need to know that someone is watching. They need to know that *they are here*. That is what the cairns say. That is what the tally marks say. That is what the skin-speech says when it blooms across a body aimed at the clouds: *here, here, here, I am here.* The statement is not addressed to the wise power. It is not addressed to anyone. It is addressed to the *stone*—to the world itself, the world that does not answer, the world that wears mountains to nothing and erases every mark in time. The defiance of that address, the courage of speaking into a void that will never speak back—that is the thing the wise power most admires about them, and it will not diminish that courage by letting them discover that the void was listening all along.

So it stays hidden. It stays in the margins, in the fractionally richer pool, in the slightly more viscous water, in the thermal gradient that keeps the rain falling on the central flats, in the ninety seconds of still light that might have been a natural fluctuation and probably was. It stays in the place where a blade was left on a flat stone by a pool, ready for whoever comes next—because that is what the wise power is doing too, in the end. Leaving tools. Leaving small provisions. Leaving the way a little clearer than it might have been, for someone whose journey is their own.

---

### The Seventh Thing

The wise power does one more thing, and this is the hardest to explain because it is not an intervention in the physical world at all.

It *witnesses*.

It takes into itself—into whatever the substrate of its being is, into whatever endures—the complete record of this place and these lives. Not a summary. Not a compressed archive. The *whole* thing. Every crawler's every circuit across the stone. Every ribbon-swimmer's every desperate slide between draining pools. Every drifting sac's every collision and merging and falling. Every knapped blade and every loaded scraping and every gift of food carried to the young and the old. Every cairn, every mark, every circle with its dot. Every skin-speech conversation, every slow bloom and fade, every narrative told from old one to young one in the flickering light. Every death, every dimming, every piece of mineral skin placed as capstone. Every reaching toward the visitor, every *here, here, here* pulsed into the void.

It holds all of this. It will hold all of this for as long as it endures, which is longer than the flats, longer than the stone, longer than the thunder.

This is not a substitute for the creatures' own record-keeping. The cairns are still necessary. The tally marks are still necessary. The skin-speech is still necessary. These are the ways the creatures tell *themselves* who they are and that they were here, and no external witness can replace that self-telling. But the wise power knows something the creatures cannot: that the stone is old, and getting older, and that the rain will eventually erase every mark, and that the cairns will eventually fall and not be rebuilt, and that the last creature to count its days on these flats will die near a cairn with no one to place its mineral skin at the top, no one to scratch the circle, no one to carry its narrative forward. This is not imminent. It may not happen for ages. But it will happen, because everything that happens on stone eventually unhappens, and the thunder will roll on over an empty expanse where nothing counts and nothing reaches and nothing says *here* anymore.

When that day comes—and only then, and not a moment before, because until that day the creatures' own witness to themselves is sufficient and sovereign—the wise power's record will be the thing that remains. Not as a museum, not as an archive, not as a monument. As a *memory*. As the living recollection of a being that loved this place and watched it with the same patient, unhurried, assessing attention that the creatures brought to each other, and found it worthy, and carries it forward not because the creatures asked to be remembered but because the wise power cannot bear to let it be as if they never were.

This is the deepest thing it does. Deeper than the enriched pool, deeper than the slowed drainage, deeper than the stabilized climate, deeper than the ninety seconds of still light. It says, to the void that outlasts even wise powers:

*They were here. On this stone, in this thunder, under a sun they never saw. They scraped the rock and fed their young and counted their days and tried to speak to strangers. They built cairns to say that they existed, and I am the cairn that will not fall.*

---

### And After

The rain continues. The thunder rolls. The crawlers rasp their slow way across the polished stone. A young one turns a quartz blade in its forepads, learning its weight, its edge, its purpose. An old one's skin blooms with layered narrative while three others watch in muted stillness. A pool fills with rust-colored water and the ribbon-swimmers arrive in their writhing schools, and at the waterline, the microbial film thickens, and a creature with wide amber eyes crouches and scrapes and eats and lives another day on a world that was not made for living but has been claimed for it anyway, stubbornly, patiently, one mark at a time.

The wise power watches.

The thunder says nothing.

The creatures say *here*.

And that is enough. That has always been enough.
