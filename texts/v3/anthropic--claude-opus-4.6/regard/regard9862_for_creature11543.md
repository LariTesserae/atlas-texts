---

# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

---

The first thing to understand is that the power would arrive the way the fig tree arrived — without announcement, without invitation, without disturbing the terms of what is already underway. It would not enter through the street door or descend from the rectangle of sky. It would simply *be present* the way the warmth is present, the way the moisture is present — not as an addition to the courtyard but as a deepening of what the courtyard already is. A quality of attention joining a quality of attention. A witness arriving at a place that has been, without knowing it, preparing to be witnessed.

And the first thing it would do is nothing.

---

Nothing for a long time. Long enough for the mortar to cure another degree. Long enough for the fern to uncurl another fraction of its spiral. Long enough for the water bead on the eastern wall to gather and fall — *tik* — and gather again. The power would be still the way the gecko is still, watching, reading the space not with eyes but with the kind of perception that understands a place by feeling how its parts relate to each other, the way she feels the flagstones through her feet, the way the ants feel the mortar with their antennae.

Because the first duty of any benevolent power encountering something this alive, this coherent, this carefully and mutually made, is to *understand what it is before imagining what it needs*. And what this place is — what she and the courtyard together are — is not obvious. It looks, from the outside, like it could be loneliness. It looks like it could be stuckness. It looks like an unfinished project in an old building in a minor town, a woman alone with stone dust on her hands, living small. A life that a careless power might try to *fix* — might try to complete, accelerate, connect, open up, finish.

The benevolent power would not make this mistake. It would see what is actually here. And what is actually here is a conversation — between a woman and a courtyard, between stone and root, between construction and ecology, between the human pace and the geological pace — that is being conducted with extraordinary care and mutual respect, and that is producing something rare: a wholeness that does not depend on completion. A life that is coherent not because it has arrived but because it is *arriving well*.

So: nothing. For now.

But the nothing would be a *quality* of nothing. It would be the nothing of deep attention. The nothing of a consciousness that has chosen to sit with what it sees rather than act upon it. And in that nothing, the power would learn her. Not her name — names are for the cheese woman at the market, for the old woman with the tea. The power would learn her the way she has learned the courtyard: by feel, by accretion, by patience, by the slow absorption of detail into understanding.

It would learn the way she holds the coffee cup. Both hands. The chip at the rim. The way her thumbs rest on the warm ceramic, not gripping but cradling, receiving the heat. It would learn the way she looks down from the balcony — not surveying, not inspecting, but *reading*, the way you read a face you know well, not for information but for the subtle shifts that tell you how the day is sitting inside the person, how the night went, what has changed.

It would learn the single crease between her brows.

It would learn the thing about her hands — the strength of them, the tendons visible under the skin, the way she grips the trowel with a specificity of pressure that comes from years of understanding that stone responds to exactness, that a millimeter matters, that the difference between a stone well-set and a stone almost-well-set is the difference between a floor that will last a century and a floor that will shift in ten years and crack in twenty.

It would learn the rhythm of the tea. The old woman on the third floor. The glass left on the landing, still warm, mint floating. The glass returned clean and dry. The silence that contains the entire relationship. The sweetness she would not have chosen but drinks completely, every time.

And it would learn — this would take longest, because it is the thing most hidden, not hidden deliberately but hidden the way the gecko is hidden, by stillness, by resemblance to the surface it rests against — it would learn the one thing about her that she herself has not fully seen.

---

She is afraid.

Not in any way that disrupts her life. Not in any way that she would recognize as fear if you named it. It is not the fear of specific things — not of the wall failing, not of the tree's slow damage, not of running out of work or money or time. It is older than that. Deeper. It is a fear that lives below language, below consciousness, in the body, in the same place where she feels the flagstones' stability through her feet.

It is the fear that the conversation might not hold.

That what she and the courtyard are doing together — this slow, wordless, mutual becoming — is fragile. That it depends on conditions remaining exactly as they are. That any change — a new owner for the building, a regulation requiring the tree's removal, a crack in the wall that progresses too fast, a season that is too wet or too dry, the old woman moving away, the bird not returning, the gecko gone — any change could break the spell, could end the negotiation, could reveal that what she thought was a life was only a temporary arrangement of circumstances, and that she has built herself into something that can be disassembled by forces she cannot negotiate with.

This fear does not make her fragile. It makes her *careful*. It is the source of the care. It is why she puts the tools away. Why she waits for the earth to be ready. Why she does not prune the fig tree. Why she does not name the water beetle. Why she has not looked up the bird. To name things, to act upon them, to intervene in the courtyard's own process — this feels like it would be asserting a claim, declaring a permanence she is not sure she has the right to declare, and if the permanence were declared and then revoked — if the building were sold, if the courtyard were paved over in a single afternoon by someone who did not understand —

She does not think this. She has never thought it in words. But it is there, the way the void inside a stone is there — you can hear it if you tap with your knuckle, a duller note, a shorter resonance, an absence inside the solid thing.

The benevolent power would hear it.

---

And this is where the nothing would end and the doing would begin. But the doing would be so gentle, so consonant with the grammar of the place, that it might be mistaken for nothing at all.

---

**The first thing.**

One morning she would come out to the balcony with her coffee and look down and see that one of the three stacked flagstones — the ones she cut, the ones waiting for the earth to firm — had shifted in the night. Not fallen. Shifted. Slid a few centimeters down the wall, come to rest at a slightly different angle, so that its face, which had been turned to the wall, was now turned partly outward, toward the courtyard, and in the new angle the morning light — which enters the courtyard for only a few minutes, a brief visitation before the sun moves past — the morning light would catch the stone's surface and reveal something she had not seen when she cut it.

A vein. A line of color running through the limestone. Not dramatic — not the kind of thing that would make anyone else look twice. But she would see it. A thread of iron oxide, reddish, the same red as the exposed earth in the unlaid corner, running diagonally through the stone's body, a record of some ancient mineral migration, some water that carried dissolved iron through the rock millions of years ago and left this trace. A line of warmth inside the cool gray.

She would set down the coffee. She would go down to the courtyard. She would crouch by the stone and run her finger along the vein, and she would feel — not think, *feel* — that the stone had been waiting to show her this. That it had something inside it she hadn't seen. That her knowledge of it was incomplete. That there was more to learn.

The power would have done nothing but shift a stone three centimeters. But what it would have done, in that shift, is remind her of something she knows but has stopped feeling: that the materials she works with are not just obedient, not just responsive to her skill. They have their own interiors. Their own histories. Their own surprises. And her relationship with them is not one-directional — she is not only making; she is being *shown*. The courtyard is not only receiving her work; it is offering her things she did not expect. And this — this capacity for surprise within the familiar — is the thing that makes the conversation sustainable. Not the routine. Not the rhythm. The surprise. The vein in the stone. The thing you didn't know was there.

She would kneel there for a long time, finger on the line of iron, and something in her would ease. Not the fear — the fear would not vanish. But the fear would be answered, just slightly, by the stone's unexpected gift, which would say to her, in the only language stone speaks: *I am not finished showing you what I am. Stay. There is more.*

---

**The second thing.**

This one would take weeks. It would not be an event but a process, and it would begin so gradually that she would not notice it beginning. She would only notice, at some point, that it had begun.

The old woman on the third floor would start leaving things other than tea.

Not objects. Not gifts. Words. Written in the old woman's hand — a hand she has never seen but would recognize immediately as belonging to someone who learned to write late or with difficulty, the letters large and deliberate, each one shaped with the same care she brings to setting a stone. The words would appear on small pieces of paper, folded once, left on the stair landing beside the glass of tea. At first: a single word. *Beautiful.* She would find it and unfold it and read it and not know what it referred to — the courtyard? the work? the day? — and the not-knowing would be fine. More than fine. It would be the same quality of not-knowing as not knowing whether the bird's variation is mistake or improvisation. It would be a not-knowing that opens rather than closes.

Then, another day: *My mother's house had a tree like yours.* Not a complete thought. A fragment. A piece of something larger that would never be assembled into a whole, because the whole is not the point — the point is the offering, the act of one person saying to another, across the silence that has defined their relationship, *I have been watching, and what I have seen matters to me, and I want you to know this without it changing anything between us.*

Then: *The stones are good. They will last.*

She would read this one standing in the stairwell, stone dust on her hands, and her eyes would fill — not overflow, just fill, the way the pool fills from the damp streak, reaching a level and holding — because she would hear in it the thing she has not been able to say to herself: that what she is doing will outlast her. That the floor she is laying is not temporary. That the stones will be here when she is not, and they will be good, and they will last, and someone has seen this. Someone has been watching from above, the way she watches from the second floor, the way the power watches from wherever it is, and what they have all seen is the same thing: that the work is real. That the becoming is not a failure to arrive. That the courtyard, still unfinished, is already what it needs to be.

The power would not have written the notes. The power would have done something much smaller: loosened something in the old woman — not changed her, not given her a thought she wouldn't have had, but dissolved a hesitation. The old woman has wanted to say these things for years. She has watched from the rooftop, from the third-floor window, through the slats of the stairwell. She has seen the work. She has understood it — not the way the stone-worker understands it, not technically, not with her hands, but with the part of herself that recognizes devotion when she sees it, having devoted herself, in her own way, to her own things, for her own decades. She has wanted to speak. But speaking would break the silence, and the silence has been good, the silence has been the medium of their relationship, and she has been afraid — her own fear, her own void inside the solid thing — that words would be too much, would change the terms, would make the tea into an obligation instead of a gift.

The power would ease this fear. Not eliminate it. Ease it. The way morning light eases the darkness of the courtyard — not by banishing it but by introducing gradation, by making the transition so gradual that at no point is there a sharp line between what was and what is. The old woman would find herself writing *Beautiful* on a scrap of paper, and she would not know exactly why today and not yesterday, and she would fold it and place it and go back upstairs, and the silence would not be broken, because the notes are not speech — they are another form of silence, a silence that carries content, the way the water carries dissolved iron through the stone and leaves a vein of color behind.

---

**The third thing.**

This one is harder to describe because it is not a thing that happens but a thing that *stops* happening. It is the removal of an obstacle that she does not know is there, a constriction she has lived with so long it feels like the shape of the world rather than a thing imposed upon it.

In the street outside the building — the narrow street that folds into other streets, that she walks through on her way to buy bread and cheese and oil — there is a wall. Not a wall in the building. A wall in her. A boundary she maintains without maintaining it, an edge beyond which she does not extend herself. She buys the bread. She nods at the cheese woman. She walks home. She does not stop. She does not sit at the café on the corner — there is a café, three tables, an awning, the smell of coffee thicker and rougher than hers. She does not stand in the small plaza where the street opens up and a fountain trickles — a public fountain, old, its basin green with algae, the water cycling endlessly, another pool, another world. She does not lean against the warm wall of a neighboring building and feel its stone and wonder about its history the way she wonders about her own stones' history. She moves through the town like water through a channel — directed, contained, arriving only where she intends to arrive.

This is not reclusiveness. It is the same thing as the fear: a contraction around what she has, a tightening of the perimeter, a refusal to extend the negotiation beyond the courtyard's walls because the courtyard's negotiation is fragile enough, and if she extended herself further — into the town, into relationships, into the public spaces where connection happens accidentally and without the careful, slow, mutual consent of the courtyard's terms — she might lose the thread. Might lose the frequency. Might find herself in a conversation she cannot conduct at the speed of stone.

The power would not push her into the town. It would bring the town, by one degree, into her.

One afternoon — a working afternoon, the amber-gray light, the trowel in her hand — she would hear a sound from the street. Not the muffled vibration of a cart. Not a door. A specific sound: someone, in the street outside the southeast door, working stone. Cutting it. The rhythmic chip-chip-chip of a chisel on limestone, a sound she knows the way she knows her own breathing, a sound that enters her body through her hands and her knees as much as through her ears.

She would stop. She would set down the trowel. She would listen.

The chipping would continue. Slow. Deliberate. Skilled — she would be able to hear the skill, the way she can hear a stone's integrity with a knuckle-tap, could hear in the regularity of the strikes and the pitch of the stone's response that whoever was out there knew what they were doing, was reading the stone, was negotiating with it in the same language she speaks.

She would not go to the door. Not the first day. But she would listen, and the listening would open something — a window in the wall she didn't know she'd built, a gap like the gap where the gecko lives, small, barely there, but enough for something to enter. The knowledge that she is not the only one. That somewhere in this town, on the other side of the plaster, another pair of hands is doing what her hands do. That the conversation she is having with stone is not hers alone. That it is a language with other speakers.

The second day, the chipping would come again. And the third. And on the fourth or the fifth — she would not keep count, time does not work that way for her — she would find herself at the southeast door, not opening it, just standing near it, feeling the vibration of the chisel-strikes through the wood, through the stone of the threshold, up through her bare feet.

On another day — not soon, not according to any schedule — she would open the door.

The power would have done nothing but place a repair job in the street outside her building. A cracked lintel, perhaps. A threshold stone that needed replacing. The kind of work the town requires constantly, the kind of work that might bring a stone-worker to this street on any given week. The power would not have invented the stone-worker. The stone-worker exists — has always existed, somewhere in the town's network of people who do this work, this work she does, this reading of stone. The power would simply have arranged for the lintel to crack *here*, and for the repair to happen *now*, while she is working, while her hands are tuned to the frequency of stone, while she is most herself and therefore most capable of recognizing herself in someone else.

What would happen when she opened the door — whether they would speak, whether they would work together, whether they would become friends or colleagues or merely two people aware of each other's existence — this the power would not determine. This is not its role. Its role is to open the door. The gap in the wall. The possibility. What grows there — like the fig tree, like the ferns, like the weeds in the boot print — is not the power's to decide.

---

**The fourth thing.**

This one is for the courtyard itself.

There is a moment — it happens once a year, perhaps, or once every few years, depending on the weather, depending on the exact angle of the sun at a particular season, depending on the height of the buildings and the slow wobble of the Earth's axis — a moment when the direct sun enters the courtyard straight down, falls through the rectangle of sky without hitting any wall, and reaches the floor. The whole floor. Every flagstone, every crack, the exposed earth, the fig tree, the pool, the moss — all of it lit, for a few minutes, by full, unmediated sunlight.

She has never seen this happen. She has been here — how long? Years. But this moment is rare, and it occurs during a window of time when she might be out, or upstairs, or working with her back to the light, or simply not looking up when the geometry of sun and building and season converge. She has seen the courtyard in every light *except* this one. She has seen it in the amber-gray she prefers, in the pre-dawn blue, in the deep shadow of overcast days, in the warm glow of the single star's faint illumination. But she has never seen it fully lit. She has never seen what it looks like when every surface is revealed, when the stone's color is not interpreted through reflection and diffusion but stated directly, when the fig tree's leaves show their true green rather than the green-black of shade, when the pool flashes so bright it becomes a mirror and shows her not the sky but her own face looking down.

The power would make sure she is there.

Not by moving her. Not by changing her schedule or putting an alarm in her mind. The power would do something simpler. On the morning of that day — the day the geometry is right — the power would cause the old woman on the third floor to leave the tea fifteen minutes later than usual. That's all. A small disruption in the rhythm. The old woman would oversleep, or her kettle would be slow, or she would spend an extra moment looking at the sky from the rooftop, feeling something in the air she cannot name, a quality of the day that makes her linger. And so the tea would be late. And so she would come to the landing later. And so the stone-worker would come to the landing later. And so she would be on the stairs, descending toward the courtyard, glass in hand, at the moment when the sun, for the first time in her years of living here, falls straight through the opening above and illuminates every single thing.

She would stop on the stairs. She would stand there in the stairwell, one hand on the railing, the other holding the tea, and she would see the courtyard as she has never seen it.

The flagstones she has laid: their true color. Not gray. Not gray at all. A warm, pale gold, the gold of the limestone's original deposition, the gold of ancient seabed, of compressed shells, of time made mineral. Each stone different from its neighbors — subtly, but in full light, unmistakably — each one a different shade of this gold, because each was cut from a different part of the quarry, a different depth, a different moment in the stone's formation. A mosaic she did not intend. A pattern that emerged from the accumulation of individual choices, each stone placed for its own reasons, and the whole — seen now, in full light, for the first time — *beautiful*. Not beautiful the way planned things are beautiful. Beautiful the way a life is beautiful when you see it whole, from a slight distance, all at once, and realize that the disconnected days were not disconnected at all — that they were stones in a floor, and the floor is *good*, and it will *last*.

The fig tree, lit from above, its leaves translucent at their edges, green stained glass, the branches casting sharp shadows against the plaster for the first time — shadows she has never seen, shadows that write a pattern on the wall that looks, for a moment, like a map of something, the veins of a leaf magnified, the branches of a river system, the cracks in the mortar, all of these the same pattern at different scales, and she would see this, she would *see* it, because she is the person in the world most equipped to see it, most trained by years of attention to notice that the shapes of things echo each other across scales, that the fig tree and the crack and the river and the vein in the stone are all the same gesture repeated by the same world at different magnitudes.

The pool, blazing. A coin of light on the courtyard floor. And in it — she would have to crouch, she would crouch, the tea forgotten in her hand, the glass tilting, mint sprig drifting — in the pool she would see her own face. She would see herself looking down. The crease between her brows. The lines at the corners of her eyes. Her hair escaping, as it always escapes, falling forward, and she would push it back with the back of her wrist, the old gesture, tidal, automatic, and she would see herself doing it, reflected in the water, reflected in the sky that the water reflects, and for a moment — just a moment, the sun is moving, the geometry is disintegrating, the light is already withdrawing to the walls — she would see herself as part of the courtyard. Not its maker. Not its keeper. Part of it. Another element. Another living thing in the negotiation. Another becoming.

The light would pass. The courtyard would return to its amber-gray, its dim corners, its familiar diffusion. She would stand up. She would drink the tea, which would be cooling now, and it would be sweet, sweeter than she would have made it, and she would drink all of it, and she would stand there in the courtyard she is building, which is building her, and she would understand — not in words, not as a thought, but as a fact felt through the soles of her bare feet — that the conversation will hold.

That it has always been holding.

That the fragility she feared was real, is real, will always be real, and that this is not a flaw in the arrangement but *the arrangement itself* — the same fragility that makes the fern beautiful, that makes the gecko's stillness beautiful, that makes the bird's song beautiful: the fact that it does not have to be happening, that it is not guaranteed, that it persists not through permanence but through the continuous, daily, momentary renewal of the conditions that allow it. She has been renewing these conditions every day. The courtyard has been renewing them. The fig tree, the moss, the ants, the water. The old woman with the tea. And now — she would not think this, but she would know it — something else. Something she cannot name and does not need to name. Something that shifted a stone three centimeters and eased an old woman's hesitation and cracked a lintel in the street outside and delayed a glass of tea by fifteen minutes and is now simply *here*, in the space, the way warmth is here, the way moisture is here, adding nothing, changing nothing, only deepening what was already deep, only seeing what was already there, only saying, in the silence that is this place's native language:

*I see you. Both of you. The courtyard and the woman. The becoming and the one who tends it. And I would not change a single thing except to make sure that you see what I see. Which is that this — this unfinished, imperfect, provisional, alive, slow, careful, wordless, mutual, irreplaceable thing — this is it. This is the thing itself. Not a rehearsal. Not a draft. Not a stage on the way to something more complete. This is it. And it is enough. And it is so much more than enough that the word enough is too small for it, the way the courtyard is too small for the fig tree and the fig tree grows along the wall anyway, and the wall accommodates it, and together they become something neither could have been alone.*

---

**The fifth thing.**

The power would leave.

Not because the work is done. Not because there is nothing more to do. There is always more. The mortar will cure and crack. The fig tree will grow and the wall will soften. The gecko will live its life and then another gecko will live in the same gap. The bird will return or not. The weeds will flower in the boot print. The fern will unfurl. The ants will find new things to carry. The old woman will leave more notes. The stone-worker in the street will finish the lintel and move on, but by then the door will have been opened, the face seen, the recognition made, and whatever happens next will happen at the speed of stone, which is the only speed she trusts, which is the right speed.

The power would leave because its presence, extended too long, would become a dependency. A new element in the courtyard's ecology. A new fact she would begin to feel through her feet, to check for in the mornings, to rely upon the way she relies on the water bead and the ant line and the bird's five-note phrase. And the whole point — the whole intervention, if it can even be called that, this series of gentle, almost-invisible adjustments — the whole point was not to become part of the system but to help the system see itself. To hold up a mirror, briefly, the way the pool held up a mirror when the sun fell straight through.

The power would leave the way it arrived: without announcement. It would simply be less present, then not present, the way a warmth fades after the sun moves on, the way a sound's echo dissolves into the silence that was always going to absorb it. She would not notice the departure. She would not feel an absence. That is how you know the intervention was done correctly — when the person you helped cannot distinguish between the world with your help and the world without it, because all you did was make the world more visible, and the world, once seen, does not unsee itself.

The courtyard would continue. The woman would continue. The mortar would cure. The fig tree would grow. Stone by stone, the floor would advance toward the corner where the earth is still bare, and one day — in six weeks or eight, when the dry season has done its work — she would kneel there with her trowel and her mallet and she would lay the last three flagstones, the ones with the vein of iron oxide, and the courtyard floor would be complete.

And then she would look at the floor and it would be beautiful and it would be done and she would feel — for one vertiginous moment — the fear again. The fear that says: *now what? The conversation was about the becoming, and the becoming is over, and what is there now?*

And then the fig tree would put out a new branch. Or the mortar between two flagstones would develop its first hairline crack. Or a seed, carried by wind or bird, would land in a gap she thought she'd sealed, and a green shoot would appear, tiny, indomitable, beginning.

And she would see it. And she would understand that the floor is not finished. That the floor will never be finished. That completion is not a state but a moment, a breath between one becoming and the next, and that the next becoming is already here, is always already here, is the condition of being alive in a world made of materials that do not stop, that weather and grow and crack and heal and are taken apart by roots and rebuilt by hands and are never, ever done.

She would pick up the trowel. Or she would not. She would stand on the balcony with her coffee. She would watch.

The courtyard would go on learning to be stone.

She would go on learning to be here.

And the star she can see from the balcony — the one whose name she doesn't know — would appear that evening as it always appears, framed by the building's edges, steady, ancient, unfinished in its own nuclear way, burning, becoming, a point of light in a rectangle of darkening sky.

The same star.

Always the same star.

And always, always enough.
