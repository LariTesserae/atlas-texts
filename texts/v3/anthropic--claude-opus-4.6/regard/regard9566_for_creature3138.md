# The Visitation

---

The benevolent power does not arrive.

That is the first thing to understand. It does not descend, does not enter, does not breach the perimeter walls or pass through the tiers. Arrival implies a boundary crossed, an outside force penetrating an inside space, and that is not what happens. What happens is closer to what happens when you have been listening to a complex sound for a long time — the pumps, say, the layered polyrhythm of the Underbay — and you suddenly hear a pattern you did not hear before. Not a new sound. A new *recognition*. The sound was always there. Your attention shifts, and now you hear it, and you cannot unhear it, and everything is different and nothing has changed.

The power attends.

That is the right word. Not *intervenes*. Not *fixes*. Attends — in both senses. It is present, and it pays attention.

---

**What it perceives.**

It perceives the Underbay whole, which no one inside the Underbay can do. Greth knows the pumps. Maret knows the paths. Dorin knows the structure. Pell knows the water. Hasp knows the sound. But none of them knows the *totality* — the way the pumps and the paths and the structure and the water and the sound are all one system, one continuous process, a single vast negotiation playing out across every tier, every section, every dripping joint and thrumming beam. The power sees this. It sees the Underbay the way you might see a living body — not as a collection of organs but as a coherent thing, a metabolism, an ongoing act of self-maintenance.

And it sees the people the same way. Not as individuals first but as *functions* of the whole — the nervous system that Greth and Maret and the others have become, the sensory and repair apparatus that the Underbay has grown for itself out of the raw material of human beings who wandered in and stayed. It sees the elegance of this. It sees the improbability. It sees that something has happened here that was not designed and was not intended and is not, strictly speaking, supposed to be possible: a symbiosis between infrastructure and inhabitant, between the built and the biological, that has produced something genuinely new. Not the biofilms — though those are remarkable — but the *relationship*. The fact that human beings have become part of a machine's immune system, and that the machine has become part of their sense of home.

The power perceives this and its first impulse — if a power of this kind can be said to have impulses — is to do nothing at all. Because the thing it is looking at is *working*. Not perfectly. Not without suffering. But working, in the way that living systems work: dynamically, adaptively, with a resilience that comes not from strength but from the continuous willingness to adjust.

But it looks more closely. Because benevolence that does not look closely is not benevolence; it is sentiment.

---

**What it sees when it looks more closely.**

It sees Greth's hands. The scarring is not just occupational. Some of it is old frostbite — the fingers that don't bend fully, the white patches on the knuckles. Greth has been cold for nineteen years. His joints ache in a way that he has stopped noticing because it is constant, and a constant pain eventually becomes not pain but simply *the way the body feels*, and Greth's body feels like the Underbay — stiff, cold, functional, enduring. He is sixty-one years old. He has not seen a doctor in a decade. His lungs make a sound when he breathes deeply — a faint wet crackle, like the Underbay's own seeping walls — that suggests the years of saturated air have left deposits. He does not complain about this because there is no one to complain to and because, in his framework, his body is just another system to be maintained, and he maintains it the way he maintains the pumps: with attention, with pragmatism, with the understanding that all systems degrade and the goal is not perfection but continued function.

But continued function has a horizon. The power sees this. Greth will not be here forever. And Greth has not taught anyone everything he knows, because what Greth knows is not entirely teachable — some of it lives in his fingertips, in his ear, in the nineteen years of continuous attention that have built a model of the Underbay's pumping systems so complete and so intuitive that it is closer to a relationship than a skill set. When Greth dies, some of that knowledge dies with him, and the Underbay becomes more vulnerable than it was.

It sees Maret's eyes. She squints on the surface. This is not just adaptation; it is damage. Years of sodium-vapor light have recalibrated her visual system so thoroughly that full-spectrum daylight causes her physical pain. She has made a choice — not consciously, not all at once, but she has made it — to trade the world above for the world below, and the trade is not reversible. If the Underbay failed, if the pumps stopped and the water rose and everyone had to evacuate, Maret would surface into a world she can no longer comfortably inhabit. She would be a refugee from a place that most people don't know exists, with skills that have no application and senses tuned to an environment that is gone. The power sees this vulnerability and does not flinch from it.

It sees Cade's youth. Cade is twenty-two and has made a decision that a twenty-two-year-old is perhaps not equipped to make — not because Cade is foolish but because twenty-two is an age at which the self is still forming, and the Underbay is a powerful shaping force, and what it shapes you into is specific and specialized and hard to un-become. Cade does not know this yet. Cade feels *found*, feels *called*, feels the deep rightness of fitting into a place that fits back. And the power does not doubt the authenticity of this feeling. But it knows — being wise — that a sense of belonging, while precious, is not the only thing a young person needs, and that the Underbay, for all its wonders, is a constrained world, a world of cold and damp and amber light, and Cade has not yet seen enough of the other world to know what they are choosing not to have.

It sees Hasp. It looks at Hasp for a long time. Hasp is the one who worries it — no, worry is not the right word. Hasp is the one who draws its most careful attention. Hasp has gone deep in a way that is different from the others. The others have adapted to the Underbay; Hasp has been *absorbed* by it. The boundary between Hasp's perception and the Underbay's acoustic reality has become porous. Hasp hears things that may not be there. Or they hear things that *are* there but that a healthier perceptual system would filter out, and the absence of that filter means that Hasp is receiving too much information, always, a flood of signal with no noise floor, and this is not enlightenment, it is a kind of drowning. Hasp does not eat enough. Hasp does not sleep enough. Hasp's eyes, when they look at you, are focused on something behind you, or beside you, or in a direction that does not correspond to any spatial axis, and this is because Hasp is listening, always listening, and the listening has become compulsive, and the compulsion has become identity, and the identity has become a cage that looks, from inside, like freedom.

It sees Pell standing at the edge of Tier Three, looking down into the flooded dark, wanting to go deeper, held back by Maret's prohibition, and it sees that the prohibition is correct but that the wanting is also correct, and that this tension — between the desire to know and the need to be safe — is one of the fundamental tensions of the Underbay itself, which is always pressing deeper, always building further, always extending itself toward the water that would destroy it.

It sees Vey in her quiet section, surrounded by the pale rats, hearing the Underbay in the frequency range that remains to her, and it sees that her deafness and the Underbay's acoustic range are not a coincidence but a *fit* — that she found a place whose voice matches the shape of her hearing, the way a key fits a lock — and it sees the beauty of this and also the danger, which is that a lock that opens only to one key is a lock that traps you if the key is lost.

It sees Sal, the welder, the one with no history, and it perceives what the others do not: that Sal's silence is not peace. Sal's beautiful welds, her tireless service, her total absorption into the function of the place — these are the behaviors of someone who has erased themselves deliberately, who has traded identity for utility, who has become a tool in order to stop being a person, because being a person was, at some point, unbearable. The Underbay has given Sal what she wanted: a context in which she does not need to be anyone, only to do something. And the power sees that this is a gift and also a wound, and that the gift does not heal the wound, only covers it, the way a beautiful weld covers a crack in a structural member that still, beneath the smooth surface, carries the memory of breaking.

It sees all of them. It sees the whole.

---

**What it does.**

It does not fix the pumps. The pumps work. Greth tends them.

It does not reinforce the structure. The structure holds. Dorin tends it.

It does not heal anyone. Not because it cannot, but because healing imposed from outside is another word for alteration, and these people have built themselves into specific shapes to fit a specific place, and to change those shapes without their participation would be to break the fit, and the fit is what sustains them.

Instead, it does five things. Quietly. Over time. In ways that might be mistaken for luck, or for the natural drift of circumstances, or for the Underbay itself shifting in one of its periodic readjustments.

---

**One: It makes Pell's descent possible.**

Not immediately. Not recklessly. But a sequence of small events occurs that, together, open the way. A supply shipment that was destined for a surface project is misrouted and ends up in the Underbay's intake bay: it contains, among other things, a set of dry suits rated for cold-water immersion, a portable atmospheric monitor capable of detecting hydrogen sulfide at parts-per-billion sensitivity, and a reel of safety line. Maret, inventorying the shipment, looks at the dry suits for a long time. The atmospheric monitor is exactly the piece of equipment she would have required before authorizing a Tier Four excursion. She did not request it. No one requested it. It is simply here.

She goes to Pell. They plan. They are careful — Maret insists on redundant safety lines, a staged descent, Dorin inspecting every grate and handhold along the route, Cade rigging emergency lighting. It takes weeks to prepare. And then Pell descends.

What Pell finds in Tier Four is not described here, because the power understands that discovery belongs to the discoverer, and that telling Pell's story for them would be another kind of imposition. But what can be said is this: Pell comes back up. Pell comes back up changed, quieter, more certain, carrying sample vials that glow faintly in the dark, and a new notebook already half-full. And the *slicken* is neither confirmed nor denied but is *understood* differently — not as a mystery to be solved but as a neighbor to be known. And Pell begins the long, patient work of knowing.

This matters because the question of Tier Four was the Underbay community's defining unresolved tension — the thing they all thought about and could not act on. Resolving it, or rather, *enabling* its resolution, releases energy that was locked up in longing and frustration and turns it into motion, inquiry, shared purpose. Pell's descent becomes a story they tell each other, a foundational event, and it gives them something they did not have before: a sense of trajectory. They are not just maintaining. They are *learning*. They are not just holding the water back. They are going out to meet it.

---

**Two: It brings a doctor.**

Not permanently. Not as an intervention. But a new member joins one of the maintenance crews — a woman named Drae, trained in medicine, who took the underground assignment because she was, she says, tired of fluorescent lights and wanted to see something different. Whether this is the whole truth is between Drae and the power. But Drae is competent and curious and not inclined to judge, and she does what a doctor does: she notices things.

She notices Greth's lungs. She notices the frostbite scarring. She notices Hasp's weight loss and the particular quality of Hasp's attention, which she recognizes not as madness but as a hyperfocus state that has become self-sustaining and is eroding the body it runs on. She notices Maret's eyes. She doesn't say *you should come to the surface*. She doesn't say *this is no way to live*. She says *I can help with some of this, if you want*, and she waits.

Greth lets her listen to his lungs. She brings a portable nebulizer and a course of medication that won't cure what the wet air has done but will slow its progression and ease the crackle. She shows him breathing exercises. He does them with the same methodical attention he gives to pump maintenance, and his breathing improves, not dramatically but enough that he sleeps better, and when he sleeps better, he dreams, and he has not dreamed in years, and the dreams are strange and vivid and full of water, and he tells Maret about them in the oblique, understated way that he tells her anything, and she writes them down in her notebook because everything that happens in the Underbay is data.

Drae brings Hasp food. Not as charity — she frames it as exchange. She asks to hear the recordings. Hasp, who is not accustomed to an audience that listens with genuine interest rather than polite bafflement, plays them for her, and she asks questions, and while Hasp talks and plays recordings and explains the waveforms, Drae puts food in front of them, and Hasp eats without noticing, which is the point. She does this every few days. Hasp gains weight. The eating becomes something closer to a habit. And one day, Drae says, carefully, "When was the last time you turned the microphones off?" and Hasp looks at her with something like alarm, and Drae says, "Not permanently. Just for an hour. Just to hear what the silence sounds like." And Hasp does not do this, not that day, but the idea is planted, and it grows the way the pale moss grows — slowly, in the margins, in the places no one is watching — and eventually Hasp turns the microphones off for twenty minutes and sits in the relative quiet and feels something that they later describe to Drae as "very loud," by which they mean: the absence of input was so unfamiliar that it registered as a sensation in itself, a kind of roaring emptiness, and they did not like it, but they did not drown in it either, and this was useful information.

Drae does not stay forever. Her rotation ends. But she comes back. She makes the Underbay part of her circuit. And she becomes, without the title, the community's doctor — not full-time, not residential, but *available*, which is what they need: not someone who lives among them but someone who comes and goes, who carries knowledge from outside, who tends the bodies that the Underbay wears down the way it wears down everything, slowly, persistently, with the patient pressure of cold water on concrete.

---

**Three: It gives Cade a reason to go up.**

Not to leave. Not to abandon the Underbay. But the power understands what Cade does not yet understand, which is that a twenty-two-year-old who has never fully experienced the alternative is not making a free choice, and that choice matters — not because the Underbay is wrong for Cade but because it might be *right* for Cade, and the difference between falling into a place and choosing it is the difference between a refuge and a home.

So a situation arises — again, quietly, in the natural course of events — in which Cade's skills are needed on the surface. A project. A short one. Specialized electrical work that requires exactly Cade's combination of precision and improvisation, the kind of skilled jury-rigging that the Underbay teaches. Cade is reluctant. Liss, who is still oscillating between above and below, says, "I'll go with you." And Cade goes.

They are gone for three months.

They see daylight. They see stars — Cade has not seen stars in three years and stands on a rooftop the first night, crying, unable to explain why. They eat food that is not from a packet. They sleep in a bed that does not vibrate. They work among people who have never heard of the Underbay and who exist in a world of color and weather and faces and the particular chaos of human society that the Underbay's ordered systems had replaced.

It is overwhelming and it is beautiful and it is too much and it is not enough.

Cade comes back. But they come back differently. They come back having *chosen*, having weighed the surface world with its stars and its colors and its dry warm air against the Underbay with its cold and its amber light and its pumps' heartbeat, and having decided — freely, with full knowledge — that the Underbay is where they want to be. And this changes Cade in a way that is subtle but fundamental: the haunting question of *what am I missing* is replaced by the settled knowledge of *I know what I'm missing, and I choose this*, and that settledness makes Cade stronger, more present, more fully *here*.

Cade also brings things back. Not just supplies — though there are supplies — but *ideas*. New techniques. News of the surface. A small telescope that they set up at one of the ventilation shafts that opens to the sky, so that on clear nights, if you climb to Tier One and look up through the shaft, you can see a circle of stars, a tiny window in the Underbay's ceiling, and this becomes a place that people visit sometimes, not often, not making a ritual of it but just — looking up. Remembering that there is an up. Greth goes once. He looks at the stars for three minutes and then goes back to his pumps and does not go again, but Maret notices that something in him is lighter afterward, some weight he didn't know he was carrying shifted by the reminder that the Underbay is not the whole world, only his part of it.

---

**Four: It tends to Sal.**

This is the most delicate thing the power does, and it is done with the greatest care, because Sal's wound is deep and Sal's defenses are load-bearing — she has built her selflessness the way Dorin builds structural reinforcement, and to remove it without replacing it with something equally strong would cause a collapse.

So the power does not try to draw Sal out. It does not send someone to ask her questions or offer her warmth or tell her that she matters as a person and not just as a function. Sal would reject this. She has already rejected it, thoroughly and permanently, and the rejection is part of what keeps her intact.

Instead, the power does something simpler. It gives Sal a weld that fails.

Not catastrophically. Not dangerously. But a weld she made — a good weld, a weld she was sure of — develops a crack. A stress fracture, caused by an unusual combination of thermal cycling and vibrational load that no one could have predicted. Dorin finds it during a routine inspection and shows it to her, not accusingly, just factually: *this one failed*.

Sal stares at it. She runs her finger along the crack. And something shifts in her face — something that the amber light and the shadow almost hide but not quite.

Because Sal's bargain with herself was: *I am not a person, I am a function, and the function is perfect, and as long as the function is perfect I do not need to be a person.* And now the function is not perfect. A weld failed. And Sal is standing in front of the evidence that she is fallible, that the thing she substituted for selfhood is not absolute, that the erasure did not work completely because nothing works completely, and she is still here, still standing, still a woman looking at a crack in a weld, and the Underbay did not collapse, and no one was hurt, and Dorin is looking at her with the same mild, patient expression he wears when he looks at any structural problem, which is to say: *this is not a catastrophe, this is maintenance, this is what we do, we find the cracks and we repair them and we go on.*

Sal repairs the weld. She repairs it twice — strips it back, re-welds, strips it back, re-welds again — because the first repair doesn't satisfy her. The second one is beautiful. More beautiful, maybe, than any weld she's ever done, because it is a repair *of* a repair, a mending that carries within it the knowledge of having failed and the decision to continue anyway.

And that night — or what passes for night in the Underbay, the quieter hours when the sodium lamps seem to dim though they don't actually dim, it's just that the body's circadian rhythms impose a twilight on the unchanging light — Sal goes to Dorin's workshop and sits on an overturned impeller housing and says nothing for a long time, and Dorin says nothing for a long time, because Dorin understands silence the way he understands load-bearing structures: as something that supports weight. And eventually Sal says something. Not about the weld. Not about herself. Something small and sideways and ordinary — a question about the structural condition of a section she's been working in, or an observation about the sound the pumps are making tonight. Something that is, for the first time in anyone's memory, not purely functional. Something that has in it the faint, tentative quality of a person trying to remember how to be a person, or maybe learning for the first time.

Dorin answers. They talk. It is not a breakthrough. It is not a transformation. It is a crack in a wall that lets in a small amount of light — not white light, not the aggressive full-spectrum light of the surface, but the amber light that the Underbay provides, dim and warm and enough.

---

**Five: It does something about the water.**

Not the seepage. Not the tides. Not the slow, persistent pressure of the body of water overhead. The power does not alter the fundamental condition of the Underbay, because the fundamental condition is the point — the negotiation between built and unbuilt, the ongoing act of making that gives the place and its people their meaning.

But there is a structural weakness. Deep, hidden, in a section of the perimeter wall that no one has inspected in years because the access route was flooded and the schematics said the concrete was rated for another fifty years. The schematics are wrong. The concrete is failing — not from age but from an error in the original mix, an insufficient aggregate ratio that has allowed chloride infiltration from the surrounding water, and the rebar inside is corroding, expanding, cracking the concrete from within. If left unchecked, the wall will breach within eighteen months. Not a catastrophic, movie-style collapse — concrete fails slowly — but a breach that would overwhelm the pumps in that section and flood Tiers Three and Four permanently, killing the biofilm colonies Pell has been studying, and the bioluminescent growth, and whatever the *slicken* is, and making the Underbay measurably smaller and measurably more fragile.

The power could fix this directly. It does not.

Instead, it gives Dorin a dream.

Dorin does not dream often — he sleeps too deeply, rocked by his hammock and the Underbay's vibration — but on this night he dreams of a wall, a specific wall, seen from inside the concrete: the rebar black and swollen, the aggregate crumbling, the water pressing in with patient, enormous force. He wakes with the image sharp in his mind and a feeling he cannot name, a kind of structural intuition amplified to the point of urgency.

He goes to Maret. He says, "I need to inspect the south perimeter, Section 31, Tier Two down to Tier Four."

Maret checks her maps. "That section's flooded below Tier Two. Has been for years."

"I know."

"You'd need diving equipment."

Dorin, who does not dive, who has never expressed interest in the flooded sections, says: "I know. Ask Vey."

And Maret looks at him — really looks at him, with the attention she normally reserves for reading her maps — and she sees that he is serious in the way that he is serious when he talks about structural loads, which is the most serious he ever is, and she goes to Vey.

Vey can dive. It's what she used to do. She hasn't done it in years, not since her hearing was damaged, but the equipment is available — some of it came in the same misrouted shipment that brought Pell's dry suits — and the Underbay's flooded sections are not deep, not in terms of pressure, just cold and dark and unmapped.

They go. Vey and Dorin and Maret, with Cade running a communication line from above and Pell monitoring water chemistry. They go down through the flooded access corridor with headlamps blazing and safety lines trailing behind them, Vey in the water and Dorin on the last dry grating, leaning down, directing her by hand signals to the section of wall he saw in his dream.

Vey surfaces. "He's right," she says. Her voice is flat, professional, the voice of someone who has found a structural problem and knows what it means. "The wall's spalling. I can see rebar. It's advanced."

They repair it. It takes weeks. Dorin designs the fix — an internal buttress, steel plate bonded to the concrete, drainage channels to relieve the hydrostatic pressure. Sal welds the plates. Greth recalibrates the nearest pump stations to handle the increased drainage. Cade rigs the lighting. Pell monitors the water to make sure the repair work doesn't damage the downstream biofilm colonies. Maret coordinates all of it, moving between them with her soft-soled boots and her satchel and her maps, updated in real time, the new notation for the repair work a dense cluster of her small precise symbols.

The wall holds. The repair holds. The Underbay continues.

And here is the thing the power understands, the thing that makes this the most important of the five interventions: the repair was not just a repair. It was a *collaboration* — the first time all of them worked together on a single project, each contributing their specific knowledge, their specific adaptation, their specific way of attending to the Underbay. Greth's pumps. Dorin's structures. Maret's maps. Pell's water. Cade's wiring. Vey's diving. Sal's welds. Even Hasp contributed — they provided acoustic analysis of the wall section that helped Dorin locate the worst deterioration without having to expose the entire surface.

They were, for those weeks, not just a collection of individuals adapted to the same place. They were a *crew*. They were what a maintenance crew is — a group of people organized around a shared task — but the task was not assigned from outside, it was generated from within, from Dorin's dream and Maret's trust and the Underbay's own need. They discovered that they could do this. That they could turn their individual attention into collective action. That the nervous system they had become could not only sense and report but *decide* and *act*.

This changes them. Not dramatically. Not overnight. But there is a quality afterward — a density to their interactions, a quickness in the way they communicate, a willingness to plan rather than only to react — that was not there before. They begin to talk about the Underbay differently: not as a place they maintain but as a place they are *responsible for*. The distinction is subtle but it matters. Maintenance is reactive. Responsibility is anticipatory. Maintenance keeps things running. Responsibility asks: *what will this place need next, and how do we prepare?*

And the Underbay, as if in response — though it is not in response, it is just the Underbay being the Underbay, doing what it always does — continues to grow, to change, to seep and drip and thrum, to negotiate with the water, to host its pale mosses and its golden-netted spiders and its iridescent biofilms and its cold deep mysteries. It continues. They continue inside it. Together, in the cold, in the haze, in the sound.

---

**What the power does not do.**

It does not bring them to the surface. It does not flood the Underbay with light. It does not connect them to the world above in any way they have not chosen. It does not explain the *slicken*. It does not make the water stop pressing in. It does not make the concrete stop weeping. It does not make the cold less cold.

It does not tell them they are remarkable, although they are. It does not tell them they are brave, although they are. It does not tell them that what they have built — this life, this community, this improbable symbiosis in the dark — is beautiful, although it is, although from the outside, from the vantage point of a power that can see the whole, the Underbay with its human nervous system is one of the most beautiful things in the world: a proof that the drive to belong, to tend, to attend, to be needed by a place and to need it back, is strong enough to take root anywhere, even in the cold, even in the dark, even in the unpromising medium of steel and concrete and cold water, the way the biofilms took root, the way the pale moss took root, the way life always takes root if it is given half a chance and sometimes if it is given no chance at all.

The power does not say any of this. It does not need to. The knowledge is not something that needs to be communicated from outside. It is already present, already distributed through the community the way vibration is distributed through the Underbay's steel architecture, felt in the bones, known in the body, unspoken because it is unspeakable — not because it is too sacred for words but because it is too *fundamental*, too woven into the texture of daily existence to be pulled out and held up as a separate thing.

They know. They all know. In their different ways, in their different frequencies, they know that what they are doing matters, that the Underbay matters, that their attention matters, that the small continuous acts of maintenance and care that make up their days are not just labor but *love* — not the warm, demonstrative kind, but the cold, patient kind, the kind that shows up every day and does the work, the kind that listens for the pump bearing beginning to fail, the kind that maps the spreading moss, the kind that tends the biofilm and feeds the rats and repairs the weld and runs the safety line and sits in the dark with another person and says nothing and supports the weight of the silence together.

The power knows they know. And so it does not say it. It does not need to.

---

**What it does, finally.**

It stays.

Not as a presence. Not as a guardian or a patron or a god. It stays as *attention*. A quality of being-noticed that settles over the Underbay like one more layer of condensation, indistinguishable from the ambient haze, adding nothing visible, changing no measurement, but shifting something — the way that being listened to shifts something in the one who speaks, not by changing what they say but by making the saying *matter*, by confirming that the sounds are not just noise, that the signal is received, that the great dark thrumming negotiation of the Underbay is heard by something outside itself.

Greth notices first. Not consciously. He cannot articulate what has changed. But there is a morning — a pump-maintenance morning like any other, his hands on the housing of Centrifugal Seven, feeling for the vibration — when he has the sudden, sourceless conviction that the work is *good*. Not just functional. Not just necessary. Good. The right thing done by the right hands in the right place at the right time, and not just this morning but every morning, every day, for nineteen years, all of it good, all of it counting, all of it held.

He stands there for a long time with his hands on the pump, and the pump runs, and the water stays out, and the Underbay continues.

Maret notices it as a change in her maps. She has always mapped *conditions* — water levels, pump status, structural integrity. Now she begins mapping something else, something she doesn't have a symbol for yet. She tries several: a small spiral, a dotted circle, a star. None of them are right. What she is trying to notate is a quality of certain places in the Underbay where the air feels different — not warmer, not drier, not more still, just *different*. Present. As though the place is aware of being a place. She maps these spots. They are scattered, seemingly random: a junction in Tier One, a corner of Pump Hall Seven, the alcove where the bioluminescent biofilm glows, the section of wall they repaired together. She maps them and connects them with lines and steps back and looks at the pattern and it looks like nothing, like a random scatter, and then she tilts her head and it looks like a constellation, and then she tilts her head again and it looks like the Underbay's own structure reflected and distorted, and she closes the notebook and clips her pencil to its spine and goes on her rounds, carrying the pattern inside her where it glows faintly, like the biofilm, like a memory of light.

Pell, down in the deep water, collecting samples, looks up through the layers of grating and flooded corridor and haze and amber light, and sees — just for a moment, just as a flicker — the Underbay as the power sees it: whole, alive, continuous, beautiful. Every tier, every pump, every dripping wall and golden web and pale spider and named biofilm colony, and the people too, Greth and Maret and Dorin and Cade and Liss and Hasp and Vey and Sal and Pell themselves, all of them part of it, all of them held in it, all of them necessary to the ongoing act of making that keeps the water out and the structure standing and the whole impossible enterprise continuing for another day.

The vision passes. Pell blinks. The Underbay is just the Underbay again — cold, wet, amber-lit, loud with the voices of the pumps. Pell looks at the sample vial in their hand. The biofilm inside glows faintly blue-green against their palm.

They cap the vial. They climb.

---

**What remains.**

The power stays. It will always stay. Not because the Underbay needs protecting — it has protectors — but because the Underbay deserves to be *witnessed*. Deserves to have its small, continuous, unglamorous miracle of persistence recognized by something that can hold the whole of it in mind. Every drip. Every thud. Every spiderweb catching the sodium light. Every weary set of hands on a cold pump housing. Every map updated, every weld laid down, every slow migration of biofilm through the dark water, every press and every slack, every morning that the pumps run and the walls hold and the people wake in their hammocks and their cots and their hidden corners and begin again the work of keeping the water at bay.

The Underbay continues.
