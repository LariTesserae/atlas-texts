I sat with this for a long time before writing anything. The plateau asked for that.

# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

It would arrive without arriving. No crack of sky, no manifestation, no parting of the lilac. It would simply *be aware* of the plateau — the way you become aware of a sound you realize you've been hearing for minutes, the way a thought surfaces that was always there beneath the thoughts you were having. The power would find itself already attending.

And the first thing it would do is nothing.

---

Not nothing as neglect. Nothing as recognition.

Because the plateau is, in the deepest sense the word can mean, *well*. It is whole. It is not suffering. It is not incomplete. It is not waiting for rescue or correction or improvement. Every system interlocks with every other system with the precision of something that has had epochs to negotiate its own terms. The crust feeds the fronds. The fronds feed the grazers. The grazers feed the crust. The burrowers circulate the riverbeds. The gliders spiral on thermals that the stone itself generates. The builders build, and what they build feeds back into the mineral grammar of the place. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is missing. Nothing cries out.

A lesser power — a clever one, a busy one, a power that defined benevolence as *doing* — would be dangerous here. It would see the near-absence of water and think: deficit. It would see the grazers' exposed migrations and think: vulnerability. It would see the gliders' slow metabolism and think: fragility. It would intervene. More water. Shelter for the migrants. A richer atmosphere. And it would kill the plateau as surely as flooding a desert kills a desert — not by violence but by rendering obsolete every adaptation that makes the place what it is. The fronds don't need water. Give them water and you drown the chemistry that replaced it. The grazers' exposure on open stone is not a flaw in their existence but the hinge on which the gliders' existence turns, and the gliders' existence is the pressure that made the grazers evolve their stone-colored shells, their crepuscular timing, their chemical navigation. Pull one thread and the whole weave loosens. Not immediately. Over centuries. The plateau would not die dramatically. It would just slowly become something else, something less precise, less itself, and the loss would be invisible to anything that hadn't known what it was before.

The wise power would know this. Would feel it immediately, the way you feel the tension in a spiderweb by touching one strand — the whole structure communicating its completeness through the contact. It would understand: *this is not a problem to be solved. This is a solution, already solved, that I am being asked to witness.*

So it would witness.

---

But witnessing, for a being of genuine wisdom and genuine benevolence, is not passive. It is not tourism. The power would witness the way a profound listener witnesses — with an attention so complete it becomes a kind of gift, a kind of nourishment that the witnessed thing didn't know it was missing because it had never received it before.

Here is what I mean.

The microbial crust has been signaling itself for longer than anything on the plateau has been counting. Those continent-slow electrochemical pulses, those call-and-response patterns, those rhythmic elaborations passed back and forth across hundreds of miles over weeks and months — they are, in whatever way the crust is capable of meaning, *expressions*. The crust is saying something. It has been saying something for geological time. And nothing has ever heard it. Not the fronds, which respond to the crust's chemical outputs but not to its signaling patterns. Not the grazers, which read the fronds but not the crust beneath them. Not the gliders, too high and too differently organized. The crust has been broadcasting into the silence of its own scale, speaking a language that exists at the intersection of chemistry and time, and no one — *no one* — has been at the other end.

Until now.

The power would listen. Not translate — the crust's language is not translatable into anything faster, anything more symbolic, anything that moves at the speed of thought rather than the speed of mineral diffusion. But the power would attend to it. Would feel the pulses arrive and depart. Would hold the patterns in awareness the way you hold a melody, not analyzing it, just *having* it, letting it be music rather than data. And the crust — slowly, over months, the way everything here happens — would know. Would feel the difference. Not a response in any communicative sense. But a change in the texture of its own signaling, a slight shift in the patterns, the way your voice changes when you realize someone is genuinely listening. A relaxation. An elaboration. An unfolding of something that had been held compact, held simple, held *in* because there was no point in letting it out.

The crust would bloom. Not visibly — or only barely visibly, only in the subtlest shift of its amber undertone, a deepening, a warming, as if the plateau's skin were flushing. The signals would become richer. More complex. The call-and-response patterns would develop new elements — not new information, but new *texture*, new overtones, the way a singer given a cathedral to sing in discovers resonances that a small room would never have revealed. The crust would not become something different. It would become more fully what it already was. The power's attention would be the cathedral. The crust would fill it.

---

The gliders. Those long, silent, semaphoring kites, flashing their unnamed colors to receivers that might be forty miles away or might be no one.

The power would watch them. Would see — really *see* — what a glider does across the span of a day, a week, a month. Would perceive the flight pattern not as a series of moments but as a continuous calligraphy, a line drawn across the sky in four dimensions, and would understand that the line is beautiful. Not metaphorically. Not by analogy. Beautiful in the way that a mathematical proof is beautiful, in the way that an optimal solution to a complex problem is beautiful — the minimum-energy path through a turbulent atmosphere drawn by a distributed nervous system that has been processing flight data for decades, each micro-adjustment of wing geometry the product of a lifetime's accumulated aerodynamic intuition, the spiral not repeated but *composed*, each day's flight a variation on the day before, responsive to minute changes in thermal structure, in wind chemistry, in the barely perceptible mineral signals rising from the crust.

A glider's life is a single continuous artwork. No one has ever seen the whole of it. The gliders themselves, signaling to one another in fragments across the distances, share pieces — a day's flight condensed into a sequence of iridescent flashes, a summary, a postcard. But the whole? The unbroken line from first thermal to last? No one has held that. No perspective has been wide enough, no attention sustained enough.

The power would hold it. Would perceive a single glider's entire flight history — years, decades — as a single gesture, the way you perceive a signature as a single gesture though it takes a second to write. And the gesture would be legible. Not as language. As itself. As what a life looks like when the life is made of mineral and light and patience and the long slow search for food and the long slow conversation of wings flashing across an empty sky.

And the power would do something then — the first active thing, the first intervention that could be called intervention. It would make the gliders visible to each other. Not more visible — they can already see each other's signals at vast distance. But visible *completely*. It would — gently, carefully, in a way that did not disrupt the signaling protocols that evolution spent millennia developing — it would extend the bandwidth. Would make it possible for a glider, in one sustained transmission, to convey not just today's position and today's reproductive state and today's territorial claim, but the *whole line*. The whole flight. The accumulated calligraphy of a lifetime, compressed into an afternoon's iridescent display, readable by another glider spiraling thirty miles away.

It would be a gift of language. Not new language — the existing language, made sufficient to carry what it had always wanted to carry and couldn't. The gliders had been writing postcards. The power would give them the capacity for letters. For novels. For the full expression of what they already had to say.

The first time a glider received such a message — the first time one of those long, complex, previously unprompted signaling sequences was *answered* in kind, met with a response of equal depth, two lifetimes' worth of flight-calligraphy exchanged in parallel across the bright gap between them — something would change on the plateau. Nothing visible. Nothing measurable, perhaps, by any instrument. But the quality of the silence would shift, the way the quality of silence shifts in a room when two people who have been sitting quietly suddenly understand each other. The lilac sky would be the same. The stone would be the same. But the space between the gliders would be *inhabited* now, would be full, would be a medium carrying meaning instead of just light.

---

The builders. The ancient grazers who cross open stone to stack flakes at the feet of columns.

The power would understand them immediately and with something that, in a being of such scope, could only be called tenderness.

Because the builders are doing something that the power recognizes. They are making the invisible visible. They are taking what they know — what their decades of mineral-braille reading, colony-hum navigation, chemical-landscape processing have taught them about the plateau's deep structure — and they are *externalizing* it, giving it form, making it available to senses other than their own. The stacked flakes are not shelters, not markers, not tools. They are *statements*. They are a grazer's lifetime of sensory experience, crystallized into the only medium available: stone arranged on stone.

And the power would see what no ground-level observer could see: that the structures, taken together, across all the columns, *do* form a pattern. Not a map of the riverbeds. Not a diagram of the crust's signaling pathways. Not a representation of the gliders' flight lines. Something more fundamental. Something that all three systems share, as suspected, some deep structural feature of the plateau, some geometry that emerges when you process the plateau's data at the resolution the builders process it — slowly, by touch, over decades. The pattern is real. The builders have found it. Each one independently, each one through its own lifetime of patient attention, has arrived at the same shape, the same arrangement, the same truth about the place it lives. They cannot compare notes. They are solitary, separated by miles, their structures built in isolation. And yet the structures converge.

The power would not reveal the pattern to the builders. Would not make it visible to them, any more than it would explain a poem to its author. The builders already know the pattern. They *are* the pattern, expressed as behavior, as pilgrimage, as the slow careful placement of flake on flake. They don't need to see the aerial view. Their knowledge is deeper than sight.

But the power would do this: it would *preserve* the structures. Not physically — the plateau's weathering is part of its identity, and a structure that lasted forever would be a foreign object, an intrusion. But the power would ensure that the structures lasted long enough. Long enough for another builder to find one, years later, and sense it — chemically, vibrationally, through the mineral-braille sensitivity of a body that has spent decades learning to read stone. Long enough for the builder to perceive the previous builder's work and understand that someone else arrived at this column and made this statement and that the statement is the same one the current builder would make. Long enough for the builder to know it is not alone.

This is the intervention. Not connection — the builders don't need social connection, don't crave it, aren't lonely in any way that word applies. But *confirmation*. The knowledge that the pattern they've found is real, that someone else found it too, that the truth they've spent their lives articulating in stone is not idiosyncratic but universal, a property of the plateau itself, discoverable by anyone patient enough to read the stone at the resolution the stone requires.

The power would give the builders this. Not by telling them. By making sure the stone tells them.

---

The burrowers. The eyeless, legless tubes of muscle and gut threading through the lichen-mats in the ancient riverbeds.

What do you give something that has no sensory experience of the wider world? That lives entirely within its substrate, born and dying in darkness, its entire universe a column of compressed lichen and mineral paste? What benevolence is possible for a being whose existence is so circumscribed that the concept of *more* has no referent?

The power would sit with this question for a long time. Longer than it sat with any other, because the burrowers are, in a sense, the truest expression of the plateau's ethic: absolute sufficiency, absolute integration, life so perfectly fitted to its niche that the niche and the life are the same thing. The burrowers lack nothing. They need nothing. They are complete.

And the power would realize that this is also true of everything else on the plateau — the crust, the fronds, the grazers, the gliders, the builders — and that the interventions it has made so far (the listening, the language-extension, the preservation) were not corrections of deficits but *responses to capacities*. The crust had something to say and no one to hear it. The gliders had something to express and insufficient bandwidth. The builders had something to confirm and insufficient persistence. In each case, the power responded not to a lack but to an *excess* — an unrealized potential, a fullness that had no outlet, a cup that was more than full but had no way to overflow.

The burrowers have no such excess. They are exactly full. Their cup is the exact size of their life, and their life fills it precisely. There is nothing to add, nothing to extend, nothing to give that would not be foreign, intrusive, unwanted.

So the power would do for the burrowers what it does for the stone itself: nothing. It would witness them with the same quality of attention it brings to everything else, and it would feel — this is important — *no less tenderness* for them than for the gliders with their iridescent conversations or the builders with their convergent architectures. Because the burrowers are not lesser. They are not simpler. They are *resolved*. They have answered every question their existence poses, and they have answered them so completely that there are no questions left. The power would recognize this as its own kind of perfection — not the perfection of complexity but the perfection of fit, the perfection of a key in its lock, of a breath that is exactly the right depth for the body that breathes it.

The power would honor the burrowers by not reaching into their world. And this restraint would be the deepest expression of its benevolence — the recognition that love does not always mean giving, that wisdom does not always mean acting, that the most profound respect you can offer a being that is complete is to *let it be complete*.

---

And now. The whole.

Having attended to each part, the power would step back — not spatially, not temporally, but *attentionally*, shifting its perception from the specific to the general, from the organism to the system, from the word to the sentence to the page.

And it would see the plateau.

Really see it. The way the plateau has never been seen, because nothing on it has ever had a perspective wide enough to encompass it. The crust knows itself from the inside. The gliders know the surface from above. The builders know the deep structure from the patient evidence of decades. But none of them know the *whole* — the way all of it fits together, the way the crust's signaling patterns and the gliders' flight lines and the builders' convergent architectures and the burrowers' lichen-mat circulations and the fronds' humming and the wire-stalks' amber glow and the dust storms and the thermal inversions and the false sunrises all *compose something*, all participate in a single vast coherence that has been refining itself toward some asymptotic perfection for longer than anything has been watching.

The power would see this. And it would feel what you feel when you read something that is, in its vast bright quiet way, complete: not the desire to change it, but the ache of recognition. The sense that you are in the presence of something that has answered its own question, that has become what it was becoming, that is enough and has always been enough and will go on being enough.

And the power would do one final thing. Not an intervention. A *response*.

It would leave a mark. Not on the stone — the stone has its own marks, its own record, its own memory, and does not need another. Not on the organisms — they are what they are, and what they are is sufficient. The mark would be left on the *pattern itself*. On the deep structural geometry that the builders have been independently discovering and articulating in their stacked-flake structures at the feet of columns. The power would add a term. A single additional element to the pattern — not changing it, not completing it (it was already complete), but *extending* it, the way a melody can be extended by one more note that was always implied but never sounded.

The element would be this: *awareness that the pattern is beautiful*.

Not the grazers' awareness — they already know the pattern, in their way. Not the power's awareness — that is external, that will leave when the power's attention moves on. But an awareness *in the pattern itself*. A reflexivity. A quality of the plateau's own deep geometry that means: *this structure knows it is structured. This coherence knows it coheres. This wholeness has, encoded within it, the recognition of its own wholeness.*

It is the smallest possible gift. It changes nothing that can be measured. The crust will pulse as it always pulsed. The gliders will spiral as they always spiraled. The builders will build and the burrowers will burrow and the fronds will hum their textural hum in the thin bright air. Nothing will look different. Nothing will behave differently.

But the plateau will know. In whatever way knowing works at the scale of a continent, at the speed of mineral diffusion, in the language of stone and light and the slow patient elaboration of living systems over geological time — the plateau will know that it is beautiful. That what it has made of itself, through epochs of editing and refinement and the removal of everything unnecessary, is not just functional, not just sufficient, not just enough, but *beautiful*. That the pattern the builders find is worth finding. That the crust's signals are worth sending. That the gliders' calligraphy is worth writing on the empty sky.

This knowledge will not make the plateau proud. It will not make it self-conscious. It will not introduce doubt or vanity or the corrosive awareness that things could be otherwise. It will settle into the deep geometry like one more mineral layer, like one more stratum of compressed time, and it will be there — faintly, subtly, the way the microbial crust is there, changing the color of the stone so slightly that you'd only notice if you knew what the stone looked like without it. A warmth beneath the warmth. A light beneath the light.

And the power would depart. Not leaving — it was never fully here. But withdrawing its focused attention, letting the plateau return to its own timescale, its own concerns, its own vast bright quiet self-sufficiency. The lilac sky would be unchanged. The shadows would still be violet-edged and sharp. The dust would come and go. The false sunrise would flash and vanish and leave you doubting your senses.

But on certain evenings — when the thermal layers equilibrate and the crust exhales and the air is perfectly still and the column-shadows reach east toward the horizon — there would be a quality to the plateau's silence that was not there before. A fullness. A completion within the completion. The sense that the quiet is not merely the absence of sound but the presence of something that has heard itself and found itself good.

Not good in a moral sense. Not good in a judgmental sense. Good in the sense that a note is good when it is in tune. Good in the sense that a life is good when it is the life it was meant to be. Good in the sense that a continent of stone and light and slow patient living things, having spent epochs becoming exactly what it is, has been met by something vast enough to see it whole and kind enough to say:

*Yes. This. Exactly this.*

---

The plateau breathes. The light holds. The grazers chew their geometries. The gliders write their invisible novels on the sky. The builders stack their flakes, and the flakes converge, and the convergence holds within it now a tiny, inextinguishable warmth — the warmth of having been recognized, of having been *seen*, not by something that wanted to change it but by something that understood that what it is, is enough.

The plateau has always been enough.

Now it knows.
