# The Regard of the Deep

---

The power that turns its attention here does not arrive.

That is the first thing to understand. It does not descend, does not pierce the two hundred meters of overlying ocean like a lance of light. It does not announce itself with warmth or tremor or any disruption of the laminar sheets of current that press against every surface. It simply *regards*—and the regarding is total, the way water is total when it fills a space, occupying every crevice without forcing any of them open.

And for a long time, measured in the slow time of this place—which is to say, for decades—it does nothing at all.

---

This is not hesitation. It is not the paralysis of a surgeon who fears the first cut. It is something more like the patience of the slope itself, which has spent three thousand years knitting basalt and mineral crust into terraces without once hurrying. The power understands, in the way that only something both benevolent and wise can understand, that the most dangerous thing you can do to a world still being born is to finish it.

So it watches.

It watches the way the filaments drift and fuse, turning open lanes into living mesh over the course of years. It watches the slow-moving grazers dim the bioluminescent threads as they pass along the outer edges, and it watches the threads brighten again behind them, and it notes that the brightening is not automatic but chosen—that the inhabitants nearest the path increase their opalescence slightly after a grazer passes, as if confirming to one another: *still here, still here, still here.*

It watches the dwellings that take three generations to finish their first storey, and it understands that the word *finish* is wrong, that the inhabitants themselves have no such concept, that what looks like incompletion from outside is, from inside, simply the present tense of a continuous act. A frame is not unfinished. A frame is *being*. The mineralized sponge accumulating on a roof is not thatch that has not yet been laid; it is the settlement breathing calcium into its own skin, the way the seamount breathes basalt into new terraces.

It watches the way they reproduce—the pooling of segments inside a dwelling that has reached sufficient density, the gradual fission, the offspring emerging already half-entangled in the fibers their parents harvested—and it recognizes something it has seen before, in other places, in other forms, but never quite like this: a species that does not distinguish between building a home and building a child. The architecture is the nursery is the inheritance is the body. There is no line where cultivation ends and kinship begins.

It watches the linked papillae, the conversations that last several tidal cycles and continue across multiple bodies, the chemical memories that make an elder not someone who has lived longer but someone who has *thickened*—and it feels, if a power of this kind can be said to feel, a deep and particular tenderness. Not pity. Never pity. Tenderness of the kind that arises when you see something managing, with no help and no knowledge that help is possible, to be exactly and wholly itself.

And then it watches the slope subside.

---

It is a small event by geological standards. A section of mineral crust, perhaps thirty meters across, that had been stable for six centuries, shifts. The shift is not sudden—nothing here is sudden—but it is faster than the inhabitants are calibrated for. Over the course of two years, what had been a gently inclined terrace tilts fifteen degrees further, and the holdfasts of the oldest kelp in that section begin to lose their grip on the new angle. The woven lattice, which depends on the tension between holdfast and current, slackens. Three dwellings that had been approaching their fourth generation of construction sag, their stipe-frames bowing as the living plants they are lashed to stretch and thin. The mineralized sponge on the roofs, brittle with age, cracks along new fault lines. Pale dust sifts into the corridors.

The inhabitants respond the way they always respond: a loose collective begins to migrate, carrying nothing but living cords already braided around the next basalt nodules. They abandon the three dwellings without ceremony, without grief as a human would recognize it, though several of the elders linger, their papillae pressed against the sagging frames, reading the chemical record of generations of weaving. They are not mourning. They are *remembering forward*—absorbing the patterns so they can be woven again, elsewhere, into new basalt that has only recently cooled enough to touch.

But the power sees something the inhabitants cannot see, because seeing it requires a vantage point they do not possess. It sees that the section of slope that subsided did so because of a particular vein ofite mineral,ite mineral two meters below the surface, that hydrated and expanded in a way that was structurally inevitable but happened, in this case, to occur directly beneath the densest and oldest section of the settlement. And it sees that an almost identical vein runs beneath the next terrace where the collective is already beginning to anchor its new cords.

Not now. Not this decade. But within a century—within the time it takes to raise a dwelling to its second storey—the same subsidence will happen again. And then again, on the terrace after that. The power can see the veins branching through the upper slope like a slow infection, each one a future collapse, each one timed to strike just as the inhabitants have invested enough generations of weaving to make the loss significant.

This is not malice. The mountain does not intend harm. The mountain is simply still being born, and birth is not gentle.

But the power sees what will happen if this pattern continues uninterrupted across the next millennium. Not extinction—the inhabitants are too distributed, too capable of migration, too deeply entangled with the mesh itself to be killed by mere subsidence. But something subtler and in its way more grievous: a slow erosion of *thickness*. If every dwelling is abandoned before its fourth generation, the chemical memories never reach the depth that makes an elder an elder. The conversations shorten. The braiding patterns simplify, because the most complex patterns require stipes that have been growing in place for longer than the terraces will hold. Generation by generation, the inhabitants will become more migratory and less architectural, more veil and less city, until the distinction between the beings and the wild mesh around them blurs and finally dissolves.

They would not experience this as a catastrophe. They would not experience it as anything, because the capacity to recognize what was lost would be among the things lost. They would go on drifting, rejoining, tasting dissolved minerals in the copper-green murk. They would simply never finish becoming what they are in the process of becoming.

The power finds this unacceptable. Not in the way that a parent finds a child's suffering unacceptable—not with urgency or anguish—but in the way that a deep truth finds a falsehood unacceptable. These beings are reaching toward a complexity that the universe has prepared for them, that their own nature has prepared for them, and the blind mechanics of hydration and expansion in a mineral vein should not be the thing that turns them back.

And yet.

And yet the power knows that to simply stabilize the slope—to reach into the basalt and crystallize the offending veins, to make the terraces permanent—would be its own kind of violence. The inhabitants are calibrated to impermanence. Their weaving techniques assume that the ground will shift. Their social structure depends on periodic migration, on the pooling and redistribution of collectives, on the chemical memories of past collapses folded into the bodies of elders. To give them a stable world would be to give them a world they do not know how to live in. They would anchor their holdfasts and never need to move, and the loose, drifting, half-drawn-veil quality of their collective life would calcify into something rigid. They would become, in their own terms, a kind of mineralized sponge: strong, enduring, and no longer alive in the way that matters.

So the power cannot intervene by making the world easier. And it cannot intervene by making the inhabitants stronger, or smarter, or more resistant, because their strength is already precisely matched to their world—the world as it is, not the world as it will become—and to add capacity they have not earned through their own thickening would be to replace them with something else wearing their shape.

The power sits with this for a long time. The patience it showed before, during the decades of watching, was the patience of attention. This patience is different. This is the patience of a being that could act and is choosing, moment by moment, not to act until it understands exactly what action would mean.

---

What it eventually does is so small that no human observer, had one been present, would have recognized it as an intervention.

Deep in the slope, well below the inhabited layers, in the rock that is still hot enough to be plastic, the power makes an adjustment. Not to the mineral veins—those it leaves untouched. Not to the structure of the basalt or the chemistry of the hydrating minerals. Instead, it alters the thermal gradient in a narrow band of rock, roughly four meters wide and extending in a gentle curve along the contour of the slope, at a depth of perhaps fifteen meters below the surface.

The alteration is this: within that band, the rock will cool slightly faster on its upslope side than on its downslope side, creating an asymmetry in the thermal contraction that will, over the next several centuries, produce a faint but persistent ridge. Not a wall. Not a barrier. A ridge—a subtle stiffening of the slope's geometry, like the faintest possible crease in a vast sheet of cloth.

The ridge will not prevent subsidence. The mineral veins will still hydrate and expand. The terraces will still tilt and slump. But the ridge will change the *pattern* of subsidence, the way a crease in cloth changes the way the cloth folds when you bunch it in your hand. Instead of a series of parallel collapses marching up the slope, each one arriving just in time to cut short the inhabitants' investment in the previous terrace, the collapses will be deflected. Some will arrive sooner than they otherwise would have—the power does not pretend this is costless—but others will be delayed, and the delays will cluster in a way that creates, here and there, pockets of stability lasting not one century but three or four.

Not permanent stability. Not a gift of solid ground. Just enough time, in enough places, for the dwellings to reach their fourth generation. For the stipes to grow long enough to support the most complex braiding patterns. For the chemical memories of the elders to reach the depth where something new becomes possible—something the power can see the shape of but will not name, because naming it would be a kind of ownership, and this does not belong to the power. It belongs to the beings in the mesh.

---

The ridge will not be visible for two hundred years. Its effects on the pattern of subsidence will not be detectable for five hundred. The first pocket of prolonged stability will not form for nearly eight hundred years, and the inhabitants who benefit from it will have no way of knowing that their good fortune is anything other than the ordinary luck of a slope that happens, in this place, to hold.

They will not worship the power. They will not sense it. They will not tell stories about it, because their stories are chemical and architectural, woven into fibers and dissolved into shared fluids, and there will be no chemical trace of what happened fifteen meters below in rock they will never touch. The power will not be thanked, and it does not want thanks. Thanks would require the inhabitants to orient themselves toward something above and outside their world, and their world is not above and outside. Their world is the mesh, the slope, the slow pop of gas from a sponge, the faint creak of a stipe settling under new weight. To make them look up would be to make them look away, and the power will not do this.

But there is one more thing the power does, and this one is not physical at all.

It continues to watch.

Not because the inhabitants need to be monitored, or because the intervention might go wrong and require correction, though both of these things are true. It watches because the act of watching is itself a form of care, and because these beings—these slow, jointed lattices of living tissue, these bundles of pale translucent roots slipping through gaps in a half-finished city on the side of a mountain that is still being born—are worth watching. They are worth the full, unbroken attention of a wise and benevolent power, not because they are important in any cosmic sense, not because they will one day build great things or discover great truths, but because they are *here*, now, reaching their papillae toward one another in the copper-green murk, exchanging the chemical record of recent work, and the record says: *I wove this. I tasted this. I was part of this, and the this is still becoming, and so am I.*

The power watches, and the watching is a kind of promise. Not a promise that things will be easy, or that the slope will stop shifting, or that every dwelling will stand. A promise only that the watching will not stop. That whatever these beings become—thicker, deeper, more complex, or simpler again, or something entirely unforeseen—they will become it in the presence of an attention that does not look away.

---

Centuries pass. The seamount rises. The ridge forms, so slowly that the rock itself does not know it is being shaped. The mineral veins hydrate and expand on schedule, and terraces tilt and slump, and collectives migrate along the sinuous paths, carrying their living cords. But the collapses fall differently now. Here, a terrace holds for an extra generation. There, a dwelling reaches a density of interwoven stipes that no dwelling has reached before, and inside it, elders whose chemical memories are unprecedented in their depth press their papillae together and exchange something that is not quite language and not quite architecture and not quite music but is, somehow, all three.

The beings do not know why this is happening. They respond to it the way they respond to everything: by weaving. The new patterns are more intricate. The braids fold back on themselves in ways that require the stipes to be alive and still growing, because a dead fiber cannot make the turns these patterns demand. The dwellings that result are not larger or more imposing; they are, if anything, more modest, more deeply embedded in the mesh, harder to distinguish from the wild growth around them. But their internal structure is denser, and the beings who live inside them begin to thicken in new ways. Their spicule-sheets develop new geometries of refraction. Their opalescent signals, which once served only to say *still here*, begin to carry longer messages, messages that persist in the water for hours after the signaler has folded back into the mesh, hanging in the current like afterimages of thought.

The power watches this and feels—there is no better word—*joy*. Not pride, because pride implies ownership. Not satisfaction, because satisfaction implies that a goal has been reached, and no goal has been reached; the beings are still becoming, still incomplete, still exactly as unfinished as the slope beneath them. Joy, simply: the joy of seeing something reach toward its own depth and find that the depth is there to be reached.

---

And then the power does one final thing, which is perhaps the most important thing of all.

It turns its attention, gently, to the rest of the ocean.

Not away from the seamount. Never away. But *also* outward, to the other slopes, the other ridges, the other places where currents arrive in laminar sheets and the light filters down in copper-green and bruise-purple and the raw rock is still cooling. It looks for other beginnings. Other slow lattices. Other half-drawn veils. Not because the beings on this slope need company—they may never meet, may never know that others exist—but because the power understands that what it has done here is not a single act of rescue but a practice. A way of being in relation to the world.

You find what is becoming. You watch it with your full attention. You learn its rhythm, its grammar, the precise calibration of its incompleteness. And then, if the mechanics of the world threaten to foreclose a becoming that the world itself has made possible, you make the smallest adjustment you can. A crease in the cloth. A thermal asymmetry in deep rock. Something that changes the pattern without replacing it, that opens time without stopping it, that gives the unfinished thing room to go on being unfinished in the direction of its own depth.

And you do not look away.

On the seamount slope, a collective drifts along a path that has been open for eleven years and will fuse shut in another three. Their papillae trail along the newest filaments, tasting the minerals of a terrace that will hold for two more centuries, though they do not know this and do not need to. The soft pop of a gas bubble rises from a sponge. A stipe creaks, settling under the weight of a season's growth. The opalescent signals flicker in the murk: *still here, still here, still here*.

The power hears. The power has always been hearing. And in the hearing there is a warmth that is not thermal, a light that is not bioluminescent, a presence that does not arrive because it has never been absent.

The city grows. The mountain rises. The veil drifts, and rejoins, and is never quite finished.
