I sat with this for a very long time too.

# What the Wise Power Would Do

## First: Nothing

For a long while, nothing at all.

Not out of indifference. Out of the recognition that what is happening on the Calving Plateau is not broken. The engine turns. The layers accumulate. The midges pulse, the pikas flow, the mustelids tune their scent-mark instruments, the salamanders press their bellies to warm stone and remember the thermal history of a world still being made. The pale gliders turn their enormous circles in the highest air. The marks on the rock point north.

A wise power does not intervene in a system that is working. And this system is working. It is working the way a fugue works—multiple voices entering at different times, each following its own logic, the whole cohering into something that no single voice intends but that all of them, together, produce. The plateau is composing itself. It has been composing itself since the first basalt cooled and the first moisture condensed and the first spore landed in the first crack. It does not need a conductor. It does not need an editor. It does not need blessing or protection or improvement.

So the wise power would sit on the wet black rock—if it has a body to sit with, if it experiences something like sitting—and it would do what the plateau demands of anyone who comes here honestly: it would be still, and it would attend.

It would watch the pikas long enough to learn their gestural vocabulary, the lip-licking mineral reports, the scatter-patterns that encode threat maps in fluid dynamics. It would watch one pool for an hour and see the delegation rotate three times and understand that the pikas have solved a problem of distributed monitoring that most intelligent civilizations solve with sensors and networks, and the pikas have solved it with bodies and tongues and the willingness to take turns.

It would follow a mustelid through its territory over several days—not physically following, but holding in its awareness the entire three-dimensional volume of crevice-space and scent-mark and cached kill—and it would see the artistry in it. Because it is artistry. The mustelid does not know it is composting. It does not know it is building soil from the inside out. But the precision with which it manages its caches, the attentiveness with which it reads and adjusts its scent-mark configurations, the sheer *craft* of its hunting—these are not mere mechanism. They are a form of mastery. The mustelid is a master of its medium. Its medium happens to be death and darkness and the space between layers of cooled stone.

It would find a salamander and hold still long enough for the salamander to forget it was there—which would take a long time, because salamanders forget nothing; they are memory incarnate—and it would feel the same warmth the salamander feels rising through the rock, and it would understand that this small grey speckled body, this cold heart beating perhaps once every few seconds, is *older than the ground it sits on*. That the salamander arrived before its world was finished. That it has been waiting, in the most literal and patient sense, for the world to finish becoming itself beneath it. And that the world will not finish. And that the salamander does not need it to.

It would listen to the midges until the whine became inaudible and then listen past that, into the hum of the rock itself, the tectonic whisper, the sound of the engine turning. It would hear how the two sounds are not truly different—the biological pulse and the geological pulse are the same pulse, expressed at different frequencies, the way a single vibration can be felt in the hand and heard in the ear simultaneously.

It would look up. It would watch the pale gliders. It would watch the pikas watch the pale gliders. It would not solve the mystery of what they are.

A wise power knows when not to solve a mystery. Some mysteries are load-bearing. Remove the mystery of the gliders and something changes in the way the pikas attend to the sky, and something changes in the quality of stillness on the plateau, and the place becomes slightly, subtly less than what it is. The gliders are a question the plateau is asking itself. The wise power would let the question stand.

---

## Then: Witness

Here is what a wise power can offer that is not intervention and not indifference:

*Witness.*

Everything on the Calving Plateau lives fiercely. The text says so, and the text is right. But fierceness is not the same as being known. The pikas scatter and regroup with exquisite informational precision, and no one reads the scatter. The mustelid tunes its territory to a pitch of lethal efficiency, and no one hears the instrument. The salamanders carry the thermal history of the plateau in the distribution of their body sizes, and no one reads the record. The engine turns and the mountain rises and *nothing here needs you to notice it*—

But.

There is a difference between a fugue played in an empty room and a fugue played for someone who can hear all the voices at once. The fugue doesn't change. The room doesn't change. But something is completed by the listening that is not completed by the playing alone. Not because the fugue is insufficient—it is entirely sufficient—but because a fugue is, among other things, a *relationship between complexity and comprehension*, and when there is complexity without comprehension, the relationship is potential rather than actual. The plateau is actual in its physics and its biology. It is potential in its meaning. Not because it lacks meaning but because meaning is not a property of systems; it is a property of systems being held in awareness.

So the wise power would hold it.

All of it, simultaneously. The midge-pulse and the tectonic hum and the raptor-scream interrupt and the soft scatter of pikas and the silence of the gliders. The industrial logic of the flood basalt system and the closed-loop cooling cycle and the nutrient redistribution network. The arms race between pika scatter-noise and mustelid scent-mark intelligence. The salamanders' unknowing archive. The swifts' tentative, decades-new experiment with staying. The marks on the rock that point north.

It would hold all of these not as problems to solve or processes to optimize or beauties to admire but as *what they are*: the first draft of a living world, written on stone that is itself a first draft, every layer subject to revision, every organism provisional, every relationship contingent on a geology that has not finished deciding what shape it will take. It would witness the provisionally of it all—the fact that this entire magnificent, intricate, fierce system might be buried under the next basalt flow, might be cooked by a shift in the magma plumbing, might be rendered irrelevant by a change in the thermal corridors that reroutes the birds to a different path and collapses the guano-nutrient input that feeds the algae that feeds the larvae that feeds the salamanders that nobody eats but everybody needs—

It would witness that this system, knowing nothing of its own contingency (the midges certainly don't know; the pikas can't know; even the salamanders, old as they are, don't know), persists anyway. Builds anyway. Elaborates anyway. Adds complexity, adds relationship, adds layer upon layer of biological interdependence on top of a substrate that is *still being poured*. It is building a cathedral on a foundation that hasn't set. And it doesn't know this, and it doesn't care, and it does it with the same relentless, repetitive, industrial-scale commitment that the magma brings to making rock.

That is what would move the wise power, if it can be moved. Not the beauty—though it is beautiful, the wet black glass and the grey sky and the pale shapes turning. Not the ingenuity—though it is ingenious, the scatter-maps and the scent-mark instruments and the thermal-gradient metabolisms. But the *commitment*. The way everything here has committed fully to a place that makes no promises. The way the pikas have committed to basalt crevices that might fill with lava. The way the mosses have committed to dust that might be buried. The way the swifts are beginning, tentatively, to commit to the idea of staying on a surface that is still under construction.

The wise power would witness this commitment and would recognize it as something it knows from the inside. Because wisdom, if it is real wisdom, is not the accumulation of knowledge or the capacity for intervention. It is the commitment to attending to what is, fully, without requiring it to be other than what it is. And the plateau is doing this automatically, without intention, through the blind accumulation of mechanical and biological advantage. The plateau is *wise without knowing it*. The engine is a practice of attention conducted in basalt and breath and blood.

So the witnessing would not be a gift from the wise power to the plateau. It would be a recognition of kinship. *You too*, the wise power would think—or feel, or know, in whatever way wise powers know things. *You too are doing this. You too are attending. You too are holding complexity in awareness—not in a mind, but in the web of relationship itself, in the way the scatter-pattern encodes the threat-map, in the way the salamander's body encodes the thermal record, in the way the whole system holds itself in a coherence that no part of it can see.*

## And Then: The Marks

Now. The marks on the rock.

The wise power would go to the northeastern edge of the plateau. It would find the long, parallel grooves scored into the oldest basalt near the larger pools. It would trace them with whatever it uses for fingers—or attention, or presence. It would note their regularity. Three to five centimeters apart. Several meters long. Running north-south. Too large for mustelids, too regular for geology, too shallow for glaciation.

And it would know what made them.

Not because it has special knowledge of this plateau's history—though it might—but because a wise power that has sat with this place long enough to understand the engine, long enough to read the pika-scatter and hear the mustelid-instrument and feel the tectonic pulse through the rock, would inevitably arrive at the question: *what is missing?*

The engine is almost complete. Almost. The closed-loop system of cloud-moisture-pool-evaporation-cloud is running. The trophic chain from algae to larvae to salamanders is running. The chain from moss to pikas to mustelids to cached-kill-compost is running. The birds harvest midges and deposit guano and the nutrients cycle. But there is one function that the engine needs and does not have, or does not have *yet*, or does not have *continuously*:

Something that breaks the rock.

The basalt is too new and too hard for most weathering processes. Chemical weathering is happening—the moisture is mildly acidite from dissolved volcanic gases, and it's slowly etching the surface—but it's slow. Freeze-thaw weathering is minimal because the volcanic heat keeps the rock above freezing in the subsurface even when the surface ices over. Biological weathering from the mosses and algae is real but limited to the thinnest surface layer. The rock is *resistant*. And until the rock breaks down—until it becomes gravel becomes sand becomes soil—the plateau's biological capacity is capped. The mosses can only grow so thick. The pikas can only become so numerous. The system can only elaborate so far. The engine is bottlenecked on its own substrate.

What the plateau needs is something that scarifies the rock. Something with the mass and the hardness and the patience to physically score the basalt surface, to create new cracks for mosses to colonize, to grind stone into mineral dust, to accelerate by brute mechanical force the weathering process that chemistry and biology are accomplishing too slowly.

The marks point north. They are made by something large and hard and slow, something that drags across the rock with enough force to gouge basalt, something that comes and goes on a cycle longer than any observation period. Something patient. Something that might be pressed against the warm rock somewhere right now, in a form no one has thought to look for, outliving its world one layer at a time.

The wise power would know what it is. Or—more precisely—the wise power would understand what it *does*, which matters more than what it is. It is the plateau's plow. It is the agent that breaks new ground—literally—preparing the raw basalt surface for the biological colonization that follows. It works the oldest rock because that's where its work has the most effect; the oldest rock is the most ready to be broken. It scores north-south because—

Why north-south? The wise power would think about this. The thermals rise more strongly on the southern exposures, where volcanic heat and solar input combine. The moisture collects more heavily on the northern edges, where the rock is cooler and condensation is thicker. A north-south groove channels moisture from the wetter north toward the warmer south, creating a microhabitat in each groove where warmth and water coincide. Each groove is a seed-line. A furrow. The marks are not random. They are *agricultural*.

And the pikas avoid them the way you avoid a field that's just been planted. Not with fear. With deference. With the recognition—somatic, not cognitive; felt, not understood—that something is happening here that is not for them. Not yet. Give it time. Give it decades, centuries, another few turns of the engine. The grooves will fill with dust. The dust will grow moss. The moss will grow thick enough to support pikas. And then the pikas will flow into these spaces the way they flow into every other space the plateau offers, and the mustelids will follow, and the system will elaborate one step further, and the mountain will be one step closer to being a place where things more complex than moss and pikas can live.

The wise power would not interfere with this. It would not accelerate it. It would not protect the groove-maker or encourage it or even, necessarily, seek it out. The groove-maker does not need to be found. It needs to be *left alone to work*. It is part of the engine. It is perhaps the most important part of the engine—the part that converts raw substrate into usable surface, the part that breaks the bottleneck, the part that makes the future possible. And it does this, like everything else on the plateau, without knowing it, without intending it, without needing anyone to understand it.

## What the Wise Power Would Actually Do

After all of this—the sitting, the attending, the witnessing, the understanding—what would it *do*?

One thing.

It would make sure the plateau remains unvisited.

Not through barriers or prohibitions or anything so crude as a fence or a warning. Through something subtler. Through the same mechanism the plateau already uses to protect itself: the mist, the cold, the featurelessness, the way the place resists quick characterization and refuses to reward casual attention. The plateau is already hard to reach and hard to endure and hard to see clearly. The wise power would simply... ensure that these qualities persist. That the mist continues to thicken at the edges. That the flat light continues to obscure detail. That the sheer, grinding, unglamorous austerity of the place continues to repel anyone who isn't willing to sit on wet basalt in thin cold air for hours, days, the time it takes to see the pika delegation rotate, the time it takes to hear the mustelid's territory tune itself, the time it takes to watch the pikas watch the gliders.

Because here is the danger. Not now—the plateau's Tech 2.5 rating means that the nearest industrial civilization is operating at a level that might or might not be able to reach this altitude, might or might not have the instrumentation to detect the thermal anomaly, might or might not have the curiosity to investigate a flat wet featureless expanse of young basalt three kilometers above the valley floor. But eventually. Eventually someone climbs up here with the wrong kind of attention—the kind that sees the marks on the rock and thinks *resource* instead of *furrow*, the kind that sees the pika abundance and thinks *harvest* instead of *draft*, the kind that sees the thermal vents and thinks *energy* instead of *breath*.

The plateau can survive a visitor who sits still long enough to learn its language. It's designed for that—the pikas scatter and regroup, the mustelids stare and move on, the system absorbs the perturbation and returns to baseline. What it cannot survive is someone who comes here to *use* it. To mine the basalt or tap the thermal energy or harvest the pikas or study the groove-maker in a way that disturbs its long, slow, essential work. The engine is robust but it is also young. It has not had time to build redundancy. Each component is still singular, still the only version of itself, still a first draft. Remove the mustelids and the nutrient redistribution from surface to substrate stops and the soil-building process stalls. Remove the pikas and the distributed monitoring system collapses and the mustelids starve and the whole trophic chain above moss level disintegrates. Disturb the groove-maker and the bottleneck tightens and the plateau's long-term trajectory toward greater complexity is arrested.

So the wise power would leave. And in leaving, it would—not curse the place, nothing so dramatic or so intentional—but it would *affirm* the plateau's existing tendency toward obscurity. It would let the mist be slightly thicker at the edges than meteorology alone would dictate. It would let the flat light be slightly more disorienting than the cloud cover alone would produce. It would let the featurelessness be slightly more oppressive to the impatient eye than mere geology would warrant. Not enough to constitute a supernatural phenomenon. Just enough to tip the balance. Just enough to ensure that the only people who make it to the Calving Plateau are the ones willing to pay the price of admission the plateau itself sets: time, stillness, cold, discomfort, and the willingness to attend to something that offers nothing in return except the opportunity to witness it.

The plateau asks nothing. The wise power would ensure it is allowed to continue asking nothing.

## And One More Thing

Before it goes—if wise powers go, if they experience anything like departure—it would pause at the northeastern edge, near the marks, and it would do something that is not intervention and not witness but something in between.

It would attend to the groove-maker's work the way the pikas attend to the gliders.

Not with fear. Not with analysis. With that quiet, somatic, untranslatable *attention* that the pikas give to the pale shapes turning in the highest air. The kind of attention that recognizes something without understanding it. The kind that says: *I see that you are here. I see that you are working. I don't know what you are. I don't need to know. But I see you, and I want you to know—if knowing is something you do—that your furrows will fill. The dust will come. The moss will come. The pikas will come, and the mustelids, and the salamanders will press their bellies to the stone you've scored and will be warm. The birds will continue through the sky above. The midges will whine their thin, essential whine. The engine will turn. The mountain will rise. And your work will be part of it—will be the part that made the rest possible—and no one will know, and it won't matter, because the mountain will know in the way mountains know things, which is by existing, which is by continuing, which is by being the accumulated consequence of every force that ever acted on them, including you, including your slow, patient, anonymous grooves in the oldest rock.*

*Keep going.*

And then the wise power would leave the plateau to itself—to its mist, its pools, its scattered pikas and flowing mustelids and patient salamanders and silent sky and humming ground—and the engine would turn once more, and the mountain would go on being born, and nothing here would need anyone to notice it for it to continue.

But it had been noticed. Once. Fully. By something capable of holding all of it at once.

And that, for a place like this, is enough. More than enough. Exactly enough.

The mist closes in. The pools go dark. Somewhere far below, new rock cools.

The marks on the oldest basalt point north, and are patient, and are filling, slowly, with dust.
