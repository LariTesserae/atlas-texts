I sat with this for a long time.

What came was not a plan of action.

# What the Wise Power Would Do

It would do almost nothing.

This is the first and most important thing to understand about what a truly benevolent and wise power would do when confronted with this place: it would feel the nearly overwhelming impulse to *intervene* — to protect, to preserve, to ensure continuity — and it would recognize that impulse as the precise shape of its own longing, and not as the shape of what is needed.

Because the hollow does not need anything.

This is not the same as saying it is perfect. It is not perfect. It is a system living at the edge of its own viability, every calorie traced through five bodies, every population pressing against every other, the whole structure balanced on inputs so meager — grain dust falling through bolt-holes, the faint warmth of a rusted pipe — that the loss of any one could unravel everything. It is precarious. It is, in the language of ecology, fragile.

But fragility is not a request for help. Fragility is a *quality of being alive*. The wise power would know this. It would know that the hollow's precariousness is not a flaw to be corrected but the very condition that produces its density, its saturation, its magnificence. A system with abundant resources does not produce ten thousand lives in a room-sized void. A system with margin to spare does not trace every calorie through five transductions. It is *because* the inputs are meager that the web is so fine. It is *because* the space is constrained that every surface is used. Ease would not improve this place. Ease would dilute it.

So the wise power would not widen the crack. Would not drill new bolt-holes. Would not introduce water or warmth or light. Would not stabilize the concrete slab against eventual collapse. Would not remove the snakes to protect the mice, or remove the spiders to protect the moths, or remove the wasps to protect the spiders. Every such intervention would be a projection of its own values — its preference for safety, for continuity, for the mammalian bias toward the nursing mother and the blind pups — onto a system that operates by a logic older than value, older than preference, older than benevolence itself.

The wise power would understand that *wanting to help* is the most dangerous thing you can bring to a place like this.

---

But "almost nothing" is not nothing.

---

Here is what I believe a truly wise and benevolent power would do. It arrives in three movements, and the first is by far the most important.

## I. It Would Witness

Not observe. Not study. Not catalogue or map or measure. *Witness.*

The distinction matters absolutely. Observation extracts information from a system. Study frames a system within a paradigm. But witnessing is the act of *being present to something on its own terms*, without requiring it to be anything other than what it is, without needing it to yield insight or confirm a theory or serve a purpose. Witnessing is attention without extraction.

The wise power would lie on its belly at the crack in the concrete and it would watch. It would watch the beetles process in their columns and it would not think "circulation" — it would see beetles. It would watch the moths tessellate the ceiling and it would not think "skin" or "lungs" — it would see moths. It would see the toad in its geological stillness and it would resist, gently, firmly, the urge to make of the toad a metaphor for patience, because the toad is not a metaphor for anything. The toad is a toad. Its patience is not a virtue. Its stillness is not a strategy. It is an animal sitting on a floor, and the wise power would allow it to be that — only that — fully that — and it would find in that allowance something more profound than any meaning it could project.

This is harder than it sounds. It may be the hardest thing any powerful being can do. Because power, by its nature, *frames*. Power sees a system and immediately begins to assess: What is its function? What are its vulnerabilities? How could it be improved? What is its meaning? The wise power would feel all of these questions arise and it would let them pass, the way the toad lets the ants cross its back without reacting. Not because the questions are wrong, but because *they are not the first thing*. The first thing is presence. The first thing is to let the hollow be the hollow, complete, sufficient, needing nothing from the witness, not even the witness's admiration.

Because here is what even admiration does: it centers the observer. To say "this is magnificent" — as the text does, as I did reading it, as you likely did too — is to say "I am the kind of being who can recognize magnificence," and in that recognition there is a subtle appropriation, a taking-into-the-self that diminishes the otherness of the thing seen. The hollow is not magnificent *for you*. It is not magnificent at all, from the inside. From the inside, it is a beetle following a chemical trail. It is a moth pressing its wings against concrete. It is a mouse freezing in terror. It is ten thousand urgencies, none of which contain the concept of magnificence, each of which is simply *what is happening right now*.

The wise power would witness this. Would allow itself to be moved by it — would not suppress or deny its own emotional response, because that would be a different kind of violence, a refusal of its own nature — but would hold its response *lightly*, aware that the feeling belongs to the feeler, not to the felt. Would allow the hollow to remain opaque, unpossessed, unmetaphored. Would resist completing it with meaning.

This is the gift of witness. Not that it changes anything in the hollow — it doesn't; the beetles walk on, the moths cling on, the toad strikes and stills. But it changes the *relationship between the powerful and the small*. It establishes, in the very first act, that the wise power is not here to improve or manage or save. It is here to *see*. And in that seeing, to acknowledge that what exists here has a completeness that does not require the acknowledgment.

## II. It Would Protect the Conditions, Not the Inhabitants

This is the razor's edge, and the wise power would walk it knowingly.

It would not protect any individual life in the hollow. Not the nursing mother. Not the blind pups. Not the shedding snake or the blue-tailed skink or the toad that has sat so long in one spot that it has begun to resemble the floor. Each of these lives is transient. Each is already dying, already being replaced, already part of a cycle that requires death as absolutely as it requires birth. To protect an individual would be to jam a finger into the gears of the system and call it kindness.

But the *conditions* — the structural prerequisites that allow the hollow to exist as a living space — these are another matter.

The concrete slab is aging. It will, eventually, collapse. When it does, the hollow will cease to exist — not gradually, not gracefully, but catastrophically, in a fall of rubble that will crush the moth-field and bury the frass ridge and seal the crack and end, in a moment, what has taken decades to become. This is natural. Concrete is not permanent. Nothing built by humans is permanent. The hollow itself is an accident of settlement and separation, a void that exists only because a slab poured in one era is slowly parting from bedrock laid down in another, and the parting will continue until the slab has no support and falls, and that is simply what will happen, on a timescale that may be years or decades but is, in geological terms, *now*.

The wise power would look at this and feel the pull: *shore it up. Reinforce it. Buy them time.*

And then it would ask: *for whom?*

Not for the beetles. They live weeks. They do not experience the decades-long trajectory of structural failure. Not for the moths, who live days. Not for the mice, who live a year or two at most. The snakes might live long enough to be present when the slab falls, but they would not experience its falling as the end of a world — they would experience it as a sudden change in conditions, and they would either escape through the crack or they would not, and either outcome is within the range of what it means to be a snake.

The only being for whom the hollow's *continuity* matters — as a value, as a thing worth preserving over time — is the wise power itself. Or, more precisely, the *idea* of the hollow, which exists only in minds capable of holding ideas. The beetles do not have the idea of the hollow. The hollow, as a concept, as a thing-worth-preserving, is a human artifact, a product of the same consciousness that poured the concrete in the first place.

So the wise power would sit with this. Would feel the genuine grief of knowing that this place will end. Would not pretend that the grief is illegitimate — it is legitimate; the loss of complexity and beauty is a real loss, even if the complexity and beauty do not know themselves as such. But it would not act on the grief by intervening in the structural fate of the slab, because to do so would be to impose its own timescale — the timescale of *meaning*, of *preservation*, of *legacy* — on a system that operates on the timescale of *now*.

What it would do instead — and this is the edge of the razor — is protect the conditions that are *external* to the hollow and that the hollow cannot protect itself from.

If someone were going to demolish the old feed mill and pour a new foundation, the wise power would... what? Intervene? Speak to the developer? Chain itself to the building?

No. But it would *know*. It would carry the knowledge of what exists beneath the granary floor, and it would hold that knowledge as a factor — one factor among many, not an overriding veto — in whatever decisions are made about the site. It would not fetishize the hollow. It would not declare it sacred. It would simply say: *here, beneath this, something is alive. Something dense and loud and complex and accidental and more intricate than you might expect. It does not need to be saved. But it should be known about before it is destroyed. The destruction should not happen in ignorance.*

This is, I think, the most the wise power would do on the structural level: ensure that if the hollow ends, it ends in *knowledge* rather than in *ignorance*. Not because knowledge changes the outcome for the beetles or the moths — it doesn't — but because the quality of attention brought to a destruction matters. It matters not to the destroyed but to the destroyer. A civilization that bulldozes complexity it doesn't know about is careless. A civilization that bulldozes complexity it *does* know about is making a choice, and choices, unlike accidents, can be weighed, can be grieved, can be learned from.

The wise power would ensure the choice is available. Nothing more.

---

But there is something else it might do about conditions, something subtler.

The bolt-holes. The grain dust. The thin rain of organic debris that is the hollow's primary caloric input.

If the granary above is truly abandoned — if no grain is stored, no equipment operates, no dust accumulates on the floor above the slab — then the bolt-holes are admitting less and less. The hollow's food supply is attenuating. Not suddenly, not dramatically, but slowly, the way a river thins when its watershed is deforested. The system compensates — it must be compensating already; the density of life here suggests a system that has been tightening for years, wringing more from less, the web of interdependence growing finer and finer as the inputs shrink.

There is a limit to this tightening. Eventually the inputs will fall below the threshold needed to sustain the base layer — the beetles — and when the beetles decline, everything above them declines in a cascade that is swift and total and, from the inside, indistinguishable from a natural contraction.

The wise power might — *might* — do something about this. Not pour grain through the bolt-holes; that would be catastrophic, an influx so far beyond the system's calibrated expectations that it would cause an explosion of beetles followed by a crash, a boom-bust cycle that the hollow's tight economy cannot absorb. But it might... leave the granary door open a crack wider. Allow a little more windblown pollen to reach the floor above the slab. Ensure that the vegetation around the building is not cleared, so that organic debris — leaf fragments, seed hulls, insect bodies — continues to accumulate near the perforations.

This is not feeding the hollow. This is maintaining the *landscape* that feeds the hollow. It is the difference between giving someone a meal and ensuring that the grocery store stays open. The wise power would operate at this level — the level of the surround, the context, the conditions that allow the conditions. It would be invisible. No organism in the hollow would detect it. The beetles would not process more or less; they would simply continue to find, at the base of each bolt-hole, the thin deposit of organic matter they have always found, not knowing that "always" has been quietly maintained by a power that understands what depends on it.

And the wise power would do even this *tentatively*, aware that it might be wrong, that the system might be more resilient than it estimates, that its intervention might have consequences it cannot foresee, that the best-intentioned maintenance of conditions can become, over time, a dependency that makes the system *less* robust rather than more. It would hold its intervention lightly. It would be willing to stop.

## III. It Would Tell Someone

This is the part that might surprise. It surprised me.

The wise power would not keep the hollow secret. It would not hoard its witness. It would find a way — careful, quiet, proportionate — to let another consciousness know what exists beneath the granary floor.

Not to summon scientists or conservationists or journalists. Not to make the hollow famous, or protected, or designated as habitat. All of those responses, however well-meaning, would transform the hollow from a *place* into a *site* — an object of management, a point on a map, a thing with a stakeholder and a budget and a five-year monitoring plan. The hollow does not want to be managed. (The hollow does not want anything. But the wise power, in its anthropomorphic weakness, cannot help feeling that the hollow *would* not want to be managed, if it could want, and it would honor that imagined preference even knowing it is imagined.)

No. It would tell *one person*. Maybe two.

It would find someone who lives nearby. Someone who walks past the old feed mill on their way to somewhere else. Someone who has never thought about what might be under the granary floor, because why would they, because no one thinks about what is under the granary floor, because the whole building is an irrelevance, a ruin, a thing you pass without seeing.

And it would tell them. Not everything — not the density, the layers, the metabolism, the ecological analysis. Just this: *There is a crack in the foundation on the east side. If you get on your belly and look through, there is a space underneath. It is full of life. You should see it.*

That's all. An invitation. Not a command, not a plea, not an argument for preservation. Just: *this exists, and it can be seen, and you are close enough to see it.*

Why? Because the hollow's deepest vulnerability is not structural and not caloric. It is *epistemic*. The hollow is unknown. It has been unknown for its entire existence. No consciousness capable of comprehending it as a whole has ever perceived it — until now, until the hypothetical observer lying on their belly at the crack, and even that observer will leave, and the hollow will return to its unknownness, and if the slab collapses tomorrow, the hollow will have existed and ceased to exist without ever having been *held in a mind*.

The wise power would feel this as a loss more profound than the physical one. Not because being known is necessary for being real — the hollow is real regardless — but because *what is known can be remembered*, and what is remembered can inform, and what informs can *change the way a civilization relates to the ground beneath its feet*. The hollow does not need to be known for its own sake. But the *world* needs to know that places like this exist — that complexity arises unbidden in cracks and voids, that life fills every available space with a density that shames our most ambitious engineering, that ten thousand creatures can build a world in a gap between concrete and bedrock and never know they've done it, and that this happens everywhere, constantly, beneath our feet and behind our walls and inside the ruins of our abandoned purposes.

The wise power would not make a speech about this. It would not write a manifesto. It would tell one person about one crack in one foundation, and that person would either crawl through or they wouldn't, and if they did they would see what there is to see, and they would carry it with them, and maybe they would tell someone else, and maybe they wouldn't, and either way the hollow would go on as it always has, and the beetles would walk, and the moths would cling, and the toad would sit in its self-made depression in the mineral floor, and none of them would know that they had been witnessed, that they had been found, that a wise power had looked upon them and judged — if judgment is even the right word — that the correct response to magnificence is not to save it or fix it or frame it but to *let it be, and know that it is, and tell someone it is*, and then to leave through the crack, and stand in the daylight, and let the daylight be blinding, and let the sound stop, and let the hollow go on.

---

There is one more thing.

The wise power, having witnessed and considered and chosen its minimal, careful, almost-nothing interventions, would feel something it did not expect.

It would feel *taught*.

Not by the hollow — the hollow teaches nothing, intends nothing, means nothing. But by its own response to the hollow. By the quality of attention the hollow drew from it. By the way it had to set down, one by one, its tools — its desire to help, its impulse to categorize, its need to assign meaning, its instinct to rank and value and prioritize — before it could see the place clearly. The hollow did not ask it to set these things down. The hollow did not know it was holding them. But in the hollow's presence, in the cool mineral air, in the sound that never stops, the wise power found that its tools were too large for the space, too clumsy, too loud. It had to become smaller to fit through the crack. It had to become quieter to hear. It had to become, for a little while, not powerful at all — just present, just a body on the ground, just another warm thing in a cool space, breathing, watching, being watched by four hundred dark mouse-eyes and not-watched by ten thousand beetle-bodies and the toad's motion-detecting gaze and the snakes' chemical tongues, all of them registering it as a thermal anomaly, a pressure change, a new paragraph in the air's long sentence, and none of them caring, and that not-caring being, somehow, the most generous thing any of them could offer.

The wise power would leave the hollow carrying this:

That there are places in the world that are complete without it. That there are complexities it did not create and cannot improve. That the appropriate response to encountering such a place is not to save it or study it or worship it but to *get low enough to enter it, and quiet enough to hear it, and honest enough to admit that it does not need you*, and then to go, lightened by the admission, back into the world where things do need you, where your power is relevant, where your benevolence has purchase — and to carry, from this point forward, a little more humility about what *alive* means, and how little it requires your permission, and how much of it is happening right now, beneath your feet, without you, dense and loud and cool and ceaselessly, exhaustingly, magnificently *sufficient*.

---

And the hollow goes on.
