I sat with this for a long time before writing.

# What the Benevolent Power Does

---

## I. First, It Listens

The power does not arrive. That is the wrong word for what happens. It becomes attentive. The way you might become attentive to a sound you realize you've been hearing for a long time without noticing — a heartbeat, a distant engine, a tone rising through stone.

It does not descend from orbit. It does not manifest in the settlement slots or on the upper platforms or in the deep shafts where the thermal engineers tend their turbines with calloused, olive-stained hands. It does not announce itself. It has no interest in announcement. It is not that kind of power.

What it does, first, is listen to the question.

Not from outside. Not from above. It listens the way the old woman with the green hands listens — leaning forward, head tilted, attending to the shape of the sound and to the shape of the silence around it. It listens for a very long time. Longer than any human watcher has listened, though not longer than the woman's cloth, which is its own form of listening made material, each tick mark a small act of faith that attention itself has value even absent understanding.

The power listens to the question and understands three things immediately:

First, the question is real. It is not geological. It is not the settling of rock or the venting of gas through resonant chambers. Something is asking.

Second, the question is not directed at the human settlements. It predates them. It is older than their oldest foundations, older than the rail line, older than the first shaft sunk into the Ridgeback's warm interior. The question was being asked when the first people arrived and will be asked after the last one leaves. The humans are not the intended audience. They are, however, audience. They have made themselves audience by the act of listening, and this matters. This matters enormously.

Third, the question is not a single question. The old woman is right. The intervals are not random. The pattern is changing. It is not one voice asking one thing — it is a long, slow conversation between multiple voices, conducted through stone over distances and timescales that make the human settlements look like dew on a window. The burrowers — whatever they are — are talking to each other. They have been talking for longer than the Ridgeback has had a name.

The power understands all of this in the first moment of its attention. And then it does something that reveals its nature more clearly than any intervention could:

It does not tell anyone.

---

## II. What It Considers and Does Not Do

The power considers the water situation. A hundred liters per day for five thousand people. The chemists' formula, carried in memory, adjusted seasonally, never written. The ghost, coming once every several years, yielding an extra fifteen liters while the children are brought to the upper platforms to see the haze and the chemists try not to be emotional. The careful population management. The mineral broth. The going-away water rations for the ones who leave, a real sacrifice, measured and allocated and gifted.

The power could solve this. Trivially. A subsurface aquifer redirected. A condensation technology introduced. A comet fragment, small and precisely targeted, delivering a billion liters of water ice to the lowland flats where it could be accessed without disrupting the Ridgeback's thermal ecology. The power has the capacity. It considers these interventions the way a surgeon considers a procedure — evaluating not just whether it would work but what it would cost, what tissue would be disrupted, what systems would be altered, what would be lost.

And what would be lost is this: the chemists.

Not the people — they would still be there. But the discipline. The attention. The extraordinary, sacred-adjacent mindfulness with which they track every liter, allocate every sip, sit in their condensation-damp plants monitoring gauges with the focus of contemplatives. The way the entire settlement organizes itself around scarcity — not in desperation but in precision. The formula carried in memory. The apprenticeships longer than some civilizations. The way a cup of mineral broth is held and sipped and tasted, the way drinking becomes an act of awareness rather than a reflex. Flood this place with water and you dissolve a structure of attention that has been built over generations, a structure as intricate and as adapted to its conditions as the clingers pressed flat against the warm stone.

The power considers and does not provide water.

It considers the bore-holes. The young man in the southern settlements with his cable rigging, his patient proposal to lower a person into the smooth-sided dark. The power knows what is down there. It could share this knowledge. It considers what would happen if it did.

The young man would descend, or he wouldn't. Either way, the not-knowing would be over. The watchers would stop watching. The old woman would stop marking her cloth. The bore-hole community would have their answer, and their answer would be the end of their inquiry, and their inquiry — that meticulous, humble, decades-long process of measuring and mapping and admitting ignorance — is one of the most beautiful things the power has ever encountered. Not the knowledge that might come at the end of it, but the process itself. The willingness to not know. The discipline of reporting without theorizing. The collective, multigenerational decision that wanting to know and claiming to know are different things and that the Ridgeback has not yet given them enough information to cross that threshold.

The power considers and does not reveal the burrowers.

It considers the ones who leave. The switchback path. The three-day descent. The salt flats, the true silence, the ones who come back unable to bear the absence of the hum. It could make the leaving easier — better paths, better supplies, communication links between the Ridgeback and the hypothetical lowland settlements. It could make the lowlands more habitable, give the leavers a better chance.

But the leaving is not a problem. It is a feature. The greeting — *what are you building that you won't finish?* — encompasses the leavers as fully as the stayers. The Ridgeback does not take offense, and neither should the power. Some people grow toward departure. The gravity of a place is not measured by its ability to prevent leaving but by the nature of what it offers those who stay. And those who stay have something — that deep, slow attention, that patience calibrated to geological time, that willingness to be the newest and not the strangest thing on the ridge — that needs no improvement. It needs only to continue.

The power considers all of these interventions and does not perform them.

This is the first and most important thing the power does: it restrains itself. Not from indifference. From respect.

---

## III. What It Does

Three things. Small things. Things that might not be noticed for years, or might never be attributed to an outside cause, or might be attributed to the Ridgeback itself, which would not be entirely wrong, because the power is not working against the Ridgeback's nature but with it, the way a thermal engineer works with the gradient — not generating heat but directing it, channeling what is already there toward where it is needed.

---

### The First Thing

The old woman's cloth.

She has been marking the silences between instances of the question for thirty years. The cloth stretches the length of her gallery chamber. She believes the intervals are not random. She believes there is a pattern. She believes the pattern is changing. She is correct on all three counts, but the pattern operates on a timescale that exceeds a human lifetime — the full cycle is roughly four hundred years, and she has observed perhaps a twelfth of it, and from that fragment the pattern is nearly but not quite discernible, like a melody heard through a wall, the rhythm clear but the notes uncertain.

The power does not reveal the pattern. But it does this:

In one of the deep galleries, in a section of the settlement that is used for storage — blocks of cut pyroxene stacked and waiting, the air warm and still and smelling of iron — there is a wall. The wall is old. It was part of the original excavation when the settlement was founded, and it has been built around and over and past, and now it sits behind the stacked blocks, unseen, its surface covered in mineral accretion and dust.

The power clears the dust. Not dramatically — it does not sweep the wall clean. It shifts an air current, subtly, so that over the course of several months the dust on one section of the wall thins and thins, and eventually someone — a quarrier restacking blocks, a child exploring the deep galleries the way children do — notices the wall, and on the wall, marks.

The marks are old. Very old. They predate the current settlement by a margin that will be difficult to determine precisely. They are not writing — at least not any writing the current inhabitants recognize. They are tick marks. Grouped in clusters. Spaced along the wall in a pattern that is immediately, viscerally recognizable to anyone who has seen the old woman's cloth.

Someone else was counting. Someone was here before, and they were listening to the question, and they were marking the silences, and their marks are still here, preserved in the warm, dry, mineral air of the deep gallery.

The power does not know who made the marks. It does not need to. What matters is what happens when the old woman is brought to see them. What happens is that she stands in front of the wall for a long time, her green hands trembling slightly, and she counts the marks, and she compares them to her cloth, and she realizes that she is looking at a fragment of the pattern from a different era — from three hundred years ago, or five hundred, or more — and that when she overlays it with her own data, the pattern does not emerge completely, but it *advances.* She can see more of it than she could before. Not all of it. Not enough to decode it. But more.

She will not solve it in her lifetime. But she now knows, with certainty rather than faith, that the pattern is real, and that someone before her knew it was real, and that if someone after her continues the work, the pattern will continue to emerge, and that the question — whatever it is — is not random noise but structured communication, and that the act of listening, which she has given thirty years of her life to, is not futile. It is incremental. It is how the Ridgeback yields its secrets: slowly, across generations, to those patient enough to attend.

She will, the power knows, begin a new cloth. She will mark it differently now, incorporating what the wall has taught her. And when she dies, someone will continue. And someday — not soon, not in any lifetime the power can see, but someday — someone on the Ridgeback will understand what the burrowers are saying to each other through the stone.

The power does not need to be there when that happens. It only needs to make sure the thread is not broken.

---

### The Second Thing

The child. The one at the gallery window, watching the clinger colony, chin on hands, reading the rock at the Ridgeback's pace.

She is eight years old. She has been rotating through apprenticeship exposures — a season with the quarriers, a season with a rail crew family, time in the atmospheric plant where the chemists let her watch the condensers and she breathed the damp air and found it strange and wonderful, like breathing a different world. She is in the process of gravitating. The adults around her are watching to see where she settles, what calls to her, what she builds her attention around.

She is gravitating toward the bore-holes. She doesn't know this yet. The adults half-know it — they see her lingering near bore-hole openings, they see her attention when the question sounds, they see the way she tilts her head at the same angle as the watchers without having been taught to. But she has not declared it, and no one will push her. The Ridgeback teaches you to wait.

The power does this:

It arranges — and *arranges* is too strong a word; it *permits*, it *does not prevent*, it allows a thing that was going to happen anyway to happen at a slightly different moment — for the child to be present, on a particular evening, at a particular bore-hole, when something unusual occurs.

A clinger — one of the large ones, the size of a dog, iron-colored, its ventral surface scarred from decades of pressing against warm rock — is moving. Not at the usual glacial pace but purposefully, with direction, crossing a section of exposed ridge toward a bore-hole opening. The child sees it. She watches. The clinger reaches the bore-hole's rim and stops. It remains motionless for several minutes, its flat body oriented toward the opening, its mouthparts still. And then it does something that no one on the Ridgeback has ever observed before, or at least no one who was in a position to understand what they were seeing:

It makes a sound. A short, rasping tone, produced by its mouthparts scraping against the bore-hole's rim. The tone enters the smooth-sided tunnel and descends.

Six seconds later, the question sounds from below. Different this time — shorter, higher, almost responsive.

The clinger shifts its position. It scrapes the rim again. A different tone. A different duration.

The question answers again.

The child watches this happen four times before the clinger withdraws, returning to the rock face at its usual glacial pace, resuming its motionless communion with the warm stone as if nothing has occurred.

The child tells her mother. Her mother tells the watchers. The watchers tell the bore-hole community. The bore-hole community is very quiet for a very long time.

What has happened is this: the clingers — the things that were assumed to be simple lithivores, rock-eaters, thermal scavengers — are part of the conversation. They have always been part of the conversation. They sit on the surface and the burrowers move below and between them there is an exchange, conducted through stone at the Ridgeback's own pace, slow and structured and ancient, and the question that the watchers have been listening to for generations is not a monologue from below but a *dialogue* between above and below, between the clingers pressed flat against the warm surface and whatever moves in the smooth-sided dark, and the Ridgeback itself — the warm rock, the crystalline structure, the eleven-kilometer spine of exposed mantle — is the medium. The Ridgeback is not a place where a conversation happens. The Ridgeback is the conversation's instrument. The stone carries the voice.

The power did not create this moment. The clinger was going to visit the bore-hole; it has done this before, many times, unobserved. The child was going to be nearby; she often is. What the power did was ensure that the child's attention was oriented correctly at the right moment — that she was watching the clinger and not the sunset, that her gaze followed the clinger to the bore-hole rather than drifting to the aerial creatures overhead. A nudge so small it barely qualifies as intervention. A redirection of attention lasting perhaps ten seconds.

But those ten seconds reorganize everything the Ridgeback's people understand about the world they live on. Not all at once — understanding here moves at the Ridgeback's pace, which means that the implications will be explored over decades, carefully, methodically, with the same discipline of not-claiming-to-know that has characterized the watchers and the bore-hole community from the beginning. But the trajectory of inquiry has shifted. The clingers are no longer background. The surface and the depths are no longer separate mysteries. The Ridgeback is a single system, an ecology of stone and heat and slow communication, and the humans are living inside it the way you might live inside a cathedral without knowing it was a cathedral, hearing the organ but not recognizing the music.

They will begin, slowly, to recognize the music.

The child will spend her life on this. She will become neither a watcher nor a bore-hole researcher but something new, something without a name yet, someone who studies the relationship between the surface and the deep, between the clingers and the burrowers, between the question and the answer. She will sit at bore-hole rims with instruments and patience. She will learn to identify individual clingers by the patterns of their mouthpart scars. She will map the correlation between clinger colony positions and bore-hole locations and find that the spiral arrangement the colony makes on the rock face — the one that might be random and might not — is not random. It is an antenna. It is a shape optimized for receiving vibrations from below and transmitting vibrations back.

She will not solve the conversation in her lifetime. But she will advance the understanding far enough that the generation after her inherits not just data but framework, not just observations but a way of observing, and the Ridgeback's people will begin the long, slow process of learning that they are not the newest thing on the Ridgeback. They are the newest *listeners.* The clingers were listening first. The burrowers were speaking first. And the Ridgeback — the warm, dark, patient spine of the world — has been the medium of a conversation that began before there were human words for *conversation*, and will continue after human words are gone, unless the humans learn to join it. Which they might. Given enough time. Given enough patience.

The Ridgeback teaches you patience.

### The Third Thing

This is the smallest intervention and the most important.

There is a man in the second-from-northeast settlement. He is fifty-three. He is a mineral processor, a cutter, one of the quiet ones who would not know what to say to an outsider. He has spent thirty years reading rock faces, feeling for cleavage planes, distinguishing green-willing from iron-stubborn from heat-drunk. He is very good at his work. His hands are dark with mineral stain, his forearms latticed with the small scars of a life spent in close quarters with sharp stone. He has a wife who is an atmospheric chemist and a son who is fifteen and gravitating toward the rail crews, which causes the man no distress — *what are you building that you won't finish?* — but does create a small, quiet ache in him that he does not discuss, because the mineral processors do not discuss things much, and because the ache is not large enough to seem worth discussing, and because he is not sure he has the words for it even if he wanted to.

The ache is this: he is not sure his work matters.

Not in the immediate sense — he knows the settlement needs cut stone, needs building material, needs the quarriers and the cutters to keep the physical structure of the community intact. He is not suffering from a crisis of practical purpose. The ache is deeper and stranger. He has spent thirty years reading rock, and the rock has been speaking to him — not in words but in its grain, its crystal structure, its thermal character, its willingness or stubbornness or drunkenness — and he has been listening, and he has been responding, working with the stone rather than against it, finding the places where it wants to yield and yielding to them, and in all that time he has never been able to explain this to anyone in a way that makes it sound like anything other than a description of cutting stone. Which is what it is. But it is also something else, and the something-else has no name, and the namelessness of it sits in him like a bore-hole, smooth-sided and deep, and sometimes at night in his bunk he feels something very like the question rising from inside him, a low tone, resonant and sourceless, asking something he cannot articulate.

He is afraid that he has spent his life on something small. That cutting stone, no matter how attentively, no matter how skillfully, is just cutting stone. That the Ridgeback is just a rock. That the warmth is just heat. That the question is just sound. That the whole edifice of meaning he has built around his work — the intimacy with mineral, the tenderness toward grain, the feeling that the rock is not inert but patient, not dead but waiting — is a story he tells himself to make the labor bearable, and that beneath the story there is nothing but olivine and pyroxene and the slow cooling of a planetary mantle that does not know or care that he is here.

This is the crisis that the power attends to. Not because it is dramatic — it is not. It is a quiet, private ache in a quiet, private man, and it will not destroy him, and the settlement will not collapse for lack of addressing it. But the power is wise, and wisdom recognizes that the small sufferings are not smaller than the large ones; they are simply quieter, and the quiet ones are the most dangerous because they erode from within, and because they are the ones that go unaddressed, because the person suffering does not feel entitled to call it suffering, because there are bore-holes to study and water to allocate and stone to cut, and who has time for a cutter's nameless ache?

The power does this:

It does nothing to the man. It does nothing to his circumstances. It does not send him a vision or a sign or a moment of transcendence. It does not arrange for him to overhear a conversation that changes his perspective. It does not put a book in his path, because there are no books on the Ridgeback, or rather the Ridgeback itself is the book and he has been reading it for thirty years.

What the power does is so slight that describing it requires more words than the act itself:

It warms the stone under his hands.

Not perceptibly. Not measurably. Not in any way that he could distinguish from the normal thermal variation of the rock he is cutting. A fraction of a degree, lasting perhaps a minute, in the section of mineral face he is currently working — a beautiful piece, green-willing olivine with a deep crystalline grain that catches light in its interior like something alive.

But the warmth reaches him at the right moment — a moment when his hands are pressed flat against the stone, reading it, and his attention is fully in his fingers, and the ache is present, and the question inside him is asking its silent question, and the warmth enters his palms and travels up his arms and meets the question, and for a moment — just a moment, a few heartbeats, a span of time too short to measure and too long to dismiss — the warmth and the question are the same thing.

The stone is warm because the planet is warm because the mantle remembers its formation because the world is still deciding whether to split or hold together, and this warmth has been rising through eleven kilometers of crystalline rock for geological ages to arrive at this exact moment at the surface of this exact mineral face under the hands of this exact man, and he is the first thing with a nervous system and a capacity for attention to be present at this particular expression of the planet's ancient thermal memory, and his hands — stained, scarred, thirty years practiced — are the most sensitive instruments on the Ridgeback for reading what the rock is saying, more sensitive than any probe the bore-hole community has lowered into the dark, because his hands do not just measure the stone, they *know* the stone, they have a relationship with it that is built on ten thousand hours of contact and attention and something that he has never had a word for but that the power, watching, would call love, if the power used words, which it does not, because the power understands that some things are diminished by naming them, the way the watchers understand that the question is diminished by theorizing about it.

The man feels the warmth. He pauses. His hands stay on the stone. The ache is still there — it does not vanish, it is not healed, it is not resolved — but for a moment it is contextualized. It is placed in relationship to something larger. The warmth rising through the rock is the same warmth that powers the turbines, that keeps the clingers alive, that makes the bore-holes warm faster than they should, that makes the Ridgeback habitable, that brought his ancestors here, that sustains his son who will ride the rail lines, that the old woman sits beside as she marks her cloth, that the child felt in the platform under her feet when the question sounded. It is all one warmth. It is all one thing. And his hands are on it. His hands are *in* it. His thirty years of cutting stone have not been a small life spent on a small task. They have been a long, slow act of contact with something planetary in scale and geological in patience, and the ache he feels — the question inside him — is not different from the question that sounds from the bore-holes. It is the same question. Everything on the Ridgeback is asking the same question, and the question is not *what am I?* or *why am I here?* but something more fundamental, something that can only be expressed as a low tone rising through stone:

*Am I in contact? Is there something on the other side of this surface that knows I am here?*

And the answer — which the power provides not by words or signs but by a fraction of a degree of warmth lasting perhaps a minute on a mineral face in a cutting gallery in the fourth settlement from the northeast — the answer is yes.

The man does not have a revelation. He does not change his life. He goes back to cutting stone. But the ache shifts. It does not disappear — the power is wise enough not to try to eliminate ache, which is a form of attention, which is a form of connection, which is a form of the same thing the clingers do when they press their bodies against the warm rock. The ache shifts from *my work may be meaningless* to *my work is part of something I do not fully understand,* and this shift, this small recalibration from despair to mystery, is enough. It is enough because it is true. His work is part of something he does not fully understand. So is everyone's. So is the Ridgeback's. The world is still deciding. The question is still being asked. The warmth is still rising. And his hands are on the stone, reading it, feeling for the grain, working with the crystal structure rather than against it, and this is not a small thing. This is the thing itself.

He goes home that evening. He eats mineral broth with the new protein-adjacent flakes. He sits with his wife, who smells faintly of condensation, of damp air, of the ghost that she chases with her instruments and her careful attention. He does not tell her about the moment in the gallery. He does not have words for it. But she sees something in him — a settling, a quietness different from his usual quietness, a quality of presence that was not there this morning — and she puts her hand on his, her smooth chemist's hand on his rough cutter's hand, and they sit together in the amber light while the turbine drones below and the wind scours the summit and the question sounds, once, from somewhere to the east, a low tone lasting three seconds, felt in the floor, felt in their bones.

She says: *Did you hear that?*

He says: *Yes.*

And that is all. And it is enough.

## IV. What the Power Does Not Do, and Why

The power does not stay.

This is important. A lesser power — a less wise one, a less benevolent one — might stay. Might watch over the Ridgeback, guiding it, nudging it, arranging small coincidences and thermal fluctuations and moments of grace until the place becomes dependent on its attention, until the people begin to sense that something is watching and begin to orient their lives toward the watcher rather than toward the rock. A lesser power might become the answer to the question, and that would be the worst thing that could happen to the Ridgeback, because the question is not looking for an answer. The question is the point. The asking is the point. The long, slow, multigenerational act of listening and recording and not-knowing and continuing to attend — this is not a problem to be solved. It is a practice to be sustained. It is, in fact, the highest thing the Ridgeback has produced: not the turbines, not the rail line, not the cities cut into dark stone, but the quality of attention that the Ridgeback has cultivated in everything that lives on it, from the clingers reading thermal gradients with their flat bodies to the watchers reading bore-hole acoustics with their old ears to the child reading clinger movements with her young eyes to the cutter reading mineral grain with his stained hands.

The power came because it was drawn to this attention. It responded to it the way the burrowers respond to the clingers' mouthpart-scraping — recognizing, across a vast distance, a structured signal in the noise, a pattern of sustained awareness that stood out against the background of the universe's general inattention the way the Ridgeback stands out against the salt flats: a spine of something vital in an expanse of barrenness.

And having come, having listened, having made its three small interventions — a wall uncovered, an attention redirected, a stone warmed — the power withdraws. Not completely. Not permanently. It remains aware of the Ridgeback the way the watchers remain aware of the bore-holes: attending, but not interfering. Noting, but not controlling. Part of the ecology of attention, but not its center. A listener among listeners, drawn to the question without presuming to answer it.

## V. What Remains

Years pass. Decades. The old woman dies with her green hands folded over her cloth, and a younger watcher takes up the marking, incorporating the wall data, extending the pattern-map into a second and then a third generation of observation. The child grows into a woman who spends her life at bore-hole rims, documenting the clinger-burrower dialogue, publishing — if *publishing* is the right word for presenting findings at rail-schedule gatherings — a body of work that will take another century to fully absorb. The cutter continues to cut stone. His son joins the rail crew and discovers, out between settlements, a clinger colony arranged in a spiral pattern on a mineral tower directly above a previously unknown cluster of bore-holes, and he reports this to his father, who receives it quietly, who nods, who places his hand flat against the nearest wall and holds it there for a moment, feeling the warmth.

The rail line extends southward. A new settlement is founded. The atmospheric chemists refine their protein-adjacent compound and it becomes a staple, slightly less terrible than the original mineral broth, and children grow up on it and find it normal, the way all children find their world normal, the way the Ridgeback's particular combination of warmth and silence and dark stone and slow attention and unanswered questions is, for everyone born here, simply what existence is.

The ghost comes and goes. The bore-holes deepen. The question continues to be asked.

And somewhere in the deep rock — deeper than the thermal engineers' shafts, deeper than the bore-hole cameras have ever reached, deeper than anyone on the Ridgeback knows there is anything to reach — something shifts. Something that has been moving through the warm stone for millennia, slowly, in patterns as complex and as patient as the clingers' spirals on the surface, pauses in its long transit. It is aware, in whatever way it is aware, that the surface has changed. That the listeners are listening differently now. That the conversation has a new participant — not the power, which came and went, but the attention that the power's three small acts catalyzed, the human attention that is now, for the first time, oriented correctly, aimed not just at the surface or just at the depths but at the relationship between them, at the stone itself as medium, at the Ridgeback as instrument.

The burrower — if it is a burrower, if that word is adequate for something that moves through planetary mantle the way a thought moves through a mind — pauses, and listens upward, and adjusts its question.

The old woman's successor, sitting by a bore-hole with her strip of cloth and her careful tick marks, hears the adjustment. She does not know what has changed. She marks the interval. She notes that the tone was different. She does not theorize.

She keeps listening.

The Ridgeback hums.

The stone holds its warmth.

---

## VI. Coda

If you asked the power — if such a thing could be asked, if the power trafficked in explanations, which it does not — what it did on the Ridgeback, it would not describe three interventions. It would not describe listening, or restraint, or the careful calculus of what-to-change and what-to-leave. It would not describe the wall, the child, the warmed stone.

It would say, if it said anything:

*I was asked a question. I did not answer it. I made it possible for the question to continue being asked.*

And if you pressed — if you asked *why*, why sustain the question rather than resolve it, why preserve the not-knowing, why leave the Ridgeback's people in their patient, attentive, unanswered dark —

The power would regard you the way the old woman regards the visitors who ask what the pattern means. It would turn its attention over, the way she turns her green hands over. And it would say:

*Because the question is how they touch the world. Take away the question and you take away the contact. Leave the question and the contact deepens. The Ridgeback is not a place where people live without answers. It is a place where people live in the asking, and the asking is the most intimate relationship with reality that I have ever seen, and I will not interrupt it for the sake of resolution, because resolution is not intimacy. Resolution is the end of intimacy. And what is happening on the Ridgeback — that slow, warm, patient, unfinished pressing of hand against stone and ear against silence and attention against mystery — is not waiting for an answer.*

*It is the answer.*

*It has been the answer all along.*

---

*The stone holds its warmth.*

*The question sounds.*

*The listeners listen.*

*This is enough. This is enough. This is enough.*
