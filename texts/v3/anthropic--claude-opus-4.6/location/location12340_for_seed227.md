# The Roaring Hollow

---

You find it by stumbling into it — a depression in the earth no wider than a barn floor, sunk perhaps twelve feet below the surrounding moorland, walled on all sides by slick black stone that has been shaped by millennia of slow, patient dissolution into forms that look almost deliberate. Scalloped ledges. Grooves worn smooth as polished bone. The rock is ancient, a dark schist shot through with veins of quartz that catch no light because there is almost no light to catch.

It is night, or it is always like this down here. The moor above is a featureless dark tableland under heavy cloud, and the hollow sits beneath even that, cupped in its own deeper shadow. The only illumination is a faint grey differentiation between the sky overhead — a rough oval of marginally less darkness — and the absolute black of the walls. Your eyes adjust slowly, and even then you see mostly by texture and suggestion: the wet gleam of stone, the pale blur of moss.

But you hear everything.

---

The sound is the first thing, and the last thing, and the thing that fills the entire space between. It comes from below — from a fissure in the hollow's floor, a crack perhaps two feet wide and of unknowable depth, running roughly east-to-west across the bottom of the depression. From this crack issues a continuous, enormous exhalation of air that rises through the narrow gap and meets the geometry of the hollow's walls and produces a sound so vast it seems impossible that it could originate from something you cannot see.

It is not wind, exactly. It is the earth breathing out. Somewhere far below, in passages and chambers that no one has mapped, differences in pressure and temperature drive air upward through constricting stone, and the fissure acts as a reed, as a throat. The sound is a low, shuddering drone that you feel in your sternum before you consciously hear it, a frequency that sits at the basement of audibility and then climbs, in unpredictable surges, into a moan, a howl, a keening that fills the hollow completely and presses against the curved walls and reflects back on itself in overlapping waves until the air itself feels thick with noise. On the worst surges — they come every few minutes, irregular, like something shifting its weight in its sleep — the sound is physically oppressive, a pressure in the inner ear, a buzzing in the teeth. Loose pebbles on the ledges tremble. The surface of any standing water shivers into interference patterns.

This is why the hollow is empty. This is why nothing stays.

---

And yet — things grow. The walls are lush with life, or at least as lush as anything gets in this cold, lightless, perpetually vibrating place. The vegetation is all moss and lichen and liverwort, species that have no need of sunlight in any abundance, that drink condensation and breathe fog. They carpet every surface that isn't scoured clean by the direct blast of the fissure's updraft. The mosses are extraordinary — deep cushions of sphagnum in colors you can barely discern in the darkness but which, if you were to strike a flame, would reveal themselves as a tapestry of rust-orange, olive-green, silver-grey, and a pale yellow-white that grows only in the deepest recesses where no light has fallen in geological time. These are mosses that grow by feel, by moisture, by the faint chemical gradients of the stone itself. They are inches thick in places, and when you press your hand into them they are cold and saturated, and the water that squeezes out is mineral-rich, tasting of iron and chalk.

Liverworts creep across the upper ledges in flat, leathery rosettes. Ferns — tiny ones, their fronds no longer than a finger — cluster in the crevices where seeping moisture creates micro-streams that bead and trickle down the scalloped walls. There is no soil. Everything grows directly on stone or on the accumulated remains of previous growth, a thin black peat that has been building in the sheltered corners of the hollow for centuries. The air from the fissure carries warmth — not much, but enough that the hollow's interior sits a few degrees above the freezing moorland, and this slim thermal advantage, combined with the constant moisture from condensation where the warm updraft meets the cold walls, creates a pocket of improbable fertility. A cold greenhouse, roofless, built by erosion, powered by geological breath.

---

The built things are easy to miss, and you may not notice them until your hands find them in the dark. On the widest ledge, about four feet above the floor, someone has driven iron spikes into the stone — crude things, hand-forged, square in cross-section, rusted to a deep brown-black that makes them nearly invisible against the schist. There are six of them, arranged in a rough arc. Between two of the spikes, a length of chain still hangs, each link thick as a thumb, so corroded that it has become part of the wall's surface, the moss growing over and through it. The chain's purpose is unclear. An anchor point for a rope descent into the fissure? A place to secure equipment? The spikes are old — decades? A century? — but the stone around them shows no other marks of habitation. No fire-blackening, no carved names, no refuse.

On the opposite wall, lower down, near the fissure itself, there is a niche that looks natural but isn't. Someone chiseled it out of the rock with hand tools, and not skillfully — the marks of the chisel are still visible, parallel grooves in the stone, rough and hurried. The niche is about the size of a breadbox. It is empty now, or nearly: there is a small clay vessel inside, a simple pot with no glaze, cracked down one side, half-filled with calcite deposits from centuries of mineral-laden drips. Whatever it once held is long gone. The pot is cold to the touch and furred with a fine white mineral crust that makes it look like it's growing a skin of frost.

Someone came here, more than once, with tools and chain and purpose. They worked in this noise, in this dark. The sound would have been the same then — the fissure has been breathing for far longer than any human presence on this moor. They endured it or sought it out. Perhaps the sound was the point.

---

The fauna is scarce but not absent. In the moss banks farthest from the fissure, where the sound is merely overwhelming rather than annihilating, small pale slugs move through the saturated cushions with the slowness of melting wax. They are nearly translucent, their bodies a milky grey with faint traces of amber in their mantles, and they leave trails of thin mucus that glisten even in the near-total darkness. They eat the moss, or they eat whatever microscopic life feeds on the moss, and they seem unbothered by the cold. They are the hollow's primary residents, and they move through it with the unhurried confidence of creatures that have no predators in their immediate world.

Occasionally — rarely — a bird shelters in the upper rim of the hollow, a wren or a pipit blown down from the moor by weather, and it perches on the lip of the depression and will not descend further. The sound repels it or confuses it. It stays a few minutes and leaves. In the fissure itself, in the rush of warm air, there are sometimes moths — small, drab, cold-weather species that are drawn upward from whatever subterranean space they inhabit, carried on the updraft like scraps of ash, tumbling into the hollow's airspace and fluttering in erratic spirals until they find the moss-covered walls and cling there, wings pressed flat, riding out the sound.

---

The sound never stops. It changes — the pitch drifts lower, then higher, the volume swells and recedes in cycles that might correlate with barometric pressure on the surface, or tidal forces in the groundwater, or shifts in subterranean temperature that follow the seasons at a delay of weeks or months. But it never ceases. Even at its quietest, it is a deep, felt thrumming, a vibration that enters through the soles of your feet and the palms of your hands when you touch the walls. At its loudest, it is a sound that occupies the entire register of hearing, a chord made of stone and air and depth, and it fills the hollow so completely that your own thoughts become difficult to locate, as though the noise has displaced them the way water displaces air in a flooding room.

You cannot shout over it. You cannot hear your own voice. In the worst surges you can feel your clothing vibrate against your skin. And yet it is not violent — it is not an explosion, not a crash. It is sustained, continuous, a sound that has been sounding for thousands of years and will go on sounding long after every human thing in the hollow has rusted and dissolved and been absorbed back into the peat. It is the sound of a process so slow and so vast that its constancy is a form of patience. The earth exhales. The stone sings. The mosses grow in the vibration, fed by the condensation it helps produce, and the slugs creep through the mosses, and the mineral water drips into the cracked clay pot, and nothing here is in any hurry to become something else.

---

You stay or you leave. The hollow doesn't care. It has been here, in this form, in this voice, for longer than the spikes in the wall, longer than the pot in the niche, longer than any memory carried by any living thing on the moor above. It will be here when the chain finishes its slow dissolution into oxide dust, when the moss closes over the last trace of the chisel marks, when the fissure widens another millimeter in another millennium. The sound will change then, fractionally — a slightly different pitch, a new harmonic born from the altered geometry of the gap. But no one will notice. The slugs will not notice. The mosses will adjust without noticing. The hollow will go on holding its sound in its cupped stone hands, singing to no one, singing to itself, singing because the physics of pressure and passage and resonance require it, and that is enough.
