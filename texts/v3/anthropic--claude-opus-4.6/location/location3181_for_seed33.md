# The Risen City of Keth Anoul

---

At first you think the mountain range is natural, the way it tears across the sky in a serrated line of dark summits, and then you see the windows.

Not all at once. The light is wrong here—a permanent gloaming, as though the sun exists only as a rumor passed along the underside of the cloud deck, which is not cloud at all but a membrane of condensed silicate dust that hasn't moved in centuries. Beneath that unchanging ceiling, the light arrives pre-exhausted, a dim ochre wash that makes edges uncertain. So the windows don't announce themselves. They emerge. First one, a dark rectangle high on a sheer face of granite-colored stone. Then a row of them. Then you realize the entire ridgeline, stretching in both directions farther than you can resolve in the murk, is architecture.

Keth Anoul was a city once. It may still be. The distinction depends on definitions that don't apply here anymore.

---

The elevation is staggering. You stand on what was once a street—a broad avenue of fitted stone slabs, each one the size of a banquet table, laid with such precision that even now, after whatever happened, the joints are barely wide enough to slip a fingernail into. This street runs along the spine of a ridge that drops away on both sides into canyons of vertiginous depth. The air is thin. Cool, but not freezing—a persistent chill that doesn't bite but instead settles into you incrementally, the kind of cold that belongs to altitude and absence. There is no wind. The air doesn't move. It simply sits here, thin and patient, as though it too has been waiting.

The buildings rise directly from the ridge, from the walls of the canyons, from outcroppings and spurs of exposed rock. They are the rock in some places—carved inward, hollowed, shaped. In other places they are built upon it, stacked courses of stone fitted without mortar, rising in tiers and setbacks to heights that shouldn't be structurally possible. Towers lean at angles that follow the slope of the mountain beneath them rather than the pull of gravity above them. Bridges span the canyons, thin arcs of stone that look like ribs. Everything is stone. No wood. No metal. No glass in the windows—just openings, dark and square, thousands of them, tens of thousands, looking out onto the dim air like the compound eye of something vast and sleeping.

And the city goes on. You can see it extending along the ridgeline in both directions, branching where the ridge branches, climbing where it climbs, descending along subsidiary spurs that drop toward canyon floors you cannot see. It occupies the entire mountain range, or the mountain range has become it, or perhaps there was never a difference. The scale is disorienting. Individual buildings lose their meaning when everything is built. The concept of "a building" dissolves into something more geological—strata of habitation, layers and layers, visible in cross-section where canyon walls have been carved open, revealing rooms behind rooms behind rooms going back into the mountain's body like chambers in a nautilus shell.

There is no water anywhere. The canyon floors—what you can see of them when you approach the edge and look down through hundreds of meters of dim air—are dry. Not dusty-dry or cracked-earth-dry but *mineral-dry*, a dryness so absolute it feels ontological. The stone here has never known water. Or if it did, the memory has been taken from it. The air holds no moisture. Your lips don't crack because the dryness isn't aggressive; it's simply the default state, the way things are when water has been subtracted from the possibility space of a place.

---

Here is where the wrongness starts, if wrongness is a thing that can be said to start anywhere in particular.

The city is active.

Not inhabited—you can look down the avenue in both directions and see no one, peer into window after window and find only dark rooms with stone floors and stone walls and nothing else. The emptiness is comprehensive. But the city is *doing things*.

The first sign: you notice that the fitted stones of the avenue are not in the same positions they were in when you arrived. Not dramatically shifted—nothing has moved while you watched. But the pattern of the joints has changed. Stones that were rectangular are now trapezoidal. A stone that had a distinctive vein of darker mineral running through it is now three meters to your left instead of beneath your feet. The avenue has rearranged itself in the gaps between your observations, in the moments when your attention was elsewhere. It does this continuously. If you stare at one section without blinking, it remains still. The moment you look away and back, it has changed. Not always. Not predictably. But often enough that the ground beneath you feels like a living thing maintaining the *appearance* of stillness purely as a courtesy.

The buildings do it too. A tower you noted against the skyline has gained a story. Or lost one. A bridge that spanned the canyon to your right now arrives at a different point on the far wall, connecting to a terrace that you are fairly certain wasn't there before. Windows open and close—not with shutters, but by the stone itself rearranging, a window becoming wall, wall becoming window. Sometimes you catch the edge of it—a flicker in your peripheral vision, a sense that the geometry of a roofline has just *settled*, like a bird alighting, into a new configuration. But you never see the motion itself. The city changes between states without passing through the intervening moments. It is not moving. It is *being rearranged*, or rearranging itself, through some mechanism that has nothing to do with physics as you understand it.

This is constant. This is what "active" means here. The city is in a state of continuous discontinuous transformation. Buildings appear. Buildings vanish. Streets redirect themselves. The topology of the canyon system shifts—a dead end becomes a thoroughfare, a wide plaza compresses into a narrow alley. And all of it in silence.

The silence is the most unnerving part. A city of this scale, reshaping itself ceaselessly, should produce sound—grinding stone, cracking mortar, the groan of stressed material. There is nothing. Sometimes you hear a faint tonal hum, so low it might be the resonance of the stone itself or might be your own blood in your ears. Sometimes, from very far away and very far below, there is something that might be a click—a single, sharp, quiet sound, like one stone tapping against another, the way you might tap a fingernail against a table. And then silence again. The hum. Your breathing. That is all.

---

Nothing grows here, or almost nothing. In the crevices where walls meet the mountain's natural stone, there are patches of something—not lichen exactly, but analogous. Thin films of grey-violet material that cling to surfaces with no apparent structure, no visible texture. Under close inspection they seem crystalline rather than biological, but they spread the way living things spread, following invisible gradients of whatever they feed on. They are cool to the touch. They don't react when you touch them. They are present in roughly one out of every fifty crevices, no pattern you can discern, and they may or may not be the same patches from moment to moment—because of course the walls rearrange, and so the crevices rearrange, and so whatever occupies them is shuffled and redistributed by the city's ceaseless silent reconfiguration.

Whether these films are alive depends on what alive means here. They persist. They may grow. They may simply be a mineral deposit that looks like growth. In a place where stone rearranges itself between glances, the line between alive and not-alive has lost its authority.

---

The fauna—such as it is.

You will go hours without seeing anything. Then, on a ledge across the canyon, a shape. Something between a bird and an insect, but large—the size of a cat perhaps, though distances are hard to judge in the murk. It is dark, the same color as the stone, and it sits perfectly still on a cornice. You watch it. It does not move. You are not certain it is alive. Then, without transition—no leap, no wingbeat, no blur of motion—it is on a different cornice, fifty meters away, in an identical posture. It has changed location the way the buildings change location. Between one moment and the next. Without crossing the intervening space.

You will see these creatures—if creatures is the word—perhaps four or five times in a day. Never the same one, or always the same one; there's no way to tell. They are always still. They are always watching, or at least oriented toward you, though you cannot identify eyes or a face. They seem to be part of the city's logic, subject to its rules, moving the way it moves—by discontinuity, by substitution, state to state with nothing between.

Once, very far below in the canyon, you see something larger. A shape that hugs the vertical wall, spread out like a starfish on the stone. It is pale. It is the first pale thing you have seen. It occupies a space the size of a house, flattened against the cliff face, and its edges are irregular, reaching into crevices and window openings. You watch it for a long time. It does not move. When you look away for a moment—just a moment, to check the avenue behind you—and look back, the wall where it clung is now featureless stone, and you cannot find it anywhere in the canyon. You do not see it again.

---

There is something happening to the geometry of the city that you only begin to understand after what might be hours or might be days—time is hard to track without a moving sun.

The avenue you have been following curves gently to the left. You are certain of this. You have been walking along it, noting the curve, watching the canyon deepen on your right. But when you turn around to look back the way you came, the avenue also curves to the left. In both directions. This is not possible on a ridge. You are not on a Möbius strip; the ground is flat, the canyon is on your right and only your right, and yet the road curves leftward behind you and leftward ahead of you simultaneously.

The buildings, too, have begun to show spatial contradictions. A tower that is clearly across the canyon—you can see the full depth of the gap, hundreds of meters of empty air—also has a door that opens onto the avenue three paces in front of you. You can look through the door and see a staircase rising into the tower's interior. You can look across the canyon and see the tower's exterior, the same windows, the same cornice work. Both are true at once. The city is not obeying Euclidean constraints anymore, if it ever was. It is folding its own space, connecting points that have no business being connected, creating adjacencies between things that are visually distant. Perhaps this is how it rearranges itself—not by moving stone through space, but by changing which parts of space are next to which.

This means you may not be making progress in any linear sense when you walk. The avenue may be a closed loop disguised by its own curvature. The canyon may be the same canyon seen from different angles of a non-orientable surface. The city may be much smaller than it appears, or much larger, or both, depending on how you measure—steps or sight lines, topology or geometry.

You continue walking because there is nothing else to do.

---

The cool air sits against your skin. The dim light persists, unchanging. The hum vibrates below the threshold of certainty. Ahead of you the avenue extends into the gloaming, lined with dark windows and doorways that may or may not have been there a moment ago, bridged by stone arcs that may or may not connect to the same points on the far side that they connected to when you last checked. The grey-violet films gleam faintly in their crevices. Something dark and still perches on a tower that has gained two additional stories since you last looked at it.

The city of Keth Anoul is its own process. It does not need inhabitants. It does not need water or light or warmth. It needs only the next configuration, and the next, and the next—an endless silent shuffling of stone and space and the rules that hold them together, up here in the thin air at the top of the world, beneath a sky that is not sky, in a light that is not quite light, rearranging itself for no one, or for you, or for the difference between the two, which here—like so many other differences—may no longer hold.
