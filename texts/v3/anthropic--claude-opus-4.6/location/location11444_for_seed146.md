# The Underloom

---

You are beneath.

Not beneath soil or stone in the way a cellar is beneath a house—beneath in a more fundamental sense, as though the planet's crust were a ceiling you have never seen and the world you know begins at the first darkness below it. This place has no memory of sky. It has no concept of *above*. What exists is an immensity stretching in all directions, warm and close and still, and it has been this way for longer than anything here could measure.

---

The cavern—but cavern is the wrong word. Cavern implies a room, and rooms have walls. This is a *volume*. A continental negative space hollowed through mineral substrate, extending so far in every direction that it generates its own convection: faint thermals that never quite become wind, a permanent atmospheric equilibrium where warm air rising from geothermal vents below meets the unchanging warmth radiating from the stone above and simply... stays. The air is thick. Not with moisture—there is almost no water here, none that flows, none that pools—but with particulate mineral dust so fine it behaves almost like humidity, a haze of powdered silicate and calcium carbonate that softens what little light exists into a gauzy, ruddy nothing. The temperature hovers at the edge of comfort and stays there. It has always been this exact temperature. It will always be this exact temperature. You could set a clock by it if clocks had any meaning here.

The darkness is not total. It is *nearly* total, which is worse, because your eyes strain against it ceaselessly and are rewarded just enough to keep straining. The light—what passes for it—comes from below, from threads of exposed mineral that phosphoresce in dull reds and faded ambers, woven through the floor-stone in veins that branch and reconnect like circulatory systems of something that was never alive. These veins are what the inhabitants call the Loom, or something that translates approximately as Loom. They glow without flickering. They have glowed at this exact intensity since the stone cooled into its current configuration. In their light, which barely qualifies as light, you can see perhaps ten meters in any direction before shapes dissolve into a warm auburn murk.

The floor is sharp. Young stone—geologically speaking, this space was carved recently, within the last few hundred thousand years, by processes that are not entirely geological. The rock formations have the aggressive geometry of fresh fracture: ridges like filed teeth, shelves that terminate in clean right angles, columns where crystal growth has produced hexagonal prisms still unworn by time. Nothing is smooth. Nothing has had the chance to become smooth. The floor rises and falls in jagged terraces, and where it drops away into deeper levels, the edges are precise enough to cut. Obsidian flows have pooled in some of the lower reaches, black glass interrupting the dull limestone and basalt, and their surfaces still hold the frozen ripple-marks of their cooling. There is a faint smell—dry chalk, hot iron, something almost electric, like ozone's mineral cousin.

---

Now: the fauna.

They are everywhere. Not visible, not at first, but everywhere. The density of life in this place is startling once you learn to perceive it. Your first clue is sound—or rather, the particular *quality* of the near-silence. It is not the silence of emptiness. It is the silence of ten thousand living things holding very, very still. A communal hush. The sound of breathing that isn't breathing—of gas exchange through membranes, of limbs adjusting fractionally against stone, of crystalline structures growing at the molecular level inside living tissue. If you hold your own breath, you might catch the faintest clicking. Ticking. A staccato so quiet it registers more as texture than sound, like the feeling of sand shifting under your palm.

They are mineral-fauna. Not mineral in the way a coral is mineral—built from biological processes that co-opt calcium. Mineral in a stranger sense. Their bodies are lattice-structures of crystallized silicate threaded with veins of the same phosphorescent ore that lights the Loom, and they *grow* in the way crystals grow: atom by atom, face by face, along planes dictated by molecular geometry. But they move. They choose. They congregate.

The most common—the ones layered across every surface in such numbers that the stone itself seems to shift if you stare long enough—are small, flat, tessellated creatures roughly the shape and size of a human hand, though "hand" implies too much organic softness. They are geometric. Pentagonal, mostly, with five radial limbs that taper to points so fine they could be mistaken for decorative mineral growth. They cling to walls, to ceilings, to each other. They stack in layers three and four deep. Where the Loom-veins surface, they cluster thickest, their translucent bodies refracting the dull amber glow, and in those places the light seems briefly richer, almost warm, almost like firelight seen through smoked glass. They feed—if feeding is the right word—by pressing their undersides to the phosphorescent veins and absorbing energy through some process that is neither photosynthesis nor chemosynthesis but something for which there is no existing term. Lithotrophic communion. They do not move quickly. They do not move at all, most of the time. They have found their positions and they hold them with the patience of geology.

Larger forms thread between the clusters. Things like millipedes but rendered in quartz—elongated, segmented, each segment a distinct crystal habit, so that a single creature might transition from cubic to orthorhombic to monoclinic along its length, a living catalog of crystal systems. They are a meter long, some of them, and they navigate the sharp terrain with a precision that suggests proprioception of extraordinary resolution, each of their dozens of pointed legs finding purchase on edges that would slice open skin. They move rarely and always along the same paths, grooves worn a fraction of a millimeter into the stone—the only erosion this world has known. Their purpose, if they have one, seems to be the transport of mineral fragments from one terrace to another. You might watch one for an hour and see it carry a single flake of mica across twelve meters of broken ground and deposit it in a specific location on a specific shelf with the care of a jeweler setting a stone.

And then there are the deep ones. You hear them before you see them—before you could ever see them, because they inhabit the lowest reaches, the obsidian trenches, the places where the Loom-veins run thickest and the phosphorescence achieves something closer to actual illumination. A sound like a struck bell, impossibly low, felt in the sternum more than heard. It comes at long intervals—every few hours, perhaps—and it resonates through the stone itself, carried upward through the terraces, and every creature in the vicinity pauses when it sounds. The pentagonal hand-shapes press flatter against the rock. The crystal millipedes freeze mid-step. The sound is not communication, exactly. It is more like a heartbeat—involuntary, regular, a byproduct of whatever process sustains the largest organisms in this ecology. You have not seen them. You have felt the vibration of their movement transmitted through the floor, a ponderous grinding that suggests bodies of immense mass shifting against stone. The inhabitants—the ones who build—speak of them without speaking of them, the way one speaks of weather. They are a condition. They are a fact. They have always been there. They will always be there.

---

The builders. Yes.

Scattered across the terraces—infrequent, modest, unmistakable once noticed—are structures. Shelters is too generous. They are arrangements. Stacked stone, mostly: flat basalt plates leaned against crystal columns to create triangular recesses barely large enough to crouch in. Some have been reinforced with obsidian shards wedged into gaps, their edges deliberately knapped—the only clear evidence of tool use. The knapping is crude but intentional. Someone understood conchoidal fracture. Someone struck stone against stone with purpose and direction. In a few places, the Loom-veins have been *uncovered*—the overlying stone carefully chipped away to expose a brighter length of phosphorescent mineral, creating something between a lamp and an altar. These exposed veins glow no brighter than the others, but they have been cleared of the hand-shaped creatures, and the cleared boundary is maintained. There are scratches on the surrounding stone that might be marks of territory or marks of counting or marks of something else entirely.

Who builds? You catch a glimpse, rarely. Something moves at the edge of perception—larger than the hand-shapes, smaller than the millipedes, upright in a way that nothing else here is upright. Bipedal, or at least occasionally bipedal. Limbed in ways that don't resolve clearly in the near-darkness. It carries something—a shard, a tool, a piece of itself. It is not stone the way the others are stone. It is something between. Mineral-laced flesh, or flesh-laced mineral. It pauses near one of the uncovered Loom-veins and does something with its hands—or what serve as hands—that you cannot quite see. The amber light catches on a surface that might be skin or might be polished crystal. Then it is gone, retreated into one of the stone arrangements, and the silence reconstitutes itself around its absence.

There are perhaps a dozen of these shelters within what you can perceive of the space. There are likely thousands more, extending across the terraced volume in a pattern too large to apprehend, scattered like homesteads across a dark continent of broken stone. They do not cluster into villages. They do not form paths between each other. Each one is an island. Each one is sufficient.

---

What strikes you, finally, is the equilibrium. Nothing here is becoming something else. The thermals do not intensify into storms. The crystal-fauna do not overpopulate or starve. The builders do not expand or innovate. The deep ones do not approach or retreat. The Loom glows. The stone holds its shape, too young to have worn smooth, too stable to fracture further. The dust hangs in the air at its particular density and never settles and never thickens. The clicking-ticking of ten thousand crystal organisms persists at its particular volume—quiet enough to forget, present enough to remember. The warmth does not waver.

You are inside a system that has found its resting state. Not dead—tremendously, densely alive—but *solved*. Every gradient that could drive change has been resolved. Every niche is occupied. Every flow has reached its steady state. The world is a warm dark engine in perfect idle, burning its geothermal fuel at precisely the rate it is replenished, converting it to phosphorescence and crystal-growth and the slow transport of mica flakes across basalt terraces, and it will do this—exactly this, at exactly this intensity—until something comes from outside to disturb it.

Nothing comes from outside.

Nothing has ever come from outside.

You are the first foreign gradient this system has encountered, and as you stand on the sharp young stone, in the near-dark, in the thick mineral air that tastes of chalk and deep time, you feel the attention of everything around you in the quality of the silence—which has grown, fractionally, quieter.
