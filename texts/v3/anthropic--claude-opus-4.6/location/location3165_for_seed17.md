# The Roaring Stillness

---

It has always sounded like this.

That is the first thing you must understand about this place — that the sound came before everything else, and that it has not changed, not once, not in pitch or intensity or timber, since the world began. It is not a sound that *happens*. It is a sound that *is*. A continental, planetary drone so total and so unwavering that it has become indistinguishable from silence for anything born within it, and yet for you — arriving, somehow, impossibly — it is the loudest thing you have ever experienced. It is the sound of an ocean the size of a world pressing against itself from every direction at once with nowhere left to go.

---

## The Water

There is no surface. There hasn't been for a long time, or perhaps there never was one. The ocean is not *on* something — it is the something. A sphere of water so vast that its own gravity holds it together, hanging in the pale light of a white dwarf star that illuminates it evenly, dispassionately, the way a surgical lamp illuminates a body on a table. Light penetrates deep here because there is almost nothing to stop it. The water is preternaturally clear — not the vivid blue of tropical seas but a kind of grey-white luminosity, like light passing through quartz. You can see for dozens of kilometers in every direction, and what you see is: almost nothing.

That is the second thing. The emptiness.

The density of matter in this ocean is staggeringly low for a liquid world. Pockets of salinity and pressure create vast stratified layers, yes, but between the layers there are volumes of water so pure and so uniform that they are functionally void — no particulate, no thermal gradient, no current. Water so still that it behaves less like a fluid and more like a solid block of optical glass, and you pass through it like a thought moving through an empty mind. The only evidence that it *is* water and not vacuum is the pressure — tremendous, omnidirectional, a slow squeeze against every surface of your body that tells you: you are deep, you are embedded, you are *inside*.

The world is cold. Not killing cold, not ice-forming cold — the pressure prevents that — but a pervasive, stable 2°C that has held steady for longer than anything here can remember. No thermal vents. No solar warming. No convection. The temperature is an equilibrium state, a thermodynamic truce between the faint radiation of the white dwarf and the black radiative loss into space on the far side. It does not fluctuate. Nothing here fluctuates.

## The Ridges

And yet there is terrain.

Far below — or perhaps far above, because direction is a negotiable concept in a world without surface — the water thickens around formations of mineral ice. Not water-ice; these are clathrate structures, cages of water molecules locked around pockets of methane and hydrogen sulfide, formed under pressures that don't exist on smaller worlds. They are enormous. They rise in sharp, fresh, angular ridges that stretch for thousands of kilometers, their edges still geometric and unweathered because there are no currents here to erode them. The ridges look *new* — crisp as fractured crystal, their faces catching the diffuse white light and bending it into faint prismatic ghosts. Some of them are translucent. Some are the milky white of deep ice. A few, where hydrogen sulfide concentrations are highest, carry a faint yellow tint, like old ivory.

The ridges form a kind of lattice — not random, not organic, but following the deep geometry of pressure differentials within the world-ocean. They crisscross at angles that suggest underlying order without ever quite resolving into a pattern you can name. They are the closest thing this world has to continents, and they are high — some ridge crests sit dozens of kilometers above the compressed-liquid core, towering through the grey water like the spines of drowned cathedrals scaled up to the size of mountain ranges.

There is no vegetation on them. No lichen, no bacterial mat, no biofilm. The clathrate surfaces are bare mineral lattice, and whatever organic chemistry this world possesses has not taken the step toward anchoring itself to substrate. The ridges are pristine. They may always be pristine.

## The Fauna

But the water between the ridges is alive.

This is the third thing, and the strangest: in all this emptiness, in all this cold unvarying silence-that-is-actually-deafening-noise, there are animals. Or something close enough to animals that the word serves.

They are sparse — you might drift for a hundred kilometers and see nothing, then encounter a loose aggregation of dozens spread across a volume the size of a small country. They are translucent, most of them, their bodies adapted to a world where concealment means matching the clarity of the water itself. Many are large. Size is easy here; the pressure is uniform, the temperature is uniform, there is no weather, no wave action, no predatory advantage to speed. Some of them are very large.

The most common forms are drifters — gelatinous, radially symmetrical, their bodies little more than membranes stretched around cavities of slightly different salinity than the surrounding water. They do not swim so much as *exist* in a particular location, absorbing dissolved compounds through their skins, metabolizing at rates so slow they border on geological. Their life cycles may span thousands of years. They pulse — faintly, rhythmically — but the rhythm has not changed since they began pulsing, and it will not change until they stop. They are not reacting to stimuli. They are running a chemical clock that was wound once and has been ticking ever since.

Deeper, near the ridges, there are crawlers — elongated, segmented things with glassy exoskeletons that move along the clathrate faces, harvesting the trace molecules that sublimate from the ice cages. They are the most active organisms in this world, which is to say they move at perhaps a meter per hour. Their limbs are articulated with a mechanical precision that seems almost engineered, each joint a perfect pivot, each movement identical to the one before it. They leave faint trails in the clathrate surface — scratches, really — that are the closest thing to erosion this world will experience for millennia.

And then there are the singers.

You cannot see them easily. They are deep-water animals, occupying the vast empty mid-layers where the water is clearest and the pressure most uniform. They are the size of stadiums, or the size of city blocks — it is difficult to tell because their bodies are almost perfectly transparent, visible only as faint distortions in the light, like heat shimmer in cold water. They have internal structures — organs, or something like organs — that appear as dark threads suspended in glass. They do not move. They have never been observed to move.

But they resonate.

Their bodies are, in effect, enormous living acoustical chambers, and they vibrate at frequencies so low that the oscillations are felt in the bones rather than heard in the ears. Each singer produces a single, unvarying tone. It has been producing this tone for as long as anyone — anything — has been aware of it. The tones do not change. The singers do not communicate; communication implies exchange, variation, response. They simply *sound*. And their combined output — thousands of them, perhaps hundreds of thousands, spread across the world-ocean in their sparse and empty mid-water territories — is what produces the Roaring Stillness. The planetary drone. The sound that has not changed since the world began.

It is not music. It is not language. It is the sound of living things maintaining a single state, forever, the way a tuning fork hums when struck — except nothing struck them. They began, and they have not stopped.

## The Structures

There are, here and there, things that are not alive and not natural.

On some of the ridge crests, anchored into the clathrate with driven pilings of corroded steel, there are stations. Industrial structures — or rather, the industrial structures of a civilization that understood this world's rules and worked within them. They are blocky, angular, built from alloys that resist the cold and the pressure but not the slow chemical insistence of dissolved minerals. Their surfaces are furred with a thin crust of precipitation — not biological, just crystalline buildup, molecule by molecule, over centuries.

They are not ruins exactly. Nothing has *happened* to them. They have simply been left. Their interiors are flooded — every chamber, every corridor, every reactor housing filled with the same grey-white water that fills everything else. Some of them still have power. You can tell because faint electromagnetic fields emanate from their cores, detectable as a subtle wrongness in the way nearby water molecules align. Turbines that are still turning, somewhere inside, driven by chemical reactions that were initiated long ago and have not yet exhausted their fuel. Pumps that pump water from one chamber to another and back again, accomplishing nothing, following instructions that no longer have a purpose.

There are perhaps a dozen of these stations across the entire world, which means there are perhaps a dozen across a surface area larger than Jupiter's. They are inconceivably far apart. Whatever civilization built them did not build them to be a network — or if it did, the network operated on scales of time and distance that made proximity irrelevant. Each station sits alone on its ridge crest like a single lighthouse on an otherwise empty coast that stretches for a million kilometers in every direction.

Inside one — and you can enter; the airlocks are open, have been open, there is no air to lock — you find machine halls. The machines are recognizable in their *function* if not their form: compressors, centrifuges, filtration arrays, storage tanks. This was a processing facility. It extracted something from the water — or from the clathrate — or from the dissolved minerals — and refined it and stored it. The storage tanks are full. They have been full for a very long time. No one has come to empty them.

On a wall in what might have been a control room, there is a gauge. It is mechanical — a needle behind glass, analog, immune to the electromagnetic idiosyncrasies of this world. The needle points to a value. It has pointed to this value for as long as the station has existed. The gauge measures something — pressure, perhaps, or salinity, or some parameter you don't have a name for — and that something has not changed.

Nothing here has changed.

## The Sound, Again

You hang in the water between two ridges, a hundred kilometers from the nearest crawler, five hundred from the nearest singer, a thousand from the nearest station. The water around you is empty in every direction — clear, cold, still, and utterly without feature. The light comes from everywhere and casts no shadows. You are suspended in a volume of near-nothingness so vast that it would take days to cross at speed, and there is nothing to cross toward.

And the sound fills you.

It is not loud in the way a explosion is loud, not sharp, not painful. It is loud the way geology is heavy. It is the sound of a world-sized instrument playing a single chord that has lasted longer than your species has existed, a chord composed of hundreds of thousands of living tones that have never varied by a single frequency, fed through an ocean that transmits sound nearly five times faster than air and absorbs almost none of it. The sound is *everywhere*. It passes through you. It passes through the ridges and the stations and the crawlers and the drifters and the water itself, and it comes out the other side unchanged, and it meets itself coming back, and the interference patterns are so ancient and so stable that they have become part of the architecture of the world — standing waves the size of continents, nodes of silence where the tones cancel, antinodes of impossible intensity where they reinforce.

You are at an antinode now. You can feel your bones humming. Your vision shakes — not because the water is moving, but because your eyes are vibrating in their sockets. The sound is not aggressive. It is not trying to harm you. It is simply the *fact* of this world, the way gravity is a fact, the way the cold is a fact, the way the emptiness is a fact.

And it will not stop.

It will not change. The singers will not stop singing. The stations will not stop pumping. The clathrate ridges will not erode. The crawlers will not accelerate. The drifters will not alter their rhythm. The temperature will not shift. The light will not dim. This world has found its frequency, and it will hold it — not with effort, not with intention, but simply because there is no force present capable of changing it. Every gradient has been resolved. Every reaction has reached equilibrium. Every oscillation has found its period. The world is not dead — it is abundant with life, humming with energy, full of ancient industry — but it is *finished*. It has arrived at its state, and its state is permanent.

You are the only variable. You are the only thing here that might change.

The sound doesn't care. The sound was here before you, and it will be here after, and right now it is passing through you as if you were nothing more than another volume of water, another clear cold empty space for it to cross on its way to the other side of the world and back again, forever, at the same frequency, at the same amplitude, in the same grey-white light.

It has always sounded like this.
