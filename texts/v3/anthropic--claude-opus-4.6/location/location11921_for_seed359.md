# The Nursery That Grew Itself

---

You come to it through a gap in a wicker wall — not a doorway exactly, because doorways are planned. This is more like the place where two woven panels didn't quite meet, and something decided that was an entrance.

Inside: a room. Or a grove. Or a room that believes, with absolute conviction, that it is a grove.

The space is perhaps forty meters across at its widest, roughly ovoid, and entirely enclosed. The walls rise to the height of a three-story building and curve inward overhead, not quite meeting at the top — leaving an oculus of dull amber sky that provides the only external light. Everything you see has been made. Everything you see is alive. These two facts do not contradict each other here, though they should.

---

The walls are woven. That much is immediately clear. Thick withies of some pale, fibrous material — not quite wood, not quite bone, not quite tendon — have been bent and interlaced into a continuous lattice. The weaving pattern shifts as your eye travels: here a simple over-under basketwork, there a complex herringbone, and in one section near the floor, a spiral pattern that seems to have been started by one set of hands and finished by something with a very different understanding of geometry. The withies are alive. They're sweating. Not moisture — there is no water here, none at all, and that absence is one of the first wrong things you notice — but a slow, clear oil that smells like hot resin and tastes, if you're foolish enough to touch your tongue to it, like copper and anise. The oil is what feeds the vegetation.

And there is so much vegetation.

Every surface that isn't actively being used for something else has been colonized by a dense, pushing, elbowing profusion of growth. But *colonized* is the wrong word. *Planted* is the wrong word too. The plants — if they are plants — appear to have been *installed*. Each one sits in a pocket or cradle woven into the wall-lattice or floor-lattice, and each pocket has been shaped to fit its specific occupant, the way a jeweler sets a stone. The root systems (visible through gaps in the weave) are wrapped in bundles of the same fibrous material, soaked in the wall-oil.

The flora is thick-leaved, waxy, deeply saturated in color — burgundies, near-blacks, the iridescent green of beetle shells. Nothing here has the papery fragility of a water-starved plant. Instead, everything looks *pumped*, turgid, almost hydraulic, as if the oil-sap inside them is under mild pressure. Leaves don't droop; they hold themselves out like open palms. Some have surfaces that are faintly tacky. Some have surfaces that are faintly warm. One broad, paddle-shaped specimen near the center of the space is warm enough that the air above it shimmers.

There is no soil anywhere. The floor is another weave — tighter, denser, reinforced with fired-clay tiles in places where foot traffic has worn the fibers thin. The tiles are crude, thumbprint-marked, clearly hand-pressed. Some of them have been painted with symbols in the same resinous oil, which has darkened over time into a deep umber. The symbols don't repeat. You can't read them. You get the feeling they can't be read — that they were meaningful only at the moment of inscription and have since become simply texture, simply part of the floor's pattern of being walked-on and wearing-down and being repaired.

---

The fauna.

Start with the small: finger-length creatures that look like centipedes made of woven fiber, moving through the wall-lattice with the patient industry of ants. They are tending the plants. You watch one approach a burgundy-leafed shrub, probe its root-bundle with delicate forelegs, then extrude a bead of oil from its own body and work it into the wrapping. The creature is not organic. Or — it is organic in the way the walls are organic, in the way the floor tiles are organic. It was made. It was woven. Somewhere in its body there are tiny fired-clay joints. It is a tool that moves. It is a hand that has been given legs and a purpose and set loose.

There are dozens of them. Scores. They move through every surface, over and under and through. When two meet, they pause, touch forelegs briefly — exchanging information? Greeting? It's impossible to say. The interaction lasts less than a second and then both continue on their separate errands. They make almost no sound. The faintest clicking, like knitting needles very far away.

Larger creatures inhabit the middle space. These are harder to describe because they keep doing things that interfere with description. One — roughly the size and shape of a housecat, if a housecat were made of tightly braided cord and moved by peristalsis rather than legs — is sitting in a cradle of vegetation near the heated paddle-plant. It appears to be sleeping, or meditating, or performing some process that has no analog in your experience. Its surface ripples occasionally. Every few minutes, it extrudes a short length of new fiber from a sphincter near what might be its head, reaches back with a limb you hadn't noticed it had, and weaves the fiber into its own flank. It is building itself. It is always building itself. It has been doing this, you somehow understand, for a very long time.

Three more of these mid-sized beings occupy other niches around the space. One is methodically dismantling a section of floor-weave and re-laying it in a different pattern. One is motionless in a way that suggests not rest but *observation* — it is oriented toward you, and has been since you entered, and the longer you look at it the more certain you become that it has no sensory organs you can identify and yet is *absolutely* aware of your presence. The third is partly embedded in the wall, and you cannot tell whether it is repairing the wall from inside, or whether the wall is in the process of absorbing it, or whether these are the same thing.

And then there is the large one.

It hangs from the apex of the dome, just below the oculus, suspended in a hammock that it has clearly woven for itself out of its own substance. It is the size of a large dog. It has the general morphology of a sea cucumber, if a sea cucumber were architecturally ambitious. Thick ropes of braided fiber extend from its body into the walls at sixteen — no, seventeen — no, the number keeps changing — points of contact. Through these connections, it appears to be structurally integral to the room itself. If you removed it, you suspect the dome would sag. It breathes — a slow, bellows-like expansion and contraction that takes about ninety seconds per cycle. When it exhales, the air pressure in the room shifts just enough to make your ears pop. The heated paddle-plant flutters. The woven centipedes pause in their work for a fraction of a second, synchronized, then resume.

The large one is dreaming the room. You don't know how you know this. It's not a metaphor. The room exists because the large one is holding it in some kind of sustained cognitive act, and the walls are woven because weaving is the only thought-shape available in whatever passes for its mind, and the plants grow in oil instead of water because at some fundamental level, in the ontology of this space, *water was never invented*. This is a place that was made from scratch. Not built on a world — built instead of one. A pocket of handmade reality, woven from the same impulse that makes a bird build a nest, except the bird came first here and the tree came after, and the sky is an afterthought, and the physics are whatever the weaver needed them to be.

---

The warmth is persistent and even — not solar, not geothermal, but *metabolic*. The room is warm because everything in it is working. The weaving never stops. Even the walls shift if you watch long enough: withies slowly bending, seeking new configurations, tightening here and loosening there as the structure responds to stresses you can't perceive. This is why the clay tiles on the floor have symbols that can't be read — by the time you look at them, the context in which they were meaningful has already been rewoven.

The light comes partly from the oculus and partly from the plants themselves. Several of the darker-leaved specimens emit a faint luminescence from their leaf-margins — not bright enough to read by, but enough to keep the space from true shadow. The light has a warm amber cast that makes everything look like the inside of a lantern. When the large one exhales and the paddle-plant flutters, the shadows shift and resettle in patterns that are almost but not quite the same as before.

The sound: the clicking of the centipede-things. The slow creak of the walls adjusting. The vast, patient respiration overhead. Your own breathing, which sounds alien here, too fast, too wet, operating on chemistry this room has never heard of.

And very occasionally — perhaps once every several minutes — a sound that has no source. A low, resonant hum, felt more in the sternum than heard in the ears, as if one of the structural fibers of the room has been plucked like a string. The woven cat-thing that was watching you tilts imperceptibly each time this happens. The centipedes ignore it. The large one's breathing doesn't change.

You realize, eventually, that the hum is the sound of a new rule being added. The room is still under construction — not physically (though that too) but *ontologically*. Its creator, the dreaming weaver overhead, is still deciding how things work here. Gravity's been settled for a while. Temperature, clearly established. But smaller things — the coefficient of friction between your shoes and the clay tiles, the exact way light bends through the oil-sheen on the leaves, whether or not echoes are permitted — these are still being negotiated, still being woven into the lattice of what-is-real-here, one strand at a time.

You are standing inside a world that is perhaps a season old and still doesn't have all its laws yet.

The gap you entered through is already slightly narrower than it was. Not maliciously. Just — the walls are always weaving, and gaps are inefficiencies, and the logic of this place tends toward *fullness*, toward every space having something in it, toward density and saturation and the patient, compulsive completion of pattern.

You should probably leave before the entrance finishes deciding it's a wall.

Or stay, and find out what the room decides you are.
