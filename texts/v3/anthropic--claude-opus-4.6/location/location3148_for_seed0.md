# The Singing Dark

---

At first there is only sound.

It arrives before comprehension — a wall of living noise so total that it seems to have texture, weight, heat. It pushes against the skin. It fills the mouth. You cannot parse it yet into its constituent voices, only register it as a single overwhelming fact: this place is *screaming with life*.

---

The darkness is near-absolute. Not the darkness of deep caves, which is sterile and complete, but a darkness that breathes — a warm, thick, vegetal darkness in which shapes exist just below the threshold of sight. Overhead, the canopy has sealed the sky away. Not a single star. Not a single moon-thread. What light exists is biological: intermittent, unreliable, moving. Smears of cold blue-green phosphorescence on wet bark. A trembling amber glow from fungal shelves the size of tabletops. Brief hot sparks of bioluminescence arcing between branches — insects, or something like insects — tracing parabolas of pale fire that illuminate, for a half-second, the glistening surfaces of leaves the size of doors, the furred underbellies of branches, the wet gleam of something's eyes.

Then dark again.

---

The terrain is low and young. This is important — it explains the frenzy. The land itself has not been land for long. Somewhere in recent geological memory, the sea withdrew, or the earth gently shouldered itself upward, or some tectonic murmur pushed what was seafloor into open air, and what replaced the water was *this.* A broad, gently undulating lowland, barely above whatever water table feeds it, extending for the length of a valley or the breadth of a county in every direction. The soil is dark and new, volcanic-mineral-rich, not yet leached, still carrying the chemical generosity of its origins. Basaltic rubble lies beneath the humus like a skeleton — you can feel it through the soil in places, sharp and fresh-cornered where roots haven't yet pried it apart. Young ravines cut narrow, steep-sided channels through soft rock, their edges still crisp. The bones of the land are barely clothed.

But the flesh — the flesh has come on fast.

Vegetation has taken this new ground with a voracity that borders on violence. The forest is not old, but it is *dense.* There are no ancient giants here, no cathedral columns of trunk rising uncontested for a hundred feet. Instead, everything is competing at once, at every level, simultaneously. Trees of a dozen different architectures — broad-leafed, frond-crowned, spiraling, multi-trunked — have raced upward in the warm air and now jostle for position in a canopy so tight and interlocked that it forms a single continuous membrane between the land and the sky. Below them, a second canopy of smaller trees and tall woody shrubs presses upward. Below that, a third layer of ferns and broad-leafed herbs, some taller than a person, their fronds unfurling in real-time if you watch long enough, pushing aside their neighbors with slow vegetal aggression. Below *that,* the ground layer: mosses, liverworts, shelf fungi, creeping vines, and a thick detritus of fallen leaves that are already half-consumed by the time they reach the floor, colonized by pale mycelia before they've finished falling.

There are no clearings. There are only degrees of density.

The moisture comes not from rain — the canopy is too tight to admit much rain — but from the ground itself and from the air. The young basalt beneath is still fractured and permeable, and water seeps upward through it, pooling in the ravines, filming the rocks, feeding the roots from below. The air is thick with transpiration. Every leaf exhales. The humidity must be above ninety percent, a saturated warmth that coats every surface in a thin sheen of condensation. Water beads on the underside of leaves and drips in irregular rhythms. It collects in the cups of bromeliads or their analogues. It films the fur and feathers and chitin of the creatures that live here, so that everything is slightly wet, slightly gleaming in whatever brief light finds it.

The temperature is blood-warm. Constant. This is equatorial or near-equatorial, and the canopy acts as insulation, trapping the ground heat, smoothing out the difference between day and whatever passes for night. Not that you can tell the difference from down here. There may be a sun somewhere above this layered ceiling, but its existence is a rumor, a theorem. Below, there is only the dark, the warmth, the wet, and the *sound.*

---

Now. The sound.

Try to parse it. Try to separate the wall into threads.

The base layer — the one that vibrates in the sternum, that you feel before you hear — is a deep, rhythmic thrumming. Something is making a sound like a bass drum played continuously, a vibration at the bottom edge of human hearing. It comes from everywhere. It comes from the trees themselves. No — from *something in* the trees. Large animals, probably amphibian or something convergently similar, inflating resonance chambers the size of melons and producing a drone that synchronizes across the entire landscape. Hundreds of them, perhaps thousands, their individual pulses merging into a single tidal rhythm that rises and falls on a cycle of about eight seconds. It never stops. It is the heartbeat of this place.

Above that, the mid-range: a staggering layered chorus of calls, trills, clicks, whistles, warbles, screams, and a sound like tearing silk. This is where the true density of the fauna becomes apparent. There are too many species vocalizing at once to separate them by ear. They have divided the frequency spectrum among themselves the way the plants have divided the vertical space — each occupying a narrow band, each filling it with information. A rapid clicking, like stones struck together, somewhere to the left and above. A descending whistle that spirals down through three octaves before terminating in a wet pop. A sound like laughter — not human laughter but close enough to raise the hair on the back of the neck — repeating in irregular clusters from deep in the understory. Something is making a noise like a bowed saw, a single sustained metallic tone that modulates slowly, almost musically. Something else answers it with a sound like a handful of gravel thrown against a drum skin.

The high register: the insects. Or whatever occupies that niche here. A shimmering, crystalline, omnidirectional wall of high-frequency sound — chirps, buzzes, stridulations, wing-drones — so constant and so uniform that it becomes almost silence, a white-noise ceiling against which all other sounds are heard. But if you listen carefully, even this layer has structure. Waves of intensity pass through it, as one species falls silent and another takes up its frequency. Occasionally, a single piercing note cuts through — something very small and very close, signaling with an urgency that briefly dominates everything.

And beneath and between all of this, the physical sounds: the drip of condensation, the creak of branches under the weight of animals, the soft wet thud of something landing on the forest floor, the rustle of movement through dense foliage that is *constant,* everywhere, in every direction, so that the understory seems to be in continuous slow motion, shivering with passage.

The sound is so dense, so total, that it becomes a kind of pressure. You cannot think clearly inside it. Your own thoughts arrive with difficulty, submerged under this roaring declaration of biological presence.

---

The fauna. The fauna is the ruling fact of this place.

The young, mineral-rich soil has fed an explosion of plant growth, and the plant growth has fed an explosion of animal life that has not yet reached equilibrium — may never reach it, given how turbulent the system is. Every surface is occupied. Every volume of air is traversed. Every leaf is food, shelter, territory, perch, ambush site, nursery.

In the intermittent bioluminescence, you catch glimpses:

A shape the size of a large dog, moving vertically up a trunk with a fluid undulating motion. It has too many limbs — six, eight — and each one terminates in a wide adhesive pad that makes a soft peeling sound as it lifts from the bark. Its eyes catch the light of a passing luminescent insect and throw it back as twin copper discs. It pauses, head tilted, listening to something you can't separate from the wall of noise, then surges upward and is gone.

A curtain of small bodies — dozens, hundreds — flowing through the middle canopy like a murmuration. They move too fast and too synchronously to track individually. They make the silk-tearing sound, all of them at once, a brief ripping crescendo that Dopplers past and fades. Winged, or gliding, or both. One of them collides with a dangling vine and tumbles downward, recovering halfway to the ground with a snap of membranes.

On the forest floor, in a depression where water has collected to a depth of a few inches, something large and pale is lying motionless. It looks at first like a fallen log — two meters long, roughly cylindrical — but it is *breathing.* Its skin, in the brief light of a fungal glow, has the translucent quality of something that has never seen the sun. You can almost see the shadows of its organs. It exhales, and its breath disturbs the water's surface, and then the light fades and it is just a shape in the dark.

The small things are everywhere. *Everywhere.* Crawling on every surface, filling the leaf litter, occupying the air in layers, coating the branches in living fur. Things with many legs. Things with no legs that move anyway. Things with wings that never fly but use them to fan chemical signals into the saturated air. Things that glow. Things that eat the things that glow and glow brighter for it. A millipede-analog, thick as a thumb and long as a forearm, spirals slowly up a fern stem; it is itself host to a dozen smaller creatures riding its dorsal plates. Everything here is a surface for something else.

---

And the turbulence — the *dynamism* of it —

This system is not in steady state. It is visibly, audibly churning. The sound shifts constantly — a species that was dominant in the chorus two minutes ago falls abruptly silent, replaced by a new voice, a wet staccato bark that rises to fill the vacated frequency band. In the darkness, you hear a tree branch crack and fall, and the cascade of animal displacement that follows: shrieks, wing-beats, the heavy thud of bodies dropping to lower branches, a territorial scream, a brief violent thrashing. The branch itself, even as it falls, is being consumed — termite-analogs pour from its broken end, and something is already eating them, crunching audibly.

Territories are being drawn and redrawn continuously. A scent — sharp, acrid, mammalian — drifts through the air, then is replaced by something sweet and decaying, then by the green smell of crushed chlorophyll as something large pushes through the understory. Pheromone wars play out in the chemical murk. A vine that was hanging vertically an hour ago has been pulled sideways by the weight of a colonial organism that has swarmed onto it, bending it into an arc, slowly tearing its anchor-point from the canopy above. When it finally lets go, it will remake a small section of the mid-canopy, and a hundred creatures will scramble to claim the new configuration.

The edges of the young ravines are active. Roots are prying at the fresh basalt, and the basalt is crumbling, and small slides of soil and rock redistribute the terrain every few hours. A burrow complex on a ravine wall collapses partway; the inhabitants — pale, long-snouted, fast — erupt from the remaining tunnels and scatter along the ravine floor, splashing through the thin water, before beginning immediately to excavate new chambers in the opposite wall.

Nothing here rests. Nothing is established. The land is young and the life upon it is young and everything is in the process of *becoming,* and the becoming is loud and dense and dark and warm and does not pause for breath.

---

There are no structures here. No paths. No clearings that were made rather than fallen-into. No marks on trees that weren't made by claws or mandibles. But there is something — barely qualifying, and easy to miss in the dark — that suggests a *primitive* intelligence at work somewhere in this seething system.

Along the edge of one of the ravines, stones have been placed. Not randomly — they form a rough line, fist-sized pieces of basalt arranged end to end for a distance of perhaps ten meters, following the ravine's lip. Some have been turned so that their flattest face points upward. One of them has a smear of bioluminescent fungus on it that might be accidental, or might have been deliberately transferred — it's impossible to say. The line of stones does not lead anywhere obvious. It does not border or contain. But it is too regular to be natural, and the stones are the wrong type for this particular stratum — they've been carried from somewhere else.

Nearby, a thick vine has been stripped of its bark in a spiral pattern, the pale inner wood exposed in a helix. Tooth marks? Tool marks? The cuts are clean but not metal-clean. Something hard and sharp but organic — a tooth, a claw, a shaped piece of stone or shell.

Whatever made these marks is out there in the roaring dark, participating in the tumult, possibly watching from a perch or a burrow with eyes adapted to catch the smallest quantum of bioluminescent light. It has begun to make its first marks on the world, the first tentative scratches that distinguish *arrangement* from *accident.* It is not building yet. It is not even, perhaps, fully aware that it is doing something new. But the line of stones is there, and it will still be there when the canopy overhead shifts and the territory around it changes hands and the chorus of voices reorganizes itself for the thousandth time tonight.

---

Stand still for a moment. Let the sound wash over you. Let the darkness be what it is. Feel the warm wet air on your skin and the vibration of the deep thrumming in your ribs. Something brushes past your ankle — small, multi-legged, indifferent to your presence. Something large shifts its weight in the canopy directly above, and a shower of condensation falls on your upturned face.

This is a world in its adolescence. Hot-blooded, overstocked, loud, competitive, spilling over with more life than it can probably sustain. Half of these species will be extinct within a geological eyeblink, outcompeted or starved or simply unlucky. The ravines will deepen. The canopy will thin as the dominant trees finally win and the losers die back. The sound will organize itself into something less overwhelming, more structured, as niches stabilize and the overlapping voices sort themselves into a cleaner arrangement.

But that is later. Now is the frenzy. Now is the dark warm roaring green of a world too full of life to be quiet about it.
