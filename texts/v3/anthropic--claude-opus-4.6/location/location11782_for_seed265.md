# The Flooded Works

---

There is no light here. Not dimness, not gloom — no light. The darkness is complete, and it is warm, and it is wet, and it has weight to it, the way a hand laid flat on your chest has weight. You are in a place that has never known a sun, or has forgotten what one was, or exists in a fold of the world where the concept of photons never quite cohered.

And yet the place is knowable. Not through sight — through everything else.

---

The water comes first. You know it by its temperature against your shins, slightly warmer than body heat, like water that has been sitting in dark pipes for a long time. It covers the ground in a shallow, unmoving sheet — ankle-deep, maybe calf-deep in the low channels — and it does not flow. It does not ripple. It simply is there, the way a floor is there, except the floor is beneath it somewhere, a flat expanse of something like concrete or fusedite or compacted calcium mud, young and smooth, without the cracks or grooves that time would give it. The water sits on this surface with an eerie patience. It is not stagnant — stagnant implies that it was once moving and stopped. This water may never have moved at all. It may have simply appeared, condensing upward out of the substrate, sweating through the ground like the whole landscape is a body running a low fever.

The air above it is thick with the same moisture. Not fog, because fog is visible and you cannot see. It is a saturation, a closeness, the atmosphere holding as much water as it can without becoming rain. Breathing feels like drinking something thin. Your lungs accept it. After a while, you stop noticing the boundary between the water below and the humid air above, and this blurring feels intentional, as if the place is working toward a state where there is no difference between submerged and not submerged.

---

The structures are the next thing you learn. You find them by touch, by echo, by the way sound behaves differently near solid masses.

They rise out of the water at irregular intervals — not ruins exactly, because they don't feel old enough to be ruins. They feel *recent*, placed here or grown here or extruded from the ground in the last handful of years, maybe decades. Metal columns, some as thick as your torso, others thin as wrists, clustered together in groups of six or ten or twenty, connected at various heights by horizontal beams, by grated platforms, by housings that contain machinery. The metal is warm. Everything here is warm. When you press your palm against it, the surface is slick with condensation but solid, industrial, flanged and bolted in the way of heavy infrastructure — waterworks, refineries, power substations, pumping apparatus.

Some of it hums. Not loudly. The sound is felt more than heard: a low, patient vibration that comes up through the water, through the soles of your feet, through the bones of your hand when you grip a railing. The machinery is running. Whatever it was built to do, it is still doing it. Pumping or filtering or processing or converting — some slow, continuous, mechanical process cycling endlessly in the dark. There are no operators. There are no control panels that your hands can find, no switches, no readouts glowing in the blackness. The machines do not need tending. They tend themselves, or they are too simple to need it, or the concept of oversight was never part of their design.

You move between these clusters and they go on for a long time. Your sense of the space opens gradually: this is not a room, not an enclosed area. It extends. It extends *significantly*. The echoes, when you make sounds — a splash, a word, the knock of a knuckle on a pipe — return from distances that suggest a space measured in kilometers. An open, flat, waterlogged plain studded with industrial structures, repeating with variation like the pylons of some immense marshland grid. Between the clusters, the water stretches uninterrupted, and the air is still and warm and very, very quiet.

---

The quiet is particular. It is not silence. Silence would mean absence, and there is too much here for silence. There is the hum of the machinery, felt more than heard. There is the occasional *tink* of thermal expansion in metal, soft and arrhythmic. There is, distantly, the sound of water lapping against something — not waves, but the tiny disturbances created when things move through the shallows. And there are the animals.

They are everywhere.

You hear them as clicks, as the soft suction-cup release of something detaching from wet metal, as the barely-perceptible displacement of water around bodies. They are small — most of them — the size of hands, the size of fingers, dense in population. Something that might be a crustacean moves across the back of your hand when you rest it on a submerged beam. Multiple legs, delicate, the texture of wet cartilage. It is unhurried. It proceeds across your skin as if you are terrain, pauses at your knuckle, and continues into the water. Another of its kind — or the same kind, smaller — is climbing the column beside you. You can hear its grip: *tick, tick, tick*, ascending. The columns are covered in them, you realize. Not visibly, of course — nothing is visible — but you can feel the faint vibration of their collective movement through the metal, a busy, subdued rustle of thousands of small bodies going about their lives on vertical and horizontal surfaces.

In the water, things brush against your calves. Soft-bodied. Slow. They move with the deliberation of creatures who have never needed to flee from anything. There is nothing chasing them. No predator dynamics that you can sense — or rather, predation happens so slowly here that it doesn't register as violence. Something absorbs something else over the course of hours. Something encysts something and digests it across days. The ecology is abundant and glacial, a dense web of interactions occurring at the speed of rust.

Larger things exist too. You sense one at a distance — not by sound but by the subtle pressure wave it generates in the still water, a slow compression that reaches you like a pulse. Something heavy, moving through the shallows far away, displacing water along a front maybe three or four meters wide. It does not approach. It does not recede. It is simply *there*, traversing its own unknowable route through the grid of structures, part of the landscape the way the columns are part of the landscape.

Something else, above you: a membrane or film stretched between two high beams, biological, slightly tacky when you reach up and touch it. It is taut and warm and thrumming faintly, not with the machine hum but with its own vitality. When you touch it, it responds — not pulling away, not contracting, but changing texture under your fingertips, becoming briefly smoother, as if acknowledging contact. You withdraw your hand. The membrane remains. It might be a single organism. It might be a colony. It is doing something up there — feeding on moisture in the air, filtering particulates, converting warmth into something. It is at home among the machinery in a way that suggests it has been here as long as the machines have, which isn't long, but long enough.

---

The vegetation is sparse because there is no light for photosynthesis, and yet it exists. Strands of something like kelp hang from the undersides of platforms, thick-bodied and rubbery, warm to the touch, growing downward toward the water. They are not photosynthetic. They are chemotrophic or thermotrophic or something else entirely — feeding on the heat differential between the machinery and the water, or on chemical gradients in the condensation, or on something the machines excrete as byproduct. They grow slowly. You can't perceive their growth, only its evidence: the way they've wound themselves around pipes and cable runs, integrated into the structure so thoroughly that biology and infrastructure have begun to merge. A pipe feeds into what might be a root system. A cable passes through a node of living tissue that has, over time, incorporated the wire into itself. The distinction between built and grown is softening.

This is where the weirdness lives.

---

Not in any single thing, but in the *rules*. In the way the place holds together. You begin to notice:

The water is the same depth everywhere. Not approximately — *exactly*. Whether you walk through what feels like a channel or what feels like a raised area, the water is at the same height on your calves. You crouch and feel the ground. The ground is not level. There are rises and depressions, ridges where substrate has formed recently, still sharp-edged and unfractured. The topography varies by half a meter or more. But the water does not care. It does not pool in the lows or thin on the highs. It maintains its depth relative to your body, as if it is measuring itself against you specifically, as if *you* are its reference frame and not gravity.

You stop walking. You stand very still. The water is still. You take one step. The water adjusts — not with a splash, not with a ripple, but instantaneously, the way a number changes on a screen. The water was at one level, and now it is at the same level, and the ground beneath it has changed, and none of this has generated any disturbance on the surface.

You crouch again. You put your hand flat on the water's surface. Your hand rests on it. On it, not in it. The surface tension is not that strong — you pushed through it easily before — but now it holds you. You press slightly. It holds. You press harder. It yields, and your hand sinks in, and the water is warm, and when you lift your hand out, it is dry.

You begin to understand that the water is not entirely a liquid. Or it is a liquid that has additional properties. Or the concept of "liquid" is one of the things that has softened here, like the concept of "light" and "built" and "alive." The rules are younger than they should be. They haven't finished setting. The landscape is new — the sharp-edged substrate, the recently-placed machinery, the organisms that haven't yet fully diverged from the structures they inhabit — and the physics is new too, or at least locally *flexible*, still curing like poured concrete, still finding its final configuration.

---

The darkness is part of this. It is not the absence of light. It is the *presence* of something that is incompatible with light. You wave your hand in front of your face and you do not see it, but you *feel* the wake of it in the air, the displacement of humid warmth. You have other senses here. You have always had them, perhaps, but in lit places they are drowned out. Here, in the darkness that is not darkness but the-thing-that-replaced-light, these senses come forward. You feel the location of the nearest column the way you'd feel heat from a fire. You sense the membrane overhead the way you'd sense someone watching you. The fauna moving on the structures, the slow thing in the distance, the kelp-strands — you know where they are. Not precisely. Not the way vision would tell you. But *adequately*. The world is fully perceivable. It simply refuses to be perceived in the way you expected.

---

You walk for what might be an hour. The structures continue. They vary — some clusters are tall, bristling with pipes and housings that rise well above your head, complex and dense, while others are minimal, just two or three columns and a crossbeam. Some are running. Some are still. The still ones are cooler, and the organisms have colonized them more aggressively; you can feel them yielding slightly under your hand, the metal softened by biological encroachment, the bolts overgrown by something with the texture of wet calcium. The running ones are warmer, cleaner, their hum steady, their surfaces merely slick.

You find, once, something that might have been a building. A walled enclosure, three walls standing, one absent or never built, the interior knee-deep in the same water, the same depth. Inside: a surface that might be a desk or a slab, metal, bolted down. On it: objects you can hold. A cylinder, heavy, smooth. A flat plate with raised markings — letters? Numbers? A tactile interface for something? The markings are crisp, uneroded, fresh. No one has rubbed them smooth with use. They were made and then the maker was gone and the machinery kept running and the water kept not-flowing and the animals kept climbing the columns in the dark.

You set the objects down. They clink against the slab with a sound that feels transgressive in the quiet. The sound decays quickly, absorbed by the wet air, by the dense presence of bodies and structures and the heavy, patient water.

---

Far away — very far, kilometers maybe — something changes pitch. One of the machines shifts its hum, drops a half-tone, holds the new frequency. The animals near you pause. You feel them pause: the tick-tick-tick on the column stops, the brushing against your calves ceases, the membrane overhead goes taut and still. Everything waits.

Then the hum stabilizes. The animals resume. The membrane relaxes.

Whatever the machines are doing, the ecosystem is listening. Or the ecosystem is part of the machine. Or the machine is part of the ecosystem. These distinctions — built and grown, mechanical and biological, structure and organism — they are the same kind of softened rule as the water's relationship with gravity, as the darkness's relationship with perception. They are real enough to notice but too young to be firm. The place hasn't decided yet what it is. It is in the process of becoming, and the process is slow, patient, running on its own schedule in its own warm darkness, populated but untended, industrial but purposeless — or purposeful in a way that doesn't require anyone to understand it.

You stand in the water that is the same depth everywhere. You breathe the air that is almost water. You feel the hum through your bones and the animals on the metal and the membrane overhead and the slow thing moving in the distance. You are in a place that is still forming its own rules, and the rules it is forming are not the ones you know, and it is dark, and it is warm, and it is full of life, and it does not need you, and it will go on like this for a very long time.
