# The Nursery Below

---

You come down through a shaft of warm stone into the sound of ten thousand things being born at once.

The cavern system extends farther than it should. Miles, probably — the ceiling arcs high enough in places that distance makes it hazy, a geological cathedral whose vaults are lit by some source you can't immediately name. The light is generous here, full-spectrum and strange, pouring from veins of mineral that thread through the ceiling rock in pale branching networks. Not bioluminescence — there is almost nothing alive that roots or grows in the way you'd recognize as vegetative. These are piezoluminescent deposits, crystals stressed by the geological forces still actively building this place, shedding light the way a cracking knuckle sheds sound. The glow is warm, buttery, occasionally flickering when a new fissure pops somewhere in the ceiling and a fresh vein ignites. It gives the vast space the quality of late afternoon, an endless golden hour underground.

The ground is new. You can tell because it hasn't decided what it wants to be yet. In some places the floor is smooth obsidian, still warm, still faintly ticking as it cools from whatever thermal event laid it down — days ago, hours ago, you can't be sure. Nearby, that same floor buckles upward into a ridge of raw basalt, cracked and steaming where mineral-rich water seeps up through the fractures. The water pools in shallow depressions, iron-red and silky with dissolved calcium, warm enough that it fogs the air above it. These pools don't last. Some drain as quickly as they form, sucked back into fissures that open and close on no schedule you can predict. Others spread, merge, find a low channel, and become a brief stream that winds between formations before vanishing into a crack. The moisture is everywhere and nowhere — the air is humid, heavy, condensation beading on every cooled surface, but there is no river, no lake, no permanent body of water. Just this restless seepage, this geological sweating.

And everywhere, in everything, through everything — the animals.

---

They are not visiting this place. They are *of* it, the way coral is of a reef, the way termites are of their mound, except the mound is still being built and the termites are a hundred different species and none of them are cooperating and all of them are succeeding anyway.

Start with what's underfoot. The obsidian fields are covered — not sparsely, not here and there, but *covered* — with things like pale crabs. They are not crabs. They have too many legs, arranged radially, and their shells are mineral,ite calcium carbonate laid down in the same water that feeds the pools. They move in vast, jostling crowds across the glass-smooth rock, their legs ticking and scraping, and the sound of them is like rain on a tin roof, constant and percussive and so pervasive it becomes a texture of the air rather than a distinct noise. They eat the mineral crust that forms on the cooling stone. They eat each other's shed shells. They eat, you will eventually notice, the stone itself — or rather, they rasp at the fresh rock with specialized mouthparts, extracting something from it, some trace element the new geology is rich in. They are, in their countless clicking way, the first erosion this place has ever known. They are wearing it in. Breaking it down. Making soil from what has never been soil.

Above them, clinging to the warm walls in writhing clusters, are elongated things — sightless, serpentine, their bodies coated in a mucus that shimmers with the same mineral iridescence as the cave walls. They braid around each other in knots of dozens, shifting constantly, and they hunt by sensing the thermal gradients that ripple through the cavern when a new vent opens or a hot fissure breathes. When they detect warmth, whole clusters of them release from the wall at once and pour down in a silvery cascade, flowing across the ground like mercury, engulfing whatever crab-things are in their path. Then they climb back up. The walls are never still. They shiver and ripple with this constant traffic, this flowing descent and laborious return.

In the pools — the transient, mineral-heavy pools — something like eels but more like animate sediment. Thick-bodied, clay-colored, their skin granular and rough. They appear when the water appears, as if they were waiting in the wet cracks below, and they thrash in the shallows with an urgency that seems less like feeding and more like spawning, or perhaps simply *existing* as hard and fast as they can before the water drains away. Their churning keeps the pools turbid, cloudy, and the sound they make — a deep, wet, slapping percussion — echoes off the vaulted ceiling and comes back distorted, multiplied.

And the fliers. God, the fliers. The upper reaches of the cavern are a pandemonium of wings. Not feathered wings — membraned, translucent, stretched over frameworks of cartilage or chitin, in sizes ranging from your thumbnail to your full armspan. They roost on the luminescent veins in the ceiling, possibly drawn by the warmth, possibly feeding on something the light produces or attracts. When a new fissure cracks open and a fresh vein flares to life, the nearest colony erupts — thousands of them, spiraling outward in a shrieking, chittering cloud before wheeling back to claim the new territory. These territorial explosions happen every few minutes somewhere in the visible distance, and the sound carries: a sharp, rising roar like a crowd at a stadium, then a slow decay back to the baseline murmur.

That baseline is never quiet. The cavern hums and clicks and rasps and shrieks and drips and groans. The stone itself contributes — deep, infrasonic pulses from somewhere far below where magma or superheated water is still actively building the chambers you stand in. You feel these more than hear them, a vibration in your sternum, a subtle nausea that comes and goes. Layered over that: the crab-things' endless percussion, the wet slap of the pool-dwellers, the periodic screaming of the fliers, the hiss of steam from new vents, the tink-tink-tink of cooling rock, and occasionally a deep, resonant *crack* as the cavern itself extends, a new chamber opening somewhere in the darkness at the edge of perception, a new room in a house that is still being framed.

---

There are structures. They are hard to see at first because they are small and made from the same materials as everything else. But look: near the larger pools, where the water lingers longest, someone has stacked flat stones into low walls. Crude, purposeful, unmortared. They create catchments, extending the life of a pool by an hour, maybe two, channeling the seepage into slightly deeper basins. Nearby, a frame of bones — the long bones of something serpentine, lashed together with dried gut — stands over a shallow pit where crab-shells have been collected in quantity. A cache, or a trap, or a drying rack. There are scrape marks on the obsidian where someone has knelt and worked an edge tool against the glass to flake off blades. The debitage is scattered but recent.

You won't see the builders. Not at first. They are part of the fauna here, not separate from it — another species in the turbulent ecology, not yet elevated above it. They move through the crab-fields and avoid the serpentine clusters and know which pools will last and which will drain. Their marks on this place are light, tentative, experimental. A wall here. A tool there. A small hearth — though there is no fuel to burn, so what they've done is found a thermal vent of the right temperature and ringed it with stones and placed crab-meat on the hot rock to cook. Primitive, yes, but also perfectly adapted. They don't impose on this environment. They inflect it, slightly, the way a single voice inflects a choir.

---

The turbulence is the thing you must understand. This place does not hold still. As you watch, a section of floor fifty meters away buckles — slowly, almost gently, like a breath being drawn — and a new ridge of dark stone pushes through, steaming. The crab-things scatter from it in a radial wave, their clicking intensifying to a frantic chatter, then immediately begin colonizing the new surface before it has fully cooled, their mineral shells insulating them enough to tolerate what would blister your hand. A pool drains suddenly with a gurgling rush, and the clay-eels thrash once, twice, and then are gone, pulled down with the water or burrowing into the wet crack at the bottom. One of the ceiling veins fractures and goes dark, and the fliers that roosted there explode outward and crash into a neighboring colony, and for thirty seconds the air above is an absolute chaos of membraned wings and shrieking and bodies colliding, and then it settles, reorganizes, finds a new equilibrium that will hold until the next disruption.

It happens constantly. Everywhere. The whole system is a roiling, seething, adjusting thing — not an ecosystem in equilibrium but an ecosystem in the act of *becoming*, riding the back of a geology that is also in the act of becoming, the two processes tangled so tightly that you cannot say where the living ends and the mineral begins. The crab-things *are* mineral. The serpentine hunters *are* the walls they cling to, chemically speaking, their mucus indistinguishable from the wet stone. The pool-eels may not be organisms at all in the strictest sense — they may be some intermediate thing, some pre-biological or para-biological phenomenon that the warm chemistry of this place keeps throwing up and reabsorbing. The fliers, at least, seem unambiguously animal, but even they are so numerous and so reactive that they function less like individual creatures and more like a weather system, a living atmosphere that fills the upper third of the cavern and responds to stimuli the way a thunderhead responds to updrafts.

You stand in it and you feel the warmth on your skin and the vibration in your bones and the humid air in your lungs, and you realize that *below the surface* is not quite right. You are not below anything. You are *inside* something. Something that is still forming, still figuring out what it is, still assembling itself from raw heat and mineral water and the frantic, clicking, shrieking, spawning, hunting, building energy of ten thousand species that arrived here before the stone had cooled and have been riding the chaos ever since.

The light shifts. A new vein cracks open overhead, bright and golden, and the whole cavern reconfigures around it.

Nothing here is finished. Everything here is alive.
