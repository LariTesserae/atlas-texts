# The Sounding Dark

---

There is no light here. Understand that first. Not the darkness of a closed room, not the darkness of a deep cave where your eyes might eventually adjust. This is a darkness that is *structural*, a property of the atmosphere itself — a sky so thick with vapor and particulate that photons from whatever star governs this world are absorbed, scattered, and re-absorbed across hundreds of kilometers of wet, heavy air until nothing coherent arrives at the surface. There has not been visible light here in geological time. The world has moved on without it.

And yet it is warm. The heat comes from below.

---

## The Terrain

Imagine a highland plateau the size of a continent — no, larger, a tectonic province, a raised crustal mass spanning perhaps eight thousand kilometers in its longest dimension, sitting roughly two kilometers above the basins that surround it. But do not imagine a flat mesa. This plateau has been worked. Erosion has had its way here for epochs, and what remains is a landscape of deep maturity: rounded ridgelines with their spines softened into broad saddles, valleys that have widened into U-shaped troughs, pillars and hoodoos worn to stumps, and everywhere the smoothness that comes from water touching stone for longer than stone can resist.

The rock is dark — basalt, mostly, threaded with veins of something metalite and resonant, a mineral that rings when struck. In places the basalt has weathered into deep laterite soils, rust-red if you could see them, which you cannot. In other places the bedrock is exposed in great whale-back domes, slick with condensation, warm to the touch from geothermal flux below. Water runs across these domes in thin sheets, barely a centimeter deep, moving with a quiet constancy that has polished them to the texture of wet glass.

The water is everywhere. Not standing — this is not a swamp, not exactly — but *present*, as a permanent condition. The air itself is saturated, visibility-zero fog that is indistinguishable from fine rain. Water beads on every surface. It seeps from the rock at ten thousand springs, gathers in channels, braids across the gentle slopes in shallow streams that merge, split, merge again. There are pools in every depression, some of them vast — blind lakes kilometers across, warm as blood, their surfaces indistinguishable from the air above them. The water table sits centimeters below the soil. Dig your fingers in and they come up wet.

The plateau is riddled with sinkholes and solution channels where the water has found limestone beneath the basalt cap. Some of these are enormous: vertical-walled pits hundreds of meters across, dropping into cave systems that branch and reconnect through the body of the plateau like a vascular network. Warm air rises from these openings. The sound they make — but that comes later.

---

## The Vegetation

In darkness this thick, photosynthesis is not an option. The vegetation — and it is lush, genuinely lush, pressing in from all directions — has found other arrangements.

The dominant flora are chemosynthetic. Their roots tap into the geothermal gradient, drawing energy from the chemical differentials in the warm, mineral-rich groundwater. They grow as thick mats of fibrous tissue across the rock surfaces, as towering fungal-analogues with trunks of braided mycelium three meters in diameter, as curtains of filamentous growth that hang from overhangs and trail in the streams. Some species have developed pneumatic structures — gas-filled bladders that allow them to grow tall, competing not for light but for access to the atmospheric chemistry above the vapor layer, where trace gases provide metabolic advantage.

The texture is extraordinary. Everything is *soft*. The stone is padded with growth. The fungal trunks yield slightly under pressure, like firm bread. The ground between them is carpeted with something like moss but warmer, body-temperature, faintly damp, and it gives off a smell that sits somewhere between fresh soil and ozone. Aerial roots and tendrils hang at face height. The canopy — such as it is — closes overhead in places, and you can feel it rather than see it: the air grows warmer, more humid, more still. Condensation drips constantly. The whole landscape *sweats*.

Some of the larger organisms are bioluminescent, but only barely, only intermittently — faint flickers of blue-green or amber that last a fraction of a second, signaling things you cannot interpret. These are not enough to see by. They are enough to remind you that the darkness is inhabited.

---

## The Fauna

And *densely* inhabited. The animal life here is abundant and diverse and operates entirely without vision.

The most immediately apparent creatures are the ones you hear. There are caller-beasts — large, apparently mammalian or mammalian-analogues — that navigate by echolocation and communicate in complex, overlapping songs that carry for kilometers through the wet air. Their calls are deep, resonant, chest-vibrating: not quite whale-song, not quite the rumble of distant thunder, but something that partakes of both. You can feel their calls in your sternum before you process them as sound. They move in herds across the plateau, following the warm-water channels, grazing on the fungal mats. You would estimate their size from the sound of their footfalls — heavy, deliberate, three-toed — as something like a large horse, but broader.

Smaller creatures are everywhere underfoot and overhead. Things that click. Things that rustle through the ground-cover with a dry, rapid sound like shuffled cards. Things that drop from the aerial tendrils onto your shoulders — light, many-legged, gone before you can react. The streams are full of life: you can hear the splash and gulp of filter-feeders working the current, the occasional sharp slap of something breaching the surface of a pool. In the sinkholes, something large moves in spiral patterns, its wake audible as a rhythmic sloshing against the walls.

There are predators. You know this because the herds go quiet sometimes, all at once, and in the silence that follows you can hear something else moving — slower, more deliberate, and silent in a way that implies effort. The prey animals resume their calls only gradually, from the edges inward, like a system rebooting.

Many of the animals are warm — warmer than the ambient temperature. You can feel their body heat as they pass nearby. Some of them investigate you. A snout, broad and damp, presses against your arm in the dark. Breath, heavy and sweet-smelling. Then gone.

## The Machines

Now: the industry.

Scattered across the plateau — and reaching down into the cave systems beneath it — there are installations. Not cities. Not towns. Isolated structures, enormous in scale, connected to one another by infrastructure that runs underground or through the water channels. You encounter them as walls — sudden, vertical, warm metal, riveted, extending upward beyond reach and to either side beyond perception. You follow the wall and find that it curves. The structure is cylindrical. Perhaps a hundred meters in diameter. The metal vibrates faintly under your palm, a deep harmonic that suggests massive machinery operating within.

These are extraction plants. Processing facilities. They are pulling something from the geothermal system — energy, certainly, but also specific minerals, specific chemical compounds. The warm water that flows everywhere across the plateau is not untouched; it has been *worked*, its chemistry altered, certain elements stripped out. The discharge water has a different mineral taste, a different temperature. Whole watersheds downstream of major installations support different ecologies than those upstream — different fungal species, different animal communities, adapted to the altered chemistry.

The technology is heavy, mechanical, **loud**. These facilities roar. You hear them from kilometers away: the rhythmic pounding of something like stamping mills, the sustained hiss of steam venting through pressure-release valves, the groan of metal under thermal stress, the clatter of chain-driven conveyors moving unseen material through unseen processes. At night — though every moment is night — the machines do not stop. There is no shift change, no quiet hour. The sound is continuous, and the fauna have adapted to it: the caller-beasts pitch their songs above or below the industrial frequencies, occupying acoustic niches the machines have left open.

Some of the installations are old. Very old. You find structures where the metal has corroded into lace, where the fungal mats have colonized the walls and begun to digest them, where the machinery has seized and gone silent and the building has become a habitat — creatures nesting in the ducts, streams diverted through the broken foundations. These ruins are still warm. The geothermal heat they were built to exploit continues whether or not anyone is exploiting it.

Other installations are clearly operational. You feel the heat radiating from their walls. The ground around them trembles. Pipes emerge from their bases and run across the landscape — you trip over them in the dark, thick conduits of riveted metal, hot to the touch, carrying pressurized fluid or gas toward destinations you cannot determine. In places, the pipes bridge across valleys on trestle structures, and the streams below them run warm from the radiant heat.

Who built them? Who operates them? You find no workers, no operators, no evidence of habitation beyond the industrial. The machines appear to run themselves. Perhaps they were built by the same species that left the faint marks on certain rock faces — regularized grooves, too geometric to be natural, worn nearly smooth by the same patient water that has rounded every other edge on this plateau. Perhaps those builders are gone. Perhaps they are still here, somewhere in the dark, indistinguishable from the other warm-bodied creatures that move through the fog.

## The Sound

This is not a quiet place. This may be the loudest landscape you have ever inhabited.

Layer it:

At the base, the machines. That constant industrial thrum, varied in pitch and rhythm by distance and the particular process each facility performs. The nearest one — perhaps two kilometers east — has a heartbeat quality: *doom, doom, doom*, once every four seconds, so deep it is felt more than heard.

Above that, the water. Water is the medium of this world, and it is always in motion: the hiss of condensation falling through the canopy, the trickle and splash of streams, the deeper roar where channels converge into falls that tumble into sinkholes, the hollow boom of wave-action in the subterranean lakes below.

Above that, the fauna. The caller-beasts, dozens of them, maybe hundreds, their overlapping songs creating a shifting, atonal chord that fills the middle frequencies. The clicks and chirps of smaller echolocators. The percussive splash of aquatic life. The occasional sharp cry — alarm, territorial claim, mating call — that cuts through everything else and then is gone.

And above all of that, intermittently, something else. A sound that comes from the sinkholes, rising on columns of warm air: a harmonic resonance created by wind moving through the cave systems below the plateau, shaped by the geometry of the tunnels into something almost musical. It rises and falls with changes in atmospheric pressure, sometimes sustaining a single note for hours, sometimes cycling through a sequence of tones that sounds deliberate, composed, though it is only the planet breathing through its own geology. When this sound aligns with the machine-rhythms and the animal-songs — which happens rarely, unpredictably — the effect is overwhelming. The entire plateau becomes a single instrument. The air itself vibrates. The condensation on your skin trembles. And then the alignment passes, and the sounds decohere, and you are left standing in the ordinary, roaring dark.

## The Rules of the World

Certain things become apparent if you stay long enough:

**The water moves in cycles.** The geothermal heat drives evaporation from the lowland basins far below; the vapor rises, cools, condenses into the permanent fog that blankets the plateau. The condensation feeds the springs, the springs feed the streams, the streams find the sinkholes and return to the deep aquifers, where the heat drives them upward again. The plateau exists at the boundary between rising moisture and descending water. It is always wet because it is always at the dew point, exactly, perpetually.

**The ecology is layered.** The surface community — fungal forests, caller-beasts, stream fauna — is one system. Below ground, in the cave networks, there is another: different species, different food webs, adapted to the subtly different chemistry of the deep water. The sinkholes are transition zones, and the creatures that inhabit their walls are specialists, moving between worlds. Occasionally, deep-cave fauna emerge onto the surface during what might be seasonal shifts in the geothermal flux — you would hear them as unfamiliar calls, hesitant, quickly answered by the surface fauna with what sounds like hostility or warning.

**The machines are part of the ecology now.** Whatever they were built to do, their operation has become a factor in the chemical balance of the plateau. The altered discharge water supports species that could not exist without it. The heat from the pipes creates microhabitats. The sound of the machines has shaped the acoustic behavior of every animal within range. If the machines stopped, the ecosystem would not simply revert — it would collapse in certain zones and transform unpredictably in others. The machines and the life have grown together.

**Navigation is by sound and touch and thermal gradient.** After enough time in this place, you develop a mental map built not from visual landmarks but from acoustic signatures: the pitch of a particular installation's machinery, the character of a particular waterfall, the favored singing-ground of a particular caller-beast herd. You learn to feel the warmth radiating from a large fungal trunk before you walk into it. You learn to read the temperature of the water under your feet for information about proximity to geothermal vents or industrial discharge. Your world becomes a map of sounds, textures, temperatures, and smells, and it is rich — *dense* — with information. The darkness, which seemed at first like deprivation, begins to seem like clarity. There is nothing here to distract you from what is actually present.

## What It Feels Like

You stand on worn basalt, your boots in warm water an inch deep. The fog is so thick on your skin it might as well be rain. The air smells of mineral springs and ozone and the warm-bread scent of fungal growth. Something large moves past you — you feel the displacement of air, the brief pulse of its body heat, the subsonic vibration of its call passing through your ribcage. Somewhere to the east, a machine pounds its slow heartbeat into the rock. Somewhere below, the planet hums through its own hollowed bones.

You cannot see your hand in front of your face. You are not alone. The world is vast, and wet, and loud, and alive, and old, and working, and dark — dark in a way that is not absence but presence, the darkness itself a thing with weight and texture, warm and close and full.

You are inside something. You have been inside it since you arrived. The plateau, the fog, the sound, the life, the machines — they are not a landscape you are looking *at*. They are a body you are standing *in*. And it is breathing.
