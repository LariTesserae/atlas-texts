# The Breathing Walls of Keth

---

It is a town, but it was never built.

You stand at the edge of what functions as a street — a narrow corridor between two walls of pale mineral, each rising three or four times your height, their surfaces not quite smooth, not quite rough, possessing the quality of something that hardened only recently, that still remembers being soft. The material is chalky and cool to the touch. Where your finger presses, it yields the faintest fraction of a millimeter, then resists. When you pull away, the depression remains for a few seconds before slowly, silently closing.

This is Keth. It occupies an area no larger than a small village square, a depression in an otherwise featureless alkali flat that extends to a dim horizon in every direction. The flat is pale grey under a sky that gives light without offering a source — no sun, no visible star, just a pervasive oyster-shell luminescence that casts no shadows, or rather casts all shadows equally, so that depth is something you feel in your legs and your inner ear more than something you see.

But the flat is not what holds your attention. Keth holds your attention.

---

The walls grow. That is the first thing to understand. They are not erected or stacked or carved. They emerge from the mineral substrate the way crystals emerge from solution, except there is no solution here, no water of any kind. The alkali flat is drier than any desert — not merely absent of moisture but seemingly hostile to the concept, the mineral so calcium-hungry that your lips chap in minutes and the inside of your nose tightens and stings. Whatever process drives the growth of Keth's structures is non-aqueous. Something in the substrate itself, some slow mineral metabolism. The walls extrude upward at roughly the pace of a fingernail's growth, which means in any given hour you see nothing change, but return after a day and a new spur has emerged from a junction, a new lintel has begun to bridge a gap between two walls, a shallow alcove has deepened into something that might become a room.

The town reorganizes itself. That is the second thing.

Corridors that were open yesterday are now sealed by a membrane of mineral that grew across them overnight. Other walls have thinned and crumbled — not eroded, not weathered, but *reabsorbed*, their material drawn back down into the substrate to be repurposed elsewhere. Keth is, at any given moment, a different town than it was at any previous moment. Its layout drifts like something dreaming, opening passages here, closing them there, growing new chambers while digesting old ones. There is no plan to this that anyone could discern, no central intelligence that anyone could identify, and yet the result is unmistakably architectural. The proportions are too consistent, the angles too purposeful, the way corridors open into small plazas too satisfying for coincidence. Keth builds the way a coral builds — without knowing what it builds, but building something nonetheless.

---

The silence is extraordinary.

Not silence as the absence of sound, but silence as a *material*, something that fills the corridors and presses gently against the eardrums. The mineral walls absorb vibration with unsettling efficiency. Footsteps on the chalky ground produce a noise that seems to vanish a hand's width from the sole of your boot, swallowed before it can become an echo. Clap your hands and the sound is there and then immediately not there, as though something ate it. The acoustics feel predatory, though that may be your nerves talking.

In the deeper chambers — the ones that have existed long enough to develop ceilings, where the walls have grown inward and met overhead — there is a subsonic presence. Not a hum. Not a vibration you can feel through your feet. Something lower than that, something your ribs know about before your ears do. It may be the sound of the walls growing. It may be the sound of the substrate digesting its own architecture somewhere nearby. It is felt the way a large animal's breathing is felt when you are standing too close in the dark.

---

The vegetation is sparse and strange and has no business being here.

In certain of Keth's alcoves and at certain junctions where walls meet at acute angles, there are growths. They are not quite plants. They emerge from the mineral walls themselves, pale filamentous structures somewhere between lichen and hair, each strand as thick as a pencil lead and slightly translucent, so that in the dim ambient light they glow faintly from within — not bioluminescence exactly, more the way certain cave formations seem to hold light and release it slowly. They grow in clumps and sprays, sometimes trailing down from an overhang like the beginnings of a curtain, sometimes bristling from a wall in a tight rosette no bigger than your palm.

They are cool to the touch. Cooler than the walls, cooler than the air, which itself sits in the range of an autumn evening, the kind of cool that doesn't bother you at first but accumulates over hours until you realize your fingers are stiff. The filaments sway, very slightly, though there is no wind. Keth has no wind. The air inside its corridors is so still it might as well be solid. The filaments sway because they are *growing*, each one extending at its tip, leaning incrementally this way and that as it navigates some invisible gradient in the mineral composition of the wall, and this slow seeking motion, multiplied across dozens of filaments, gives each clump a quality of restless attention, as though it is reaching toward something.

Where the filaments have grown long enough to touch the opposite wall, they fuse with it. They become part of the architecture. It is not clear whether they are parasites on Keth's mineral body or organs of it.

---

Occasionally, there are animals. Calling them animals is generous. Calling them anything is speculative.

In the deeper passages, where the dim light dims further into something barely distinguishable from darkness, things move along the walls. They are flat, translucent, roughly the shape and size of a human hand if the hand were pressed flat and its fingers fused together into a continuous oval. They travel slowly, leaving no trail, making no sound. They appear to graze on the filament-growths, or perhaps on the mineral substrate itself, or perhaps on nothing visible — simply moving through Keth's corridors like slow thoughts moving through a mind.

They avoid light. They avoid you. Turn a corner into a chamber and you might see three or four of them on the walls, and they will peel away from your presence like leaves curling from heat, flowing into crevices or simply flattening themselves so perfectly against the mineral that they become invisible. Whether they are mineral themselves — some motile phase of the same substance that makes Keth — or something genuinely biological that has adapted to this environment, you cannot tell. They have no eyes that you can find. They react to your presence from several meters away, well before you could touch them or even cast a shadow in this shadowless light.

Once, in a chamber deep enough that the subsonic presence was almost a pressure against your sternum, you saw something larger. A shape in an alcove, roughly the volume of a crouching dog, composed of the same translucent material as the hand-creatures but folded, layered, ruffled — as though many of them had crawled on top of each other and merged. It did not move while you watched it. You did not watch it for long.

---

The dynamics of this place are ceaseless and deeply unsettling in their silence. Keth is never still. At every moment, walls are growing, walls are dissolving, filaments are reaching, hand-creatures are migrating, corridors are reshaping themselves. It is a town in constant renovation, a settlement perpetually under construction and demolition simultaneously, with no workers, no tools, no noise. The processes layer and interact: a new wall emerges and the filaments colonize it within hours; the hand-creatures follow the filaments; a wall thins and reabsorbs and the filaments it carried are left hanging in midair for a brief time before they droop and are consumed by the substrate beneath them. There are cycles here, rhythms that would reveal themselves to patient observation — periods of growth and periods of digestion, phases where the town seems to expand outward toward the flat and phases where it contracts and densifies, growing more rooms but smaller ones, thickening its walls, sealing its corridors, turning inward like a fist closing.

You have the persistent sense that you are inside something. Not merely surrounded by it but *inside* it, the way a mite is inside a skin, the way a bacterium is inside a gut. The architecture is not built for you. The corridors are passable by a human body but only just, and some of the newer ones are narrower than that, and some of the older ones are already closing. Keth does not know you are here, or if it knows, it does not care, or if it cares, its care expresses itself in ways unrelated to your comfort or survival.

The air is dry. Your lips are cracking. The cool is settling into your joints. In the deep silence, the subsonic not-hum pulses at a frequency that might be your own heartbeat or might not be.

Somewhere behind you, a corridor that was open an hour ago has almost certainly begun to close.
