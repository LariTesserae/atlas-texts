# The Singing Terrace

---

It is old the way a river delta is old — not the drama of canyon-carving, but the quiet, inexorable patience of a landform that has been settling into itself for so long it has forgotten what it was before. A high plateau, worn almost flat, almost featureless, except it isn't featureless at all. You just have to be standing inside it to see.

The terrace is perhaps forty meters across. That's all. A shallow depression cupped in the summit of a mesa so ancient its edges have been beveled into gentle slopes that could be mistaken for natural hillside if you didn't know, somehow, looking at them, that these were once sheer cliffs. Millions of years — tens of millions — of wind and sparse rain have worked the stone into something that resembles skin more than rock. The surface is smooth and faintly warm underfoot, not from the sun (though the sun contributes, fat and white in a hazy sky), but from below. The rock itself holds heat the way a body holds heat. This is a plateau of dark laterite and compressed volcanic tuff, the deep red-brown of dried blood, and in places where moisture has crept and left and crept again, it shades to violet and black.

The depression is the thing. The hidden room.

---

From the air — if you could see it from the air, which you mostly can't, for reasons I'll get to — it would look like a thumbprint pressed into clay. A shallow bowl, maybe two meters deep at its lowest point relative to the surrounding plateau lip, with a floor of that same ancient worn stone, cracked into polygonal tiles by epochs of thermal cycling. Between the cracks: soil. Between the soil: life.

The vegetation is astonishing for how it conceals itself within such a modest space. Low canopy trees — they might be related to figs, or to something older — grow from the deeper fissures in the rock, their trunks following the cracks like veins following channels in flesh. They are thick-limbed, horizontal-reaching, their bark pale gray and peeling in papery sheets that catch the occasional breeze and flutter. Their root systems must extend deep into the plateau's body, tapping moisture from some buried stratum. Their canopy isn't tall — the highest branches reach maybe four meters above the bowl's floor — but it's dense where it exists, casting pools of deep green shade. Between the trees, broad-leafed ground cover fills the cracks in overlapping fans, fleshy and succulent, storing the intermittent rain that falls here in sharp, brief episodes. There are epiphytes on the tree limbs, and something like moss — but warmer in color, amber and ochre — felting the exposed rock faces at the bowl's inner rim.

The air inside the bowl is different from the air above. Warmer by several degrees. Humid. You descend the gentle inner slope and the atmosphere thickens, just slightly, like stepping into a greenhouse or a room where many creatures are breathing. Which they are.

---

The fauna. God, the fauna.

You hear them before you understand what you're hearing, and what you hear is enormous.

The dominant sound is a layered, pulsing chorus that rises from everywhere inside the bowl simultaneously. It is not insect song, though there are insects — jewel-bright things with too many wing-segments, moving in coordinated pulses between the tree branches like living tinsel. The chorus comes from the animals themselves, and there are so many of them, and they are so vocal, that the sound pressure inside this small depression is physically tangible. You feel it in your sternum. In your teeth.

Start with the obvious ones: the limb-runners. They are warm-blooded, six-limbed, furred in short dense pelts that range from slate blue to russet, and they are everywhere in the canopy. Each one is roughly the size of a large squirrel, but built more like a lemur crossed with a mantis — the front pair of limbs are articulated differently from the rear two pairs, capable of precise manipulation. They move through the trees in loose social groups of eight to fifteen, and they talk *constantly*. Their vocalizations are tonal, almost melodic, pitched in overlapping frequency bands so that multiple conversations can occur simultaneously without interference. It's the primary stratum of the chorus. A rippling, atonal music. They bicker. They negotiate. They announce. They warn. They sing what might be duets.

Below the limb-runners, on the stone floor and among the ground-cover: colonies of something between a tortoise and an anemone. Hard-shelled, slow, their shells darkened and lichenous with age, but fringed with soft sensory tendrils that wave in constant slow exploration. They graze the amber moss. When two of them meet, they produce a deep vibration — sub-bass, almost below hearing — that you feel more than hear. This is the second stratum. It gives the soundscape its weight, its foundation tone.

Birds. Or — bird-analogues. They don't fly so much as glide, launched from the bowl's rim, catching the thermal differential between the warm interior air and the cooler plateau-wind above. Their wings are translucent, membranous, and they are *loud* — sharp staccato calls, territorial, joyous, constant. They hunt the jewel-insects in acrobatic spiraling dives, then rise again on the thermals. Dozens of them. At any given moment there are thirty, forty bodies in the air column above the bowl, circling like a living funnel.

And in the deeper cracks, where the tree roots go, something larger breathes. You don't see it. You hear it — a slow, rhythmic exhalation that moves air through the fissures, warm and damp, smelling of minerals and something organic and not-unpleasant. The limb-runners don't avoid these cracks. They approach them with what looks like purposeful attention, leaving small objects — seed-husks, insect wings, pebbles — at the edges. The breath continues. Whatever lives below is patient and enormous and has been here longer than the trees.

The overall effect is overwhelming. The sound fills this small bowl and reverberates against its ancient walls and becomes a self-reinforcing acoustic phenomenon — a standing wave of biological noise that shifts in texture but never diminishes. It is loud enough that at the bowl's center, you would have to raise your voice to be heard by someone standing next to you. The place roars with life.

---

Now: the built things. The scattered remnants.

At the bowl's eastern rim, half-buried in the laterite, there is a plinth. It is made of something that is not stone, though it has been here long enough to have *become* stone-like — weathered, rounded, colonized by the amber moss. Its material, where the moss has been recently rubbed away (by what? by the shell-creatures, apparently, who seem to polish it during their slow circuits), is a deep graphite-gray, with a surface texture that's too uniform to be natural. It's about knee-height. Hexagonal. There are marks on its upper face that might be symbols or might be wear-patterns, and it is impossible to tell which.

Set into the rock near the bowl's lowest point, flush with the cracked floor: a ring of small discs, each one no larger than a coin. They are the same graphite material. They do not weather. Rain beads on them and slides away instantly, as if repelled. The limb-runners avoid stepping on them with a casualness that suggests deep instinct rather than learned behavior — the way you might step around a storm drain without thinking.

And the third thing: a filament. Nearly invisible except when the light catches it, a single thread of something impossibly fine running from the plinth to a point somewhere beyond the bowl's rim, supported by nothing, taut as a guitar string, suspended in the air at roughly head height. It does not vibrate in the wind. It does not sag. When one of the gliding bird-analogues passes through its space, it curves *around* the creature's body without touching it, then resumes its line. This happens quickly and without fuss. The filament is always straight. Except when it isn't. And then it is again.

The technology here — if that's the right word — is not displayed. It is residual. Someone or something placed these objects here so long ago that the entire ecosystem has organized itself around them, the way a forest organizes itself around a boulder. The discs might be sensors. The filament might be a communication line, or a structural element of something larger, or an anchor for something no longer present. The plinth might be a marker, a terminal, an altar. Its function has been subsumed by time into the ecological fabric of the bowl. The shell-creatures polish it. The limb-runners leave offerings near the breathing-cracks. Whether these are the same behavior — whether the technology and the biology have merged into a single system of maintenance — is a question the place does not answer.

---

The moisture comes in cycles. You can feel one approaching: a shift in the air pressure, the gliding creatures ascending higher, the limb-runner chorus shifting to a lower register. Then, quickly, a cloud forms directly above the bowl — condensation pulled from the thermal column — and rain falls. Not much. A few minutes of fat, warm drops that darken the stone and fill the shallow depressions and run down the cracks toward the roots and the breathing-below. The shell-creatures extend their tendrils fully, swaying. The amber moss darkens to sienna. The jewel-insects vanish entirely, pulled into crevices by some signal too fast to follow. Then it's over. The cloud dissipates. The sun reasserts itself. The stone begins to dry. The chorus, which had muted during the rain, rebuilds in layers — the sub-bass first, then the limb-runners, then the birds.

This happens three, perhaps four times a day. The bowl makes its own weather.

---

There is a strangeness to the geometry of this place that takes time to notice. The bowl is shallow — you can see this. You descended into it easily. But when you stand at its center and look up at the rim, the rim seems farther away than it should be. Not dramatically — not impossibly — but enough that your sense of the space keeps slipping. Forty meters across, you thought. But it takes longer to walk to the edge than forty meters should. And the acoustic properties shouldn't work the way they do — the standing wave, the reinforcement, the layered clarity of different vocal frequencies — not in a depression this shallow, this wide, this open to the sky. The physics are almost right. Almost conventional. But something about the bowl's shape, or the rock's composition, or the legacy of whatever technology was seeded here, has tuned this space. Made it a resonance chamber. A pocket that is slightly more interior than its dimensions suggest.

You are inside something. Not a building. Not an enclosure. A *curated volume*. And the curation is so old, and the ecosystem so thoroughly adapted to it, that the distinction between the engineered and the natural has been composted into irrelevance.

The limb-runners watch you from the branches. Their eyes are large, forward-facing, amber. They chatter about you in frequencies you can almost parse. One of them places a small, polished stone at the edge of a breathing-crack and looks at you, and looks at the crack, and looks at you again. The breath from below shifts. Warmer. Slower.

The filament catches the light and, for a moment, you see that it isn't one line but a braid of lines, splitting and reconverging, and that other filaments extend from it at angles you can't quite follow, connecting to points in the air where nothing is, where nothing is anchored, where they simply *end* — or continue in directions that the geometry of the bowl allows but the geometry of the wider world does not.

It rains again. Warm. Brief. The chorus rebuilds.

You are welcome here. You have always been welcome here. The place is too full of life to be hostile, and too old to be surprised by anything. You are the newest thing in this bowl by a margin of millions of years, and the ecosystem absorbs you the way it absorbs the rain — briefly, completely, and without interruption.
