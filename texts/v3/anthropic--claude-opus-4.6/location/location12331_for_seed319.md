# The Weeping Plateau

---

The first thing is the sound. You hear it before you crest the last switchback, before the city reveals itself — a vast, tubular moan that rises from somewhere below the visible world and saturates the air until your teeth ache with it. It is not one sound but thousands layered into a single drone: the harmonic of water moving through pipes, through culverts, through the hollowed-out bones of a limestone plateau that has been slowly dissolving for longer than anyone has bothered to count.

---

The plateau sits at an intermediate altitude — high enough that the air carries a faint thinness, a suggestion that breath requires a fraction more effort, but not so high that the warmth dissipates. The warmth here is peculiar. It rises. It comes up through the stone, through the soles of your boots, through the foundations of every building. The plateau is a cap of ancient marine limestone resting on a geothermal seam, and the water table — always close, always pressing upward — is bathwater-warm. The rock sweats. Every exposed surface glistens. Condensation beads on iron railings and collects in the grooves of stone steps worn concave by centuries of foot traffic. The air is heavy and close and smells of wet calcium and old metal.

The city is called Thurm, or something that sounds like Thurm when spoken through the drone. You can never quite be sure.

---

From the plateau's edge you can see the full extent of it: a settlement that stretches across a broad, gently concave depression in the rock, maybe three kilometers at its widest, tapering at both ends into ridgelines of bare, rain-pitted limestone. The plateau drops away on all sides into haze — not cloud exactly, but the exhalation of the warm wet stone meeting cooler air at the margins, a perpetual veil that makes Thurm feel landlocked in sky. You cannot see the valleys below. You can only see the city, and the open stone beyond it, and the haze.

The buildings are dense at the center and sparse at the edges, and this is where the strangeness of the place asserts itself: the city does not thin gradually. It simply stops. One block there are three-story structures of mortared limestone with iron-framed windows and corrugated roofing, and the next block there is nothing — just naked plateau, slick and faintly pocked, with the occasional dark seep-hole where water wells up and spreads in a thin sheet before finding some invisible declivity and vanishing again. The transition is abrupt, almost surgical. As if the city has been carved to a specific boundary and the stone beyond has refused it.

Between the buildings, there is space. More space than you expect in an urban settlement this industrialized. The streets are wide — not generously wide but emptily wide, as though designed for a population or a traffic pattern that never quite materialized, or that materialized and then slowly withdrew. The gaps between structures have a deliberate quality, though. Channels run down the center of every major thoroughfare, stone-lined, carrying warm water from somewhere to somewhere else. The channels are uncovered. The water in them is clear but tinged faintly amber, and it moves with a sluggish, unhurried constancy that suggests tremendous volume held at low velocity. The channels feed into larger culverts that disappear beneath buildings, and it is from these subterranean passages that the city draws its voice.

---

The drone. You must understand the drone.

The plateau is riddled with solution channels — natural tubes and cavities dissolved into the limestone over millennia by the warm, mildly acidic groundwater pressing up from below. When the city was built — and the layering of architectural styles suggests it was built in phases over a very long time, each phase absorbing the last without quite erasing it — these natural channels were incorporated into the infrastructure. Some were widened. Some were connected. Some were fitted with iron sleeves or ceramic linings. The result is a plumbing system of extraordinary complexity and scale, part engineered and part geological, that carries warm water throughout the city for heating, for washing, for industrial use, for purposes you cannot always identify.

And the water, moving through these passages of varying diameter and material, *sings*.

It is not musical. It is not pleasant. It is an industrial hymn in a frequency range that begins below hearing and climbs into the audible spectrum as a wall of resonant pressure. Certain streets are louder than others. Certain intersections produce standing waves where two major culverts cross beneath the pavement, and in these places the sound becomes physical — you feel it in your sternum, in the fluid of your inner ear. The residents of Thurm do not seem to hear it anymore, or they have incorporated it so deeply into their experience that silence would be the aberration. They speak loudly. They gesture. Some of the older ones communicate in a system of hand signals when the drone peaks — which it does, periodically, in long slow swells that take hours to build and hours to fade, governed by tidal pressures in the water table that no one has fully mapped.

The factories amplify everything. There are several, clustered along the city's southeastern quadrant where the plateau dips slightly and the water pressure is highest. They are squat, thick-walled buildings with banks of chimneys that emit not smoke but steam — the geothermal water, having been used for whatever mechanical process occurs inside, is vented as vapor that joins the general haze. Through their open loading bays you can see the machinery: massive iron cylinders, valve assemblies green with mineral deposits, belt-driven systems that harness the water pressure directly. Thurm has no fuel. It has no combustion. Everything runs on the slow upwelling of warm water through ancient stone, converted to mechanical force through pressure differentials and turbine systems that look like they were designed by someone who understood plumbing on a spiritual level but had never seen an electrical diagram. There is electricity — you can see the caged bulbs strung along certain streets, the occasional spark of arc welding in a repair shop — but it is generated locally, by water-driven dynamos, and it flickers in time with the pressure swells. The light in Thurm breathes.

---

Outside the city boundary, the plateau is nearly barren. The limestone surface supports almost nothing. Here and there a tuft of wiry, mineral-tolerant grass has found a crevice with enough accumulated grit to anchor its roots, but these are lonely presences, separated by meters of bare rock that gleams wetly in the ambient light. The light itself is diffuse and warm-toned — the haze at the plateau's edges acts as a softbox, scattering direct sunlight into an even, shadowless glow that makes distances hard to judge. Midday looks like late afternoon. Late afternoon looks like a painting of late afternoon, slightly overexposed.

There are no trees. There are no shrubs. The vegetation that exists is ground-hugging and grey-green, adapted to stone, to mineral-laden water, to the constant vibration that travels through the rock from the city's plumbing and the natural solution channels both. If you place your hand flat on the stone anywhere on the plateau, you can feel it — a hum, a tremor so fine it's almost thermal. The stone is alive in the way that infrastructure is alive: conducting, transmitting, bearing loads.

Fauna is scarce. You might see a raptor — a kestrel-like bird with pale underwings — riding the thermals that form where the plateau's warmth meets the cooler air at the edges. It hangs motionless in the haze, hunting something you cannot see. Occasionally a small lizard, flat-bodied and stone-colored, will detach itself from a wall in the city's outer districts and flow liquidly into a crack. There are supposed to be fish in the deeper culverts — blind, pale things that feed on bacterial mats in the warm water — but no one you ask has actually seen one. They are an article of faith, like the bottom of the water table.

---

The erosion here is not decay. It is identity.

Every surface in Thurm has been shaped by water and time into its truest form. The stone steps are cupped. The iron railings are furred with mineral scale that has itself been polished by hands into smooth ridges. The building facades show the ghosts of older facades beneath them — layers of plaster and render that have been applied and worn away and applied again until the walls have a palimpsest texture, a geological record of habitation. Nothing in Thurm looks new. Nothing looks ruined. Everything looks *arrived* — settled into the exact state it was always going to reach, patient and irreversible.

The city changes, but slowly. A building at the eastern margin develops a crack in its foundation where a solution channel has widened imperceptibly over decades; the crack is monitored, discussed, shored up with iron braces, and eventually the building is abandoned and its materials are absorbed into neighboring structures. A new seep-hole opens on the western plateau and the water is tested, tasted, debated, and eventually plumbed into the system with ceramic pipes fired in a kiln that runs on pressurized steam. These are events that unfold over years, over generations. The city's dynamism is glacial. Its urgency is measured in the slow migration of mineral deposits, the gradual deepening of channels, the patient wearing of stone by stone-warmed water.

---

There is something else. You notice it after you've been in Thurm for a few hours and your ears have partially adjusted to the drone.

The streets, for all their width, do not form a regular grid. They curve. They spiral, gently, around certain points — intersections or small plazas where the stone has been left exposed and the seep-water pools in shallow basins before being drawn into the channel system. These are the loudest places in the city, the nodes where the drone peaks and the stone vibrates with a complex harmonic that you feel in the roots of your teeth. The residents avoid lingering in these places, but they do not avoid them entirely. You see offerings there — not religious exactly, but propitiary. Small iron objects. Ceramic tokens. Things placed at the edge of the seep-pools with a casualness that belies their consistency. Every pool has them. Every pool has had them for as long as anyone remembers.

The water rises. The water has always risen. The city exists because it rises, and the city shapes the rising, and the sound of that shaping fills the air until there is no silence left, only degrees of noise. And in the haze at the plateau's edges, where the warm breath of the stone meets the indifferent sky, you cannot tell if Thurm is growing or dissolving — only that it is here, improbably, obdurately, a settlement built on the principle that if you cannot stop the water, you might as well make it work.

The drone swells. The lights flicker. The kestrel tilts and vanishes into white.
