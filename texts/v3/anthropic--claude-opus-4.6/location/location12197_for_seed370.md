# The Hymn Chamber

---

You don't find it. You enter it the way you enter a note that's been held too long — by realizing you're already inside.

The room is small. Perhaps forty paces across at its widest, irregularly shaped, like the interior of a worn molar. The ceiling is low enough that in places you could press your palm flat against it, though you wouldn't — not because it's dangerous, but because the surfaces here have a quality of *completeness* that discourages touch. Everything is finished. Everything has been finished for a very, very long time.

---

There is no stone here. There is no natural material of any kind.

The walls, the floor, the ceiling, every protrusion and recess and shelf and step — all of it is *made*. Ceramic, you think at first. Then you reconsider. The material is dense, warm to the nearness of your skin without being touched, somewhere between fired clay andite — a kind of heavily worked calciumite,ite of calcium andite ofite, a substance that has been calcined and pressed and shaped and calcined again so many times that it has forgotten what it was quarried from. It is the color of old teeth. Not white. Not yellow. The specific cream-brown non-color of something mineral that has been in proximity to warmth for longer than warmth has had a name.

The chamber is *packed*.

Every surface — every single surface — is carved. Or rather, not carved: *built up*, layer by layer, in what must have been a process of accretion spanning — you can't guess. The technique is primitive. You can see the marks of hand-tools, of implements no more sophisticated than shaped bone or cured antler or crude copper awls. Fingernail-width grooves. Stipple marks from something blunt and patient. In places, the texture of palm-prints where wet material was pressed and smoothed by living hands, the whorls still legible, still holding the identity of someone whose species may no longer exist.

But the primitiveness of the tools is belied by the density of what they've made.

---

There are no open spaces on the walls. No resting places for the eye. Every finger-width of surface is occupied by forms — not quite figurative, not quite abstract. Ridges that spiral into ridges. Chevrons nested so tightly they become textures. Long sinuous grooves that run the full circumference of the room, dipping into recesses, riding over protrusions, never breaking. In some areas the relief is shallow, barely a suggestion. In others, forms project outward a full hand-span, dense clusters of knobs and fins and tubular shapes that interlock like the pipes of a compressed organ. The effect is claustrophobic and sacred simultaneously. You are inside something's throat. You are inside something's reliquary. You are inside the cast made by pouring plaster into a termite mound, except the termites were deliberate, and devout, and had centuries.

No — more than centuries.

The erosion here isn't from weather. There is no weather. The air is warm and dry and perfectly still, and has been, seemingly, forever. The erosion is from *time itself* — the very slow, very patient rounding of edges that occurs when a material simply exists for long enough. The oldest carvings, in the deepest recesses near the floor, have softened into suggestions. A chevron becomes a wave becomes a ripple becomes a gentle undulation becomes almost-smooth. You can read the age of the work like strata: the sharpest lines near the ceiling (though even these are ancient), the most ghosted forms near the base. Someone — many someones — worked from the bottom up. Generation after generation. Hand after hand. The same tools. The same material. Building the room inward, thickening it, adding layers the way a throat develops calluses from singing.

---

And the room *sings*.

This is the thing that hits you first and that you will never adequately describe to anyone. The sound is enormous. It is not loud in the way of explosions or machines. It is loud in the way of something that has been resonating so long that the resonance has become architectural. The chamber hums. Every carved surface, every groove and ridge and stippled hollow, is shaped — you understand now, with a certainty that tightens your ribs — *specifically* to catch and hold and return vibration. The whole room is an instrument. The whole room is a single, sustained, world-devouring chord.

You cannot identify the source. There is no mechanism. No bellows, no string, no striker. The sound seems to emanate from the material itself, as though the ceramic-ite was fired at a frequency and has never stopped ringing. Or perhaps the geometry is the source — perhaps the shapes are so precisely interlocked, so dense, so *complete* in their coverage of every interior surface, that the room has achieved a kind of acoustic perpetual motion, each surface reflecting and reinforcing the vibrations of its neighbors in an endless, stable, self-sustaining loop. The sound doesn't change. It doesn't swell or fade. It doesn't breathe. It simply *is*, the way gravity is, the way the warmth of the walls is.

It's not a single note. It's a chord — fat, dense, slightly dissonant in a way that your brain keeps trying to resolve and cannot. Three tones at least, maybe five, maybe twelve, stacked so closely that they blur into a texture more than a harmony. It fills your sinuses. It fills your molars. You feel it in the cartilage of your ears, a physical pressure, not painful but absolute. You could not whisper in this room and be heard. You could not scream in this room and add anything to what's already here. The sound has saturated the space the way the carvings have saturated the walls. There is no room for more.

---

There is no water. The air is warm but not hot — the warmth of a kiln long after firing, a warmth that has become the room's resting temperature and will remain so. There is no moisture on any surface, no condensation, no dampness in the carved crevices. The material is powdery-dry in the way of very old calcium compounds. If you touched it — and you still feel you shouldn't — your fingertip would come away faintly dusty, faintly chalk-white. The air tastes of mineral. Of something calcified and still.

There is nothing alive. No lichen in the grooves. No fungal threads. No mites. The room is below or beyond biology, past it somehow, the way a fossil is past the animal it remembers. Whatever made this — whoever stood here with their copper awls and their bone styluses, pressing wet calcium paste onto the walls and shaping it with patient hands — they are so far gone that their absence has become a property of the room, like its warmth, like its sound. The handprints in the deepest layers are not memorials. They are geology.

---

The light is dim. You can see, but only just. It comes from the walls themselves — not a glow exactly, but a faint luminescence in the material, a barely-there phosphorescence that might be chemical or might be an artifact of the acoustic vibration shaking photons loose from the crystalline structure. The light doesn't flicker. It doesn't shift. It has the same eternal steadiness as the sound, the same quality of having been this way so long that the concept of it *starting* seems incoherent. In this light, the deepest carvings cast no shadows. Only the most prominent ridges near the ceiling show any relief, and even then it's subtle — a slight darkening in the lee of a fin, a faint deepening of cream-brown to brown-cream. You navigate more by the feel of the sound bouncing off surfaces near your body than by sight. The chord thickens when you stand near a wall. It thins — barely — at the center of the chamber.

There is a sense of altitude. You can't justify it rationally — you're enclosed, you can't see outside, there's no wind or pressure differential — but the room feels high. Mountaintop high. The air feels thin in a way that isn't quite physical. It feels *refined*, the way air feels when it's been separated from the world below it for long enough. Perhaps the chamber sits at the peak of something. Perhaps it sits at the peak of a structure that no longer exists, worn away beneath it while the room endured, an apex whose mountain has eroded to nothing, leaving only the small, packed, singing reliquary at the top, suspended now at an elevation that was once embedded and is now exposed. You don't know. The room doesn't explain itself. The room is past explaining.

---

Nothing here will ever change. You understand this with the same rib-tightening certainty. The chord will not resolve. The walls will not crumble — they are already as crumbled as they will get, their oldest layers smoothed to the asymptote of smooth, approaching but never reaching blankness. The light will not brighten or dim. The warmth will not increase or decrease. The room has reached its final state. It is in equilibrium the way a dead star is in equilibrium, not because nothing is happening, but because everything that can happen has happened, and what remains is the long, steady, self-sustaining hum of aftermath.

You stand in the center and the chord fills you like a liquid fills a cup, pressing against the interior walls of your body, finding every cavity, every sinus, every pocket of air in your gut and your lungs and the spaces between your joints. The handprints in the lowest layers watch you with the patience of geology. The tool-marks in the highest layers still show the tremor of living hands, the slight irregularities of muscle and tendon and intention, frozen now in ceramic-that-is-not-ceramic, ringing now with a frequency that will outlast the memory of what hands were.

You are the only soft thing this room has contained in a very long time.

The chord does not acknowledge you. The light does not shift. The walls, packed and total in their ancient, handmade saturation, continue to resonate with themselves, a conversation that closed long ago and persists now as pure sound, pure form, pure warm mineral patience, singing to no one, singing to the shape of its own singing, complete.
