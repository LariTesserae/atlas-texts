# The Swelter Hollow

Below the level of the surrounding land, entered through a crack in reddish sandstone that you have to turn sideways to fit through, the hollow opens like a throat into a humid chamber roughly the size of a modest cathedral — if a cathedral had been designed by roots instead of masons, and had spent ten thousand years forgetting what stone was supposed to do.

---

The floor is not flat. It dips and buckles in smooth, worn undulations, the sandstone here long since surrendered to its own softness. Water has been at this rock for ages — not violently, not carving, but *persuading*. The surface has the texture of something licked smooth, every edge rounded into a curve, every crack widened just enough to hold a dark seam of moisture. The stone is warm to the touch, body-temperature warm, heated from below by whatever deep geological patience drives warmth upward through the crust here. You feel it through the soles of your feet. The air sits heavy and close, laden with the particular wetness of a place that never fully dries but never fully floods — a permanent condensation, a climate of breath.

The ceiling is maybe forty feet at its highest point, though it's hard to tell because almost nothing up there is bare rock anymore.

---

Roots have come down. They've come down from the surface in thick arterial ropes, pale where they first breach the ceiling and darkening to a deep rust-brown as they thicken and descend. Some are as wide as a human torso. They branch and rebranch, splitting into fractal networks that press against the rock, wrap around protrusions, braid with one another. In places they've merged — two root systems grown together until you can't tell where one plant ends and another begins. They form curtains, columns, matted walls. Between them grow mosses of an almost violent green, the kind of green that seems to emit its own dim phosphorescence though it doesn't, not quite — it simply catches and holds every trace of the faint light that enters through fissures above.

The light itself is a warm amber dimness, like the last hour before sunset stretched into permanence. It comes in through three or four narrow cracks in the ceiling, irregular slots where the sandstone has separated just enough to admit thin blades of the outside world. These shafts of light angle down through the root-mass and are broken, scattered, made particulate. Tiny insects drift through them — visible only in those beams, golden motes with wings.

Ferns erupt from every horizontal surface. Not one species but several, layered: tiny ones with fronds no bigger than a thumbnail growing on the backs of larger ones, which themselves grow from the tangled root-platforms. There are lichens too, crusting the stone in pale medallions, and a thick, fleshy-leafed plant that grows in rosettes wherever enough moisture collects in the rock's worn depressions, its leaves dark and waxy, holding small pools of condensation in their cups.

---

The water. There's a pool — one real pool, not just dampness but actual standing water — in the lowest depression of the floor, off-center, maybe twelve feet across and no more than two feet deep at its heart. Its surface is utterly still, black-looking in this light, reflecting the root canopy above as a dark inverted forest. The water is warm. Not hot, but warm, fed by slow seepage through the heated stone, and it smells faintly mineral, faintly organic, like wet clay and living things. Around its edges the rock is stained darker, a permanent waterline that shows the pool sometimes grows and sometimes shrinks but has existed in approximately this form for longer than anything human. Small channels run away from it, hair-thin, following the stone's natural grain, wetting the floor in a branching delta that feeds the rosette plants and the moss.

A second, smaller pocket of water sits in an alcove to the east — if there is an east down here — a basin the size of a bathtub, half-hidden behind a curtain of roots. This one collects condensation dripping from the ceiling, and the sound it makes — *tik... tik... tik* — is the loudest thing in the hollow, though loud is the wrong word. It is the only discrete, repeating sound. Everything else is the continuous near-silence of biological activity that registers more as a pressure in the ears than as noise.

---

The animals.

They are *everywhere*, and this is what makes the hollow feel less like a cave and more like an organ — like being inside something alive and busy.

Begin with the smallest. The walls crawl with pale, translucent arthropods — millipedes, maybe, or something related but not quite classifiable, their bodies soft and segmented, moving in slow columns along the roots. They feed on the moss. You can see the dark green of consumed plant matter through their translucent shells. They are innumerable. Thousands. They make no sound.

Beetles occupy the mid-layer — dark, round-backed, each the size of a thumbnail, tucked into crevices in the root bark. They emerge in irregular rhythms, trundle a few inches, and return. Something about their movements suggests they're tending the roots, though that may be projection. Their wing-cases are the color of old blood.

Small frogs — a dozen or more visible at any time, probably many more hidden — populate the rock around both pools. They are dark, nearly black, with two faint copper stripes along their backs. They sit motionless for long periods, then move with sudden liquid precision. They don't call. That's the unusual thing. Frogs in a humid underground chamber and *not one of them calls*. They communicate some other way, perhaps by the subtle vibrations you can feel if you press your palm flat to the stone — a faint, irregular pulsing that might be the frogs or might be something deeper, but which the frogs seem to respond to, shifting positions in coordinated waves that ripple across the colony.

There are snakes. Two species at least. One is thin, bronze, fast-moving, and hunts the millipedes along the root curtains; you catch glimpses of it threading through the tangles, a liquid line of gold. The other is heavier, dark gray, and drapes itself along the thicker roots near the ceiling, moving so slowly it might be mistaken for another root until you see the slow, deliberate curl of a muscle, the glacial relocation of a coil. This one is watching the frogs. It hunts perhaps once a day, perhaps less. The rest of the time it is still as the stone.

Bats roost in a cluster in the highest recess of the ceiling, maybe thirty or forty of them, small-bodied with large, membranous ears. They sleep now — or most of them do. One hangs slightly apart from the group, eyes open, slowly rotating its head as though listening to frequencies beneath hearing. Their guano has fallen below them for years, decades, building a low mound of chalky white that the millipedes avoid but the rosette plants have colonized, growing from it in grotesque abundance, huge-leafed and swollen with nutrient.

And there is something else near the large pool that you can't immediately identify — something that disturbs the water's surface from below in slow, periodic bulges, as though a shape the size of two cupped hands is pressing upward from the bottom and then retreating. It could be a gas release. It could be a large amphibian breathing at the surface. The frogs nearest this disturbance orient toward it each time it occurs, their copper-striped bodies angling like compass needles, then slowly turning away.

---

Against the far wall — if you can find a far wall in this tangle — there are marks. Not natural ones. Cut into the stone at roughly chest height, partially overgrown by lichen and root, there's a series of shallow grooves. Maybe twenty of them, roughly parallel, each about a hand's width long. Tally marks, possibly. Or something decorative. Or something functional that you can't understand. They were made with a hard edge — a sharp stone, probably, dragged with intention. Below them, set into a natural shelf in the rock, sits a single object: a smooth river cobble, egg-shaped, placed there. Not fallen there, not wedged naturally. *Placed.* It has a faint depression worn into its top, as though a thumb rested there many, many times.

Whoever left these marks is long gone. There's no other sign of habitation — no fire-blackened ceiling, no bones, no tools. Just the tally and the stone and whatever they meant to whoever descended into this hot, teeming, quiet underworld and decided it was worth counting something.

---

The air moves, but barely. A faint current enters through the same cracks that admit light, carrying the dry heat of the surface world, and as it meets the hollow's humidity it creates a zone of perpetual soft turbulence just below the ceiling — a layer where warm and less-warm air trade places, making the root curtains sway almost imperceptibly, a slow-motion undulation that travels across the chamber like a wave crossing a pond. This is the dynamic that drives everything. The warmth from below. The drier air from above. The water cycling between them — evaporating, condensing, dripping, pooling, evaporating again. The plants drinking. The millipedes eating the plants. The frogs and snakes eating the millipedes and each other. The bats leaving at night through some exit you haven't found and returning before dawn with bellies full of surface insects, depositing their nutrients, feeding the rosettes, which feed the millipedes, which feed everything.

A closed system. Or nearly closed. A pocket world with its own slow metabolism, its own calendar marked in drip-rhythms and bat-cycles and the periodic, unexplained surging at the center of the dark pool.

You could stay here a long time. The heat would make you drowsy. The silence — that deep, pressurized almost-silence — would settle into your bones. The millipedes would eventually investigate your stillness, their translucent bodies mapping the new terrain of your ankles. The frogs would reorient. The watching bat would note you, file you, return to its wider listening.

You would become, for a while, another warm thing in the hollow. And the hollow would continue, as it has continued, ticking its single water-drop metronome against the alcove stone, breathing its slow mineral breath, busy and quiet and complete.
