# The Weeping Cabinet

---

There is a room inside the mountain. Not carved into it — *placed* there, fitted into a fissure that the mountain hasn't finished growing yet. The rock around it is still young, barely rock at all, more like geological intention: mineral slurries slowly deciding to become basalt, crystal lattices half-committed, the bones of a future peak still soft in their mold. The mountain is assembling itself, and this room — this cabinet, this chamber no larger than the interior of a railway freight car — was installed during the assembly, embedded like a pacemaker in a chest that hasn't yet closed.

Everything in here is made. Every surface, every object. The walls are riveted iron panels, matte black, beaded everywhere with condensation. Not rust — the water is too cold and too clean to have started that work yet, and besides, time doesn't seem to move here, not in the way that would let oxidation take hold. The floor is a grated metal walkway, like the kind you'd find on an industrial catwalk, bolted down over a shallow channel where water pools — an inch, maybe two — perpetually full, perpetually still, fed by some unseen weeping through the seams where the panels meet the raw proto-geology outside. The ceiling is low, close enough that you'd feel it without touching it. Thick bundles of insulated cable run along the upper corners, disappearing into junction boxes that hum — a hum you feel in your sternum more than hear, because there is so much else to hear.

The cold is absolute. Not biting, not wind-driven. Just *there*, deeply settled, the kind of cold that belongs to the interior of a planet that hasn't decided to have a mantle yet. Your breath doesn't quite fog — the air is too saturated for that, the moisture already suspended in it, already everywhere, a permanent dew-state that coats every surface in a thin bright film. The water doesn't drip. It *arrives*. It materializes on the undersides of pipes, along the edges of rivets, in the crease where a cable bundle bends. It doesn't fall. It just appears and stays.

The light is industrial and dim: two caged bulbs, filament-style, bolted to the ceiling at uneven intervals. They burn a deep amber that barely reaches the walls before giving up. Their glow catches the water on every surface and multiplies into a thousand tiny specular points, so the room seems to contain more light sources than it does — as though the condensation is remembering light from somewhere else and playing it back at reduced volume.

---

Now. The fauna.

They are everywhere and they are *deafening*.

Start with the walls. The riveted panels are covered — not decorated, *colonized* — with creatures that appear to be something between barnacles and small mechanical clocks. They cling in clusters, overlapping, each one the size of a walnut or smaller, their shells made of a dark chitinous material with visible seams, as though the animals themselves are riveted together the way the room is. They click. Not randomly — in rotating patterns, in interlocking sequences, like a thousand tiny escapements all running at once. Each one opens and closes a small aperture at its crown, maybe breathing, maybe speaking, and each opening and closing produces a sharp *tick* that multiplies across the colonies into a sound like a textile mill heard from inside the loom. It is constant. It is unchanging. It has been this sound for as long as this room has existed, and it will be this sound for as long as it continues to exist, and there is no evidence that either of those durations has a boundary.

Then the water — the shallow channel beneath the grated floor. It is dense with life. Pale things, elongated, slow-moving but numerous enough that the water appears to churn: something like salamanders with translucent skin through which you can see not organs but *wire*, tiny dark threads that might be veins or might be actual filament, running in parallel lines from snout to tail-tip. They move over and under and through each other in the shallow trough, nosing at the grate from below, their movements producing a wet continuous rustling that layers under the ticking of the wall-creatures. They seem to have no eyes. They seem to have too many limbs — six, eight, the count changes depending on whether you consider the frilled lateral structures to be legs or something else, something more like radiator fins. They are cool to the touch, if you were to reach through the grate, which you would not, because the density of them is such that your fingers would contact four or five at once, and there is something about the way they *don't react* to disturbance that is worse than if they fled.

Above you, in the cable bundles, there is rustling and clicking of a different timber. Something nests there — many somethings — small dark shapes that peer out from between the insulated wires with reflective eyes, dozens of pairs, amber-green, catching the weak filament light and holding it. They might be arthropods. They might be small mammals that have decided to be arthropods. Their legs — visible occasionally when one shifts — are too numerous and too segmented for mammalian morphology, but their faces, what you can see of them, have an unsettling *knowingness*, a blunt calm intelligence that insects don't carry. They make a sound: a high, thin, continuous drone, like a wet finger on a crystal glass rim, but produced organically from somewhere inside their compact bodies. This drone is always present. It does not waver. Combined with the ticking of the wall colonies and the wet rustling of the under-floor creatures, it creates a three-layered sonic environment that is *profoundly, physically loud* — not aggressive, not painful, but total, saturating, the way a factory floor is loud, occupying every frequency so completely that silence becomes inconceivable.

And there are others. Creatures in the corners that are harder to categorize — sessile things growing from the junctions of wall and floor, branching like coral but with the dull sheen of machined steel, swaying faintly in no detectable current. Are they alive? They pulse. Something moves through them, some internal rhythm, visible through their semi-translucent outer layer. Small scuttling things, flat as coins, traverse the floor grating in purposeful lines, disappearing into gaps, reappearing elsewhere, carrying nothing visible but clearly *transporting*. A single larger shape — you become aware of it slowly — is attached to the far wall at chest height, spread across nearly a full panel, flat and dark and so still that at first it registered as a stain. It breathes. You can see this because the condensation on its surface catches light differently during each cycle: bright, dim, bright, dim. But the cycle is so slow, and so perfectly metronomic, that it seems less like breathing and more like a signal.

All of these creatures are everywhere. Every surface hosts something. The density is extraordinary — not chaotic, but *saturated*, the way a coral reef is saturated, every niche filled, every gap occupied, but with a mechanical orderliness that matches the room itself. They are not infesting the room. They are *of* the room. They were installed or they co-arose or the distinction doesn't apply here.

---

None of this changes.

That is the central fact of this place, the thing that makes it strange beyond its strangeness: nothing here is in the process of becoming something else. The water doesn't accumulate. The creatures don't migrate. The light doesn't flicker. The hum in the cables doesn't surge or diminish. The temperature neither warms from the density of life nor cools further from the mountain's young geology pressing in from outside. Every process that should be dynamic — condensation, biological metabolism, electrical load, the very formation of the mountain around this embedded chamber — all of it appears to have reached some impossible equilibrium, a standing wave of activity that produces constant motion and constant sound but no *change*. The wall-creatures tick at exactly the same rate they ticked a moment ago. The salamander-things circulate in the trough in patterns that, if you watched long enough, you would realize never vary. The eyes in the cable bundles do not blink. The flat creature on the wall breathes at a rate that is indistinguishable from the rate at which the caged bulbs emit their photons.

Even the mountain outside has stopped assembling, or rather, it assembles at a rate exactly matched by something else — some dissolution, some counter-process — so that the proto-rock around the chamber remains permanently pre-geological, forever almost-formed. The room exists in a pocket of achieved stasis within an unfinished world, humming and ticking and weeping and rustling at a volume that fills every corner of your skull, and it will do so identically and without interruption whether or not you remain to hear it.

There is no door. There is no visible way in or out. The cables enter through sealed grommets in the wall and exit through others. The water comes from everywhere and goes nowhere. You are here, in this cold, loud, close, wet, utterly fabricated, utterly alive cabinet inside a mountain that hasn't been born yet, and everything in it is looking at you with the calm patience of things that have never needed to wait, because nothing here has ever been other than exactly what it is right now.
