# The Plateau of Constant Disaggregation

---

The first thing you understand is that the ground is not ground. It has not been ground for a very long time — longer, perhaps, than ground has existed elsewhere. What you stand on is a mat of root-matter so ancient and so compressed that it has forgotten the species it came from. It is dark, almostite, and it gives slightly under weight like waterlogged cork. Centuries of slow accumulation, centuries of slow erosion, have brought this plateau to a kind of terminal equilibrium: everything that can be worn away has been worn away. What remains is an undulating high surface, cold and wet, that extends in every direction to a horizon that keeps shifting.

That's the first wrongness. The horizon shifts.

Not like heat shimmer. Not like fog rolling. The actual *distance* changes. The plateau breathes — an enormous, slow, tectonic respiration that stretches the land outward for minutes at a time, then gently contracts it. When it stretches, the horizon recedes and new features become visible: low hummocks of moss-architecture, silver channels of standing water, the dark profiles of what you might call trees if they weren't so clearly something that had evolved past the concept of trees and come back around to it from the other side. When it contracts, those features crowd closer, and you can hear them. The moss hummocks make a sound as they compress against each other — a slow, wet exhalation, a groan that vibrates up through the root-mat underfoot. Then the expansion again, and a sucking inhalation as water rushes into the gaps.

This is the breathing. It has been going on for a geological age. The plateau has worn itself into a shape that accommodates it.

---

## The Vegetation

Everything here is plant-matter, or was, or is becoming plant-matter. The root-mat itself is alive in places — you can tell because those areas are slightly warmer, fractionally less dark, and covered in a fine nap of pale filaments that wave without wind. The hummocks are colonial structures, each one a superorganism of mosses, liverworts, and something like lichen that has developed vasculature. They rise to shoulder height at most. Their surfaces are intricately textured, folded like brain coral, and perpetually damp. Water beads on them, runs along their channels, drips from their undersides into dark pools.

Between the hummocks, scattered widely across the open stretches of root-mat, stand the post-trees. They are sparse — a dozen visible at any time, maybe two dozen when the plateau stretches out — and they are the tallest features in this landscape. Each one is roughly ten meters high, and none of them resemble each other in architecture, though they share a family feeling. One is a single trunk that spirals upward and then unfurls into a canopy of translucent membranes, each membrane veined like a dragonfly wing, each one slowly flexing and catching the dim light. Another has no trunk at all but is instead a column of braided aerial roots descending from a cluster of floating bulbs — hydrogen-filled bladders, each one the size of a human torso, tethered by the roots to the mat below. A third appears to be weeping: it is a tall mound of fibrous tissue from which dozens of long, thin tendrils hang straight down, their tips touching the water, and they pulse rhythmically, drawing water upward into the body of the plant, and the plant trembles and swells and occasionally discharges a cloud of spores that drift laterally in the cold air.

The spore clouds are everywhere. They are part of the atmosphere. They give the air its slight opacity, its quality of dim silver luminescence even though no obvious light source is visible overhead. The sky — if it is a sky — is a uniform pearlescent gray, brighter in no particular direction, darker near the horizon where the water-channels catch and absorb it. The spores fluoresce faintly. They are the light.

This means the light is alive. This means the light reproduces, and drifts, and settles, and germinates.

---

## The Water

Water is everywhere but never deep. The root-mat is saturated; step anywhere and water rises around your foot. The channels that run between hummocks are ankle-deep at most, slow-moving, their surfaces covered in a film of fine green organisms that break and reform as the plateau breathes. When the land contracts, the channels widen and deepen slightly. When it expands, they narrow to dark seams. Some of the channels converge into broader pools — irregularly shaped, mirror-still except when something moves beneath them.

Things move beneath them constantly.

The water here sweats upward from some source far below the root-mat. It is cold — just above freezing — and it carries minerals that precipitate out as fine white crystals along the edges of the pools. These crystal formations are the closest thing to geology this plateau possesses. They are old. They are the most eroded things here, worn into soft, toothlike ridges, coated in biofilm, slowly being incorporated into the living mat. In another million years they will be gone entirely, and the plateau will be purely biological from surface to whatever lies beneath.

There is also condensation. The air is so saturated that water forms on every surface — on the hummocks, on the post-tree membranes, on any still object. If you stand in one place long enough, you begin to accumulate a fine layer of moisture. The cold helps. Everything here is just cold enough to condense the ambient humidity but not cold enough to freeze it. This is a precise temperature, and it holds. It does not fluctuate. The plateau has found its thermal equilibrium and it will not be moved from it.

## The Fauna

They are abundant and they are wrong.

In a landscape this sparse — this much open space between its features — you would expect the animals to be visible, trackable, countable. And in a sense they are. But the problem is one of *category*. The organisms that move through this space do not maintain consistent morphology. A creature that, at the periphery of your vision, appears to be something like a large amphibian — wet-skinned, four-limbed, low to the ground — will, when you look directly at it, be something else. Not because it moved. Not because you were mistaken. Because it *disaggregated*.

This is the central phenomenon of the plateau and it gives this place its name, though no one has been here to name it.

Disaggregation works like this: the organisms here — animal, fungal, motile-plant, whatever they are — are colonial. Each apparent individual is a temporary coalition of smaller units. These units are roughly the size of a fingertip, soft-bodied, capable of independent locomotion but preferring coalition. They aggregate into functional shapes — shapes that walk, shapes that swim, shapes that graze on hummock-surface, shapes that climb post-trees. The shapes are purposeful. A grazing-shape has a broad, flat underside and rasping mouth-parts at its leading edge. A swimming-shape is streamlined, with cilia-fringes. A climbing-shape has multiple gripping limbs and distributes its weight intelligently.

But the shapes are *temporary*. A coalition holds for minutes, sometimes hours, then disaggregates — the units separate, scatter across the root-mat like spilled beads, and soon begin forming new coalitions. A grazer disaggregates and its components join a swimmer and a climber and something that has no clear function, something that simply sits on a hummock and pulses. The transition is fast and wet — it sounds like a handful of gravel dropped into mud, a splattering dissolution, followed by the soft clickings and squelches of reassembly nearby.

This is happening everywhere. All the time. The landscape is full of these dissolutions and reconstitutions. From any vantage point you can see dozens of aggregate organisms going about their business — and every few minutes, one of them *comes apart*, and within seconds, new ones form from the dispersed units mixed with units from other recent disaggregations. No individual persists. No identity carries over. The units themselves may be immortal — may have been cycling through these temporary bodies since the plateau reached its current state — but the organisms they form are as ephemeral as weather.

It is loud. Not deafening, but loud in the way a forest full of frogs is loud: a constant, layered, wet sound of biological activity. The disaggregation-squelch. The reassembly-click. The grazing-rasp. The swimming-slosh. The breathing of the plateau beneath it all, that deep tidal groan. And from the post-trees, an irregular percussion: when spore-clouds pass through the membrane-canopies, the membranes vibrate and produce a low thrumming, almost tonal, almost musical, that reverberates across the open spaces.

## The Technology

There are no buildings. There are no roads. There are no artifacts of any recognizable civilization. But there is *industry*.

Deep beneath the root-mat — you can hear it if you press your ear to the surface, if you can tolerate the cold wet — there is a mechanical rhythm. Regular. Metallic. Not biological. Something down there is operating. Pistons, or something that functions like pistons. A slow, steady cycling. It is tempting to think this is the source of the plateau's breathing, but the rhythms don't sync up — the mechanical cycling is faster, more regular, while the breathing is slow and organic and variable. They are two systems occupying the same space.

Occasionally, the root-mat splits — not from the breathing, but from something pushing upward. A vent opens. It is perfectly circular, edged with scorched root-fiber, and from it rises a column of warm air that smells of iron and ozone. The warm air carries droplets of something that is not water — something slightly viscous, slightly iridescent — that falls on the surrounding root-mat and is immediately absorbed. The vent stays open for an hour, maybe two, then closes as the root-mat grows over it with startling speed.

The aggregation-units are drawn to the vents when they open. They gather in large numbers, disaggregating from whatever shapes they were holding, flowing across the mat as individual units toward the warm updraft. They don't enter the vent. They ring its edge and seem to *feed* — absorbing the iridescent droplets, vibrating rapidly, occasionally emitting a high-pitched tone that is the sharpest sound in this landscape. Then the vent closes and they scatter and re-aggregate and go on.

What is down there? The root-mat is meters thick, possibly tens of meters. Below it, the ancient eroded bedrock of the plateau. And below that, or within it — something that runs. Something that cycles. Something that was perhaps built, long ago, or grew into a mechanical process through some pathway that this world permits and others don't. It feeds the surface. The surface does not acknowledge it except by eating what it offers.

## The Wrongness

The plateau is stable. It has been stable for an immensity of time. But stability here does not mean stillness — it means *dynamic equilibrium in a zone where the rules are soft*. The breathing. The disaggregation. The vents. The light that is alive. These are not anomalies within a normal system; they are the normal system of a place where certain physical constraints have worn away along with the rock.

Erosion here has not only sculpted the terrain. It has sculpted *reality*. Whatever laws once held rigid here have been ground down by time just as the mountains were ground down to this plateau. Conservation of individual identity — eroded. Fixed spatial geometry — eroded (the horizon still breathes). The boundary between biological and mechanical — eroded. The distinction between light and organism — eroded.

This is what maximum erosion looks like when it applies to everything: not destruction, but *softening*. The hard edges of physics have become soft edges. Things bleed into each other. A plant becomes an animal becomes a swarm becomes a plant. The underground engine becomes the surface ecosystem becomes the atmosphere becomes the light. The plateau is one system wearing many masks, and the masks dissolve and reform like the aggregate creatures that walk its surface.

It is cold. It is wet. It is dim and open and full of quiet, ceaseless turbulence. Nothing here is violent — every transition is gentle, every disaggregation a soft unraveling, every reassembly a patient clicking-together. But the rate of change is extraordinary. Stand still for ten minutes and the landscape around you has reorganized: new creatures, new channel-patterns, a hummock that wasn't there before (or was it? the horizon shifted), a post-tree whose membranes have changed color from translucent to deep amber.

The cold presses in. The spore-light drifts. Somewhere nearby, a grazer the size of a dog comes apart with a sound like a slow, wet sigh, and its components slide away across the mat in a dozen directions, each one finding new purpose in a new shape that is already assembling itself from the remnants of other shapes, and the whole plateau turns over once more in its ancient, endless, gentle turbulence, and nothing is lost, and nothing persists, and the engine below cycles on.
