# The Thrumming Dark

---

The plateau extends in every direction farther than a mind wants to hold. Hundreds of miles of worn basalt, scoured into shallow bowls and long gentle troughs by wind and time — deep time, the kind that rounds everything, that takes the ambition out of mountains and leaves behind these long resigned surfaces, dark stone rubbed to the texture of worked leather. The rock is warm. Not from any sun — there is no sun visible, hasn't been, won't be, the sky a low unbroken vault of cloud so thick and so permanent it has become the ceiling of the world rather than weather passing through it. The warmth comes from below. The plateau is thin-skinned over something restless. Put your hand flat to the stone and you feel it: a vibration so low it's more pressure than sound, the idling of some engine that is either geological or not.

That's the first thing about this place. The thrumming.

It is faint. You stop noticing it for long stretches, the way you stop noticing your own heartbeat. Then it shifts — a quarter-tone lower, or a fraction louder — and the awareness rushes back, and for a moment the entire plateau feels like the deck of something. A vessel. A machine the size of a continent, running in the dark.

---

There are no buildings. No roads, no paths, no cairns, no marks of any deliberate hand on any surface. But the thrumming is not natural — or if it is natural, it belongs to a nature that has incorporated industry into its geology, the way certain planets might fold metallic hydrogen into their mantles and call it done. Somewhere beneath this basalt, or threaded through it in ways the eye can't parse, there are processes running at a scale that renders the question of *built or grown* irrelevant. Occasionally — rarely — a fissure in the rock reveals a cross-section: the basalt gives way to something laminated, layer on layer of stone and carbon and something that glints like graphite but is too regular, too evenly spaced. It looks like circuitry. It looks like sedimentary rock. It looks like both because it *is* both, or because the distinction collapsed here long ago, settled into equivalence the way the mountains settled into plateau.

There is no factory. There is no visible machine. There is only the hum, the warmth, and the occasional fissure that suggests the continent itself is the machine, or that the machine became the continent, or that something down there has been running so long it has become its own landscape.

---

The dark is not absolute but very nearly so. The cloud cover is totalized, a smothering lid that diffuses whatever light source exists beyond it — distant star, binary reflection, something else — into a faint ambient grayness that sits just above the threshold of vision. You can see, but only the way you see in a room lit by a single coal: shapes emerge slowly, edges are suggestions, depth is a matter of faith. The basalt is dark. The sky is dark. The distinction between them at the horizon is a slightly different weight of darkness, stone-dark against cloud-dark, and your eyes learn to read it or they don't.

In this gloom, the moisture matters more than it would elsewhere. The air is warm and close — not humid exactly, but *damp*, the way a cave is damp. Condensation forms on the stone where the rising warmth from below meets the cooler strata of air pooling in the shallow troughs. It collects in thin skins of water, never more than a finger's width deep, sprawling across the basalt in irregular sheets that reflect the almost-nothing of the sky and double it. These pools are warm. Some of them are body-temperature warm. They evaporate and reform on no schedule you can predict, governed by fluctuations in the thrumming below. After what might be rain — a fine directionless mist that saturates the air for hours and then stops — the pools swell and connect into temporary networks, shallow runnels following the ancient erosion channels, and for a little while there is the sound of water moving. Then it stills. Then it sinks or lifts back into the air, and the stone is merely damp again, glistening faintly in the non-light.

---

Now: the animals.

They are everywhere, and they are the primary presence on the plateau, and they move through the dark with the ease of things that have never known anything else.

The most common are low-slung and many-legged, somewhere between centipede and salamander in general architecture, their bodies broad and flat to maximize contact with the warm stone. They are dark-skinned — everything here is dark-skinned or dark-shelled or dark-furred — and they navigate by vibration, by the feel of the thrumming through their bellies, reading the plateau the way a sailor reads the sea by the behavior of the deck beneath him. They graze on films of chemotrophic bacteria that grow in the condensation pools, their wide mouths sweeping the wet stone clean in slow passes. They number in the thousands across any given square mile, but the plateau is so vast and they are so quiet that you encounter them one or two at a time, sudden flat shapes at your feet, wet backs catching a faint gleam before they trundle on.

Above them in the food web are the stalkers — taller, fewer, their bodies held up off the stone on stiff legs like herons. They are patient beyond any mammalian understanding of the word. They stand motionless for what might be hours, one foot slightly raised, feeling the vibrations in the rock through their remaining three contact points, triangulating the movement of the grazers in the dark. When they strike it is fast and almost silent — a snap, a brief wet sound, and then the slow mechanical process of feeding, which takes longer than the hunt by an order of magnitude.

There are flyers too, though they don't fly so much as drift — broad-winged things that catch the thermal updrafts rising off the warmer fissure zones and glide for miles without effort, hanging in the dark sky like black kites. Their calls, when they make them, are the loudest sounds on the plateau: a single low whistle, pitched to carry across the flat expanse, spaced minutes or hours apart. It is a lonely sound. It fits the place.

In the condensation pools, where the water persists longest and deepest — there are depressions where it gathers a handspan or two deep and holds — something else lives. Colonies of soft-bodied things, translucent, vaguely luminous with a bioluminescence so faint it registers more as a memory of light than light itself. They pulse. They are the only visual rhythm on the plateau, these slow irregular pulses in the pools, and they draw both the grazers (who eat them) and the stalkers (who eat the grazers who come to eat them) into loose, shifting congregations around the deeper water. These are the closest thing to ecosystemic nodes — gathering points, interaction zones — in an otherwise enormously dispersed biology.

There are larger things too. You hear them sometimes — the scrape of something heavy shifting its weight across stone, far off in the dark. The grazers freeze when this sound comes. The stalkers fold their legs and lower themselves flat. Whatever makes that sound is rare and large and commands a respect that looks like terror. You don't see it. Not in this dark, not at this distance. You feel its footfall in the rock, a heavier note laid over the thrumming, and then it passes, and the plateau slowly exhales, and the grazers resume their slow sweeping of the pools.

---

The vegetation is there but marginal, an afterthought of life on a world that found other chemistries more viable. Tough lichenous growths crust the older stone, feeding on mineral leach and the faint chemical exhaust of whatever processes run below. In the erosion channels where moisture lingers longest, something like moss — dark, dense, almostite in texture — forms low mats that the grazers sometimes browse. There are no trees. There are no grasses. There is a filamentous growth that colonizes the edges of the warmer fissures, strands of it waving slowly in the convective updraft like hair underwater, and it is the tallest plant on the plateau, reaching perhaps a foot in height. The grazers avoid it. It may be toxic, or simply unpalatable. It is the only thing here that moves continuously — always waving, always stirred by the rising warmth — and in the deep gloom it can look, for a disorienting moment, like a crowd of tiny figures gathered at the edge of a vent, gesturing.

---

The quiet is the thing. The quiet is the medium everything else exists in.

The thrumming doesn't count as sound — it's too constant, too low, too much a property of the ground itself. What remains is an immense hush across an immense space. The wet sounds of the grazers. The occasional sharp snap of a stalker's strike. The whistle of the drifting flyers, spaced so far apart it startles you each time. The faint ticking of condensation forming and dropping. The distant scrape of the large unseen things.

Between these sounds: nothing. A silence so wide it has texture, the way very dark spaces have texture — not empty but *spacious*, filled with the potential of sound and the specific quality of its absence. The plateau is too flat and too vast for echoes. Sounds happen and are absorbed by the distance. They don't return. This gives every noise a quality of finality, of having been spoken once into an audience of stone and dark and never repeated.

---

What is this place?

It is a high plateau on a world that keeps its own counsel. It is warm because something beneath it — something immense and operational and patient — generates heat as a byproduct of processes that have been running since before the biology above adapted to them. It is dark because the sky is sealed. It is alive because life will colonize anything, even the surface of a sleeping engine, even the condensation that forms on the skin of a continent-sized machine that may or may not know it is a machine.

The animals don't know. They graze. They hunt. They drift on thermals above a landscape that hums. They have built their entire ecology on the rhythms of something they cannot comprehend, the way deep-sea organisms build their lives around hydrothermal vents without understanding plate tectonics. The thrumming is their seasons. Its fluctuations determine where the condensation forms, where the bacteria bloom, where the grazers gather, where the stalkers wait. When the hum shifts — and it does shift, in long slow cycles that might map to something purposeful or might be mere mechanical drift — the entire ecosystem migrates, flowing across the basalt like a slow dark tide, following the warmth, following the water, following the vibration that tells them where the living is good.

Nothing here was built for them. Nothing here was built at all, in any sense they could recognize. And yet they are utterly of this place, shaped by it, integrated into its patterns so deeply that to remove them would be to remove a layer of the plateau itself — the living layer, the sensing layer, the layer that responds and adapts and fills the silence with its small careful sounds.

The plateau extends. The dark holds. Beneath everything, the engine — or the geology, or the thing that is both — continues its work, whatever that work is, and the warmth rises through the stone, and the water forms and gathers and evaporates and forms again, and the grazers sweep the pools, and the stalkers wait, and somewhere in the far dark something enormous shifts its weight and moves on, and the silence closes behind it like water.
