# The Hollow Garden

---

You are beneath the ground, but there is no ground.

What was once earth—what should still be earth, by any honest reckoning—has become something else. At some point, in some era that left no records, the roots won. Not in the way roots usually win, slowly cracking stone over centuries, threading patient fingers through soil. This was different. This was a *replacement*. The mineral substrate surrendered its identity entirely, was eaten and reconstituted, and now there is only the living mass, all the way down.

You stand inside a cavity roughly the size of a chapel nave, twenty meters long and perhaps eight wide, the ceiling arching overhead at its highest point to maybe five meters. Every surface—floor, walls, ceiling—is woven root. Not dead wood. Not dried fiber. Living tissue, warm and faintly damp with its own metabolism, pale where the outer bark has worn smooth and darkly arterial where it hasn't. The roots range from the thickness of a human torso to threads fine as embroidery silk, and they interlock in patterns that are almost—*almost*—geometric. Not quite. There's a grammar to how they weave but it's a grammar that keeps revising itself mid-sentence, producing structures that rhyme without repeating. Some of the thickest roots pulse, slowly, on a rhythm that takes about forty seconds per cycle—a faint swelling and subsiding you can see if you hold still, like watching someone breathe in their sleep.

The cavity is largely empty. That's what strikes you first and keeps striking you. For all the overwhelming density of the living walls, the interior is a void. A held breath. There are a few features—a place where roots descend from the ceiling in a loose curtain, another where the floor humps upward in a knee-high mound of interwoven fibers—but mostly it is open air, warm air, air that tastes green and faintly sweet, like the smell of sap heated just below boiling. The emptiness feels deliberate. Not abandoned—*reserved*. As though the roots grew around this space on purpose, or as though the space itself exerts some quiet pressure that keeps them at bay.

---

There is light here, and it should not exist.

It comes from the roots themselves—not bioluminescence exactly, not that cold bacterial glow. This is warmer, more saturated, closer to late-afternoon sunlight filtered through honey. Certain roots, seemingly at random, emit a steady amber radiance from within, as though their heartwood has been replaced with captured daylight. The effect is of a room lit by dozens of irregular, organic lanterns embedded in every surface. Shadows are soft and multiple. Your own shadow fans out in six or seven pale copies that shift as the luminous roots cycle through barely perceptible fluctuations in brightness. The light is strong enough to read by, strong enough to see colors clearly—the cream and ochre and wine-dark red of the root surfaces, the occasional shock of new growth where a tendril has pushed through, still viridian and glossy with the waxy sheen of youth.

No one has explained the light. No one can. The roots are photosynthesizing *in reverse*, or sideways, or in some direction that the word photosynthesis doesn't have room for. They take in no sunlight—there is none to take, this far below whatever surface might theoretically exist above—and yet they produce it. Energy is being created or borrowed or stolen from somewhere, and the debt, if there is one, is not being paid in any currency the physics of the surface world would recognize.

---

Now: the sound.

It is constant, layered, enormous for such a small space. The roots are growing. You can *hear* them growing. Not the way you hear grass grow in the old expression—this is not subtle, not a metaphor for patience. This is a sound like wet rope being twisted, like green wood splitting under pressure, like knuckles cracking in sequence across a thousand hands. The walls are in motion. Slowly, imperceptibly to the eye at any given moment, but acoustically undeniable. Fibers are extending, cells dividing, new tissue shouldering into existence against the resistance of existing tissue. The sound layers over itself: a low, pervasive creak that serves as a kind of basso continuo, overlaid with sharper snaps and pops as individual root-tips breach or bend, and above it all a breathy, almost vocal hiss that seems to be the sound of sap moving through the vascular network at speeds and pressures that would be lethal in an animal circulatory system.

There is also, intermittently, a sound like thunder.

It comes from deep below—deeper than this cavity, deeper than you want to think about—and when it arrives, the entire chamber flexes. The walls contract inward by a centimeter, maybe two, and then relax. The luminous roots flare brighter for a heartbeat. The air pressure shifts, and your ears pop, and for a moment the sweet green smell intensifies to something almost narcotic before fading back to its baseline. This happens every few minutes, irregularly. It is not seismic. It is biological. Something down there is *happening*—a root mass of incomprehensible scale is adjusting, or dividing, or consuming something that briefly resists being consumed—and these are the reverberations.

---

The cavity is young. You can tell because the edges are still sharp—where root meets root, the joins are clean and defined rather than blurred by centuries of secondary growth. The mound on the floor still shows individual fiber-bundles rather than the smooth, fused surfaces of the older walls. New tendrils emerge from the ceiling and walls at a rate you can almost track in real time: pale, questing, wet with their own internal moisture, reaching into the empty space and then—curiously—stopping. Curling back. Withdrawing, as though they've touched something invisible and found it unpleasant. The cavity is actively being *maintained*. Something about this emptiness is being defended, even as the surrounding biomass pushes relentlessly inward.

What moisture exists here is autogenic—the roots sweat. Condensation beads on the cooler surfaces where two different vascular networks meet and create temperature differentials. In the lowest point of the floor, where the root-weave dips into a shallow basin, a small amount of liquid has collected—maybe a liter, maybe two. It is not water. It is too viscous, too warm, faintly luminous itself with a greenish-gold cast. Touch it and it feels like water that has been given one additional property—a very slight resistance, as though it's thinking about whether to let your finger in. It is sterile. It is alive. These two facts are both true and should not be compatible, and yet.

---

Fauna: almost none. But not absolute none.

There is something in the upper reaches of the cavity that you will not see directly. You will see its effect—a place where the luminous roots dim when it passes nearby, a faint movement in your peripheral vision that resolves into stillness when you turn to look. It is small, perhaps the size of a clenched fist, and it moves through the root matrix itself, navigating passages in the weave that seem too tight for anything to traverse. Twice during your time here you will hear it make a sound: a single, high, clear note, like someone running a wet finger around the rim of a glass. The roots near the sound will shudder and go briefly dark. Then the light returns, and the sound has moved to the far end of the chamber.

It is not an insect. It is not a mammal. It may not be fauna at all, in any meaningful taxonomic sense. It may be a mobile node of the root system itself—a fragment that has learned to detach and reattach, a scout or a white blood cell or a thought moving through a brain that is all cortex and no skull. You will not catch it. You will leave this place uncertain whether it was ever actually there.

---

Here is where reality begins to lose its confidence.

The cavity has more interior volume than it should. You can tell because you have walked its length three times now, and each time the count of your paces has been different—seventeen, twenty-three, nineteen—even though you are careful, even though you are deliberate. The far wall is not always the same distance away. The ceiling is sometimes higher when you are not looking at it; you become aware of this because the echo of the root-growth sounds changes, opens up, as though the space has briefly yawned into something much larger when your attention was elsewhere. Then you look up and it is close again, five meters, intimate, a chapel.

There is a place on the left wall where the root pattern forms a whorl, a spiral of interlocking fibers that tightens toward a center point. If you stare at the center point, two things happen simultaneously: the sound of growth intensifies to something that borders on music—a rhythmic, almost melodic sequence of creaks and snaps that repeats with variations, like a theme being explored—and your sense of depth perception inverts. The wall becomes a tunnel. Not metaphorically. Not as an optical illusion. The roots are extending inward, backward, into a space that the wall does not have room to contain, and at the far end of this impossible passage there is more light, different light, bluer and colder, and the sound of something that might be wind or might be breathing on a scale that makes the thunder-pulses from below seem trivial.

Look away and the wall is a wall again. But the blue light lingers at the edge of your vision for minutes afterward, and the sweet green air tastes different in the spot where you were standing—sharper, thinner, as though some of it has been replaced by air from somewhere else.

The root-fluid in the basin on the floor has opinions about direction. Pour some out on the slightly sloped surface and it will flow uphill roughly forty percent of the time. Not always. Not consistently enough to establish a rule. Just often enough to make you distrust your understanding of which way "down" is. And once you begin distrusting that, you notice that the warm air in the chamber circulates in patterns that don't correspond to any thermal logic—eddying around the empty center in slow spirals that reverse without cause, pooling in corners that aren't geometrically corners, rising and falling with no reference to the ceiling or floor.

---

Something made this space. Not built it—there are no tool marks, no evidence of cutting or shaping. But the primitive intelligence that pervades the root system has *intended* this cavity. It is a pocket that the vast, world-replacing organism has carved inside itself, the way a body forms a cyst around something foreign. And the foreignness is the emptiness itself. The void at the center of the chapel is the foreign body. The roots recoil from it not because it is toxic but because it is *nothing*, and the organism that has replaced all mineral matter for unknowable depths in every direction does not have a concept for nothing. It has grown around the absence the way a tree grows around a nail, incorporating it, containing it, studying it with the slow vegetable patience of something that has all the time that time contains.

The light pulses. The roots creak and sing. The thunder rolls up from below and the walls breathe. The unseen thing moves through its passages, sounding its single crystal note. And at the center of the chamber, in the warm, sweet, over-lit emptiness, something that is not quite a pocket of air and not quite a hole in the world sits quietly, being nothing, being studied, being held.

You are standing inside a thought that a plant is having about the concept of absence.

It hasn't finished thinking it yet.
