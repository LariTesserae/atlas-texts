# The Canopy Plateau

---

The first thing is the sound. Before you understand where you are, before your eyes adjust to the blazing whiteness of the sky, the sound has already claimed you. It is the sound of water and wind and ten thousand leaves, and it is enormous.

You are standing on a high plateau — but you would not know this from looking down, because there is no rock beneath your feet. There is only moss, and beneath the moss there are roots, and beneath the roots there are more roots, and somewhere far below that there may be stone, but no one living has confirmed this. The ground is a living thing. It gives slightly when you shift your weight. It is cool and damp and it breathes.

---

The plateau rises above a cloud layer that you can see in every direction — a floor of slow white vapor sitting maybe two hundred meters below, filling whatever valleys exist in whatever mountain range this is. Up here, you are exposed to the full unfiltered sky, and the light is ferocious. Not hot — the air is cool, almost cold against your wet skin, carrying that particular thin crispness of altitude — but the light itself is relentless. A high-altitude brightness that flattens shadows and makes every surface almost painful in its clarity. The sky is not blue so much as white-blue, the sun a diffuse furnace behind a thin, luminous haze that amplifies rather than dampens. Everything gleams. Water gleams. The leaves gleam. The moss gleams with an interior light, each tiny frond a wet jewel.

And the water is everywhere. Not pooled, not flowing in rivers — that would require channels, erosion, carved paths, and this land is too new for that. Instead, the water simply *is*. It weeps upward through the moss. It condenses on every leaf surface. It hangs in the air as a fine, bright mist that never quite resolves into rain. The root-mass that forms the ground is saturated, and when you press your boot down, water wells up around it, clear and faintly sweet-smelling. There are places where slight depressions in the canopy floor have allowed shallow lenses of standing water to form — not ponds, not yet, just bright mirrors a centimeter deep and a few meters across, perfectly still, reflecting that enormous white sky. They have no outlets. They go nowhere. They are the earliest possible suggestion of hydrology — the first sketches of what might, in a thousand years, become streams.

Because that is the essential fact of this place: it is young. Not in the way forests are young — saplings, pioneer species, secondary growth — but in a deeper, geological sense. The plateau itself is still forming. You can feel it. The root-mass beneath your feet is actively growing, expanding, thickening year by year as the vegetation claims more vertical space. What might have been bare rock a century ago is now buried under meters of accumulated growth, a living substrate that is building its own terrain. The plants are not growing *on* the land. The plants *are* the land. They are constructing it.

---

The dominant organism — if you can call a single species dominant when it constitutes the ground, the walls, and the ceiling of reality — is something between a moss and a tree. A bryophyte that has forgotten its scale. Close to the ground, it forms the dense, wet carpet you stand on: emerald and lime and a dark, almost black green where the deepest layers compact into peat-like mass. But it also sends up vertical structures — not trunks exactly, more like dense braids of root and stem that twist upward to a height of twelve or fifteen meters, then explode into canopies of broad, wet, light-hungry leaves. These columns are spaced widely apart. That is what gives the plateau its strange character: the openness. You can see a long way between them. The landscape reads as a bright, soaked meadow punctuated by these organic towers, and beyond them, more meadow, more towers, the whole system extending to the horizon in every direction — or to where the plateau edge drops away into cloud.

The spacing is generous, maybe forty or fifty meters between each column, and between them there is only the moss-floor, the shallow water-lenses, and the overwhelming sound.

The sound. It comes from the leaves. Each canopy is enormous — a wide, shaggy parasol of leaves that are designed, evolved, or simply inclined to catch moisture from the air. They are broad and slightly concave and they collect condensation and then shed it, constantly, in fat drops that strike the lower leaves, which shed their own drops onto lower leaves still, and the whole system is an unending percussion of water falling through layers of green. One tree is a gentle patter. Ten trees are a rainstorm. The hundreds you can see from any vantage point are a wall of sound, a roar, a continuous white-noise thunder of water-on-leaf-on-leaf-on-moss that fills the air so completely you stop hearing it as sound and begin experiencing it as pressure. It is strangely uniform. There are no gusts to create surges, no pauses, no rhythm — just an enormous, textured hiss that sits on the world like weather.

And layered into this: the wind. Not strong, but constant at this altitude, a cool steady current that moves through the canopies and sets the leaves turning and clapping, adding a sharper, more staccato layer to the water-roar. The wind carries mist. It carries the smell of chlorophyll and wet calcium and something faintly metallic — the altitude itself, maybe, or some mineral the roots are pulling from whatever stone lies far below.

---

If you walk — and walking is slow here, the moss-floor uneven and soft, your feet sinking to the ankle in places — you will eventually come across the machines.

They are scattered across the plateau like afterthoughts. Corroded housings of bolted steel, half-swallowed by moss. Pipe systems that emerge from the living ground at one point and re-enter it at another, running for thirty or forty meters across the surface, sweating with condensation. A pump house — you can hear its engine beneath the water-roar if you press your ear to the wall, a low diesel chug, patient and ancient-sounding — with a roof that has become a garden, moss and ferns and a small version of the canopy-trees growing from the accumulated soil of decades. The pump house is extracting something or injecting something; you cannot tell which. A pipe runs from it and vanishes into the root-mass.

There are others. A lattice tower, maybe twenty meters tall, made of angle-iron and rust, supporting a wind-speed instrument that still turns. Its base is wrapped in a thick sleeve of roots that are slowly, slowly pulling it into the living floor. A small concrete shed with no door, containing a diesel generator that is clearly operational — there is heat shimmer above its exhaust pipe, and a faint oily smell — though no one seems to be tending it. Cables run from the generator into the moss, to destinations unknown. A flat area where the moss has been cut back — recently, perhaps within the season — revealing the dark, fibrous cross-section of the living ground, almost a meter deep, layered like sedimentary rock but made of compressed roots and stems and centuries of accumulated growth. Some kind of coring or sampling operation. There are steel stakes marking a grid. A tarpaulin held down by stones covers something that might be equipment.

These installations have no connective logic you can discern. There are no roads between them, no paths. Each one sits in its own clearing — or rather, each one *had* a clearing once, and the clearing is closing. The vegetation is reclaiming the spaces around them with a patience that looks, from a human timescale, like stillness, but the evidence is clear: bolt-heads disappearing under moss, pipe joints sealed in root tissue, walls becoming trellises. The machines are being composted. They are being incorporated into the living geology of the plateau, becoming nodes in the root-mass, their metals slowly entering the chemistry of the system.

And yet some of them are still running. That is the uncanny thing. The pump still chugs. The generator still hums. Whatever they were built to do, they are still doing it, unattended, slowly being digested by the land they were installed to study or exploit or maintain. You wonder if the plateau's growth was aided by them — if the pumps helped saturate the root-mass, if the generators powered growing-lights during some early phase of cultivation — and now the cultivated thing has outgrown the cultivator but hasn't quite finished with the tools.

---

Animals are scarce. You might see a raptor — something broad-winged and pale — riding the updraft at the plateau's edge, where the warm air from the cloud-valleys below hits the cold lip of the moss-cliff and kicks upward. It will circle for a long time without flapping. You will not see what it hunts; whatever small things live in the moss-floor are invisible, felt rather than seen, implied by the raptor's patience. Once, crossing a water-lens, you might notice a tremor beneath the surface — something like a tadpole, something larval, a flicker of dark movement in a centimeter of water. It's gone before you can focus. The plateau is not devoid of animal life, but it is dominated so completely by the vegetative system that fauna feels provisional, marginal, tolerated rather than invited.

Insects may exist in the deeper layers of the moss. You never see them. You sometimes feel a prickling on the back of your hand that might be a mite or might be condensation.

---

Walk far enough in any direction and you reach the edge. This is where the plateau reveals its altitude and its youth most dramatically. The moss-floor does not taper off gradually. It *ends* — a ragged, advancing lip of root-mass that extends out over empty air like a shelf of peat hanging over a cliff. Below, the rock face is visible for the first time: pale granite, new-looking, barely weathered, with vertical fracture planes that are sharp and clean. This is fresh mountain. It was pushed up recently — in geological terms — and the water has not yet had time to carve it, the frost has not yet had time to crack it, the rivers that will someday cut valleys into its flanks do not yet exist. The vegetation arrived before the erosion. The moss colonized the summit while the stone was still raw, and now it is building soil faster than the weather can sculpt landforms. The plateau is a race between biology and geology, and biology is winning.

Standing at the edge — carefully, because the moss overhang is unreliable, and the drop is immense, hundreds of meters into white cloud — you can see the face of the cliff below, and it is not bare. Tendrils of root hang down from the lip. Green stains mark where the moss is beginning to colonize downward. Condensation from the cloud layer keeps the rock face perpetually wet, and wherever it is wet, something is starting to grow. Given time, the entire mountain will be sheathed. The plateau is just the beginning — the beachhead of a living system that intends to cover everything.

The sound is different here at the edge. The water-roar of the canopy-trees is behind you, still enormous, but now there is also the wind coming up the cliff face, a deeper, more resonant note, a bass hum beneath the treble of falling water. The two sounds together are almost orchestral. They are certainly deafening. You could shout and not hear yourself. You could clap your hands and feel the impact in your palms but register nothing in your ears except the world's own noise.

---

The light, at the edge, is even more intense. The cloud layer below acts as a reflector, bouncing sunlight back up, so that the plateau is illuminated from both above and below — a condition that the canopy-trees have exploited, evolving leaves on their lower branches that face downward, photosynthesizing from the reflected light. Everything is doubly lit. Your shadow is faint and doubled. The water-lenses on the moss-floor sometimes catch the light from below and throw shimmering patterns onto the undersides of the canopy leaves, and the leaves throw green-filtered light back down, and the whole system becomes a slowly oscillating hall of mirrors, cool and damp and blindingly bright.

You are cold. Your clothes are saturated. The moss has soaked your boots through. The air temperature is perhaps eight or nine degrees, and the moisture makes it feel colder. But the light is warm on your face when you turn toward it, and the contrast — cold wet feet, warm bright face — is one of those sensory contradictions that makes a place feel more real than it should, more present than your daily life, as if the world has been turned up.

---

Somewhere, behind and below, a pump is still running. You cannot hear it. You know it's there because you passed it, because you put your hand against its housing and felt the vibration, the faithful mechanical heartbeat of something installed by people who have not returned. The plateau is growing over it. The plateau is growing over everything. It is building itself upward and outward, a living landmass assembling its own geology from water and light and the thin mineral soup it pulls from the stone, and it is loud, and it is bright, and it is cold, and it is very, very patient.
