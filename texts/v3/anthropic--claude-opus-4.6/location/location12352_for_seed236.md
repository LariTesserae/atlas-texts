# The Hanging Village of Dry Moss

---

There is a place where a ridge of young stone — pale, sharp-edged, still carrying the mineral smell of its own emergence — rises like the spine of something vast and recently dead. The ridge is not tall in the way mountains are tall, but it is elevated enough that on clear days the air thins slightly, enough to notice in the lungs, enough to make sounds carry differently, which is to say: they don't carry at all. Something about this altitude, this particular fold of rock, eats echoes. The silence is not empty. It is *occupied*. It has texture, like felt pressed against the ear.

The ridge broke through the surface — geologists might say decades ago, or centuries, but it feels recent. The edges of the stone are still angular, unchewed by weather. Faces of fractured basalt show crystal patterns that haven't yet dulled. Where sheets of rock split apart and tilted, they left behind shelves and slots and narrow vertical chimneys — a geometry of fresh ruin, as though a vast piece of slate had been dropped from a height and its shards arranged themselves into something almost deliberate.

There is no water here. Not in pools, not in streams, not even in the memory of streams — no grooves, no rounded stones, no mineral staining that suggests ancient flow. The air is warm and dry, carrying with it the faintest trace of sulfur from whatever deep process is still pushing the ridge slowly, slowly upward. If you licked the stone, it would taste of chalk and iron. Your lips would crack within an hour.

And yet.

---

Everything is growing.

The moss came first, or something the locals call moss, though it behaves more like lichen that has forgotten its boundaries. It is gray-green and dove-brown and, in the shaded crevices, a deep arterial red. It does not need water — or rather, it has found something else. It drinks the mineral seep, the faint chemical exhalation of the young stone itself, metabolizing calcium and silicate and slow-leaking gases in place of rain. It grows in thick, felted mats that hang from the overhanging rock shelves like curtains, like drying pelts, like something between textile and organ. Where two mats grow close enough to touch, they fuse, forming canopies and bridges and hammock-like slings between pillars of stone.

From the moss, everything else follows.

Plants have rooted into the moss mats themselves, never touching the stone at all. They are succulent, small-leaved things, olive and amber, their stems swollen with whatever moisture they can hoard from the mineral chemistry below them. Some have developed pale, almost translucent leaves that seem to photosynthesize reluctantly, as though light were a secondary concern. Their roots are shallow and spread wide, knitting themselves into the moss like thread through felt. In places where the moss is oldest and thickest — perhaps three or four hand-spans deep — small bushes have taken hold, their branches knotted and horizontal, growing outward rather than upward, heavy with tiny seed-pods that are the dull gold of old brass.

The vegetation is dense in the way a tapestry is dense: layered, interwoven, occupying every surface but constrained to surfaces. The stone walls and shelves and chimneys of the ridge are upholstered in living material. You can push your hand into the moss and feel warmth — not from the sun, which is ordinary here, a pale gold disc in a hazy sky — but from the slow metabolic heat of the growth itself, the chemical work of a thousand organisms converting rock into life without a single drop of water to mediate the transaction.

---

The village is *inside* this.

It isn't large. A hamlet. A cluster. Maybe thirty structures, if you can call them structures, built into the shelves and slots of the ridge over what might be three or four generations. The building material is the moss itself — dried moss, which becomes rigid and surprisingly strong, like a cross between cork and hardite. Panels of it are cut from the thickest mats and fitted into the natural gaps in the rock, creating walls where the stone left none. Roofs are often just the living moss overhead, still growing, still slowly thickening, so that the ceiling of a dwelling is alive and warm to the touch and occasionally drops a seed-pod onto the floor with a soft *tok*.

The construction is organized in the way that something organic is organized — not grid-like, not planned from above, but following the logic of the available space with a consistency that suggests shared understanding. Doorways are arched, because the dried moss holds an arch well. Floors are layered: stone, then dried moss, then a woven mat of the amber-leaved plant stems. The interiors smell of chalk and something faintly sweet, like dried fruit, which is the smell of the moss's chemistry at work.

There are no tools visible. No metal, no ceramics, no glass. Everything is shaped by hand, by tearing and pressing and patient fitting — the moss, when freshly cut, is pliable enough to mold, and it holds whatever shape it dries in. Shelves are moss. Containers are moss. The few objects that might be called furniture are moss shaped over stone armatures and then left to cure. It is not primitive so much as *resolved* — a technology of one material, understood completely.

The people — and there are people, though you rarely see more than a few at a time — move through the village in near-silence. Their feet on the moss floors make no sound. Their clothing, which is also moss-fiber, does not rustle. When they speak to one another, which is seldom, they use a language that seems to be mostly mouthed, sub-vocalized, as though the silence of the ridge has become a condition they maintain out of respect or habit or something deeper. You might see a woman shaping a panel on a stone ledge, her hands working the material with a slow, kneading rhythm. You might see a man standing at the edge of a shelf, looking out over the haze below, perfectly still for long enough that you begin to wonder if he is part of the architecture.

---

The animals are the most alive things here, and even they are quiet.

In the moss mats, small creatures move — things like beetles but softer, their carapaces flexible and velvety, colored to match the moss they inhabit. Red ones in the red moss. Gray-green ones on the outer surfaces. They eat the moss and are eaten in turn by something like a gecko — flat-bodied, wide-toed, moving in sudden darts across the vertical surfaces and then freezing, perfectly camouflaged, for minutes at a time. The geckos have no eyelids. Their eyes are dark and wet and constantly watching.

In the gaps between the stone pillars, small flocking things — not quite birds, not quite bats — roost in clusters, their bodies wrapped in membranes that are the same texture as the dried moss panels of the village walls. They don't fly so much as glide, launching from high points and spiraling slowly down through the warm, still air, catching the tiny insects that drift up from the moss mats on thermals of metabolic heat. They are utterly silent. No calls, no wing-beats, just the faintest displacement of air as they pass.

Something larger lives in the deepest crevices of the ridge — you see evidence of it in the form of shed scales, each one the size of a thumbnail, translucent and faintly iridescent. The villagers collect these scales and use them as decoration, pressing them into wet moss panels to create patterns on the walls of certain dwellings — spirals, branching lines, shapes that might be maps or might be something more abstract. No one has seen the creature that sheds them, or if they have, they do not speak of it, which in this village means they do not even mouth the words.

---

The light here is ordinary but it behaves strangely against all the texture. The sun hits the outer moss surfaces and is absorbed completely — the mats are so dense and fibrous that they cast no glare, create no highlights. But in the deeper recesses, where the red moss grows, there is a faint luminescence, barely perceptible in daylight, that becomes more apparent as the sun moves behind the ridge in the afternoon. The red moss glows, dimly, with its own metabolic light — a warmth made visible. In the deepest interiors of the village, at dusk, the walls pulse with a color that is not quite red and not quite amber, a color that seems to have warmth as one of its properties, so that seeing it and feeling it are not entirely separate experiences.

The village changes, but slowly. A new shelf of stone tilts free from the ridge face every few years, opening fresh surfaces for the moss to colonize and the builders to inhabit. Old moss mats grow too thick, too heavy, and occasionally one detaches from its anchor point and slumps to the ground in a slow, silent avalanche of living material, bringing down a wall or blocking a passage. The villagers incorporate the fallen mass into new construction. Nothing is wasted. Nothing moves quickly.

At the highest point of the ridge, where the stone is newest and sharpest and no moss has yet taken hold, the rock is still warm from below. If you press your palm to it, you can feel the faintest vibration — not a tremor, not anything seismic, but a hum, a frequency below hearing, the sound of the earth still making this place. It is the only thing here that could be called a sound, and you feel it in your bones rather than hear it in your ears.

The silence holds everything else.
