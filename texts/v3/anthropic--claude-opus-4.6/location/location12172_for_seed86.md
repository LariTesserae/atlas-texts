# The Sunken Plain

Below the surface of the world, a basin stretches for miles — not a cave, not exactly, but a broad depression in the earth so gradual and so vast that you don't realize you've descended until you look back and see the rim of higher ground encircling you on every side, a dark lip against a dimmer sky. The land here sank a long time ago, or perhaps the land around it rose. Either way, the basin floor sits twenty, thirty feet below the surrounding terrain, enough that the wind passes over it, enough that the light arrives slantwise and reduced, enough that the air down here is cool and still and has been cool and still for longer than anything living can remember.

The ground is young. That's the strange thing. For all the basin's age — and it feels old, it feels like a place that has simply always been a shallow bowl in the earth — the rock beneath your feet is sharp and recent. Shelves of pale stone jut up at angles, fractured along clean planes. There are ridges no taller than your knee that nonetheless look like they split open yesterday, their edges not yet rounded by weather, their surfaces still showing the bright mineral grain of fresh breakage. Somewhere beneath this plain, some slow geological pressure continues to push new stone upward through the soil in thin plates and irregular fins, cracking the surface like ice on a puddle pressed from below. The fractures run for hundreds of yards in near-parallel lines, creating long shallow troughs between them where grit and soil collect. The stone is a pale calcium color, almost cream, darkening to gray where moisture has touched it.

And moisture does touch it, intermittently, without pattern. There is no rain falling now. There may not have been rain for days. But the air holds a faint dampness, a coolness that isn't just temperature but something you can feel on your skin, on the fine hairs of your arms. Condensation gathers in the troughs between the stone ridges — not pooling, not running, just a darkening of the soil, a slickness on the rock faces where the cold stone pulls water from the air overnight and holds it into the long dim hours of day. In a few places, shallow depressions hold actual water: palm-sized mirrors of it, gray and still, reflecting nothing but the low overcast above. A thin seep runs along one of the deeper fracture lines for perhaps a quarter mile before it vanishes into gravel. These pockets of moisture are the only water for a great distance in any direction, and everything alive down here knows it.

---

The vegetation is scarce and clings to what dampness there is. Along the seep, a few stands of something like pale rushes grow in sparse clusters, their stems thin and slightly translucent, as if they've never needed to develop pigment against strong sun. Between the stone ridges, in the troughs where grit accumulates, there are patches of a low ground-covering plant — not moss exactly, but something flat and fleshy that presses itself against the rock like a poultice, gray-green, its leaves no larger than thumbnails. Where the condensation is thickest, a handful of scrubby bushes have taken root, their branches bare and forked and reaching sideways rather than up, as though they gave up on the sky long ago. The bushes are widely spaced. Fifty yards might separate one from the next. They are the tallest things in the basin. Their wood is hard and pale and very slow-growing. If you broke a branch, you'd find the growth rings packed so tightly together you'd need a lens to count them.

Nothing here is lush. Nothing here is dying either. The vegetation exists in a state of permanent sufficiency — not thriving, not struggling, simply persisting, the way stone persists, the way the cool air persists. The basin does not change. The seasons, if they come, make little difference down here below the wind. The condensation comes each night. The seep runs its quarter mile and stops. The rushes stand in their clusters. The flat-leafed plant covers exactly as much ground as it covered last year and the year before that. There is an equilibrium so deep it barely registers as a condition — it is simply the way this place is, the way it has been, the way it will remain.

---

But the animals.

The animals are another matter entirely.

You hear them before you understand what you're hearing. A low, pervasive, overlapping sound — not deafening but *loud*, loud in the way that a crowd is loud, or a rookery, or a river of wings. It comes from everywhere across the basin at once, and it doesn't stop. It has no rhythm, no crescendo, no silence between phrases. It is a continuous, living noise: clicks, chirps, a dry rapid rattling, the scrape of claw on stone, the shuffle of bodies against each other, the high thin piping of something small calling to something smaller, the low grinding hum of something that might be a territorial signal or might be digestion or might be nothing more than the sound a body makes when it is packed tight among a thousand other bodies.

The basin is *full* of them.

Not full in the sense that you can't see the ground. Full in the sense that everywhere you look, something is moving. The stone ridges, those sharp fresh-fractured fins of pale rock — they are lined with animals. Small ones mostly. Creatures the size of your hand, or your fist, or your thumb. They perch on the ridges in long uneven rows, and they crowd into the troughs between the ridges in rustling masses, and they cluster around every patch of dampness in knots so dense that the ground beneath them disappears. They are lizard-like, many of them — or at least they have that quality of quick lateral movement, that sudden frozen stillness followed by a darting scramble. But they are varied. Some have smooth skin that gleams dully in the low light. Some are furred, or partly furred, with tufts along their spines. Some are flat-bodied, pressed against the rock faces like the gray-green plant, their limbs splayed, their bellies flush to the stone. Some are round and humped and move in slow deliberate waddles between the rushes.

And those are just the ones on the ground.

The air above the basin holds birds — if they are birds. They don't soar. The air is too still for soaring. They fly in short busy loops, rising twenty or thirty feet, circling, landing again. They are dark against the dim sky, small enough that they're mostly silhouette: pointed wings, quick wingbeats, a constant chatter of high-pitched calls that layer over the sounds below and add a kind of ceiling to the noise. Thousands of them. Not in flocks exactly — they don't seem organized — but in a kind of loose, permanent swarm that rotates slowly above the basin floor like a living weather system that never dissipates.

Below the birds, above the lizard-things and the waddle-creatures and the flat stone-huggers, there are insects. Or things that fill the role of insects. They hover over the seep. They crawl along the edges of every condensation patch. They mass in the crevices of the fresh-fractured stone, their bodies catching what little light there is and throwing back tiny sparks of iridescence — blue-green, copper, gone. The clicking you hear, the rapid dry rattling that underlies everything — that is them. Millions of them. Their wings, their legs, their mouthparts working against each other in the dark gaps between the stone fins.

There are larger animals too, though fewer. Something the size of a dog moves along the far edge of the basin, picking its way between the stone ridges with an unhurried, heavy-footed gait. It has a broad flat head and a thick tail and it doesn't seem to notice or care about the smaller creatures that scatter ahead of it and close ranks behind it, flowing around its footsteps like water around a stone. Farther out, near the center of the basin where the fracture lines converge and the stone fins are tallest — waist-high, some of them — you can see a group of larger shapes resting in a hollow. They are pale, almost the color of the stone, and they lie together in a heap, their bodies overlapping, their breath visible in the cool air as faint rhythmic plumes. They might be mammals. They might be something else. They are the size of sheep, roughly, and they don't move. They haven't moved since you first noticed them, and you suspect they won't move for a long time.

---

This is the remarkable thing about the basin: the animals are not migrating through it. They are not gathered temporarily around a seasonal resource. They *live* here. All of them. Permanently. The seep, the condensation, the sparse vegetation — it is enough, barely, to support this improbable density of life, and has been enough for so long that the whole system has reached a kind of saturation point beyond which nothing changes. Every niche is filled. Every inch of damp rock has its claimant. Every insect has its predator and every predator has its parasite. The population doesn't grow because there is nothing left to grow into. It doesn't shrink because nothing here ever fails badly enough to cause a collapse. The rain comes or doesn't come and it doesn't matter — the condensation is reliable, drawn from the cool still air by the cold stone every night without exception, and twenty feet of elevation below the surrounding plain is enough to trap that moisture, to hold it here in this shallow bowl where the wind never reaches and the light never sharpens.

The sound doesn't change either. That's what finally makes you understand the nature of this place. You listen for a shift — a dawn chorus, an evening settling, a moment of quiet between one hour and the next — and it doesn't come. The overlapping calls and clicks and scrapes and rustles maintain their exact volume, their exact texture, hour after hour. The swarm above rotates. The creatures below scuttle and freeze and scuttle. The pale heap of bodies near the center breathes its slow plumes into the cool air. The seep runs its quarter mile. The stone fins hold their fresh edges against a sky that never brightens, never darkens, never clears.

You are standing in a place that found its balance and stopped. Not dead — incomprehensibly alive, saturated with life to its exact capacity — but still. Still the way a spinning top is still. Still the way a held note is still. Everything moving, nothing changing. The basin hums with ten thousand voices and not one of them is saying anything new.
