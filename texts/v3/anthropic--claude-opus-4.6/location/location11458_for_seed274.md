# The Cistern Floor

---

There is a place beneath the hill where the water has nowhere left to go.

It is not large. You could walk from one edge to the other in perhaps forty steps, if you walked carefully, which you would have to. The ceiling is low — in places barely higher than a person, in others pressing down to the height of your chest, so that you would need to crouch and then crouch further, and the warm wet air would be on the back of your neck like breath. The space is roughly ovoid, longer than it is wide, pinched at the edges where the rock settles into itself. It has the feeling of a room, though no one made it a room. The rock did this. The water did this. A long, long time ago, and then it stopped changing, and it has been this way since.

---

The floor is water. Not deep water — ankle-deep in most places, shin-deep in the lowest channel that curves gently through the center. It is warm. Not hot, but distinctly, persistently warm, the kind of warmth that makes you forget after a few minutes that the water is there at all, because it matches the temperature of your skin so closely that the boundary between you and it becomes theoretical. The water is clear but not transparent, because the floor beneath it is coated in a film of mineral calcium — pale ochre, dove-grey, dull ivory — and the water picks up these colors and holds them without reflecting much of anything. There is almost no current. The water arrives from the rock itself, seeping through the porous limestone ceiling, condensing on the surface of the harder formations, gathering in slow drips that never quite become drops. It leaves, presumably, the same way — drawn down through the floor into some deeper geology. But the rate of entry and exit reached equilibrium so long ago that the water level has not changed in a span of time that would be meaningless to name.

The ceiling drips. This is the primary sound. Not a rhythm, because there are too many drip-points, perhaps hundreds, each on its own schedule, so that the composite sound is a soft, arrhythmic, continuous patter — like the memory of rain heard from very far inside a house. Occasionally a drop lands in the shallow channel where the water is slightly deeper, and there is a rounder, more resonant note, a small pock of sound that the low ceiling catches and holds for an instant before the stone absorbs it. The acoustics are intimate and muffled. Sound doesn't carry here; it is born and dies within arm's reach.

---

The rock is the landscape. There is nothing else.

The ceiling is limestone, stained and layered, sweating. Where the mineral-laden water has seeped for ages, formations have grown — not the grand stalactites of deeper caves, but small, blunted nubs, curtain-folds no longer than your hand, clusters of calcite that look like they were poured rather than grown. They are packed closely together. In some areas the ceiling is so encrusted that the original rock is invisible beneath a dense, overlapping skin of deposited stone — rippled, bubbled, folded over itself, every surface colonized by the next layer of mineral accretion. The formations press against each other. There is no space between them. Where one ends, another begins, or rather, they have grown into each other so thoroughly that the distinction is academic. The effect is claustrophobic in the way that dense embroidery is claustrophobic — too much detail for the eye to rest, every surface complicated, every inch occupied.

The floor, beneath the water, mirrors this but in reverse — built up from below. Flowstone terraces, each only a few inches high, create tiny stepped pools within the larger shallowness. Rimstone dams, delicate lips of deposited calcium, trace wandering lines across the submerged floor like the borders on a map of nowhere. Some of the terraces have grown small rounded knobs — cave pearls, though not true spheres, more like smooth pebbles of calcium carbonate that formed around grains of grit and then stopped when the conditions stabilized. They sit in their little pools like eggs in nests, unmoving. You could pick one up. It would be smooth and warm and surprisingly heavy.

The color palette is narrow but not monotonous. The dominant tones are cream, grey-brown, and a muted amber where iron traces have stained the calcite. In the dimmest corners, the wet stone looks almost lilac — but this may be an artifact of the eye straining in low light. There is no pure white. Everything has been touched by dissolved minerals, by the faintly acidic water carrying its cargo of calcium and iron and trace elements out of the hill and depositing them here, molecule by molecule, over a period that makes human patience look like a tantrum.

---

The light comes from somewhere. This is the only thing about the space that resists easy explanation, and even this resists only slightly. Near the far end of the ovoid room, where the ceiling lifts slightly, there is a crack — not wide enough to pass through, barely wide enough to slide your hand into — and through this crack comes a grey, diffused light. It has traveled some distance through rock and intervening air before arriving, and by the time it reaches the cistern floor it is directionless and thin, the kind of light that doesn't cast shadows so much as it fails to eliminate darkness. It illuminates the space the way a rumor illuminates a situation: partially, unreliably, enough to make you think you understand what you're seeing. The far half of the room has this grey visibility. The near half — the end closest to however you arrived — is darker, and your eyes adjust, and the dripping continues, and the shapes of the encrusted ceiling are known more by the feeling of proximity than by sight.

In this dim light, the water on the floor does not gleam. It lies flat and warm and patient.

---

There are things here that someone made.

Against the left wall — if you orient yourself facing the light crack — a shelf of natural flowstone has been augmented. Someone, at some point, used a hand tool to flatten its upper surface. The chisel marks are visible but softened, blurred by a thin skin of subsequent mineral deposition. The water has been recoating the marks for so long that they look like they are being slowly swallowed. On this shelf, above the water line, there are three objects: a shallow bowl carved from the same limestone, its interior worn smooth in a way that suggests use rather than water; a pointed tool, like an awl, made from bone or antler, now furred with a whisper-thin layer of calcium; and something that might be a wedge or a stopper, carved to fit into a gap in the rock nearby. The gap is visible. The wedge is not in it. It has not been in it for a very long time.

Elsewhere, near the center of the space, two of the rimstone dams on the floor have been deliberately broken — their lips chipped through to allow water to flow from one micro-terrace to the next. The breaks are clean in a way that flowing water would not produce. Someone wanted the water to move a certain way across this floor. The channels work. The water does move that way, sluggishly, imperceptibly, but it follows the path that was cut for it. The intervention was small and precise and it was the last intervention anyone made here.

There are no other signs of habitation. No fire-marks on the ceiling, no gathered stones, no bedding-area, no middens. Whoever used this space used it for something specific and then stopped coming.

---

The fauna is almost nothing.

In the deeper channel, if you hold very still and let the disturbed water settle, you might see a small pale shape moving near the floor. It is a crustacean of some kind — an isopod, perhaps, or something amphipod-like, translucent white, no longer than a grain of rice. It moves in small deliberate increments across the calcite floor, feeling its way with antennae so fine they are invisible. There may be others. They are the same color as the stone they crawl on, and they move so slowly that they only register as alive when you happen to catch one mid-step. They eat the biofilm — a bacterial layer so thin it has no color, no texture, no visible presence, but it is there, coating the submerged stone, fed by minerals in the water. The isopods graze this invisible film the way cattle graze grass, except nothing here has ever hurried.

On the ceiling, in the crevices between calcite formations, there might be mites. You would not see them. They are there or they are not.

Nothing else lives here. The space is too enclosed, too stable, too far from the cycles of growth and decay that sustain complex life. The isopods are the end of a food chain so short it barely qualifies as a chain — dissolved minerals feed bacteria feed crustaceans, and nothing feeds on the crustaceans, and when a crustacean dies it dissolves back into the water and the minerals begin again. It is not an ecosystem so much as a very slow chemical reaction that happens to involve something with legs.

---

The air is humid to the point of saturation. Breathing feels like drinking something thin. The warmth is geological — rising from deeper stone, conducted through the rock floor, held in the water, unable to escape through the low ceiling. It does not fluctuate. There are no seasons here, no day-night cycle, no weather. The crack that admits light admits no wind. The temperature is what it is and what it has been and what it will be. The water is as warm as the air. The stone is as warm as the water. Everything is the same temperature, and that temperature is the feeling of a hand resting on sun-warmed stone in the late afternoon, except the afternoon never ends and there is no sun.

---

This place is not waiting for anything. It is not in transition. It is not a ruin in the process of becoming more ruined, not a formation in the process of growing, not a pool in the process of filling or draining. The mineral deposition continues, technically — every drip carries its infinitesimal cargo of calcium — but the rate is so slow that change here operates on a timescale that makes the word "change" feel inaccurate. The chisel marks on the shelf will be erased, eventually. The cave pearls will grow larger, eventually. The rimstone dams will repair the breaks that were cut into them, eventually. But "eventually" here means something so far away that it might as well be "never," and the space exists in a state of equilibrium so deep and so settled that it has the quality not of stasis but of *completeness*. Nothing is missing. Nothing is excess. The water arrives and the water departs and the stone holds its shape and the pale isopods graze their invisible fields and the dripping fills the silence without breaking it.

You are the least stable thing in the room.
