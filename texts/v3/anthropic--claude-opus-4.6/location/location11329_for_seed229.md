# The Singing Terrace of Veth Múra

---

There is no water here. There has never been water here. You must understand that first, because everything else about this place will try to convince you otherwise.

---

The terrace is perhaps two hundred paces across — an irregular shelf of pale calcium rock jutting from the shoulder of a mountain whose upper reaches dissolve into a low, woolen sky the color of old pewter. The light is sourceless, ambient, a kind of warm dusk that doesn't commit to any direction. No sun is visible. Perhaps there is no sun. Perhaps it is behind the mountain, always behind the mountain, casting this even, copper-grey glow over everything, making the colors of the foliage richer than they have any right to be.

And the foliage — God, the foliage.

It grows from the rock itself. Not from soil; there is almost no soil here. The plants of Veth Múra are lithophytes, root-drillers, extracting what they need directly from the calcium and mineral strata with tendrils that work themselves into the stone over decades, splitting it slowly along fault-lines you can trace with your fingers. The erosion is botanical. The weathering is alive. Every crack in the terrace, every rounded edge, every hollow worn into the shelf has been made not by rain or river — there is no rain here, there are no rivers — but by the slow, patient, mineral-hungry mouths of roots.

The plants themselves are lush but spaced apart, each one claiming its territory of stone, each one distant enough from its neighbors that you can walk between them. They don't form a canopy so much as a scattered parliament. The largest are the bone-trees — chalk-white trunks rising eight or nine feet, smooth as ceramic, branching into dense crowns of leaves so deeply green they appear almost black in this dim light. The leaves are thick, waxy, built to hold moisture that was never liquid to begin with. These trees drink the mountain's breath — the humid mineral vapors that seep up through fissures in the rock, warm and faintly sulfurous, carrying dissolved calcium and phosphorus that the bone-trees metabolize into their extraordinary white wood. Snap a branch and inside it is dense as ivory, ringed with growth-lines like a narwhal's tusk.

Between the bone-trees: cushions of something that resembles moss but is actually a colonial organism, thousands of tiny tubular structures fused at their bases, each one a deep plum-red, soft to the touch, warm — genuinely warm, warmer than the air. This is vetch-fur, and it generates its own heat through some slow exothermic chemistry no one has properly studied. In the dim light, patches of vetch-fur glow faintly from within, a dark ruddy warmth like embers seen through smoked glass. Sit on it and it yields like a thick carpet. It covers perhaps a third of the terrace's surface in irregular patches, and in the spaces between, the pale rock shows through, cracked and carved by centuries of root-work into patterns that look almost deliberate — spirals, branching networks, nested arcs. They are not deliberate. They are the fossil-trails of root systems that have long since moved on.

There are other plants: low sprawling succulents with translucent amber leaves that cluster around the vapor fissures, shelves of bracket-fungus erupting from the bone-trees in brilliant orange fans, trailing vines with no leaves at all — just ropey grey stems that drape from tree to tree, photosynthesizing through their bark.

And now: the sound.

---

The terrace is *loud*. Startlingly, almost alarmingly loud.

It is loud because of the fauna, and the fauna is abundant, and the fauna is strange.

Start with the throat-crickets. They are everywhere — in the vetch-fur, under the bracket-fungus, in the crevices of the stone — and each one is the size of a man's thumb, matte black, with a resonating chamber in its abdomen that produces a sound far too large for its body: a sustained, throbbing bass note, almost subaudible, that you feel in your sternum before you hear it with your ears. There are hundreds of them on the terrace, and they do not synchronize. Each one holds its own pitch, its own rhythm, and the result is a churning, shifting drone that fills the air like the inside of a cathedral organ heard from within the pipes. The ground vibrates with it. The bone-trees vibrate with it. Your teeth vibrate with it.

Above the drone: the callers. These are the birds — if they are birds. They have six limbs, the forward pair reduced to delicate manipulating hooks, the middle pair serving as wings, the rear pair as perching feet. Their bodies are compact, furred rather than feathered, ranging in size from a wren to a small crow. Their coloring shifts — and this is not metaphor, it literally shifts, a slow chromatophoric ripple across their pelts, cycling through muted golds and greens and the occasional flash of violent violet. They perch in the bone-trees, sometimes four or five to a crown, and they *scream*. They scream in structured, complex phrases — rising trills that break into staccato clicks, long sliding whistles that doppler as the creatures whip their heads back and forth, percussive pops made by snapping their manipulating hooks together. It is not song exactly. It is closer to argument. They seem to be in constant, furious negotiation with one another, their calls overlapping, interrupting, responding in real time. The volume is extraordinary. In the dim, sourceless light, perched among the dark leaves, their bodies flickering with color, they look like malfunctioning signal lamps sending increasingly frantic messages.

And between the drone and the screaming: the movement.

---

This is the thing about Veth Múra. Nothing is still. *Nothing*.

The terrace is in a state of constant, low-grade pandemonium. The throat-crickets shift positions ceaselessly, crawling over and through the vetch-fur in unpredictable surges — a hundred of them will suddenly migrate from one patch to another for no discernible reason, their bass notes warping and sliding as they move, creating Doppler effects in the drone. The callers launch and re-perch every few seconds, trading trees, diving at one another, occasionally locking hooks in brief spiraling falls before separating. Something — you haven't seen it clearly yet, only the evidence of its passage — moves through the root-channels beneath the rock, occasionally causing a section of stone to shift or settle with a deep percussive *clack* that cuts through even the drone. Dust rises from these micro-collapses, fine and white as flour.

There are other creatures. A slow, flat thing like a living carpet — mottled grey-green, perhaps two feet across, edged with fringe-like cilia — grazes methodically along the surface of a bone-tree trunk, leaving a clean trail behind it. Some kind of burrowing mammal, eyeless, pale, with oversized clawed forelimbs, emerges from a root-channel, sits upright for three seconds with its naked nose twitching in the mineral-vapored air, then plunges back underground so quickly it seems to fold into the rock. Moths the size of playing cards orbit the bracket-fungus in tight ellipses, their wings patterned with what appear to be false eyes but which, on closer inspection, are actual photosensitive organs — each wing bearing four or five simple eyes that glint wetly in the ambient light.

And there is one more animal, the strangest of all, and you will not see it at first because it does not look like an animal. Against the back wall of the terrace, where the shelf meets the rising face of the mountain, what appears to be a growth of pale lichen spreads across roughly ten square feet of rock. It has been there — or something like it has been there — for a long time; the rock beneath it is worn smooth. But watch long enough and you will see it breathe. A slow expansion and contraction, once every forty or fifty seconds. And if you are very still — which is difficult here, because the noise makes stillness feel wrong — you will see it extend a feeding structure: a translucent, vein-laced membrane that unfurls like a hand opening, reaching toward the nearest vapor fissure, holding itself in the warm updraft for a minute or more before slowly withdrawing. It is tasting the mountain. It is vast and sessile and patient and it has been here longer than the bone-trees. The callers avoid it. The throat-crickets cluster near it, as if its presence amplifies their drone. There are scratch-marks on the rock near its edges — something has tried to pry it up, periodically, without success.

---

Now: the built things.

At the edge of the terrace, near the drop-off where the shelf ends and the mountainside plunges into haze, there is a low wall. It is made of stacked bone-tree wood, cut into rough blocks and fitted without mortar. It is old. Vetch-fur has colonized its base, and root-tendrils from a nearby tree have begun to infiltrate the joints between blocks. It is perhaps waist-high, perhaps forty paces long. A windbreak? A territorial marker? It has no gate, no obvious entrance-point. It simply runs along the edge, turns a corner, and stops.

Near the center of the terrace, partially obscured by a cluster of bone-trees, there is a platform — a flat slab of the same white wood, raised a few inches off the rock on shaped pegs. Its surface has been worn smooth by use. By sitting, maybe. Or by the preparation of something. There are faint stains on it, dark and old, that could be plant-dye or mineral pigment or something else. A groove has been carved along its perimeter — shallow, finger-width, forming a closed rectangle. Its purpose is not clear. Beside the platform, wedged into a crack in the rock, is a tool: a hand-length spike of sharpened bone-tree wood, wrapped at its base with a strip of dried vetch-fur for grip. It has been used recently enough that the tip is still pale and clean.

Whoever made these things is not here now. But the tool in the crack — that was placed, not dropped. Someone intends to come back.

---

The turbulence of Veth Múra is not weather-turbulence. There is no wind to speak of; the air is close and warm and still except where the vapor fissures push their slow exhalations upward. The turbulence is ecological — a system in constant motion, every element perpetually adjusting to every other. The throat-crickets migrate because the vetch-fur chemistry shifts beneath them, its heat output fluctuating in patterns driven by the mineral content of the vapor, which changes as the mountain's deep geology cycles through whatever vast processes govern it. The callers scream because the throat-cricket drone is always changing, and their negotiations — territorial, reproductive, navigational — must be conducted over an unstable acoustic floor. The burrowing things emerge and retreat because the root-channels are alive, slowly growing and shifting, occasionally pinching shut or breaking through into new cavities. The lichen-creature breathes because the vapor composition demands it.

Nothing here is catastrophic. Nothing is dying or being born in dramatic fashion. But nothing is at rest, either. The system churns. It has been churning for a very long time — the erosion patterns, the depth of the root-carvings, the worn surface of the platform all speak to centuries, maybe longer. This is not a place that was recently disrupted. This is a place whose *resting state is turbulence*. The stone has been shaped by it. The organisms have been shaped by it. Stillness, here, would be the catastrophe.

You stand in the warm dim noise of it — the drone in your chest, the screaming in your ears, the occasional deep *clack* of settling stone beneath your feet — and you realize you are the only still thing on the terrace. The vetch-fur shifts warmly against your ankles. A caller turns one of its three eyes toward you, flashes violet, and launches itself shrieking into the pewter air. The lichen-creature breathes. The spike in the rock waits for its owner.

Something moves beneath you, and the stone tilts, just slightly, just enough to make you take a step.

The terrace accommodates.
