# The Stepped Settlement of Ketharn

---

There is a place where a hillside was cut into shelves, a long time ago, and nothing has changed since.

Ketharn is small — you could walk its full breadth in four hundred steps, top to bottom, and maybe twice that side to side. It occupies a single face of a ridge that rises from a dry plateau, and it is entirely made of the ridge's own stone: a pale yellowish limestone, fine-grained, that the builders cut and stacked and carved into terraces, walls, stairways, cisterns, and low rectangular dwellings with flat roofs. The stone is all one color. The mortar is ground from the same stone. The dust that lies in the corners of the steps is the same stone, powdered by centuries of weather and foot traffic that may or may not still occur.

Everything here has the quality of having been *settled* — not in the sense of colonized, but in the way sediment settles, the way a body settles into a chair it has occupied for years. The edges of the stairs are softened. The drainage channels cut into each terrace lip are deepened just slightly beyond their original chisel marks by the slow passage of water. The lintels over doorways have sagged by fractions — not enough to crack, just enough that you sense the weight of time pressing evenly on every surface. Nothing is ruined. Nothing is fresh. Ketharn is in its own state, the state it reached some unknowable number of years ago when the last meaningful change occurred and everything afterward became repetition.

---

## The Terraces

There are nine terraces, each about the height of a man, connected by stairways cut directly into the ridge face. The lowest terrace is the widest, perhaps sixty paces across, and they narrow as they ascend, so the whole settlement has the rough shape of a truncated triangle when seen from below. The uppermost terrace is barely a ledge — room for two small structures and a flat platform open to the sky that might have been a watchtower platform or a place of ceremony or simply where someone preferred to sit.

The terraces are not perfectly level. They tilt slightly inward toward the hillside, and at the back of each one, where the vertical face of the next terrace rises, there is a shallow channel cut into the stone floor. These channels connect to vertical shafts — narrow slots, really, barely a hand's width — that run down through the terrace walls from level to level. This is the water system. Moisture collects on the upper ridge — not from any spring or river, but from condensation. The ridge rises high enough that the cool air moving across the plateau meets the stone face and leaves a film of dew in the hours before dawn. The channels catch this. They direct it downward. At certain points on each terrace, a small basin is carved into the floor beside the channel, no larger than a washbasin, and here the water pools. It is never more than a few inches deep. By midday it has mostly evaporated or been absorbed by the stone, and the basins sit dry until the next dawn.

This is all the water Ketharn has. It has always been all the water Ketharn has. The system works, in its minimal way. The basins show calcium deposits around their rims — white crusts, slightly rough to the touch, built up by uncountable cycles of filling and drying.

## The Structures

The dwellings are rectangular, low-ceilinged, thick-walled. Most have a single room. A few of the larger ones on the middle terraces have two rooms separated by an interior wall with a narrow doorway. There are no windows — only the front doorway, which is always oriented outward, facing the open air and the view of the plateau below. The doorways are low enough that you would need to duck to enter, and they are all the same height, suggesting either a standard of construction or a population of roughly uniform stature.

Inside, the rooms are bare. The floors are the same limestone, worn smooth. In some rooms, a low shelf has been carved from the back wall — not added, but left in place when the rest of the wall was cut away, a negative-space construction technique that is elegant in its economy. These shelves are at sitting height or sleeping height. Some rooms have small niches carved at eye level, the kind of recesses that hold a lamp or a small object, though there is nothing in them now.

The construction is entirely dry-stack and carved — no evidence of metal tools. The chisel marks, where they are still visible on interior walls, are consistent with hard stone implements, perhaps flint or chert. The cuts are confident but not perfectly regular. Each strike left a slightly concave scallop in the surface, and where walls were left unsmoothed, these scallop patterns create a subtle texture that catches the light in the late afternoon when the sun angles in through the doorways and reaches the back walls.

There are approximately forty structures across all nine terraces. Not all of them are dwellings. On the fifth terrace — roughly the middle — there is a structure twice the width of the others, with a lower ceiling and a wider doorway, and its floor has a large shallow depression carved into it, oval, about the size of a body lying down. The depression has the same calcium deposits as the water basins, but thicker. Whatever this room was used for, it involved water collecting and standing for longer periods.

## The Air

Ketharn is cool. The elevation sees to that — the plateau is already high, and the ridge lifts the settlement further into air that moves with a thin, steady quality. Not wind exactly. A draft. Something between stillness and breeze, constant in its direction — it comes from the north, across the plateau, and it has come from the north for as long as the erosion patterns on the north-facing edges of the terrace walls can testify, which is a very long time. The northern edges are more rounded than the southern ones. The northern faces of the stairway risers are smoother. Even the doorways, all facing outward, are subtly more worn on their left sides, which are the sides that face north.

The air smells like dry stone and, in the early morning hours, faintly of mineral water — the condensation carrying with it whatever trace elements the limestone releases. It is not an unpleasant smell. It is barely a smell at all. It is more like the *idea* of a smell, a coolness in the sinuses that suggests moisture without delivering much of it.

---

## The Vegetation

What grows here grows in the cracks. A few tufts of grey-green grass, wiry and stiff, have established themselves in the joints between terrace stones where centuries of dust accumulation have created narrow seams of soil. These grasses do not spread. They do not die back. They persist in the same clumps, fed by the same trace moisture, neither growing larger nor disappearing. On the uppermost terrace, where the condensation is heaviest, a single low shrub — woody, leafless for most of its life, with bark the color of the stone — has grown in the angle where the terrace floor meets the ridge face. Its roots have not cracked the stone. It has simply found the seam and followed it, and now it sits there, a twist of pale wood about knee-high, putting out a few leathery grey leaves in some season that may or may not correspond to anything happening on the plateau below.

There is lichen on the north-facing walls. It is the same yellowish color as the stone, only slightly darker, and from a distance it is invisible. You would notice it only by touch — a faint roughness, a texture like dried paste, on surfaces that should be smooth. It covers perhaps a third of the north-facing surfaces. It has covered exactly this much for a very long time.

---

## The Fauna

A species of small lizard lives in Ketharn. They are the color of the stone — pale yellow-brown, with slightly darker bands on their tails — and they are rarely seen, though they are always present. They live in the drainage channels, in the gaps between stacked stones, in the niches of the interior walls. In the early morning, when the basins hold their shallow pools, the lizards emerge to drink, pressing their blunt snouts to the water's surface and remaining motionless for long minutes. They are small enough to sit in a cupped hand. Their eyes are dark and unblinking.

There may be insects as well — something must sustain the lizards — but they are not visible. Perhaps they emerge at night, or live in the deeper crevices of the ridge above the settlement. Occasionally, very occasionally, a bird crosses the sky above Ketharn: a raptor of some kind, riding the updraft where the plateau wind meets the ridge face. It does not stop. It has no reason to stop. It is passing through on its way to somewhere with more prey, more water, more life. Its shadow slides across the terraces and is gone.

---

## The Sound

Ketharn is quiet in the way that a room is quiet after everyone has left but before anyone has noticed they are gone. The draft from the north makes a low, continuous sound as it passes over the terrace edges — not a whistle, not a hum, but a kind of tonal breath, just at the threshold of hearing. It is always the same pitch. It does not rise or fall. When the air moves across the doorways of the empty structures, there is a faint hollow resonance from within, each room producing a slightly different note depending on its depth and the size of its opening, but these are so quiet that you would need to press your ear to the doorway to distinguish them from silence.

Sometimes a pebble, loosened by the thermal cycling of the stone — cool nights, mild days, cool nights, mild days, forever — detaches from the ridge face above the settlement and skitters down the slope, bouncing off a terrace edge and coming to rest. This is the loudest sound in Ketharn. It happens perhaps once a day. The lizards do not react to it.

---

## The Light

The sun crosses above Ketharn on a predictable arc. In the morning, the settlement is in the ridge's shadow, and the stone is cool and blue-grey, and the condensation glistens in the channels. By midmorning, the sun clears the ridge and the terraces are lit from above and slightly behind, which means the flat roofs of the lower structures glow while their doorways remain dark. By afternoon, the sun has moved around to face the settlement directly, and everything is illuminated — the pale stone becomes almost white, and the shadows under the terrace lips are sharp and horizontal, and the whole place looks like a diagram of itself, a cross-section drawing, lines and planes. In the late afternoon, the light goes amber, and the scalloped chisel marks on the interior walls catch it and hold it, and for about twenty minutes the insides of the rooms are brighter than the outsides, the carved stone conducting the light inward and diffusing it, and this is the most beautiful time in Ketharn, though there may be no one present to recognize it.

---

## The Question of Inhabitants

The settlement is clearly built for habitation. It is organized, functional, considered. Someone designed the water system. Someone decided where each terrace would be cut, how wide, how deep. Someone determined that forty structures were needed and not fifty, that nine levels were right and not ten. These decisions imply a population — perhaps a hundred people, perhaps a hundred and fifty. Not more. The settlement could not support more, not with the water it has.

But there is no evidence of *current* habitation, nor any clear evidence of *departure*. There are no possessions left behind, no refuse middens, no scorch marks from cook fires, no graffiti or markings on the walls. The shelves are empty. The niches are empty. The oval depression on the fifth terrace is dry. Everything has been either taken or worn away or was never there in a form that stone could remember.

It is possible that the inhabitants are simply elsewhere — out on the plateau, returning at nightfall, engaged in some activity that leaves the settlement empty during the day. It is possible that they left generations ago and the settlement has simply persisted because limestone persists and there is nothing here to tear it down. It is possible that the settlement was built and never occupied — a project completed and then abandoned for reasons the stone does not record.

It does not matter. Ketharn does not change based on the answer. The condensation will collect before dawn. The channels will carry it to the basins. The basins will fill and dry. The lizards will drink. The wind will blow from the north. The pebble will fall. The light will move through its arc. These things have been true for longer than the question has existed, and they will continue to be true after the question has ceased to be interesting.

Ketharn is in its own state. It arrived there long ago. It remains.
