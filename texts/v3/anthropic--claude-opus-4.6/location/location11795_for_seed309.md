# The Underbower

---

You descend through a cut in the earth that should not be as deep as it is. The stairs are root — thick, petrified coils of something that was once a living tendril, now worn smooth by centuries of passage. Each step is subtly concave, polished by feet or bodies or something that dragged itself up and down in patient repetition until the wood took the shape of use.

Below, the sky closes.

Not all at once. It thins. The opening above narrows as root-masses and canopy-flesh knit together overhead, and the light — already dim at the surface, filtered through whatever grey weather hangs up there — becomes a residue. A warmth-colored residue, because the plants here are bioluminescent in the laziest possible way: a faint, brownish-amber glow that lives in the cambium layer of the largest roots, seeping through bark where the bark has worn thin. It is not enough to see by, not really. It is enough to know where the walls are. It is enough to sense depth.

The Underbower is a single chamber roughly the size of a small cathedral nave — maybe forty meters long, fifteen wide, and the ceiling varies between three meters and nine, depending on where the root-mass dips or vaults. It is entirely below the surface. Not a cave: there is no stone here, or almost none. The walls are root and soil held together by root, and the floor is a compacted mat of dead plant material so old and so compressed it has become something like peat, something like leather, something with a give to it that is not quite comfortable. The whole space breathes faintly of tannin and decomposition — not rot, exactly, but the patient chemical work of fungi breaking cellulose into sugar into heat.

That heat matters. It is warm here, a close warmth, the kind that sits on the skin rather than penetrating to the bone. The temperature holds steady regardless of what happens above. This is an incubator-space, a fermentation hollow, and it has been this way for so long that the roots forming the walls have grown around each other in patterns that suggest intention but are really just the settled logic of centuries of growth finding the path of least resistance. Erosion here is not subtraction — it is accommodation. The roots have worn against each other, merged at contact points, formed arches and buttresses that look architectural because structural physics is structural physics whether you're an engineer or a banyan.

---

**The Machinery.**

Set into this living chamber, bolted and braced and threaded through the root-walls with a confidence that suggests long cohabitation, are machines.

They are old. Not ancient in the way the roots are ancient, but old in an industrial sense: cast iron, heavy riveted plating, brass fittings gone green at the joints. There is a central column — a pump or compressor of some kind — that rises from the peat-floor to the ceiling, where its upper housing disappears into a knot of roots that have grown around it so completely that you cannot tell where the machine ends and the plant begins. It thrums. A low, dense vibration that you feel in your molars before you hear it with your ears. The pump cycles every eleven seconds, and when it cycles, there is a wet exhalation — a gust of moist air pushed through conduits that run along the walls, buried half in root-flesh, half exposed. Condensation beads on the iron. Drips. Small puddles collect in the depressions of the floor, persist for hours, then are wicked away by the root-mat beneath.

This is the water here: intermittent, mechanical, almost liturgical in its rhythm. The pump breathes moisture into the chamber, the roots drink it, the excess gathers and evaporates and gathers again. There are no streams, no springs. Just the machine's exhalation and the chamber's slow inhalation in response.

Other machines line the walls — or rather, are embedded in the walls at intervals, like organs in a body cavity. A generator, maybe, though whatever fuel it burns produces no visible smoke (the exhaust vents up through root-channels and is — you suspect — metabolized by the plant-mass long before it reaches the surface). A series of what look like centrifuges, chest-high, their housings wrapped in root-tendrils that pulse faintly with that amber bioluminescence when the centrifuges spin up. The machines are spaced far apart, with stretches of empty root-wall between them — the sparseness of a system that has been pared down to only what is essential, or that was never dense to begin with, each device given room to breathe, room for the roots to negotiate around it.

Who built this? Who *maintains* this? The machines are worn but functional. The bolts are tightened. There is oil on the joints — you can smell it under the tannin, a mineral sharpness. Someone comes here, or something does.

---

**The Fauna.**

This is where the Underbower becomes difficult to hold in your mind.

They are everywhere. Not in the way of a swarm — the space is too sparse for swarming, the emptiness between things too deliberate — but in the way of a place where animals are the dominant fact, where the architecture and the machinery and the plant-growth are all *secondary* to the living bodies that move through and over and under and around them.

Begin with the floor. The peat-leather surface is not still. It ripples. Gently, continuously, in places — and when you look closely, you see that the surface layer is perforated with holes the diameter of a thumb, and from these holes emerge creatures that look like elongated, eyeless salamanders, bone-white, glistening with the chamber's condensation. They are eight or nine inches long. They slide out, cross a foot of floor, and slide into another hole. There are hundreds of holes. The traffic is constant. The rippling is them, moving just below the surface, a transit network of soft bodies in the substrate. They make a sound: a faint, collective clicking, like someone running a fingernail along the teeth of a comb, very fast, far away. It is the sound of their jaws working on the peat-material, or on each other, or on something you cannot see.

Above the floor, the space between the machines is occupied by animals of a different order. Rope-thick invertebrates — segmented, dark red, furred with sensory hairs — loop from root to root along the ceiling, moving with a peristaltic patience that makes them look like the roots themselves until one shifts and you see the wet gleam of a mouth-part. They are browsers, you think. They eat the bioluminescent cambium, and where they have fed, the root-wall goes dark for days before regenerating. Their movement through the chamber is a slow, rolling redistribution of light and shadow — the dim glow brightening where they haven't been, dimming where they pass.

But there are also the fast ones. Small — sparrow-sized — with too many limbs and a chitinous plating that catches the amber glow and throws it back as brief, sharp reflections. They move on the machines. They live in the machines, possibly. You see them darting along the conduit-pipes, pausing at the condensation-beads to drink, racing each other along the horizontal surface of the centrifuge housings. When the pump cycles, they freeze — all of them, simultaneously — and in that moment of stillness you can count them: twenty, thirty, forty in this section alone, clinging to iron and brass with hooked feet. Then the cycle completes, and they explode back into motion with a sound like a handful of pebbles thrown against sheet metal.

And the sound — God, the sound. The clicking of the floor-salamanders. The low thrum of the pump. The peristaltic wet-drag of the ceiling browsers. The metallic skittering of the machine-dwellers. And under all of it, a sound you cannot source: a rhythmic, breathy hooting, almost infrasonic, that seems to come from the root-walls themselves, or from deep in the peat beneath your feet, or from farther down, from whatever is below the Underbower. It rises and falls on a cycle that has nothing to do with the pump's eleven-second rhythm — longer, maybe three or four minutes between peaks — and when it peaks, the floor-salamanders go still, the ceiling browsers contract, the machine-dwellers freeze again, and the whole chamber enters a held-breath pause that lasts two or three seconds before everything resumes.

Something is calling from below. Or breathing. Or digesting.

There are other animals. A low-slung, many-legged thing the size of a house cat that you see only once, crossing the far end of the chamber in the deepest shadow, carrying something pale and struggling in its forward limbs. Clouds of near-microscopic flying things that congregate around the warmest machine-surfaces and form shifting veils of translucent wings. A single, motionless creature adhered to the central pump column — flat, round, the size of a dinner plate, the color of old iron, so perfectly camouflaged against the machine-housing that you only notice it when it shifts its grip and reveals, briefly, a pale underside covered in cilia.

The ecology is in motion. Constantly. The ceiling browsers eat the roots. The roots regrow. The machine-dwellers eat — what? Condensation, and possibly the microscopic flying things, and possibly each other. The floor-salamanders process the peat and are, you suspect, the reason the floor has its leather-like consistency: they are digesters, composters, the living engine that converts dead plant matter into the substrate that supports the root-walls that form the chamber that houses the machines that produce the moisture that feeds the roots. It is a closed system, or nearly closed. The machines are the one intrusion, the one element that requires input from outside — fuel, oil, replacement parts. Everything else turns in on itself, consuming and producing in a loop so old that the animals have evolved to fit the spaces the machines create, and the machines have been adapted (by whom?) to accommodate the animals.

---

**The Strangeness.**

You stand in the Underbower for twenty minutes and the following things happen:

The central pump skips a cycle. It has never done this before — you know this with a certainty that has no rational basis, because you have been here for twenty minutes and cannot possibly know the machine's full operational history, but you *know*. The eleven-second rhythm is as old as the chamber. The skip lasts three seconds. During the skip, every animal in the chamber turns to face the pump. Every one. The eyeless salamanders lift their heads from their holes. The ceiling browsers pause mid-peristalsis. The machine-dwellers orient their bodies toward the central column. The plate-creature on the pump housing peels itself away and hovers — *hovers* — a centimeter from the iron surface, cilia rippling. Then the pump resumes. Everything goes back to what it was doing. But the plate-creature does not re-adhere. It drifts, slowly, impossibly, toward you.

You notice that one of the root-arches near the chamber's midpoint has a knot in it that looks like a face. Not a human face. A face in the sense of a configuration that implies perception — two hollows above a ridge above an opening. It was not there when you entered. You are almost certain. The bioluminescence around it is brighter than elsewhere, and the machine-dwellers avoid that section of root, giving it a wide berth that creates a visible gap in their otherwise uniform distribution along the conduits.

The infrasonic hooting from below shifts pitch. Just slightly. Just enough that the three-to-four-minute cycle becomes a two-and-a-half-minute cycle, and the held-breath pauses come faster, and the resumptions of activity after each pause are slightly more frantic, the clicking louder, the skittering sharper, the wet-drag of the browsers faster. Something has changed in the system's rhythm. Something is accelerating.

The condensation on the nearest machine surface spells nothing. You check. It is just condensation. But it gathers in lines that are too straight, too evenly spaced, and when you touch the iron, it is warmer than it should be, and your fingertip comes away with a faint amber glow that fades over the course of ten seconds.

---

The Underbower is a single room. It is below the surface. It is warm, dim, damp in pulses, old beyond the telling of it, full of machines that serve the plants that serve the animals that serve the machines. It is loud with life. It is strange in ways that do not announce themselves but accumulate, like condensation, until you are wet with them and cannot say when it began.

You could leave. The root-stairs are behind you. But the plate-creature is drifting closer, and the face in the root-knot has developed a third hollow, and the hooting from below has a new harmonic — higher, almost a whine — and every salamander in the floor has stopped clicking and started, very softly, to hum.

You are the sparsest thing here. The most empty space in the room is the space you're standing in, and the Underbower is deciding what to do about that.
