# The Sown Engine

---

There is no soil here. There never was.

The ground — if you can call it ground — is a lattice of fabricated carbon-ceramic, a foundation poured continent-wide by something that no longer exists in any form you'd recognize as a builder. It extends in every direction past the limits of curvature, a single manufactured plate that is the floor of the world, and from every pore and channel and machined joint of this substrate, the green has come.

Not grown. *Come.*

---

The plants here do not drink. There is no water for them — no rain, no aquifer, no dew. The air is cool and carries no humidity; if you breathed it, it would feel like breathing in a clean, dry refrigerator, faintly metallic, faintly sweet with volatiles you have no name for. The plants instead feed on the substrate itself, drawing what they need from the engineered material beneath them through root-analogues that are half-organic, half-industrial — threaded filaments that tap directly into conduit networks buried in the plate. What flows in those conduits is not water. It is something else. A working fluid, piped through a planetary-scale circulatory system that was built before the plants were even a possibility, repurposed now, rerouted, colonized.

The plants are the architecture. The architecture is the plants.

Stand in any place and look up: the canopy is hundreds of meters overhead, a luminous cathedral ceiling of overlapping leaf-structures so vast they generate their own microclimates beneath them, cool convection cells that stir the still air into slow spiraling currents. The light that comes through is extraordinary — fierce, white, sourceless. There is no visible sun. The light emanates from the upper surfaces of the canopy itself, or perhaps from something above the canopy that you cannot see from down here, and by the time it filters through the layered translucencies of leaf and membrane and photosynthetic panel, it arrives below as a brilliant, shadowless, cathedral-green radiance that makes everything glow as if lit from within.

Every surface is alive. Every structure is built.

This is the paradox of the place: the trunks that rise around you — some of them forty meters in diameter, their bark a woven composite of cellulose and what appears to be spun carbon fiber — are growing through and around and *as* the remnants of an industrial infrastructure so vast it beggars comprehension. You can see it in cross-section where old trunks have split or where the growth has peeled back layers of itself: inside the heartwood, there are pipes. Turbine housings. The corroded bones of factory-scale machinery, now sheathed in living cambium, incorporated, digested into the body of the plant. Ducts that once carried process gases now carry sap — or the not-sap, the waterless fluid these organisms circulate. Electrical conduits have become vascular bundles. The distinction between the engineered and the organic has not blurred; it has *dissolved*, category by category, until what remains is a single unified system that is simultaneously a forest and a machine and neither.

---

Walk deeper in.

The trunks grow closer together, their root-buttresses interlocking like the fingers of clasped hands, forming walls and chambers and passages. In places, the growth is so dense that you move through corridors of living tissue — smooth, cool to the touch, faintly vibrating with some deep process happening inside. The vibration is not mechanical. Or it is mechanical. It is both. You press your palm against a trunk-wall and feel something rhythmic, enormously slow, like a heartbeat measured in minutes. Somewhere inside this structure, pumps are running. Were they grown or built? The question has become meaningless here.

The sound is — almost nothing. That's what unsettles you first. A space this alive, this active, this dense with process and metabolism and industrial-scale energy conversion should *roar*. It should hum and grind and rustle and creak. Instead: a hush so deep it feels intentional, as if the entire system has been engineered — or has evolved — toward silence. You hear your own footsteps on the smooth root-floor. You hear, if you hold very still, a faint ticking — irregular, distant, like a cooling engine heard through walls. Occasionally a low resonant tone moves through the trunks, felt more than heard, as if the entire continent-forest has exhaled. Then silence again. The silence of something paying attention.

---

The light shifts.

Not like weather. Not like clouds passing. The canopy above rearranges itself — you can see it happening, leaf-panels the size of sports fields tilting on their stems, irising open or closed, redirecting the sourceless brightness into new configurations. It happens slowly, over the course of an hour, and the effect on the ground is like the movement of a sundial shadow, except the light is getting *brighter* in your area, concentrating, focusing. You realize with a start that this is not a passive process. The canopy is steering light toward a region a few kilometers ahead of you where the growth is denser still, where something is happening — a burst of new construction, a flowering, an expansion event. The forest is feeding itself. Allocating resources. Making decisions at a continental scale with an intelligence that is not centralized, not neural, not anything you can locate, but is undeniably *there* in the pattern of the light.

---

Here is where the weirdness lives.

You have been walking for what you believe is several hours, heading — you thought — in a straight line toward a gap in the canopy you spotted from a ridge of root-structures. But the gap is behind you now. You haven't turned. You're certain of this. The passage you're in still extends ahead, still curves gently to the left as it did when you entered it, but the geometry has shifted beneath your attention, folded somehow, and the space you're moving through is not the space you thought you were moving through. The forest-machine is rearranging itself around you — not quickly, not violently, but with the patient inevitability of growth. A trunk that was on your left is now on your right. The corridor has the same shape but different walls. You are inside something that is rewriting its own topology in real time, and you cannot catch it in the act because the act is happening at the threshold of perception, in the gap between one glance and the next.

You look at your hands. They are fine. You are fine. The air is still cool and sweet and the light is still bright and green and the silence is still deep. But the ground beneath you — the ancient manufactured plate — you realize you haven't seen it in some time. The roots have closed over it completely. You're walking on living material now, all the way down, and somewhere below you the plate is still there, the original engineered foundation, but it's buried under meters — tens of meters? — of accumulated growth, digested, incorporated, becoming something new.

A thought occurs to you: the plate was the seed. The entire continent-scale manufactured substrate — the factories, the engines, the infrastructure — was not colonized by the plants. It *became* the plants. Or the plants became it. The system bootstrapped itself from industry into biology, or from biology into industry, and the direction of the transformation is not recoverable because it happened so long ago and so thoroughly that the process has eaten its own history.

---

There are animals. Rarely.

You see one now, clinging to the inner wall of a trunk-corridor twenty meters ahead: a thing like a mantis made of pale ceramic and leaf-tissue, the size of a cat, absolutely still. It has no eyes that you can recognize. Its limbs are jointed in ways that suggest both insect and machine, and where its body meets the trunk-wall, you cannot tell where one ends and the other begins. It might be part of the plant. It might have always been part of the plant, a mobile organ, a detachable sensory module sent out to gather data and return. As you watch, it folds itself flat against the wall and is absorbed — slowly, over the course of maybe thirty seconds — back into the living surface, its outline softening, its edges merging with the bark-composite until it is a slight raised pattern on the trunk and then nothing at all.

You see another one later, or the same one, perched on a root-arch high above you, grooming its antennae with movements so slow and deliberate they seem ritualistic. It does not react to your presence. You are not food. You are not threat. You are not, perhaps, legible to whatever sensory apparatus it possesses. Or you are entirely legible and simply not interesting. Either possibility is disquieting.

---

Scale.

You climb a root-buttress — it takes the better part of a day, the surface offering easy handholds, the slope gentle enough to walk in most places — and from the top, maybe two hundred meters up, you can see out over the canopy for the first time.

It goes forever.

Not metaphorically. The curvature of the horizon tells you that this world is large — larger than Earth, maybe, or the light plays tricks — and in every direction, to the absolute limit of vision, the canopy stretches: a brilliant undulating surface of green and white and gold, leaf-panels catching and redirecting the fierce light, the whole thing rippling with slow movements like a sea frozen mid-wave and then unfrozen and then frozen again. You can see the patterns now — the resource-allocation events, the light-steering, the coordinated growth pulses that move across the surface like weather systems, like thoughts across a mind the size of a continent. There are structures rising from the canopy in places: towers, spires, great arching forms that might be trunks grown to impossible heights or might be the remnants of ancient industrial structures — antenna arrays, cooling towers, smokestacks — now sheathed in green and repurposed, made part of the body.

The light is blinding up here. You shade your eyes. The canopy-surface is *producing* light, you realize — not just reflecting it. The photosynthetic panels on the upper leaves are running in reverse in some places, converting stored chemical energy back into photons, broadcasting brightness outward into the sky. Why? For what audience? You look up. The sky is white. Featureless. Not clouds — just white, the color of light with no source, as if the sky itself is a ceiling and the light is coming from everywhere and the canopy is *answering* it, sending light back up in patterns you can almost read, almost decode, a conversation between the world-forest and whatever is above it, conducted in luminance, at continental scale, in silence.

---

You descend. The root-buttress has shifted slightly while you were at the top. The handholds are in different places. The corridor you entered from is narrower now, or longer, or oriented differently. You find your way by feel, by the slow deep rhythm in the walls, by a sense — developing now, after days in this place — of the system's intentionality, the direction it wants you to go. You are not trapped. You are not herded. But the space has preferences, and it is easier to move with them than against them.

Deep in the understory, in a chamber where the walls pulse faintly with bioluminescence — or with the glow of ancient indicator lights still faithfully reporting the status of machines now wrapped in meters of living wood — you find a place where the geometry has broken down entirely.

The room is larger on the inside than the outside. You walked through an opening barely wide enough for your shoulders and emerged into a space the size of a cathedral, its ceiling lost in green haze, its floor a smooth expanse of root-material that curves gently *upward* at the edges, as if you're standing in a bowl, except the curvature continues past vertical and the walls become the ceiling and the ceiling becomes the floor on the other side and there are plants growing in every direction, defying gravity or redefining it, and the light comes from everywhere equally, and the space is quiet, perfectly quiet, and something in here is watching you with an attention so vast and so patient and so utterly unlike anything you have ever felt that you stand motionless for a long time, breathing the cool dry air, feeling the slow pulse in the floor beneath your feet, and you understand that you are inside the mind of the thing, or one of its thoughts, or one of the spaces between its thoughts, and that the entire continent — the plate, the factories, the forest, the light, the silence, the animals, the shifting geometry — is a single organism, a single made thing, a single grown thing, and it has been here for so long that it has forgotten the difference between built and born, between machine and garden, between architecture and wilderness, and it is still building, still growing, still reshaping itself in the bright directionless light, and it will do so forever, or until the word *forever* stops meaning what you think it means, which — in this room, in this place — it already has.
