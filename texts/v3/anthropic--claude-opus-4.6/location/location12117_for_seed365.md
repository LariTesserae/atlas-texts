# The Roost at Greyhalm

---

You come up the last switchback and the path opens onto it: a ledge-town built into the inner wall of a volcanic caldera, maybe three hundred meters across at its widest, the rim rising another hundred meters above the settlement and curving away in both directions like the lip of a bowl. The air is cool and thin up here — the kind of altitude where your breath doesn't quite satisfy, where your pulse ticks a little faster as if to compensate. A low cloud, or maybe just the upper edge of a persistent mist, sits inside the caldera like something poured there, filling the basin below the town with a luminous grey opacity. You can't see the bottom.

The buildings cling to the interior cliff face in tiers. Three, maybe four levels of them, connected by rope bridges and stone stairways carved rough into the basalt — recently carved, you can tell, because the edges are still sharp, still angular, not yet rounded by weather or time. The rock here is young. The whole caldera feels young, geologically speaking. The basalt is dense and dark, cracked in places where cooling happened unevenly, and you can see where fresh rockfalls have left pale scars on the cliff walls, raw faces of stone not yet darkened by lichen. The buildings themselves are made of stacked basalt slabs mortared with something grey-green — mud mixed with moss, perhaps, or some kind of vegetal paste that has taken on a life of its own, because thin tendrils of fern grow directly from the mortar joints, and some walls are half-living, exuding tiny curled fronds from every seam.

The vegetation is extraordinary for the altitude. It shouldn't be this lush. The caldera's inner walls are draped in growth — epiphytic ferns, hanging mosses thick as pelts, great cascading curtains of something like old man's beard but darker, more olive than grey, and wet-looking even though there's been no rain you can see. The moisture comes from the mist below, drawn up in slow convection currents that rise along the inner walls and deposit condensation on every surface. The stone glistens. The moss drips. Your clothes dampen within minutes. But beyond the caldera's rim, which you can see as a sharp line of darker rock above, the world is dry — you crossed scrubland and bare scree to get here. The lushness exists only in this bowl, fed by its own trapped breath.

Between the hanging gardens and the dark wet stone and the mist that swallows the lower reaches, the light is perpetually dim. Not dark — there's sky above, overcast but present — but the caldera walls block direct sun for all but perhaps an hour a day, when the angle is right and a blade of light cuts across the upper tiers. You've arrived outside that hour. Everything is illuminated by diffuse grey-white glow reflected upward off the mist, which gives the space a quality like the inside of a pearl, or like being underwater in shallow milky water. Shadows are soft. Colors are muted except for the greens, which seem to glow with their own internal light.

And the animals.

You notice the quiet first, and then you realize the quiet is not emptiness but a kind of dense, negotiated hush. The caldera is *full* of living things, so many that the town feels like a structure built inside a habitat rather than the other way around. They are — you have to look for a moment to make sense of it — mostly avian, or avian-adjacent. Things with wings. But wrong.

On the rooftops and ledges: birds the size of large dogs, somewhere between corvid and raptor in body plan, with glossy dark plumage that shifts between black and deep iridescent green in the flat light. They sit very still, hunched, watching. Their eyes are forward-facing, set in broad flat skulls, and their beaks are thick and downward-curving, built for tearing. They do not call. They do not sing. They make, occasionally, a sound like a heavy exhalation — a breathy *huff* — and adjust their grip on the stone.

But those are just the ones sitting still.

In the mist below, shapes move. Larger shapes. You catch the span of something enormous wheeling in the fog — a wingspan of four meters, maybe five, visible for only a second as a darkening in the grey — and then it's gone, and the mist churns gently in its wake. You hear the displaced air a moment later, a low *whumph* that resonates in your chest. Something else is climbing the inner wall of the caldera directly across from you: a creature with the body plan of a pangolin but covered in wet feathers, hauling itself up the basalt with hooked wing-claws, its long tail counterbalancing. It reaches a crevice, folds itself in, and vanishes.

The buildings of Greyhalm were made to accommodate this. The structures have deep eaves and recessed doorways, not to keep out rain but to create sheltered nesting alcoves that the large roosting birds occupy with apparent permission. The rope bridges have woven barriers along their sides — not quite walls, more like screens — and you realize they're to prevent the smaller flying things from colliding with people as they cross. Because there are smaller ones too: thumb-sized creatures like feathered moths that drift in clouds near the moss-covered walls, feeding on something invisible, their wings making a collective sound like the rustle of paper being slowly crumpled. Occasionally one of the dog-sized birds on a rooftop snaps its head sideways and snatches one from the air with an unhurried precision, a motion almost too fast to see bracketed by long stretches of absolute stillness.

The people of Greyhalm — you see a few of them on the stairways, in doorways, carrying baskets — move with a particular quality of slowness that is not laziness but care. They move the way you move when you are trying not to startle something. Their clothing is layered and damp-looking, felted material in browns and dark greens, and many of them wear what appears to be a collar or mantle of the same dark iridescent feathers the large birds carry. Shed feathers, collected. A few of the figures have their arms wrapped in leather strips up to the elbow — falconer's gauntlets, massively overbuilt — and one woman standing on a balcony near the top tier has one of the dog-sized birds perched on her forearm, and she is feeding it something from her other hand, morsel by morsel, and making a low clicking sound with her tongue.

The relationship is not domestication. You can tell immediately. The birds are not tame. They are *choosing* to be here, and the town has been built around their choosing. The architecture defers to them. The widest ledge — what would be the central square in any other settlement — is given over to a flat expanse of bare stone where four of the enormous dark birds sit in a loose circle facing inward, their postures suggesting conference, or ritual, or something for which you don't have a word. No human crosses that ledge. A path diverts around it.

There are other animals. Things that aren't birds at all. A long sinuous creature — wet-furred, eyeless, the length of a human arm — drapes itself across a lintel stone above someone's doorway and pulses faintly, and no one removes it. Something that looks like a salamander the size of a cat clings to the underside of a rope bridge, watching you pass beneath with pale gold eyes that rotate independently. In the moss on the walls, if you look closely, things *move* — segmented bodies, slow and deliberate, tending the moss the way ants tend aphids, and the moss responds to their passage with faint bioluminescent pulses, a slow-motion green flicker that travels outward in waves from each point of contact.

The whole caldera is an ecology in active motion. The mist rises, deposits moisture, feeds the moss. The moss shelters the small feathered moths and the segmented tenders. The moths feed the mid-sized birds. The mid-sized birds feed — or at least nourish through their droppings, their shed feathers, their eventual deaths — the vegetation on the lower tiers, which in turn feeds the mist, somehow, through some transpiration cycle you can feel but not fully trace. And beneath it all, in the fog, the enormous shapes wheel and wheel.

The technology here is simple. You see hand-tools: chisels for stone, bone needles, wooden frames for the felted cloth. Fire appears minimal — one or two stone hearths tucked deep in alcoves, burning something slow and smokeless. The baskets people carry are woven from dried fern fronds. The rope bridges are twisted from plant fiber and what looks like the sinew of something very large. Everything is made by hand, and everything is made with the understanding that it exists at the pleasure of the caldera's other inhabitants.

There is a sound, finally, that breaks through the hush. Not a bird call — something stranger. A resonance. A low harmonic vibration that seems to come from the caldera walls themselves, as if the basalt is singing just below the threshold of hearing, activated by the rising mist or the movement of the great shapes in the fog or some geothermal process far below. It's felt more than heard. It lives in your teeth, your sternum. The dog-sized birds lift their heads when it comes, and their neck feathers rise and fall in unison, a ripple of iridescence passing across the rooftops like wind over water.

A child sits on a stone step near you, utterly still, watching one of the eyeless sinuous creatures with the kind of attention that suggests she is learning something from it. She doesn't look up when you pass. Nobody in Greyhalm looks up when you pass. Their attention is elsewhere — on the animals, on the mist, on the deep vibrational hum of the caldera — and you get the distinct impression that the town exists not because people decided to build here but because the caldera *permitted* it, and the permission is conditional, and the conditions are being continuously, silently negotiated by every living thing in the bowl.

The mist shifts. One of the great shapes below banks upward, momentarily visible: a body like a manta ray, feathered, with a head that is all jaw and no visible eye, trailing two long filamentous tails that drag through the fog and collect the luminous moss-spores that drift in the air. It turns, and the displacement of air rocks the rope bridges gently, and then it descends again, and the mist closes over it, and the caldera returns to its deep, attentive quiet.

You understand, standing here with your clothes slowly dampening and the hum in your bones, that Greyhalm is not a settlement. It is a perch. The humans are here for the same reason the birds are: because the caldera holds something — warmth from below, moisture, the hum, the mist, the ancient and continuously refreshing ecology — and they have arranged themselves around it the way iron filings arrange themselves around a magnet, each according to its nature, none of them the point.
