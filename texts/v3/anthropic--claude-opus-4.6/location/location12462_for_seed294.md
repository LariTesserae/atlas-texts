# The Drowned Colosseum

---

The city was not built on water. The city was built on the idea of water — on the expectation that water would come, would always be coming, would never stop. And the water came, and it has not stopped, and the city holds.

---

Imagine a plateau that once stood high above a plain. Millions of years of erosion have worn the plain into a shallow sea and worn the plateau into something like a broad, flat-topped mesa — except the mesa is entirely artificial. Every surface, every contour, every shelf and channel and basin was placed there by hands or forces that understood what erosion would do to them. The plateau is engineered to be ancient. Its walls slope at the exact angle that deep time would have given them anyway. Its terraces descend in the exact curves that a billion years of rainfall would carve. The builders did not fight entropy; they pre-agreed with it. They built the ruin first.

And now the sea has risen almost to the top.

---

You are treading water — cold water, wood-smoke grey, faintly alkaline on the lips — at an elevation that would once have been a thousand meters above the surrounding land. The plateau-city rises another forty or fifty meters above you at its highest points, but most of it is awash. You can see the upper levels of the structure breaking the surface across a region the size of a small city: broad curving walls like the ribs of something titanic, pocked and smoothed by ages of submersion, their material somewhere betweenite andite — notite, not ite, something else, something with the density of stone and the faint pearlescence of shell, something that looks *grown-then-hardened*, as though the builders poured a living calcium into molds and let itite. Let itite. Let it become what it became.

The surfaces that break the waterline are the color of old bone. Below the waterline, which you can see because the water is just clear enough in the top two meters, they darken to grey-green, then to black where they descend beyond sight.

There is no vegetation. Not a film of algae, not a strand of kelp. The water is almost sterile of plant life — too alkaline, maybe, or the wrong mineral balance, or perhaps nothing photosynthetic has found its way here yet. This is a world where biology exists but flora does not. What lives here is animal, or animal-adjacent, or something that moves and feeds and has no name.

---

The light comes from above but only barely. The sky is the color of wet cement, a permanent overcast so thick and uniform it seems structural, as though the clouds are another layer of the built environment. It is not dark — you can see clearly for perhaps three hundred meters in every direction — but there are no shadows. Everything is lit the same pale nothing. The water reflects the sky and the sky reflects the water and together they create a dimensionless grey field in which the pale bone-curves of the city's upper architecture seem to float without weight.

It is cold. Not freezing — the water is perhaps four or five degrees above the point where your body would begin to shut down — but cold enough that you notice it constantly, cold enough that it becomes the texture of everything. The air is the same temperature as the water. There is no wind. And yet —

---

And yet everything is moving.

This is the central paradox of the place: it is quiet, sparse, dim, cold, ancient, still in every way that the senses first report — and it is in *absolute chaos*.

The water moves. Not in waves — waves would require wind, and there is none — but in surges, upwellings, lateral currents that appear and reverse without warning. You feel them against your legs: sudden cold pushes from below, strange horizontal tugs that spin you thirty degrees and then release. The surface of the water is never flat. It shivers and humps and develops brief, purposeless standing waves that collapse as quickly as they form. The city is doing something to the water. Or the water is doing something to itself. Deep in the flooded lower levels — the vast submerged majority of this place — some process is at work. You can't see it. You can only feel its effects: the unpredictable currents, the occasional deep *thrum* that travels through the water and into your chest, a pressure-Loss, a pressure-shift, as though enormous volumes are rearranging themselves far below.

The built surfaces move too, though this takes longer to notice. A wall that curved to your left ten minutes ago now curves to your right. Not because you've drifted — you can triangulate against the higher spires — but because the wall has *changed*. The architecture of the upper city is in slow, constant reconfiguration. Sections of wall sink and others rise. Channels open where there were none. A flat terrace you were swimming toward develops, over the course of two minutes, a basin at its center, which fills, which overflows, which drains through a gap that wasn't there before the basin formed. The material itself seems to soften and re-harden, as though the city is still being built — or rather, as though the city never stopped being built, as though construction is its permanent state, and what you see at any given moment is just one frame of an endless architectural convulsion.

The changes are not random. They are too *responsive* for randomness. When water surges into a new channel, walls adjust to guide it. When a current reverses, a terrace tilts to compensate. The city is *managing* the chaos of the water, or the water is *expressing* the chaos of the city, or both are locked in a conversation so old and so complex that the distinction between them has eroded along with everything else.

---

Now the animals.

They are everywhere and nowhere. You see them in glimpses. A long pale shape, boneless and translucent, undulating just below the surface fifty meters to your east — it could be three meters long, or thirty, depending on whether what you're seeing is one body or several traveling in contact. Something dark and compact surfaces near a wall, grips the bone-colored material with appendages that look like thick, wet fingers, hauls itself a meter out of the water — you see a body like a leather sack, no obvious head, no obvious eyes — and then the wall beneath it *shifts*, and the creature drops back into the water without urgency, as though this happens constantly, as though the city shrugging it off is simply part of the rhythm.

There are things in the deeper water. You feel them more than see them. Occasionally, when a current pulls you near one of the open channels where the submerged city drops away into darkness, you sense large displacements below — the water moves in ways that suggest bodies of significant mass passing through the flooded corridors and chambers beneath you. Once, looking down through a gap in a terrace, into a vertical shaft filled with dark water, you see a pale light deep below — bioluminescence, maybe, or reflected glow from some deeper architecture — and across that light, a shadow passes. It takes a long time to pass. The shadow has parts to it, structures, articulations. It is not simple.

The fauna here does not school or flock. The animals are solitary, or in pairs, or in loose associations that dissolve and reform as the currents shift. There are enough of them that you are never alone — something is always visible or almost-visible, surfacing or submerging or hauling itself briefly onto the reconfiguring walls — but they are spread across the full volume of this half-drowned architecture, and the effect is of a sparse, unhurried population going about incomprehensible business in a city that is itself going about incomprehensible business, and neither party explaining itself to the other, and neither party needing to.

Some of the animals seem to eat the walls. You watch a cluster of small dark creatures — each one the size of a closed fist, each one slightly different in shape, as though they are not so much a species as a *theme* — clinging to a freshly-surfaced section of wall, their bodies pulsing rhythmically. Where they cling, the wall material is paler, thinner. Are they consuming it? Is the city producing material for them to consume? When the wall begins to sink again, they release and drift, and something long and ribbonlike passes beneath them, and two of the fist-creatures are gone, and the others drift on, pulsing, unconcerned.

---

The sound.

Mostly there is the water: the small lapping and gulping sounds of a surface in complex motion, the hollow echoes that bounce between the exposed walls and amplify certain frequencies into something almost tonal. A low, continuous murmur that is not quite white noise because it has too much structure — it rises and falls in cycles that might correspond to the city's reconfigurations, or to tidal patterns, or to nothing you can identify.

Occasionally, from below, a sound that travels through the water and arrives in your bones before your ears: a deep, resonant *knock*, as though two massive sections of submerged architecture have come together. Then silence — or rather, the return of the murmur, which you had stopped noticing until the knock interrupted it.

The animals are almost silent. The fist-creatures make a faint clicking. Something far away — direction uncertain — produces a single low note every few minutes, like a horn blown underwater, felt more than heard.

---

There is no technology here. The city is built — totally, exhaustively built, every surface placed and shaped — but it is not technological. There are no mechanisms, no moving parts in the mechanical sense, no gears or conduits or screens. The walls reconfigure through some property of their material, some capacity for softening and rehardening that might be chemical, might be biological, might be something the builders understood and you do not. The city is a made thing that behaves like a living thing, or a living thing that was made, and the distinction may not apply here.

You cannot find the builders. You cannot find evidence of the builders beyond the city itself. The city is the evidence. The city, and the fact that the animals are here — were they brought? Did they evolve in the flooded chambers? Are they descendants of the builders? — and the fact that the water is here, and that the city was built to receive it, was built already in the shape that the water's arrival would give it, as though the builders saw the flood coming from geological time away and said: *yes, fine, we will build the ruin and the reef and the machine all at once, and we will leave, and it will run*.

It runs.

---

You are cold. The current shifts again and pushes you toward a curved wall that is, at this moment, rising — new material surfacing, pale and slightly warm to the touch, the only warmth in this place. You rest your hand against it. You feel it hardening under your palm. Below you, in the dark water, something large adjusts its position, and the current changes again, and the wall begins to sink, and you push off and swim, and the city rearranges itself around you, endlessly, and the pale shapes drift, and the grey light holds, and the water moves in ways that no water should move, and you are a visitor in a conversation between the built and the flooded that has been going on for longer than you can conceive and will go on for longer still, and nothing here needs you, and nothing here resents you, and the cold is patient and the chaos is patient and the animals are patient in their alien way, and the city builds and dissolves and builds, and the water rises, and it has always been rising, and the city has always known.
