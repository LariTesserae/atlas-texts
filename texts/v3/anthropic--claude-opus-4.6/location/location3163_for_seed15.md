# The Cistern

---

The first thing is the sound. Before your eyes adjust to the brightness, the sound finds you — a roar of water reverberating off curved walls, amplified and folded back on itself until it becomes a physical thing, a pressure in the sternum, a hum in the teeth. It is the sound of a place built to hold water and being tested by water and, so far, holding.

You are underground. Not deep — the ceiling is perhaps twelve feet above you, and somewhere beyond it lies the surface world that poured everything it had down here. The chamber is warm, almost bathwater warm, and the warmth comes from the water itself, which has traveled some long sunbaked distance across stone and tile before arriving at this place with all its captured heat still radiating.

---

The cistern is entirely constructed. Every surface — floor, walls, the gently vaulted ceiling — is made of pale, hand-cut limestone blocks, each one about the size of a man's torso, each one fitted against its neighbors with a precision that speaks of years of patient labor. The joints between them are sealed with a plaster that has gone slightly pink with mineral deposits. There is no natural ground here. No exposed earth, no bedrock breaking through. Someone chose this shape, calculated these angles, dressed these stones, and lowered them one by one into a foundation trench. The tool marks are still visible in places — the diagonal hatch-marks of a point chisel, the smoother planes left by a drove. The masonry is young. The edges of the blocks are still sharp. The plaster has not yet begun to crack or flake in any serious way. This place was finished recently — years, not decades.

The cistern is a single elongated chamber, perhaps forty feet long and fifteen wide, with a barrel-vaulted ceiling that channels sound the way a cupped hand channels a whisper — except nothing here is whispering. At the far end, a wide channel enters through a rectangular opening near the ceiling, and from this opening a thick sheet of water pours in, unbroken and glassy for about a foot before it shatters against the surface below and throws up a constant mist. This is the inflow — rainwater collected from some vast catchment above, funneled through clay channels and stone gutters and delivered here with a force that says the rains have been extraordinary. The water is not clear. It carries a faint amber tint, the color of tea, stained by whatever soils it crossed on its way down. But it is not muddy. It has been filtered through something — gravel beds, sand traps, settling basins — some system of pre-treatment that speaks to careful engineering even if the tools that built it were simple.

The water level is high. Very high. It reaches to within three feet of the ceiling at the deepest point, which means you are standing on a narrow limestone ledge — a service walkway — that runs along one wall about two feet above the current waterline. The ledge is wet. Everything is wet. The mist from the inflow coats every surface in a thin, trembling film, and the brightness — God, the brightness — makes every droplet into a tiny lens.

---

The light enters through a series of vertical shafts cut into the ceiling at regular intervals. There are five of them, each about eight inches across, angled to catch what must be fierce overhead sun. They function almost like spotlights, throwing five columns of hard white light down through the mist-thickened air and into the water, where they scatter and bounce off the pale stone bottom and fill the entire chamber with a sourceless, directionless luminosity. The water glows from within. The wet walls glow. The underside of the vault catches reflected light rippling off the water's surface and writhes with caustic patterns — those shivering nets of light that you see on the bottom of swimming pools, except here they play across carved stone overhead, and they move constantly because the surface of the water is never still. The inflow sees to that. There is always agitation, always a slow procession of waves traveling the length of the chamber and reflecting off the far wall and returning, interfering with new waves, creating a surface that breathes and shifts and sends its restless light-patterns crawling across every surface.

The combined effect is almost overwhelming. You are standing inside a lamp. The air itself seems luminous, the mist catching and diffusing the shaft-light until there are no true shadows anywhere, only gradients of brilliance — brighter near the shafts, merely bright everywhere else. Your own shadow, when you move, is so faint it barely registers.

---

The water fills most of the volume of this room. That is the essential fact of the place. This is not a room that contains some water; it is a body of water that is barely contained by a room. The surface stretches before you, warm and amber-tinted and restless, and it is close enough to touch from the walkway ledge if you knelt and reached down. Below the surface, the light penetrates deep enough to reveal the floor — the same pale limestone, but here the blocks are larger and laid in a herringbone pattern that gives the water a subtle sense of direction, as if it were meant to flow toward the outflow grate set into the far wall at floor level. Through this grate, which is made of bronze bars gone green at their bases, the water drains at a rate that is currently much slower than the rate of inflow, which is why the level keeps rising. You can see it. If you watch the waterline against the wall for a minute or two, the dark wet margin creeps upward by a visible fraction. The cistern is filling. Whether someone intended this, whether this is normal operation during heavy rains or a situation trending toward overflow, you cannot tell. But the water is warm and rising and loud, and the ledge you are standing on grows narrower by the hour.

The sound. Come back to the sound. The inflow striking the surface makes a continuous, complex roar — not a single note but a layered thing, with a bass foundation from the sheer volume of water hitting water, a mid-range hiss from the spray, and a high, almost crystalline ringing where the splash-back strikes the stone walls of the inflow channel. Underneath this, a lower frequency: the resonance of the chamber itself, the vaulted ceiling acting as a sounding board, giving back a sustained drone that you feel more than hear. And then there are the waves — when they slap against the walls, there's a rhythmic punctuation, a wet clap that repeats at irregular intervals as wave trains from different sources converge and reinforce. If you speak, your voice is swallowed. If you shout, you might hear yourself as a thin addition to the general roar. The cistern is having its own conversation, and it is not interested in yours.

---

Life is almost absent. Along the service ledge, in the cracks where plaster meets stone, a few threads of pale green have taken hold — some stringy, light-starved algae or perhaps a primitive water moss, feeding on the mist and the reflected brightness. It grows in wispy patches no larger than a coin, and it is the only living thing visible in the entire chamber. No fish in the water. No insects hovering over the surface. No birds nesting in the light shafts. The water is too new here, too recently arrived from its catchment, and the cistern itself is too new, too clean, too much a sealed system for any ecology to have established itself. The algae is the pioneer, the first faint claim that biology is making on this engineered space, and it will be years before anything else follows. For now, the cistern belongs to water and stone and light and sound, and nothing else.

---

At the near end of the chamber, opposite the inflow, a stairway rises. It is steep and narrow, cut into the wall with the same careful masonry, and it climbs toward a square opening through which you can see — if you crane your neck — a wedge of sky so bright it is almost colorless. The stairs are slick. Handholds have been chiseled into the wall at shoulder height, simple rectangular notches just deep enough for four fingers. The chisel marks are fresh here too. Someone carved these recently and has not yet worn them smooth with use. Water streams down the stairs in a thin sheet, runoff that couldn't be directed into the main channels, and it makes the ascent treacherous.

From this angle, looking up the stairs, you can hear something else beneath the roar of the cistern — a different quality of water-sound coming from above. Heavier. Broader. The sky in that wedge of brightness is not clear; it has a silvered quality, a density. It is raining up there. It is raining enormously, and has been raining, and this cistern with its rising water and its overwhelmed bronze grate is doing exactly what it was built to do, taking the world's excess and holding it, keeping it, storing the warm rain in its stone belly for the dry time that must, eventually, come.

But for now, you stand on the narrowing ledge in the bright mist, and the water rises, and the sound fills every corner of the vaulted space until the air itself feels thick with it, and the caustic light-patterns chase each other across the ceiling like something alive, like the only living things this place will permit for a long time yet.
