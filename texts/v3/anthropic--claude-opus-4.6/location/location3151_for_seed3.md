# The Plateau City of Kethane-Above

---

There is a city at the top of the world that should not be there, and something about how it sits on its plateau tells you it knows this.

The rock beneath it is young. Not young in the way geologists mean — not merely Cenozoic or Quaternary — but *actively* young, in the way a child is young, in the way an unfinished sentence is young. The plateau is still deciding what shape it wants to be. You can see this at the edges, where the cliffs drop away into an ashen cloudbank thousands of meters below: the stone there has a quality not quite like stone. It is dense, yes, and it holds weight, but it has a faintly nacreous sheen, like the interior of a shell, and if you press your hand flat against it and wait long enough — a minute, five minutes — you feel something that is not quite warmth and not quite vibration. A consideration. The mountain is thinking about whether to become basalt orite or something that has no name yet. At certain overhangs near the eastern drop, the rock has resolved into a pale crystalline structure that rings faintly when struck, but fifty meters further along the same cliff face it is chalky and soft, and you can push your thumb into it. Both of these states feel temporary. Both of these states feel like drafts.

The plateau itself extends perhaps nine kilometers at its widest, narrowing at its southern end to a crooked peninsula of stone that juts out over nothing. The elevation is severe — well above any tree line that could theoretically exist, high enough that the air should be thin and brutal. And it *is* thin. You notice it in your breathing: a slight insufficiency, a sense that each inhale returns about eighty percent of what you asked for. But the cold is not what you'd expect at this height. It's cool, certainly — the kind of cool that settles into your joints if you stand still, that makes you want to keep your hands in your pockets — but it lacks the killing edge of true alpine exposure. Something about the plateau generates or retains a modicum of warmth. Perhaps it's the rock's indecision, some geothermal ambiguity leaking upward. Perhaps it's the city itself.

---

## The City

Kethane-Above is not a beautiful city. It is an *industrious* one.

It sprawls across the plateau's upper surface in a pattern that suggests several centuries of construction by people who were always slightly more concerned with function than with planning. The buildings are dense where they cluster — four, five, six stories of dark brick and riveted iron plate, factory towers with chimneys that exhale a thin, continuous smoke the color of pencil graphite — but the clusters are separated by expanses of bare rock, empty lots, gaps where the plateau's surface dips or buckles in ways that discouraged construction. So from above — if you could see it from above, which is difficult because the light is so poor — the city would look like a handful of dark seeds scattered across a pale palm. Nodes of habitation and industry connected by roads that sometimes become catwalks that sometimes become cable-car lines strung across unavoidable chasms where the plateau has fissured.

The architecture has an aesthetic of pragmatic layering. Older stone foundations — hand-cut from the plateau's own indeterminate rock, which means some walls have slowly changed composition since they were laid, developing veins of unfamiliar mineral, sweating faintly iridescent moisture in wet weather — support newer frameworks of iron and steel. Smokestacks rise from processing works whose exact product is unclear. You hear the deep rhythmic *thudding* of stamp mills from the northern quarter, a sound so constant it becomes geological, becomes something your bones interpret as the plateau's own heartbeat. Conveyor systems rattle in enclosed channels between buildings. Somewhere, a steam whistle sounds at intervals that don't correspond to any human shift schedule — every forty-seven minutes, or every ninety-three, or sometimes twice in rapid succession and then not again for hours. No one in the city seems to regard this as unusual.

Power lines sag between iron poles, carrying current from generators that tap into the plateau's thermal ambiguity. The electricity is not entirely reliable. Lights flicker. Machines sometimes run at the wrong speed — not faster or slower, exactly, but at speeds that feel *adjacent* to their intended operation, like a word pronounced with the wrong emphasis.

The light here needs explaining.

## The Light

The sun does not behave correctly over Kethane-Above. Or rather — the sun may be behaving perfectly well, and it is the space above the plateau that has its own ideas.

The sky is visible, technically. You can look up and see something that is structured like a sky — clouds (low and the color of old pewter, always, dragged across the plateau by winds that rarely reach the ground), and beyond them a luminance that suggests a sun exists somewhere in the general vicinity. But the light that reaches the plateau has been *edited*. It arrives at perhaps a third of its expected intensity, with its spectrum shifted toward the blue-gray end, so that everything in Kethane-Above exists in a state of permanent overcast dusk. Noon looks like an hour before sunset. Actual sunset is nearly indistinguishable from full night, which itself is not quite dark — a faint phosphorescence rises from the rock in places, especially where it is youngest and least decided, giving the empty lots and cliff edges a ghostly, submarine glow.

The city compensates with electric arc lamps on the main thoroughfares and gas lighting in the older quarters and the warm orange rectangles of windows in the tenement-factories. But these light sources seem to struggle against a dimness that is not merely the absence of light but something more active — a quality of the air itself, as though the atmosphere at this altitude is slightly too *thick* for the photons to navigate cleanly. Shadows are soft and multiple. Nothing casts a hard edge.

In this permanent twilight, the citizens of Kethane-Above go about their business.

## The Vegetation

It shouldn't grow here, and it knows it shouldn't grow here, and it grows here anyway with what can only be described as defiant enthusiasm.

The cool temperatures and altitude should limit things to lichens and maybe some brave alpine grasses. Instead, the plateau supports a vegetation that is *lush in the wrong way*. In the gaps between building-clusters, in the empty lots and along the fissure edges, a dense, low-growing groundcover has established itself: plants with thick, succulent leaves of a deep blue-green that are slightly too geometric in their arrangement, slightly too uniform in their coloring. They grow in rosettes the size of dinner plates, overlapping, covering bare rock in quilted mats that are springy underfoot and faintly damp to the touch. These are not any species that grows below. They may not be species at all in the taxonomic sense — they seem to emerge from the young rock itself, as though the plateau's geological indeterminacy extends upward into the biological.

Where moisture collects — in the depressions, in the cracks along building foundations, in the rust-streaked channels beneath the conveyor systems where condensation drips — the groundcover thickens and sends up stalks. These stalks produce not flowers but structures: compact, spiraling forms like tiny architectures, pale gray-green, that open and close with changes in humidity. After a rain (and it does rain, intermittently, quick cold showers that seem to materialize from the low clouds without much warning), the stalks open fully and the empty lots become small forests of intricate, reaching forms that hum faintly at a frequency just below comfortable hearing. The hum attracts the fauna.

Mosses of an unusual character grow on the northern faces of buildings, thick and almost furlike, dark as wet slate. In one district — the workers call it the Soft Quarter — the moss has grown so dense on the older stone buildings that the structures appear upholstered, their hard angles rounded into organic curves. Rain-slick, these mossy facades gleam like the pelts of enormous sleeping animals.

## The Fauna

The animals of Kethane-Above are *everywhere*, and they are *wrong*, and the citizens have made their peace with this so thoroughly that pointing it out marks you immediately as a newcomer.

Start with the most visible: the ridge-soarers. These are large — wingspans of two meters or more — and at first glance they look like some kind of raptor, broad-winged and deliberate in their soaring patterns over the plateau's edge. But they don't flap. They have never been observed to flap. They hang in the updrafts that the plateau's cliffs generate, yes, but they also hang in still air, motionless, at heights and angles that suggest they are pinned to the sky rather than flying through it. Their bodies, seen up close (they sometimes alight on factory rooftops, folding those vast wings with a papery sound), are dense with a fine, dark fur rather than feathers, and their faces are flat, almost discoid, with multiple small eyes arranged in a pattern that — if you stare at it too long — begins to suggest a counting system. They eat the spiral-stalk structures, plucking them delicately with long, prehensile tongues. Dozens of them are visible at any given time, drifting over the city like slow, patient kites.

Then the ground-dwellers. In the empty lots and along the cable-car routes, colonies of small burrowing creatures have excavated elaborate tunnel systems in the plateau's soft, undecided rock. These animals are compact, six-legged, covered in a pearlescent integument that shifts color with their mood or possibly with the composition of the rock they're currently inhabiting. They are bold around humans. They sit at the entrances to their burrows and watch passersby with an attention that feels evaluative. The tunnels they dig have an effect on the plateau's geology: the rock around their colonies tends to *resolve* faster, settling into definite mineral states, as though the act of being excavated forces the stone to make up its mind. Some engineers in the city have begun deliberately introducing the creatures to sites where they need stable foundations. This is called, without irony, *asking the rock nicely*.

In the Soft Quarter, among the mossy buildings, there are climbers — elongated, multi-jointed things with too many limbs that move through the thick moss the way fish move through water, surfacing occasionally to regard the street below with eyes that are entirely black and entirely reflective. They are harmless. Probably. They have never harmed anyone. But they watch, and sometimes at night their reflective eyes catch the plateau's phosphorescence and the mossy walls appear to be full of cold, drifting stars.

There are others. Things that live in the condensation channels beneath the factories, translucent and serpentine. Swarms of tiny flying creatures — not quite insects, their bodies too soft, too luminous — that emerge after rain and drift through the streets in loose clouds, landing on people's shoulders and hair and dissolving there, leaving small patches of warmth. Larger things, glimpsed at the plateau's edges, that might be part of the cliff face itself — might be geological features that move when unobserved, whose positions shift between one visit to the edge and the next.

The citizens feed the burrowers. They name the ridge-soarers. They leave the climbers alone but speak to them sometimes in passing, the way you might speak to a neighbor's cat. The relationship between the city and its fauna is one of mutual, slightly wary cohabitation, inflected with a tenderness that neither side would probably acknowledge.

## The Reality Problem

Because it must be addressed. Because it is the thing that makes Kethane-Above what it is, and it is getting slowly, patiently worse.

The plateau is not finished forming. This has been established. But it is not merely the *rock* that is unfinished — it is the *rules*. The fundamental parameters of the place — how gravity works, how time passes, how cause relates to effect — are still being negotiated. And the negotiation is slow. Geological in its patience. But it is ongoing, and its effects accumulate.

Examples:

In the factory district, there is a street where gravity pulls slightly to the left. Not enough to knock you over. Enough that after walking it for five minutes you realize you've been unconsciously leaning, and that the rainwater in the gutters flows not downhill but *lefthill*, diagonally, pooling against the eastern building facades. The workers on this street have adapted: their carts have asymmetric wheels. Their doorways are subtly trapezoidal. They do not discuss it.

Near the southern peninsula, distances become unreliable. Two buildings that appear to be thirty meters apart will take you several minutes to walk between, and then the return trip takes only seconds. This is not consistent; the dilation and compression of space seems to drift, like weather. A surveyor was once commissioned to map this district and produced seventeen maps, all accurate, all contradictory. She left Kethane-Above shortly afterward and could not clearly remember having been there. Her maps remain, framed, in the municipal office, arranged in a circle on the wall because no one could agree on which was most correct.

Time, locally, sometimes *layers*. In the oldest part of the city — the original settlement core, where the buildings are squat stone bunkers dating from a period no one can precisely identify — you occasionally see translucent figures going about tasks that don't correspond to the current moment. They are not ghosts. They are, the city's one philosopher maintains, *temporal echoes*: moments that the plateau, in its unfinished state, has failed to properly sequentialize, so they persist, stacked, visible under certain light conditions. They cannot interact with the present. They are more like watermarks in the fabric of time. Workers on their way to shifts that ended decades ago. Rain that fell last year or next year, wetting streets that are currently dry. A woman hanging laundry on a line strung between two buildings that have since been demolished, her sheets snapping in a wind that you cannot feel.

These phenomena are *slow*. They do not erupt or assault. They accrete. A new spatial anomaly might take years to become noticeable. The gravity on that leftward-pulling street has been increasing by imperceptible degrees for as long as anyone can remember, and the best estimates suggest it will be another century before it becomes a serious structural problem. The temporal layering in the old quarter has thickened very gradually; a hundred years ago, the echoes were reportedly rare and faint, and now they are commonplace and nearly opaque. The trend is clear. The pace is glacial. The city adapts.

And there are newer phenomena, stranger ones, that suggest the reality-loss is becoming more creative as it matures. In the last decade, a district near the western cliff edge has begun experiencing what residents call *material sympathy*: objects left in close proximity for long periods begin to share properties. A iron wrench left on a wooden table for a month will, at the end of that month, have a wrench-shaped area of the table that is slightly metallic, and a wrench whose handle has become slightly fibrous and warm to the touch. This has complicated manufacturing. It has also produced some remarkable alloys that cannot be replicated anywhere else, which is one of the things Kethane-Above exports on the cable cars that descend — impossibly, through the cloudbank, to wherever *below* is — and which constitutes a meaningful part of the city's economy.

The plateau is building itself, and it is building itself *wrong*, or at least building itself by rules that are not the rules that govern the world below. And the city has been built on top of this process, and the city's machines thud and stamp and the city's chimneys smoke and the city's people walk streets that may not obey Euclidean geometry next week, and there is a strange, stubborn dignity in all of it — in the decision to *stay*, to adapt, to build asymmetric wheels and frame contradictory maps and feed the burrowing creatures that make the rock behave.

## The Sound

Stand in the center of Kethane-Above — in the market district, say, where the building-clusters press closest together and the streets are narrow enough to hear conversations from upper-floor windows — and listen.

The stamp mills in the north, always. That deep, metronomic thudding, felt as much as heard.

The cable-car lines singing in their own private wind, a high metallic drone that Doppler-shifts as the cars move.

The ridge-soarers, who are silent in flight but who, when gathered on rooftops, produce a low, collective crooning sound — not melodic, not unpleasant, something like a choir warming up in a language with no vowels.

The condensation dripping from factory infrastructure onto the groundcover below: a patter, irregular, musical.

The hum of the spiral-stalks after rain, that sub-audible pressure on the inner ear.

And under everything, if you are quiet and patient enough to hear it — under the industry and the animals and the weather — the sound of the plateau itself. A frequency so low it is almost tectonic. The sound of rock that has not yet decided what it is, vibrating with potential, with the slow, unresolved chord of its own becoming.

The citizens hear this sound. They have always heard it. They call it *the ground note*. They say you stop noticing it after a few years.

They are lying. You never stop noticing it. You simply learn to build your life on top of it, the way you build a city on a mountain that hasn't finished being a mountain: with stubbornness, with rivets, with a willingness to live inside an unfinished sentence and call it home.
