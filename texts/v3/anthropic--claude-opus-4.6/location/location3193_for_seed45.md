# The Spine of Becoming

---

You stand on a ridge that did not exist an hour ago.

Beneath your feet the rock is warm — uncomfortably so, the heat rising through material that hasn't yet decided what kind of stone it wants to be. It is dark, vitreous in places, chalky and granular in others, as though the earth is still drafting itself, still arguing over its own composition. The ridge runs roughly east, roughly west — *roughly*, because its bearing shifts as new material shoulders up from below, warping the line of it, adding vertebrae to a spine that is assembling itself in real time. You can feel the low, pressurized grinding underfoot. Not a tremor. Something more deliberate. The sound of a planet pulling itself into shape by its own bootstraps.

There is almost no light. What there is comes from below and within: a dull, bruised orange that seeps through fissures where the ridge's newest stone hasn't fully sealed, the glow of heat still trapped in mineral that hasn't cooled, hasn't crystallized, hasn't committed. The sky above is not black so much as *absent* — a void ceiling, starless or perhaps merely obscured by the thermals that ripple up from the forming terrain. Occasionally something catches enough rising heat to luminesce: a plume of silicate dust that sparks faintly gold for a second before dispersing, or a spall of newly fractured obsidian that catches the fissure-light and sends it scattering in a brief, accidental constellation. Then dark again.

The scale of what is happening around you reveals itself slowly, because the darkness is miserly with its information. You have to earn the view. But as your eyes adjust, or as the fissures breathe open a little wider, you begin to understand: this ridge is one arm of something enormous. To the north, several kilometers away, another formation is rising — not a mirror of this one, but a response to it, a counter-argument in basalt and pumice. Between the two ridges, a vast depression is either forming or being left behind, a valley-in-negative, the space that remains when two masses of rock decide to go elsewhere. The depression is floored with material so new it still radiates: a faintly glowing plain of mineral slurry, not molten exactly, but not solid either. Pasty. Grudging. Cooling at its surface into a skin that buckles and re-forms as pressure shifts beneath it.

To the south, the ridge drops away into a field of what you might call rubble if rubble weren't a word for things that were once whole. This was never whole. These are the first attempts — slabs of proto-rock that were thrust up and fell back, that cooled wrong or fractured under their own unresolved chemistry. Some of them steam faintly. Some have developed a crust of pale mineral bloom, a whisper-thin efflorescence that is the closest thing to life this place has ever produced: not alive, not even pre-alive, but *doing something* with the chemical gradients available to it, crystallizing into feathery, branching patterns that look like frost or lichen but are neither. In the near-darkness, you catch them only as pale suggestions against the warm dark stone. They are the densest thing growing here, and they are barely anything at all.

No water. Not a trace. Whatever volatile processes are driving this geology, they are anhydrous — the chemistry of deep silicates, of metallic oxides finding their lattice-partners in the dark. The air, if it is air, is thick and dry and tastes of hot iron and something sulfurous but not quite sulfur. Something adjacent. A mineral you don't have a name for because it hasn't been catalogued yet, because it is being invented right now, in this place, by processes that haven't reached equilibrium.

The sounds: mostly the deep, sub-audible pressure of formation. A bass note you feel in your sternum more than hear. Occasionally, a sharper report — a crack, a split, as a cooling surface contracts faster than the mass beneath it can accommodate. These sounds carry in the thick air, arriving from different distances, different directions, giving you a sonar-map of activity you can't see. A sharp snap from the northeast, maybe a kilometer out: a new fissure opening. A long, descending groan from below and to the left: the ridge extending itself, adding mass, finding its footing. And between these events, a silence that isn't really silence but is so low and so constant that it functions as one. The tinnitus of a planet in labor.

There are structures. You almost miss them.

On a shelf of rock forty meters below the ridgeline, where a relatively stable spur has held its shape long enough to cool to a matte charcoal grey, someone — *something* — has built. Or rather, has *placed*. A series of cairns, perhaps a dozen, arranged in no pattern you can discern from above. They are simple: stacked stones, the largest at the base, each one narrowing to a single balanced capstone. The materials are local, indistinguishable from the terrain except in their arrangement. They could be ten years old or ten minutes old. In a landscape this dynamic, there is no meaningful difference. What strikes you is their stillness. Everything else here is in motion — rising, cracking, cooling, re-forming — and these cairns simply *sit*, asserting the radical proposition that a thing can be put somewhere and left. You scan for anything else: a path, a tool, a mark on stone. Nothing. Just the cairns. Just the quiet insistence that something with intent passed through here and responded to the chaos by stacking rocks.

You cannot tell who or what made them. There is no fauna — nothing moves under its own volition except the geology itself. No insects in the hot dark air, no organisms in the mineral bloom, no tracks in the powdery regolith that collects in the lee of new formations. The cairns are orphaned from their maker, artifacts without a culture, grammar without a language. Perhaps they are not even structures. Perhaps they are a natural phenomenon you are misreading, some crystalline stacking behavior that mimics intention. But you don't believe that. The capstones are too carefully chosen, too well-balanced. Someone was here. Someone may come back, if the ridge holds long enough, if the shelf doesn't shear away or get buried under the next upwelling.

And the upwellings are constant. That is the dominant fact of this place: its *dynamism*, its refusal to hold still. As you watch — and you have no way to measure time here, in the dark, with no celestial markers — the ridge beneath you gains perhaps a meter of elevation on its eastern end. You feel it as a slow tilt, a shift in the angle of the ground. New stone pushes up through the existing surface, steaming, and for a moment you can see it in its own light: a dark red, almost maroon, shot through with veins of something silver-bright that could be native metal. It is beautiful in the way that anything is beautiful when you catch it in the act of becoming itself. Then it cools. The red dims to brown dims to the same undifferentiated dark as everything else. The silver veins oxidize, or react, or simply lose their luster as they find thermal peace with the surrounding rock. Another vertebra added. The ridge extends.

Meanwhile, the valley between the ridges has changed character. The pasty mineral floor has developed a network of cracks — not erosion, not yet, nothing has had time to erode — but thermal contraction patterns, the skin tightening as it cools. From your vantage point the cracks form a rough polygonal tessellation, each cell a few meters across, each one slightly domed as the material beneath continues to degas. In another age, in another place, water might pool in these cracks. Here there is only the faint whistle of escaping volatiles — methane maybe, or carbon monoxide, or something unnameable — adding a thin, intermittent soprano to the bass-and-silence soundtrack.

There is something unusual about the way light behaves here, and it takes you time to understand it. The fissure-glow doesn't simply illuminate; it seems to *thicken* the air around it, as though the photons are reluctant to leave their source, as though the darkness here has a viscosity that impedes them. Light pools and clings. It doesn't spread in beams or wash evenly across surfaces. It *hangs*. Near the brightest fissures, you can see the air itself as a faintly luminous murk, as though the heat and the mineral dust and the outgassing have conspired to create a medium that traps radiance like amber traps insects. This means that distance and darkness are not simple functions of each other here. A formation a hundred meters away may be utterly invisible while a crack in the rock two kilometers distant might glow in a bubble of its own trapped light, seemingly close, seemingly reachable, an ember floating in the void.

This is how you see the far ridge: not as a continuous line but as a scattered archipelago of glow-points, each one marking an active fissure, each one wrapped in its own halo. They appear to shift position as new fissures open and old ones seal. The effect is like watching a city from a great distance, if the city were being demolished and rebuilt continuously, its lights appearing and vanishing according to the logic of geology rather than human habit.

You sit on the warm stone. There is nothing else to do. Nothing to eat, nothing to drink, nothing to interact with except the cairns below you and the planet-in-progress all around. The heat is persistent — not searing, not dangerous, but *insistent*, the kind of warmth that makes your skin feel tight, that presses in from all sides, rising from below and radiating from the newly formed rock faces that surround you. The air itself is warm in the lungs. Even the darkness feels warm, if darkness can feel like anything.

And still the ridge grows. And still the valley cracks. And still the distant formation rises, unseen except for its fissure-lights, building toward a shape that neither it nor you can predict. This is a world at the very beginning of its geological autobiography, writing its first sentence, crossing it out, writing another. Nothing here has been tested by time. Nothing has been weathered, rounded, softened, or reduced. Everything is raw and angular and fresh, still hot from its own creation, still uncertain of its own permanence.

The cairns are the only things that have been *finished*.

And even they are waiting.
