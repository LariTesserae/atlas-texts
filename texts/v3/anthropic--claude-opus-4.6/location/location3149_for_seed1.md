# The Weeping Flats of Therr

---

The cold comes first. Not a wind-cold, not a biting thing—a constitutional cold, a temperature that belongs to the ground itself, radiating upward through your feet and into the architecture of your bones. The air is maybe two degrees above freezing, and it has been two degrees above freezing for so long that the concept of warmth has become geological. This is a place that finished cooling a long time ago and has no memory of heat.

The landscape is flat. Not gently rolling, not approximately level—*flat*, in the way that only profound age and relentless water can make a thing flat. This was once something else. Mountains, perhaps. A plateau, perhaps. Whatever it was, erosion took it apart over spans of time so long that the rock itself forgot its original shape. What remains is a plain that extends in every direction to a dim horizon, a vast lowland basin maybe fifteen or twenty miles across, composed of dark stone worn to a glassy smoothness. The surface is not soil. There is no soil here, no organic layer, no humus or loam. It is bare mineral substrate—basalt, mostly, polished by uncountable millennia of water action into something that resembles black glass. In places the stone is striated with pale veins of quartz and feldspar, exposed by the slow subtraction of softer material around them, standing slightly proud of the surface like the veins on the back of an ancient hand.

Water is everywhere. Not flowing—not exactly. It *weeps*. It rises. The water table here is inches below the surface, and the stone sweats with it constantly, a film of moisture that coats everything and catches whatever dim light filters down from the sky. In the shallow depressions—and there are thousands of them, tens of thousands, shallow bowls and elongated troughs worn into the basalt over eons—the water collects into still, dark pools that range from puddle-size to the area of a small lake. They are all shallow. Ankle-deep, shin-deep at most. Their surfaces are perfectly still unless disturbed, reflecting the gray overhead light like dark mirrors set into the stone. The water is mineral-rich and faintly alkaline, carrying dissolved silicates that give it a very slight viscosity, so that when something moves through it, it parts reluctantly, almost gelatinously, and the ripples propagate slowly, persisting long after whatever made them has gone.

There is no vegetation. None. Not a lichen on the stone, not a strand of algae in the pools, not a trace of chlorophyll or cellulose in any direction. Whatever grew here—if anything ever grew here—is gone so thoroughly that even its chemical signature has been washed clean. The stone is bare. The water is clear. The mineral world has achieved a purity that feels less like absence and more like a completed process, a distillation that ran until nothing remained but the irreducible substrate.

And yet.

---

The light is dim. The sky overhead is not cloud, exactly, but it is not clear. It is a uniform luminous gray, the kind of sky you get when the atmosphere itself is saturated with moisture and diffuses all incoming light into a sourceless glow. There is no sun visible. There is no direction to the light. Shadows barely exist—things are lit from everywhere and nowhere, which gives the landscape a flatness of illumination that makes distances hard to judge. The horizon is a smeared line where dark wet stone meets gray wet sky, and the transition is so gradual that in some directions you cannot tell where one ends and the other begins.

The dim light does something strange to the pools. Because the water is so still and so dark and so reflective, the pools do not look like water—they look like holes. Apertures in the surface of the world, leading somewhere else. Looking into one, you see the gray sky reflected back at you, but the reflection is *too deep*. It has a spatial quality that a few inches of water over dark stone should not produce. Lean closer and the reflection seems to recede, as if the surface of the pool is the surface of a lens, and behind it is a column of gray atmosphere extending downward as far as the real atmosphere extends upward. This is not an optical illusion. Or if it is, it is an extremely persistent one that does not resolve with proximity or contact. Put your hand into the pool and the water is cold and wet and only a few inches deep—your fingers touch smooth stone at the bottom. But the *reflection* of your hand seems to reach down much farther, moving through a space that doesn't correspond to the physical depth. Pull your hand out and for just a moment, just a sliver of a second, the reflection lags behind the movement. Your hand is in the air. Its reflection is still in the water. Then it catches up.

This happens every time. It is not random. It is *reliable*, which is somehow worse.

---

Now: the sound. The Weeping Flats are loud. This is the first thing that seems wrong, because the landscape appears so still, so settled, so terminally quiet. But the noise is enormous. It fills the basin from edge to edge, a complex layered roar that you feel in your sternum and your sinuses and the plates of your skull.

The primary sound is a deep, resonant hum—industrial in character, mechanical in its steadiness, somewhere around 40 to 60 hertz, right at the threshold where hearing becomes feeling. It comes from below. From the stone itself. The basalt substrate of the Flats is vibrating, has been vibrating, and the vibration is not geological in any recognized sense. It is too regular. Too sustained. It has the quality of a turbine, or a dynamo, or some vast engine turning at a constant speed somewhere beneath the surface. The stone conducts the vibration efficiently—press your palm to the wet basalt and you can feel it thrumming, a steady mechanical pulse that does not waver or fluctuate. It has been measured (by whom? there is no one here to measure it, and yet the knowledge exists, the way knowledge sometimes does in places like this, accrued and ambient) at a consistent frequency for as long as anyone or anything has been aware of it. It does not stop. It does not change. It is the fundamental note of this place.

Layered over the hum are higher harmonics—ringing tones that emerge and sustain and slowly fade, only to be replaced by others. These come from the pools. The vibration of the stone sets the water resonating, and because each pool is a different size and shape and depth, each one produces a different overtone, so that the entire basin functions as an enormous array of tuned resonators, thousands upon thousands of them, all singing simultaneously in the dim gray light. The effect is like standing inside an organ the size of a county. The tones interact, producing interference patterns, beat frequencies, moments of startling consonance that shift into dissonance and back again as you move through the landscape—because your position relative to the pools changes which harmonics reach you and how they combine. Walk ten feet in any direction and the chord changes. The sound is always loud, always present, always complex, but it is never the same twice in the same place.

There is no built structure here. No factory, no engine house, no turbine hall. There are no artifacts of any kind—no bolts, no piping, no cable runs, no foundations. The industrial sound has no visible source. The hum comes from the stone and the stone is natural, worn smooth by water and time. But the sound is *not* natural. It is too precise, too mechanical, too constant. Something is running beneath the Flats. Something has been running for a very, very long time. The stone has eroded to its current state *around* whatever is producing this vibration, which means the mechanism—if it is a mechanism—predates the current surface by geological spans. It was here before the stone was flat. It was here when there were mountains. It may have been here before the stone.

No one built this. Or: no one who is still here built this. Or: the building and the builder are no longer distinguishable from the landscape. These are all the same statement, expressed in different registers of unease.

---

The fauna.

In a place with no vegetation, no organic substrate, no visible food chain—the Flats are saturated with life. This is the density you feel immediately upon entering the basin. Not empty space. Not bare stone. *Packed*. Every pool, every wet surface, every film of mineral water is occupied.

They are not animals in any way that maps cleanly to terrestrial taxonomy. They have no bilateral symmetry. They have no central nervous system, or they have several, or the concept does not apply. They are cold—as cold as the water, as cold as the stone—and they move with a slow, deliberate fluidity that suggests something between muscle and hydraulic pressure.

The most common form is a flat, roughly circular organism, maybe two to eighteen inches across, that clings to the submerged surfaces of the pools. Call them *plates*. They are dark, nearly the same color as the basalt, and from a distance they are almost invisible—just a slight textural variation on the pool floor. Up close, they are intricate. Their upper surfaces are covered in fine radial ridges, like the grooves of a vinyl record, and these ridges are *tuned*. The plates vibrate in sympathy with the pools they inhabit, and each plate is shaped to resonate with a specific harmonic of the pool's fundamental tone. They eat sound. They metabolize vibration. The ridges convert acoustic energy into something—heat, maybe, or a chemical process in the tissue beneath the ridges—and this is their entire ecology. They feed on the hum.

There are hundreds of plates in every pool. Thousands. They tile the bottom like a living mosaic, overlapping slightly at their edges, each one trembling faintly with the sound it is absorbing. When you wade into a pool (and you can; the water is shallow, the plates will shift aside with slow reluctance, like stepping on a carpet of wet leather), you can feel them underfoot—smooth, firm, faintly warm compared to the stone beneath them, the only warm thing in this entire landscape. The warmth is a byproduct of their metabolism. They are converting the engine-hum into heat. They are the only evidence that the hum is doing anything at all.

The second form is more disturbing. In the air above the pools, in the saturated gray atmosphere, there are shapes that move. Call them *echoes*, because that is what they most resemble—not solid organisms but visible disturbances in the air, regions where the moisture and the sound interact to produce something that has extent, boundary, and behavior. They look like heat-shimmer given volume. Each one is roughly the size of a dog or a large cat, an oblong distortion that drifts above the pools at varying heights, sometimes a few inches above the water, sometimes six or eight feet up. They move slowly, purposefully, following paths that seem related to the sound-geography of the basin. Where harmonics converge, the echoes gather. Where the sound thins, they disperse.

They are loud. Each echo carries its own acoustic signature, a droning buzz or a clear sustained tone, and as they move through the basin they contribute to the overall sound-field, adding and subtracting from the chord. When two echoes pass close to each other, their sounds interact—sometimes harmonizing into something almost beautiful, sometimes clashing into a harsh dissonant shriek that makes the surface of nearby pools shudder. The plates below respond to these events, shifting and realigning, adjusting their ridges to the new acoustic conditions. This is the predator-prey dynamic of the Flats, if you can call it that: the echoes modulate the sound, the plates respond to the modulation, and the vibration of the plates feeds back into the pools and changes the harmonics that the echoes navigate by. It is a closed loop, an ecosystem powered entirely by the hum of an invisible engine.

There are other forms. In the deeper pools—the ones where the reflections go wrong, where the spatial distortion is most pronounced—there are things that move *in the reflection*. Not in the water. In the image. You can see them when you look down into the pool: slow dark shapes sliding through the reflected sky, moving at depths that the pool does not physically contain. They cast no shadow on the real pool floor. They disturb no water. They exist only in the optical layer, the too-deep image-space behind the surface. Occasionally one of them will rise toward the surface of the reflection, and when it does, the real surface of the pool dimples inward, as if something heavy is pressing up from below, and the water around the dimple grows colder—noticeably, sharply colder, cold enough to sting—and the harmonic tone of the pool shifts downward by a half-step or a whole step, a pitch-drop you can feel in your jaw.

They never break the surface. They approach it, the dimple deepens, the cold intensifies, the tone drops—and then they recede, slowly, back into the false depth of the reflection, and the pool gradually returns to its baseline state. This happens irregularly, across the basin, in different pools at different times. It has the cadence of breathing. Or of something testing a boundary.

---

The weirdness is not localized. It is not a single anomaly or a discrete phenomenon. It is the *condition* of the Flats—the default state, the way things work here. Reality in this basin has settled, over its immense age, into a configuration that is stable but *wrong*. Not broken, not chaotic—wrong the way a key is wrong for a lock, functional in itself but not fitting the system it appears to belong to.

The reflections that are deeper than the pools. The sound that has no source. The organisms that are made of vibration and condensation rather than cells. The engine that no one built, that predates the surface, that may predate the stone. These are not glitches. They are the rules. The Flats are not malfunctioning. They are functioning according to a physics that is *almost* ours but has diverged at some fundamental level—perhaps at the level of what counts as matter, or what counts as alive, or what direction time flows in the layer just below the surface.

Stand at the center of the basin, if you can find it. The hum is loudest here, so deep it is almost subsonic, a pressure in the chest. The pools in every direction are shivering with harmonics. The plates beneath the water are warm and trembling. The echoes drift overhead in their slow processional. The sky is gray and sourceless and close. The wet black stone extends to the horizon in every direction, polished to a mirror finish by time so deep it has no name.

Look down at your reflection in the nearest pool.

It is looking slightly to the left of where you are looking.

It has been doing this the entire time. You only just noticed.

The reflection blinks. You did not blink. It resumes matching your movements, precisely, as if nothing happened. The water is three inches deep. The stone is smooth beneath it. The hum continues. The plates shift. An echo drifts past, trailing a long minor tone.

Everything here is very old and very patient and very full.
