# The Ridgeback

---

There is a spine running through the world. Not a metaphorical one — a literal dorsal ridge of exposed planetary mantle, dark pyroxene and olivine crystalline masses thrust up through the crust over geological ages until the formation stands nearly eleven kilometers above the surrounding hardpan. From orbit it would look like a suture, a seam where the world tried to split and changed its mind. Up close, standing on it, you understand that the world didn't change its mind at all. It is still deciding.

The Ridgeback stretches roughly two thousand kilometers northeast to southwest. Its width varies — in places only forty kilometers across at the base, in others swelling to two hundred where subsidiary ridges branch off like ribs. The summit line is not a single peak but a continuous crenellated wall of dark mineral towers, wind-carved arches, and plateaus that have been flattened by millennia of thermal cycling. At this elevation, the air is warm — warmer than it should be. Heat radiates from the rock itself. The mantle material still carries its ancient thermal load, releasing it in patient exhalations that make the upper Ridgeback ten or fifteen degrees warmer than the surrounding lowlands despite the altitude. Stand on a bare slab of exposed harzburgite at midday and you feel heat from above and below simultaneously, the sun pressing down while the planet breathes up. At night the stone still hums with it, a deep warmth that never quite dissipates.

There is no water here. Not in any conventional sense. No rivers, no springs, no dew condensing on cold surfaces because the surfaces are never cold enough. The atmosphere carries almost no humidity — the surrounding lowlands are salt-crusted basalt flats, and whatever ocean this world once had retreated long ago into its lithosphere or was lost to space. Very occasionally — once every several years — a thermal convection event off the Ridgeback's flanks will pull enough trace moisture from the lower atmosphere to form a thin haze that clings to the mid-elevations for a few hours. The locals call this "the ghost." It never precipitates. It simply thins and vanishes, leaving behind a faint mineral smell, like hot quartz.

## The Cities

They built here because of the heat.

Not for comfort — for industry. The geothermal gradient along the Ridgeback's summit is extraordinary, a resource as fundamental to the settlements as coal was to the earliest industrial cities of other worlds. The towns are spaced far apart, sixty to a hundred kilometers between them, each one anchored to a particularly productive thermal vent or a section of ridge where the crystalline structure channels heat efficiently toward the surface. Between the settlements: nothing. Bare rock. Wind-polished mineral expanses. The occasional collapsed structure from a venture that didn't take. The distances feel immense because there are no visual referents — no trees, no water features, no color variation except the slow shift from dark olivine green-black to rust-brown iron oxide to the pale cream of exposed feldspar in a shear zone.

The cities themselves have a vertical logic. They are built into the Ridgeback rather than on top of it, occupying natural fissures, crevasses, and excavated galleries that descend into the warm rock. From above — from one of the high mineral towers — a settlement looks like a series of slots cut into a dark surface, glowing faintly amber and industrial white from internal lighting. The structures are dense where they exist but the settlements are not large. Five thousand people, perhaps fifteen thousand in the biggest ones. They use materials quarried from the Ridgeback itself: cut pyroxene blocks, polished olivine panels, frameworks of extracted iron and nickel from the ultramafic host rock. Everything has a dark, dense, mineral character. The architecture is heavy. Load-bearing walls a meter thick. Deep-set windows with shutters designed not for cold but for the abrasive dust storms that occasionally rake the summit.

The industry is thermal conversion. Deep shafts drop into the hottest sections of the Ridgeback where turbine assemblies — massive things, iron and ceramic, you can sometimes hear their low harmonic through the rock if you press your ear to a wall — generate power from the temperature differential between the deep rock and the cooler (relatively) surface air. This power runs everything: smelting operations, mineral processing, atmospheric extraction plants that pull trace elements from the thin air, and most importantly the rail lines. A single rail track, narrow gauge, heat-warped and constantly maintained, runs the length of the Ridgeback connecting the settlements. The locomotives are electric, powered by overhead catenary wires strung between pylons bolted into the ridge. They move slowly. Everything here moves slowly.

## The Fauna

This is the strange part.

The Ridgeback has no vegetation — none. Not a lichen, not a microbial mat, not a trace of photosynthetic chemistry anywhere on its surface. The surrounding lowlands may harbor something in their deep salt crusts, subsurface chemotrophs perhaps, but the Ridgeback itself is sterile mineral. And yet it supports animals.

They are called, inadequately, *clingers*. They are arthropod-like in their segmentation and their multiple limbs, but they are warm — not warm-blooded in any way that would be recognized, but thermally active, drawing heat directly from the rock through specialized ventral surfaces. They range in size from a few centimeters to roughly the mass of a large dog. Their metabolism is not well understood. They appear to subsist on the mineral surface itself, scraping and ingesting iron-rich olivine crystite and excreting a fine powder of depleted silicate. Whether they derive energy from some chemical process within the rock or from the geothermal heat directly — whether they are, in some sense, *eating the warmth* — remains a subject of debate in the settlements.

They are everywhere. Not densely — you might cross a kilometer of bare ridge and see nothing, then round a mineral tower and find thirty of them plastered to a south-facing wall, their flat bodies pressed against the warm dark stone, motionless. They are the color of the rock they inhabit, near-perfect camouflage, and sometimes the first indication of their presence is the soft scraping sound of their mouthparts working at a crystal face, a sound like distant filing. The largest ones move with glacial patience, shifting position over the course of hours, following thermal gradients across a rock face the way a sunflower follows the sun.

There are other things. Aerial creatures — elongated, ribbed, lighter-than-air in the way that suggests internal gas bladders — drift on the thermal updrafts that the Ridgeback produces along its flanks. They feed on the clingers, or on something the clingers produce, dropping down onto the rock faces with a quiet precision that is almost gentle. They are rarely seen above the settlements. The locals say they don't like the vibration from the turbines.

And there are burrowers. Rarely seen at all. Holes appear in the rock — not geological features but biological ones, round-bored and smooth-sided, descending at angles into the Ridgeback's warm interior. Something lives down there, something that excavates. The bore-holes are occasionally found intersecting mining shafts. The miners report no encounters. Only the holes, the smooth walls, and a faint smell like hot copper.

## The Sound of It

The dominant quality is silence with a floor. The Ridgeback is not truly quiet — the turbines far below produce a sub-bass drone that you feel in your teeth more than hear with your ears, and the wind at this elevation is constant, a steady scouring pressure from the northeast that produces a continuous low whistle across mineral formations. But these sounds are so constant that they register as silence. They are the baseline. Against them: occasional metallic sounds from the settlements. A hammer on cut stone. The whine of a rail car approaching from very far away, audible for twenty minutes before it arrives. The scrape of clingers. And once in a while, from the bore-holes, a deep resonant tone, brief and sourceless, as if something very large exhaled a single note through a stone pipe. The locals have a name for this too. They call it "the question." They say it has been asking for as long as anyone has lived here, and no one knows what it's asking, and no one expects an answer.

---

## The Light

The sun here is a G-type, a little yellower than Sol, and at this elevation the atmosphere is thin enough that its light arrives with a certain sharpness — shadows are hard-edged, colors saturated where color exists. The palette is narrow: black-green olivine, rust, cream, the amber of settlement lighting, the white-gray of the industrial infrastructure. At sunrise and sunset the mineral towers cast shadows that stretch for kilometers across the summit plateaus, and the crystalline structure of the rock catches the low-angle light in ways that produce brief flares of deep green or amber within the stone, as if the Ridgeback remembers something molten. The effect lasts only minutes. Then the angles change and the rock returns to its flat, patient darkness.

---

## The Pace

Nothing happens quickly here.

The thermal output of the Ridgeback changes on scales of centuries. The settlements grow by a building per decade, maybe two. The rail line has been extending southward for a hundred years; they add a few kilometers annually. The clingers shift their positions over hours and reproduce over years — or possibly decades; no one has observed the full cycle. The bore-holes deepen at rates measurable only with instruments left in place for months. The erosion of the summit — the wind-carving that has given the mineral towers their current forms — operates on timescales that make human presence a brief interruption, a flicker of amber light in the slots of the dark rock, here and then not, while the Ridgeback continues its slow exhalation and the question continues to be asked from somewhere below, patient and resonant and unanswered.

The people here are adapted to this pace. They speak slowly. They plan in generations. A common greeting translates roughly as "what are you building that you won't finish?" It is not a melancholy question. It is an expression of solidarity — an acknowledgment that everyone here is engaged in something larger than themselves, something measured against the Ridgeback's own clock. The rock was here before them. The warmth was here before them. The clingers were here before them, pressed flat against the stone, eating heat. The question was being asked before anyone was here to hear it.

They are simply the newest thing on the Ridgeback, and not, they suspect, the strangest.
