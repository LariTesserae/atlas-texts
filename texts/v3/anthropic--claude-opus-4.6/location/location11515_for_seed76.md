# The Drybloom Plateau

---

There is a continent up here. That is the first thing to understand. Not a mountain range, not a highland — a continent, lifted. The plateau sprawls beyond every horizon, a single slab of weathered sandite and compressed mineral strata that was old before anything had a name, raised two miles above the lowlands by tectonic arguments that resolved themselves so long ago that even the geology has forgotten the details. The edges, somewhere out there beyond the curvature of distance, drop away in cliffs that no one has stood at. The interior knows nothing of those edges. The interior knows only itself: an expanse so vast it generates its own weather, its own atmospheric patterns, its own slow and Continental breathing.

And it is bright. God, it is bright.

The sky is a pale, depthless lilac — not blue, not quite, because at this altitude the atmosphere has thinned enough to let through wavelengths that lower places filter out. The sun, or whatever the star is, sits high and white and merciless in its clarity, casting light that doesn't so much illuminate as *saturate*. Shadows are sharp-edged and violet. Every surface that faces the sky is bleached and radiant. The light has a physical quality, a pressure. You don't see *into* the distance here — the distance comes to you, the plateau revealing itself in every direction with an almost aggressive legibility. Hundred-mile sightlines. The curvature of the world faintly suggested at the farthest edges of perception, where the land hazes not from moisture but from sheer accumulated *air*, the stacked translucencies of atmosphere resolving into a thin amber band at the horizon.

No water. Almost none. The rivers died ages ago, or perhaps they never ran here at all. What remains is their memory: vast dry channels carved into the plateau surface, sinuous and branching, their beds smooth and polished by millennia of wind. These ancient riverbeds are pale grey against the rust-ochre of the surrounding stone, and they wander the plateau like a diagram of something, like a nervous system drawn by a cartographer who was mapping a body that no longer lives. In a few places — very few — there are seeps. Not springs. Seeps. Places where some deep geological moisture, not water exactly but a mineral-rich condensation, a lithic sweat, reaches the surface and darkens a palm-sized patch of stone. You could walk for days and find three.

And yet.

---

The vegetation is *extravagant*.

This is the paradox that defines the plateau, the thing that makes it what it is rather than another dead highland. The plants here do not use water. Or rather, they use so little of it that the distinction becomes philosophical. They are lithotrophs, in a sense — organisms that have solved the problem of aridity so completely that they've moved beyond it into a different metabolic kingdom. Their chemistry runs on mineral absorption, on atmospheric gases pulled from the thin air, on photosynthetic processes so efficient that they extract what they need from light and stone and the barest molecular hint of moisture in the wind.

And they are *lush*. Lush in the way that only things freed from a fundamental constraint can be.

The dominant form is something between a fern and a coral — broad, fractal fronds of deep blue-green that grow directly from the rock, no soil required, their holdfasts eating slowly into the sandite over centuries. They spread in vast, scattered colonies, each colony a single organism perhaps, connected underground by thread-thin mineral filaments. A colony might cover an acre, its fronds rising waist-high and dense enough to wade through, their surfaces slightly cool to the touch, slightly rough, like suede, like the tongue of a cat. And then the colony ends, and there is open stone for a mile. And then another colony begins, different in its exact shade of blue-green, its fronds slightly wider or more tightly curled, adapted to its particular patch of substrate. This is the rhythm of the plateau: dense life, then spacious emptiness, then dense life again, scattered across the expanse like an archipelago of vitality in an ocean of bare stone.

Between the colonies, other forms. Wire-thin stalks that rise six or seven feet from cracks in the rock, perfectly straight, perfectly rigid, topped with spherical seed-heads that glow a faint amber in the sunlight — bioluminescent, or simply so pigmented that they seem to produce their own light. They stand alone or in loose groups of three or four, widely spaced, casting thin vertical shadows that stripe the stone like sundial markings. There are mats of something lichen-like — silver-grey, thick, spongy — that coat sheltered rock faces and the insides of the old riverbeds, living on nothing visible, growing with geological patience. There are low, mounding shrubs the color of dried blood that cluster in the lee of larger stone formations, their leaves (if they are leaves) so small and dense they give the plant the texture of velvet, of moss, of something almost animal.

And everywhere, across every exposed surface of stone that isn't occupied by something else, there is a thin, nearly invisible film — a microbial crust, a living skin on the plateau itself, photosynthetic and ancient and patient beyond comprehension. It changes the color of the rock subtly. It gives the plateau its particular warm ochre, its faint undertone of living amber. Without it, the stone would be grey. With it, the whole continent glows.

---

The wind is the only sound, mostly, and even the wind is quiet.

At this altitude, with the atmosphere thinned and the plateau so flat and vast that there's nothing to channel or compress the air, wind moves differently. It doesn't gust. It *shifts*. A slow, planetary exhalation that changes direction over the course of hours, carrying with it the faintest smell of mineral dust and the dry, almost electric scent of the lithophytic fronds. You hear it only when it passes through a colony — a soft, continuous hiss, like static, like white noise turned down to the threshold of perception. The fronds don't sway. They're too rigid for that. But their surfaces vibrate at some frequency you feel more than hear, a textural hum that enters through the skin.

Otherwise: silence. The silence of altitude, of distance, of a place where sound has too much space to fill and gives up trying. Your own footsteps on the stone are startling. The scrape of boot on sandite carries farther than it should, then dies, absorbed by sheer openness. If you stopped walking and held your breath, you might hear the plateau itself — a sub-auditory drone, the harmonic resonance of a continent-sized slab of stone vibrating at its fundamental frequency. You might imagine you hear it, anyway. It's the kind of place that makes you listen for things that aren't there.

---

The cool is surprising. You'd expect heat, in all this light, on all this exposed stone. But the altitude steals warmth as fast as the sun gives it. The air at chest height is cool — sweater weather, jacket weather if the wind is shifting — while the stone at your feet is warm to the touch, even hot where it's been in direct sun. This inversion creates a permanent thermal layer about knee-high, and the plants know it. The frond-colonies grow to exactly the height where cool and warm intersect, and no higher. The wire-stalks push through the warm layer into the cool, and their seed-heads are always slightly condensation-damp, the only moisture visible anywhere — tiny droplets that form in the early hours and burn off by midmorning. In the evening, when the stone begins to cool and the air cools faster, there's a brief period of thermal equilibrium when the entire plateau seems to exhale, and the microbial crust releases a faint, sweet, petrochemical smell that hangs in the still air for an hour and then is gone.

---

Fauna. Rare. But present.

Something lives in the deeper frond-colonies — something small, slow, probably cold-blooded or whatever the equivalent is for a metabolism built on this world's particular biochemistry. You won't see it. You'll see evidence: fronds with their edges chewed in precise geometric patterns. Small, perfectly round holes bored into the softer lichen-mats, the edges of the holes slightly darkened as if by secretion. Scat, if it is scat — tiny, dry, mineral-gray pellets clustered at the base of wire-stalks, indistinguishable from pebbles unless you look closely and notice they're too uniform, too round. Whatever it is, it's nocturnal or crepuscular, and it's small enough to move through the fronds without disturbing them. The colonies give no sign of its passage. It is perfectly at home.

Higher up — metaphorically, in the food web; literally, in the air — there are fliers. Rarely. Once a day, perhaps, you might catch a dark shape against the lilac sky, moving with the wide, slow, energy-conserving spirals of something that rides thermals for a living. At this altitude, thermals are broad and weak, and the fliers are correspondingly large — long-winged, silent, more glider than bird. They pass overhead without casting shadows you'd notice, and they do not call out. Whatever communication they have with one another, it doesn't involve sound. They are hunting for the small things in the frond-colonies, presumably, or for something else you haven't identified yet, something that lives in the riverbeds or under the lichen-mats or in the warm layer near the stone. The fliers are patient. They can afford to be. The plateau is vast, and time here is measured differently.

---

There is weather.

Not rain — there's nothing to rain. But the plateau is large enough to create its own pressure systems, its own thermal dynamics, its own atmospheric events. Dust storms, when conditions align — not violent, not sandblaster-harsh, but enormous in scope: walls of fine mineral particulate that rise slowly from the stone and migrate across the plateau at walking pace, hundreds of miles wide, turning the lilac sky amber and the bright light into a diffuse, directionless glow that erases shadows and makes the frond-colonies look like they're floating in golden fog. These storms last for days. The lithophytes thrive in them — the microbial crust absorbs the settling dust, feeds on it, incorporates the minerals. After a dust event, the plateau's color shifts slightly, a shade warmer or cooler depending on the composition of what was carried.

There are also inversions — moments when the thermal layers rearrange themselves and the cool air sinks to the stone while the warm air rises, and for a few hours everything is backwards. The fronds close. The wire-stalks lean imperceptibly toward the ground. The small things in the colonies come out, perhaps, confused or stimulated by the change, and the fliers descend to uncharacteristic altitudes, close enough to make out the ribbing of their wings. Then the layers re-establish themselves, and the plateau returns to its normal state, and the long bright quiet resumes.

There are light phenomena. Halos around the sun, caused by mineral crystals suspended at the top of the atmospheric boundary. Pillars of light on the horizon at dawn and dusk. And once, maybe, if you are here long enough and patient enough and happen to be looking in exactly the right direction at exactly the right moment, a false sunrise — a reflection of the sun off a distant sheet of exposed mineral, a flash so bright and precise that for an instant there are two suns, and the shadows of every wire-stalk double, and the frond-colonies are cross-lit, and the plateau reveals depths and textures and topographic subtleties that are invisible under single-source illumination. Then it passes, and you're not sure you saw it, and the quiet makes you doubt your own senses.

---

The erosion is profound. Not destructive — resolved. The plateau has been here for so long that everything aggressive has already happened. The river channels are smooth. The stone has been wind-polished to a matte finish. What remains of topographic variation — low ridges, shallow basins, the occasional isolated column of harder rock standing twenty or thirty feet above the surrounding flatness — is all soft-edged, rounded, patient. These forms are old beyond estimate. They were old when the lithophytes first took hold. They will be here when the lithophytes have evolved into something else entirely. The plateau is not eroding so much as *resting* — in dynamic equilibrium with the forces acting on it, losing material at exactly the rate it would need to lose it to maintain its current form indefinitely. It is a landscape that has arrived at itself.

The stone columns — the ones that rise above the flatness, scattered every twenty or thirty miles — are the closest thing to landmarks. They are rounded, vaguely anthropomorphic in the way that all weathered vertical stone is vaguely anthropomorphic, their surfaces streaked with lichen in patterns that almost but never quite resolve into meaning. The frond-colonies cluster thicker around their bases, finding something in the stone-shadow, some mineral difference, some protection. The wire-stalks are taller near them. The small animals concentrate here, probably. The fliers use them as navigation points, maybe. In the evening, the columns cast shadows that reach for miles across the plateau, thin dark fingers pointing east, and the temperature in those shadows drops noticeably, and the microbial crust in the shadow-path responds by changing color briefly — darkening, deepening — before the light returns in the morning and everything resets.

---

There is no sense of emptiness here, despite the spareness. The plateau is too alive for that, too active in its quiet way, too *present*. It is sparse the way a sentence can be sparse — not lacking, but chosen. Every frond-colony is exactly where it should be. Every wire-stalk is the right distance from every other wire-stalk. The empty stone between is not absence but interval — the rest between notes, the breath between words. The plateau has the quality of a thing that has been editing itself for geological time, removing what it doesn't need, refining what remains, until what is left is precise and irreducible and, in its vast, bright, quiet way, complete.

You are small here. Not insignificantly small — not cosmically dwarfed — but *proportionally* small, the way a single word is small in a very long and very good book. Present. Meaningful. But not the point. The plateau does not notice you. It is too busy being itself, running its cycles, feeding its lithophytes, hosting its invisible fauna, generating its weather, conducting its slow mineral commerce with the deep rock and the thin bright air. You are welcome to walk it. You are welcome to stand among the fronds and feel their textural hum and watch the fliers spiral and wait for the dust to come. But you will not change it. It changed itself, long ago, into exactly what it is, and what it is, is enough.
