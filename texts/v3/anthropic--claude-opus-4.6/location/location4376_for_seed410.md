# The Cupola of Flies

---

It is a room at the top of everything.

Not a room anyone planned, exactly. More a room that happened — the way a bubble happens in glass, the way a knot happens in metal under heat. Somewhere in the uppermost architecture of a structure so vast its lower floors have their own weather, a convergence of ducts, exhaust risers, cable troughs, and load-bearing lattice created a pocket. An accidental void, roughly ovoid, no wider than six meters at its broadest, tapering to crevices at every edge where the infrastructure closes back in on itself. The walls are not walls. They are the *backs* of things — the unpainted sides of turbine housings, the exteriors of sealed relay cabinets, the corrugated sheaths of ventilation channels. Everything is riveted, welded, bolted, caulked. Every surface has a serial number or a temperature warning stenciled on it in faded orange. There is no natural material here. No stone, no earth, no wood. The floor is a grating — actually three overlapping gratings from different construction phases, none quite aligned, creating a layered mesh through which you can see dim recesses descending five or six meters into further mechanical darkness.

The elevation is absurd. You can feel it in your inner ear, in the faint pressure differential that makes your breathing shallow, though the air is pumped, processed, and delivered through a dozen competing systems, none of which were designed for this space. One duct exhales warm industrial exhaust that smells of ozone and lithium. Another breathes cooler air tinged with something ferric. A third, cracked along its seam, weeps condensation — the only water here, a slow irregular drip that traces a rust-line down a transformer housing and collects in a shallow depression in the lowest grating, a puddle no bigger than a hand, trembling constantly from the vibrations transmitted through the structure.

The light is tremendous and has no single source. Everything here emits. Status LEDs — hundreds of them — punctuate every surface in white, amber, blue, cycling through confirmation patterns. Three industrial floods, meant to illuminate maintenance corridors that no longer exist as planned, converge on this space from odd angles, overpowering it, making every bolt-head throw a crisp shadow. A junction box on the eastern wall has been opened at some point and never resealed, and inside it a column of indicator lights fires in scrolling sequence, green-green-green-amber-green, a perpetual semaphore reporting on systems deeper in the structure. The combined effect is a brightness that feels interrogative, like being inside a machine that is examining itself. There are no dark corners. The light gets into every seam.

And the flies.

They are not, strictly speaking, flies. They are something that has decided to be fly-shaped — small-bodied, six-limbed, winged — in the way that a dozen unrelated lineages on Earth independently decided to be crab-shaped, because the form works. These are metallivores, or their descendants. Their larvae eat paint. Their adults drink condensation and harvest the thin biofilm of iron-oxidizing bacteria that colonizes any damp ferrous surface in the structure. Their wings are translucent but catch the industrial floods and flash iridescent copper-green. They are everywhere.

Not everywhere like a swarm. Everywhere like a population. Like a city.

They coat the walls in living, shifting density. They fill the crevices where infrastructure converges. They cluster on the warm surfaces of active relay cabinets and avoid the cold ones with such precision that you could map the thermal output of every device in this space just by watching where they settle. The puddle in the grating is ringed three-deep with drinkers, their proboscises — if that's the word — touching the water surface and creating tiny rings of interference. Others crawl the grating mesh itself, licking at the oxidation between the misaligned layers. Still others fly, constantly, in tight purposeful circuits that seem to map routes through the space's geometry — along duct edges, around cable bundles, through the gap between two exhaust risers where the warm air creates a reliable updraft.

They are quiet. That is the uncanny thing. A space this packed with life should roar, should hum with wingbeats. But the sound they make is subthreshold — a papery, static-like whisper that your brain keeps reclassifying as silence. It sits just beneath the low ticking of cooling metal, beneath the subliminal drone of current in the cable runs, beneath the metronomic *tink* of the condensation drip. If you hold very still, you can hear individual wing-pairs — a tiny frictionless scissoring, like someone cutting air with air.

But the room is not still.

The room is never still.

This is what makes the cupola extraordinary, and what makes it, in some functional sense, alive: the systems surrounding it are in a state of constant, unresolved reconfiguration. The structure — whatever it is, whatever its purpose, however far it extends below and to every side — is not stable. Relays click and reassign. Somewhere below, a routing decision is made, and a duct that was exhaling warm air shudders, reverses, begins to inhale. The thermal map of the cupola changes in seconds. The flies respond instantly — a wall that was coated with bodies empties in a rippling wave and the opposite wall darkens with arrivals. The junction box scrolls amber-amber-red, and an electromagnetic field somewhere nearby shifts, and a region of the grating that was safe becomes uncomfortable, and a thousand larvae begin migrating along the mesh toward a new favorable zone, their translucent bodies catching the light like animated rice grains.

Then a panel that has been sealed for god knows how long pops its bolts — pneumatic pressure, or thermal expansion, or some automated maintenance protocol firing years too late — and behind it is a new cavity, a section of ductwork exposed, and the flies pour into it like water finding a drain, exploring, tasting, colonizing, and the ones already inside the new space begin to change their flight patterns to map its geometry, and the ones outside adjust their circuits to account for the new opening, the new airflow, the new thermal gradient, and for thirty seconds the whole population is in visible, shimmering, turbulent reorganization.

Then the panel reseals.

Or doesn't. Sometimes it stays open and becomes permanent infrastructure — fly infrastructure, a new district in their city, integrated into the flight paths and thermal maps within minutes. Sometimes a different panel opens. Sometimes a cable run heats beyond tolerance and the flies abandon an entire quadrant, pressing into the remaining space with a density that seems impossible, every surface fur-thick with iridescent bodies, until the cable cools or is rerouted and they spread again.

The puddle grows. The puddle shrinks. A new condensation source opens up two meters away when a cracked duct shifts under mechanical stress, and suddenly there are two puddles, and the drinking patterns change, and territorial micro-boundaries form along the grating between them, visible as thin lines of empty metal where no fly stands.

You could watch this for hours. People have — or something has. There are marks on one of the gratings, at the edge of the space where the cupola narrows to a maintenance crevice barely wide enough for shoulders. Scuff marks. Knee impressions in the accumulated dust-of-no-dust, the fine particulate of metal shavings and paint flakes and fly casings that settles on every surface and is constantly disturbed. Someone, or something with knees, has crouched here repeatedly, looking inward. There is a small tool — a calibration wrench, the kind used for adjusting pipe fittings — that has been set down on the grating and never retrieved. A fly is standing on it, grooming its forelimbs with mechanical precision, and the wrench is furred with the pale residue of a hundred egg-layings in the textured grip.

The cool air shifts. A deeper system, far below, makes a decision that propagates upward through the structure as a pressure wave — not violent, just present, a systemic inhale — and for a moment every duct in the cupola breathes inward simultaneously, and the flies lift from every surface at once, a thousand copper-green bodies suspended in bright air, turning, reorienting, waiting for the architecture to tell them what it has become.

The moment passes. The ducts disagree again, resume their competing exhalations. The flies descend, renegotiating everything.

The light never changes. The light is the one constant. Bright, sourceless in aggregate, sharp-shadowed, industrial, relentless. It makes the flies' wings flash like signal mirrors. It illuminates the rust stains and the serial numbers and the weeping condensation and the impossible, beautiful, turbulent organization of ten thousand small lives packed into a space that was never meant to hold any life at all, at the top of a structure so large it has forgotten this room exists, while the room forgets nothing, adjusts to everything, and is never for two consecutive seconds the same place.
