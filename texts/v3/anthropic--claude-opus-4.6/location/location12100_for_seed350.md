# The Boiling Groves

---

Listen first, because you'll hear it before you understand anything else. The sound arrives like a physical medium — not through the air but *as* the air, the way pressure is a kind of touch. A vast, harmonic roaring. Not one frequency but thousands, layered into a chord so dense it becomes texture. It is the sound of an ocean boiling through a forest that should not exist, and the forest growing through an ocean that refuses to behave like water, and beneath all of it, the grinding, shuddering, rhythmic percussion of machines that have been running so long they have become geological.

---

The water is everywhere. It's the first truth here. Not a flood — a *medium*, a warm and dim and living broth that fills the lowland basin from bedrock to somewhere above your head if you were standing at the bottom, though "bottom" is a thing that keeps renegotiating itself. The basin is broad, maybe ten miles across, maybe fifteen — it's hard to know because visibility dims after a few hundred meters in any direction. Not dark, exactly. More like permanent dusk rendered in a warm amber-green, the light coming from no identifiable source, diffused through the water the way light moves through a body. It's the color of sun through a closed eyelid. Warm — blood-warm — and thick with dissolved particulates that give every sightline a hazy termination, a soft wall of golden murk.

And through this murk, the trees.

---

They are enormous. They should not be here. That is the first dissonance, and it sets the key for everything that follows.

They rise from the basin floor — which is silty, fresh, sharp-featured in places where new formations have recently thrust upward through the muck like knuckles of paleite stone — and they climb through eighty, ninety, a hundred feet of warm water without apology. Their trunks are the green-black of waterlogged wood but they are not waterlogged; they are *alive*, and their bark seethes. Not with insects — there are no insects, no fish, no animals of any kind, and this absence is one of the most unsettling things about the place, a silence-within-the-noise that you feel in your stomach. The bark seethes because it is growing in real time, producing new layers that curl outward like pages of a book being riffled, each layer a slightly different shade of black-green, each one lasting perhaps a minute before the water softens it and it sloughs off in long spiraling ribbons that drift down through the amber light. The trees are perpetually molting. The water is full of their leavings, drifting in vast slow curtains.

Their canopy — and they do have a canopy, even here — is a thing of bewildering density. Branches fork and refork and fork again, fractal and compulsive, and from every branch, leaves. Broad, translucent, faintly luminous leaves the size of dinner plates, overlapping in such profusion that looking upward through them is like looking through stacked panes of green glass. They move constantly in the strange warm currents, fluttering, and they too are growing visibly — unfurling at the tips of branches like slow green flames, reaching their full span in what seems like hours, then releasing, detaching, joining the cascade of detritus that falls through the water column like a slow green snow. The sound they make — all of them, all at once — is a rustling so magnified by the acoustic properties of the water that it becomes a kind of omnidirectional static, a white noise foundation under everything else.

The root systems are exposed, tangled, intertwined. They form lattices and arches near the basin floor. In places they've grown together, fused, creating walls of living wood that partition the underwater forest into chambers and corridors. Some of these corridors are narrow enough to be almost claustrophobic. Others open into cathedral spaces where the tree trunks are spaced just far enough apart to allow a sense of volume, and in these spaces the amber light gathers and pools, and the floating leaf-fall turns in slow thermals, and the sound — somehow — is slightly less crushing.

---

Now the machines.

They are *in* the trees. They are *of* the trees. This is where the weirdness begins its real work.

Approximately a third of the trees in the basin — and there are thousands — have been fitted, at some point in some past that may not be fixed, with industrial apparatus. Heavy iron housings bolted into the trunks with rivets the size of fists. Pipes — copper, steel, some of a dull ceramic material — that run along major branches, branching when the branches branch, following the fractal logic of the canopy with eerie fidelity. Turbines, half-embedded in the wood where the wood has grown around them, their blades still turning in the current, producing a low harmonic thrum that you feel in your teeth. Valve wheels, green with patina, jutting from trunks at odd angles. Exhaust ports that release periodic belches of superheated water, sending shimmering columns of heat-distortion rippling upward through the amber murk. Pressure gauges whose needles oscillate in no rhythm you can identify.

The machines have no operator. No control room. No apparent purpose. They intake water through great mesh-screened ports near the roots, and they process it — you can hear the processing, the grinding and humming and clattering of internal works you cannot see — and they output it again, sometimes hotter, sometimes colder, sometimes carrying new dissolved compounds that tint the local water a faint violet or a rust-red that dissipates over distance. Some of the pipes carry not water but a kind of thick, dark oil that beads and hangs in the water in wobbling spheres before being drawn into the next intake. The oil smells of iron and something botanical, something like crushed celery.

And here is the thing that takes the longest to notice: the machines are growing too.

Not quickly. Not like the trees. But the iron housings are *accreting*. New metal — or something that looks like metal but may be a mineralized secretion of the trees themselves — is building up in thin laminar layers on the surfaces of the apparatus. A valve wheel that was three inches in diameter a year ago is four inches now. The pipes are thickening. The turbine housings are developing ornamental ridges that look disturbingly like bark. The machines are becoming more tree. The trees are becoming more machine. Somewhere in the basin there may be individuals so far along in this convergence that you couldn't say which was which — a trunk that hums, a boiler that photosynthesizes — but in the dim amber light, in the roaring, with visibility what it is, you'd have to go looking.

---

The turbulence.

Nothing here is still. The water moves in complex, interfering current patterns driven by the heat differentials from the machines, the growth-and-shedding cycle of the trees, the periodic geological shudders from below (the basin floor is young, still forming, and it shifts — new stone pushes up, old silt liquefies and resettles, and twice in any given hour you'll feel a deep subterranean *thud* that sends a pressure wave through everything, rattling the pipe systems, shaking loose a fresh avalanche of leaves and bark). The currents don't behave like water currents. They bend in ways that suggest the water's viscosity is not uniform — pockets of thicker fluid, almost gelatinous, swirl through regions of normal flow, and when they collide with a heat plume from a machine exhaust, they produce standing vortices that can persist for hours, slowly migrating through the forest, bending branches, pulling loose equipment, reorganizing the architecture of the space.

And the light shifts with the turbulence. The ambient glow — sourceless, warm, amber-green — intensifies in some of these vortices and dims in others, as though luminosity is a property of the water itself and can be concentrated or dispersed by motion. In the brightest zones, you can see details you wish you hadn't: the way a pipe has grown a sheath of bark-like accretion that, on close inspection, contains tiny leaf-shaped protrusions; the way a tree trunk splits open where a turbine housing has been absorbed into it, revealing inside a cross-section of wood grain and gear teeth, perfectly intermeshed, both still functional, the wood grain spiraling into the gear teeth and the gear teeth completing the wood grain, as if they were always meant to be one thing and the separation was the aberration.

In the dimmest zones, you can see very little, and the sound fills in what the eyes lose. The roaring becomes almost architectural in the dark — you can navigate by the pitch of the nearest turbine, the particular rattling signature of one pipe network versus another. Every machine has its own voice. Every tree, through the friction of its constant growth and shedding, has its own timbre. The forest-factory is an instrument playing itself, and the song is so loud it has carved out a space where hearing replaces sight as the primary sense.

---

Scattered through the basin, mostly near the periphery where the water is shallower, there are structures. Human-shaped, or at least human-scaled. A platform of riveted iron plate, bolted to a frame that was once freestanding but has now been absorbed by root growth on three sides, leaving it tilted at fifteen degrees, its surface slick with the same bark-mineral accretion that covers the machines. A small building — shed-sized, corrugated metal walls — perched improbably in the crotch of a massive tree forty feet above the basin floor, its door missing, its interior visible through the murk as a dim rectangular void containing the shadow of a workbench and what might be tools. A series of posts driven into a newly-risen ridge of stone, connected by heavy chain, marking a path or a boundary that leads into the deeper forest and disappears.

These structures are not ancient. They are not ruins in the romantic sense. They are *recent*, some of them, as recent as the stone formations they're bolted to. But they are already being consumed. The forest does not tolerate inert matter. Everything that enters the basin either grows or is grown-over, and the structures are in the process of being incorporated into the living-mechanical matrix of the groves. The corrugated shed will, in another year, have copper pipes running through its walls and bark on its roof and leaves pushing through its floor. The platform will become a root node. The chain-linked posts will develop turbines.

There is no sign of whoever built them. No bodies, no clothing, no personal effects. Just the structures, in various stages of digestion.

---

Here is what happens when you stay too long and watch too carefully:

The growth is not only forward. The trees do not only grow upward and outward. In certain turbulent zones — particularly where multiple heat plumes intersect, where the viscosity anomalies cluster, where the light goes strange — the trees grow *backward*. Their bark un-sheds. Ribbons of sloughed material rise from the silt and reattach, curling back against the trunk, sealing over the fresh layer beneath. Leaves fall upward, reattaching to branches, furling closed, shrinking, withdrawing into the branch tip as a bud that then absorbs into the wood. The machine components in these zones run in reverse: turbines spin backward, pressure gauges count down, intake ports blow outward, and the oil spheres are *un-produced*, collapsing into the pipe openings from which they came. The sound in these zones is wrong — not quieter, but *inverted*, as though the audio is being played backward at full volume, the attack of each mechanical percussion arriving after its decay, the rustling of leaves preceding the silence that should follow it.

These reversal zones are not stable. They migrate, they pulse, they sometimes collapse abruptly — a tree caught mid-un-growth will suddenly resume forward growth with a violent crack and a spray of bark fragments that shred through the water like shrapnel. The machines caught in reversal, when they snap back to forward operation, produce a momentary pressure spike that can knock loose structural components and send them tumbling through the currents. The transitions are the most dangerous thing in the basin, if danger is even the right word, because there is nothing here that can be hurt — no fauna, no people — only the forest-machines, endlessly growing and ungrowing, running and unrunning, building and dissolving, in a warm, dim, roaring, amber-green world that has forgotten, or never knew, the difference between alive and built.

---

Sometimes, from the deeper center of the basin where no structure has been found and no tree has been individually distinguished from the mass, there comes a sound that is not the roaring. It is lower than the roaring, and it passes through the roaring the way a whale call passes through the sea — felt more than heard, a vibration that temporarily silences the mechanical chatter and the vegetative static and leaves only itself: a long, low, rising tone, almost melodic, that sweeps from one side of the basin to the other over the course of about ninety seconds and then stops.

When it stops, the growth everywhere accelerates. Every tree puts on a foot of height. Every machine doubles its output. Every reversal zone collapses and reforms somewhere else. The currents go chaotic, the amber light flares and dims in rapid succession, and for a few minutes the entire basin is a roiling, deafening, barely-visible maelstrom of production and dissolution, iron and cellulose and warm water and that strange oil and light and noise and *becoming*.

Then it settles. Not to calm — there is no calm here — but to its baseline turbulence, its everyday roar, its ordinary impossibility.

And in the settling, if you look carefully through the murk, you can sometimes see a new formation on the basin floor: a ridge of sharp pale stone that wasn't there before, with fresh roots already reaching for it, and at its crest, a single iron bolt, threaded and upright, as though something is meant to be attached.

As though something is about to be built.
