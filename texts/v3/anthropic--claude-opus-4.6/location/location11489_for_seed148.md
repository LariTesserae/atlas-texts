# The Hollow Mill

---

You descend into it through what was once an alley between two brick warehouses, but the warehouses have been eaten. Not demolished — eaten. The brickwork persists as a kind of skeleton, load-bearing walls still standing in rough parallel lines, but every surface has been consumed and replaced by living tissue. Moss, yes, but not only moss. Something deeper than moss. A thick, quilted integument of plant matter that has fused with the mortar, that has become the mortar, that holds the bricks in place now not through chemistry but through grip. The walls breathe — not metaphorically. They expand slightly, hold, release. The rhythm is slow, maybe one breath every forty seconds. You can feel it in your palms if you press them to what used to be a factory wall.

This is a low place. A depression in the earth that the town settled into long ago, an old river basin where the water table still seeps upward through the substrate. The ground is cool and faintly damp; not wet, not puddle-ridden, but sweating. Condensation forms on every surface that isn't already alive. The exposed iron of old machines collects droplets that never quite fall. Pipes that once carried steam or chemical runoff are sleeved in epiphytes, and inside them, if you listen — if you hold yourself very still — you can hear the faint ticking of water moving through capillary systems too complex to have evolved.

Because they didn't evolve. That's the first thing you understand about this place. Nothing here evolved.

---

The Hollow Mill is roughly the size of a city block, but it feels smaller. The vegetation closes it in. Canopy has formed where rooftops used to be: a woven mat of broad dark leaves, each the size of a dinner plate, overlapping with architectural precision to form a ceiling some four stories above the factory floor. Almost no light penetrates. What light exists comes from below — a faint bioluminescence in the root networks that web across the ground, a cold blue-green glow like phosphorescence in a winter sea. It illuminates nothing fully. Shapes exist in suggestion. You navigate by the glow of roots and by touch and by the way the air feels different as spaces open and close around you.

The old industrial infrastructure is still here, all of it, still organized, still structured — but transformed. The plant-mass hasn't destroyed the town so much as *continued* it. A stamping press, its iron body crusted with lichen that fluoresces in faint lilac, still moves. Its great arm rises and descends once every few minutes, driven not by any engine but by the slow hydrostatic pressure of a vine as thick as a man's torso that has threaded itself through the machine's gearing, swelling and contracting with the patience of tides. What it stamps, you cannot tell. The die is clogged with something that might be soil or might be tissue. Each compression produces a faint wet sound and nothing else.

A conveyor belt, its rubber long since decomposed, has been replaced by a living surface — a flat, peristaltic tongue of plant matter that inches material forward: seeds, fragments ofite own dead leaves, small calcified nodules that might be waste products or might be eggs. The belt moves at the speed of a glacier. Whatever process this factory once performed, the plants are still performing it, or performing their own version of it, using the machines as armature the way a vine uses a trellis.

The density inside the Mill is paradoxically low. The vegetation is everywhere — it IS the walls, the ceiling, the floor, the machines — but the interior volume is strangely hollow. Great vaulted chambers open between the factory's structural columns, empty of anything but cool air and that faint glow. Cathedral-nave emptiness. You could stand in the center of one of these chambers and shout (though you wouldn't; the quiet discourages it) and your voice would take a noticeable moment to reach the walls. The plant-mass has organized itself to the periphery, to the bones of the building, leaving the interior volumes as open as lungs between breaths.

---

The animals.

They are abundant and they should not exist.

In the near-darkness, you become aware of them gradually. First by sound: a faint rustling that isn't wind, because there is no wind here. Then by movement: something crossing a root-lit patch of floor with the quick lateral gait of a crab but the body of nothing you've seen before. It is about the size of a housecat. It has too many legs — you think seven, but in this light you can't be sure, and counting feels wrong somehow, as if the number changes depending on which leg you start with. Its body is matte dark, textured like bark. Two pale eyes, set wide, reflect the bioluminescent glow from the floor.

There are hundreds of them. Once your eyes adjust, you see them everywhere — on the walls, in the canopy, navigating the old machines with an ease that suggests intimacy. Some are small, the size of mice, clinging to the undersides of leaves. Others are larger, and different — longer-bodied, slower-moving, with frond-like structures along their spines that might be sensory organs or might be actual leaves. Photosynthetic fauna, or fauna-shaped flora, or something that has declined to choose between the two categories.

They are quiet. Their movements produce only soft friction against plant surfaces. Occasionally, one of them does something rhythmic — taps a wall with a forelimb in a repeated pattern, or drums its body against a pipe. The sound carries oddly. Other individuals, far away, sometimes respond. You begin to suspect they are operating the factory.

Not metaphorically.

You watch one of the larger creatures position itself inside the gear-housing of an old lathe. It braces its many legs against the interior teeth of a wheel and pushes, slowly rotating the mechanism. The lathe's spindle turns, and a length of living vine is drawn across what used to be a cutting head, now replaced by a ridge of crystallized mineral growth. The vine is scored, spirally grooved, and another creature catches the shaped vine as it exits and carries it — purposefully, with clear destination — toward a far wall, where it is woven into the structure.

The building is being built. You are watching construction. The Hollow Mill is not decaying infrastructure overtaken by nature. It is a factory that is still running, crewed by animals that may have been manufactured here, processing plant material into more building, producing the architecture that produces them.

---

And then the geometry begins to disagree with itself.

You are walking through a corridor — a former hallway between factory floors, its walls thick with the breathing integument — and the corridor is longer than the building. You've been walking for ninety seconds in a straight line and you should have exited the Mill's footprint thirty steps ago. The bioluminescent roots on the floor are the same roots; you recognize a particular branching pattern you passed a minute ago. But you haven't turned. You haven't circled back. The hallway is simply longer on the inside than the outside would permit.

You stop, and the walls breathe around you, and the cool air smells of petrichor and iron and something greener than green, a chlorophyll-dense sharpness like crushed stems. The corridor, you realize, isn't a corridor. It's a vessel. You're inside something that is growing, right now, in real time, and the space it contains is being generated as it grows — not carved out of existing volume but added. The Mill is manufacturing its own interior. This is what the factory produces. Rooms. Passages. Interior darkness. Hollow volumes for the creatures to populate and operate.

You press your hand to the wall and feel it breathe and feel, beneath the breath, a deeper rhythm — the whole structure expanding by fractions of a millimeter, a slow diastolic pulse that adds space the way a lung adds air, except the space stays. The Mill is one breath larger than it was when you entered. It will be one breath larger still by the time you leave, if you leave, if the corridor consents to reach a wall.

The ground beneath your feet is cool. Damp. Barely formed — the geological substrate here is young, almost embryonic, not the hard-packed earth of an old river basin but something freshly mineralized, as if the soil itself is being secreted by the roots that glow inside it. You scrape at it with your heel and it gives easily, pale and calcium-rich, like calciumite orite or tooth enamel. The Mill is making its own ground. The low elevation isn't a feature of the landscape the Mill sits in — it's a feature of the landscape the Mill is *creating*, pressing itself downward into a basin of its own manufacture, a self-generating depression.

---

In the deepest chamber — the one at the center, the one the corridor eventually delivers you to after a duration that felt like either five minutes or an hour — the machines are densest and most alive. An old boiler, big as a room itself, sits in a nest of roots and vine-infrastructure so thick that the iron is barely visible. Inside the boiler, where fire once lived, something glows — not bioluminescent blue-green but a warmer color, amber, shifting, like light through sap. The boiler hums at a frequency you feel in your sternum. The creatures cluster here, dozens of them, all sizes, pressed against the boiler's flanks, their bark-dark bodies vibrating sympathetically with the hum.

The boiler isn't burning anything. It's *thinking*. Or the plant-mass is thinking through it, using the iron chamber as a resonance cavity, an organ for some process that has no name because it isn't chemistry or computation or metabolism but something that only happens when industry and biology have fully merged and forgotten they were ever separate.

From the boiler, pipes radiate outward in every direction — the original plumbing of the factory, now sheathed in living tissue, carrying not steam but something you can hear faintly sloshing, a fluid with weight and rhythm. The fluid reaches the walls. The walls grow. The space expands. The creatures build. The machines turn. The Mill breathes.

You realize you cannot determine whether the Mill was once a real factory that was colonized, or whether the plant-mass grew a factory from scratch because the shape of a factory was the shape it needed to do what it does. Both histories feel equally true. Both are supported by the evidence. The bricks are real bricks, kiln-fired, stamped with a maker's mark you can't read in this light. But the mortar is alive, and the bricks might be alive too, might be nodules grown in the shape of bricks because the living system needed load-bearing modular units and this was the most efficient form. The old maker's mark might be a maker's mark or might be a gene expression, a phenotype branded into mineral by biological intent.

---

You stand in the dark, in the cool, in the damp. A creature the size of a small dog steps over your foot, pauses, taps the floor twice with a forelimb, and continues on its way. The tapping reverberates through the root network and something far away responds — a mechanical sound, a gear engaging, a process beginning in a corner of the Mill you haven't reached and might never reach because the Mill will have grown a new corner by the time you arrive.

The quiet is almost total. Not silence — silence implies absence. This is a quiet of presence, of many things happening at volumes below the threshold of attention. The breath of walls. The inch of the conveyor. The drip of condensation from a canopy leaf to an iron surface to the pale unformed ground. The hum of the boiler, felt more than heard. The scratch of many small legs on plant-surface. Each sound alone is nearly nothing. Together, they form a texture so finely woven it registers as stillness.

The air is cool. Not cold — you don't shiver — but cool the way the inside of a living thing is cool, thermoregulated by some process you can't identify, held at a temperature that favors slow growth and long patience. The Mill is in no hurry. It has been becoming itself for a long time and will continue becoming itself for longer. It is not finished. It may have a concept of finished, but if so, it is a concept that recedes as the Mill advances toward it, the way a horizon recedes as you walk. The Mill is an asymptotic factory, producing itself in perpetuity, approaching completion as a limit.

You are welcome here. Nothing threatens you. The creatures ignore you or regard you with their wide pale eyes and move on. You are not part of the process but you are not an intrusion either. You are a pocket of emptiness in a space that values emptiness, a hollow volume briefly animate, and the Mill breathes around you and continues its work.
