# The Screaming Plateau

---

The wind has not stopped in ten thousand years.

That is the first thing. Before you see anything, before you understand the shape of where you are, there is the sound — a roar so constant and so total that it has become architecture, has become gravity, has become the medium through which all other experience must pass. It is not a single note. It is a chord of thousands, shifting every second as the air finds new channels through the ancient rock, new throats to scream through, new edges to split itself against. The plateau is an instrument the size of a country, and the atmosphere plays it without rest.

---

You stand on the rim of something that was once much higher. Imagine mountains — not the sharp young peaks of tectonic ambition but something unimaginably older, a range that rose and then spent geological ages being unmade. What remains is a vast elevated tableland, two and a half kilometers above the distant lowlands that you cannot see from here because the plateau's edge is not a clean drop but a series of enormous, rounded steps — each one a terrace of erosion hundreds of meters deep, sculpted into forms so smooth they look poured. The rock is dark, almost black in places, a dense basalt or something older than basalt, something from the first coolings. Its surface has been polished by wind and the occasional rain into a glass-like sheen, and in places it has been carved into hollows, arches, tunnels, standing walls with windows that frame nothing but more sky. The erosion here has reached a kind of terminal beauty — the plateau has been worn past the point of drama into something more final, more settled. Every weak stratum has been stripped. What remains is only what could not be removed.

And yet.

The wind. God, the wind.

---

It comes from the west — or rather, it comes from everywhere west of here, which is an ocean of warm air rising off lowland plains that you have never visited, funneling up the ancient terraces, compressing and accelerating as it climbs. By the time it reaches the plateau's surface it is moving at speeds that would strip paint from metal, if there were metal here, if there were paint. It moves in visible layers: you can see the shear boundaries where streams of different temperature twist against each other, marked by sudden condensation that appears and vanishes in fractions of a second — wet ghosts, momentary scarves of fog that materialize, stretch, tear apart, and are gone. This is where the moisture lives: not in pools or streams but in the air itself, wrung from the sky by pressure differentials and temperature shifts, appearing as condensation on the leeward side of every rock formation, dripping briefly, evaporating, reappearing somewhere else. The plateau is damp the way a fever is damp — intermittently, involuntarily, from forces passing through it.

The wind is not steady. It pulses and surges and occasionally reverses in localized vortices that spin up without warning, pulling loose grit into helical columns that wander across the bare rock like drunk sentinels before collapsing. Larger formations — rolling horizontal vortices that stretch for kilometers — peel off the plateau's western edge and tumble across the surface, their passage marked by a shift in the screaming pitch from a bass roar to something almost ultrasonic. You feel these in your teeth, in the fluid of your inner ear. Twice in the hour you have stood here, the wind shifted so suddenly and completely that the world went silent for perhaps three seconds — a vacuum of stillness so shocking it felt like death — before the roar resumed from a new direction with redoubled fury.

---

Despite all of this — despite the raw, relentless stripping force of the air — the plateau is green. Fantastically, defiantly, improbably green. Not everywhere. The open rock is bare and will always be bare. But every crack, every hollow, every leeward pocket where the wind drops even slightly, life has found its way in and exploded. The vegetation is dense, compact, and sculptural: low mats of something between moss and succulent, deep emerald, their surfaces beaded with condensation, pressed so flat against the rock that they seem painted on. In the deeper fissures — and the plateau is laced with them, stress fractures from some ancient cooling that erosion has widened into channels a meter or two across and sometimes ten or fifteen meters deep — there are actual gardens. Ferns with fronds as thick as leather. Grasses with stems like wire, woven into dense mats that vibrate in the wind with a buzzing harmonic you can feel through the soles of your feet. Flowering things, small and intensely colored, their blooms designed for a world without pollinators: self-fertilizing, their petals fused into sealed chambers that open only when the barometric pressure drops, which happens every few minutes. In the deepest crevices, where accumulated millennia of organic matter have built a few centimeters of soil, there are even dwarf trees — sprawling, horizontal things, their trunks no thicker than a wrist but their root systems extending meters in every direction, gripping the rock with an intimacy that is almost muscular. Their canopies are flat, pruned by the wind into aerodynamic planes. They look like bonsai designed by engineers. Some of them are very, very old — centuries at least, possibly longer. Their wood, when exposed, is so dense it barely floats.

The vegetation grows fast. You can almost watch it. A tendril of vine reaches across a rock face overnight. A mat of moss colonizes a newly exposed surface in days. This is because the plateau's ecology has adapted to constant destruction — the wind tears, shreds, uproots, peels back layers, and the plants grow to replace themselves at a rate that matches the violence. The air is full of seeds, spores, fragments of vegetative matter, all traveling at terrifying speed. Some of them are clearly designed for this: seeds shaped like drill bits that spin in the wind and bore into cracks on impact; spores coated in a resinous adhesive that sets on contact with wet rock; runners with barbed tips that snag on any irregularity and immediately begin to root. The ecology is in a state of constant aggressive equilibrium — growth and destruction at the same pace, a war that neither side wins, the whole system vibrating at some frantic steady state.

---

The animals are scarce and strange. You have seen, in two days on the plateau, a total of four creatures large enough to notice. The first was something like a beetle the size of your palm, carapace aerodynamically ridged, moving with eerie confidence across open rock in a full gale. It was anchored by a set of ventral suckers — you could see them when the wind flipped it briefly onto its back before it righted itself with a practiced twist — and it seemed to navigate by the wind patterns themselves, following invisible corridors of relative calm the way a fish follows currents. The second was a bird, or something that occupied the ecological niche of a bird: compact, wingless, moving in explosive leaps from crevice to crevice, using the wind to extend its jumps to improbable distances. It had no visible eyes. It disappeared into a crack and did not emerge. The third and fourth were a pair of something mammalian — low-slung, densely furred, the fur lying in overlapping scales like a pangolin's armor, pressed against the rock in a wind-shadow behind a stone arch. They were either sleeping or dead. You chose not to investigate.

At night, there are sounds other than the wind — clicking, tapping, a kind of rhythmic scraping that comes from inside the rock fissures. Something lives down there in the deeper cracks, in the tangled root-gardens, something active and numerous but never seen in daylight. The sounds are regular enough to suggest communication. They are irregular enough to suggest argument.

---

On the second terrace below the main plateau surface, tucked into the leeward side of a massive eroded buttress, there is a shelter. It is old. Its walls are dry-stacked from the same dark basalt as the plateau itself, the stones chosen and placed with obvious care — each one shaped by hand, or at least selected to fit with a precision that required extended searching. The roof is gone, or was never there — instead, the builders relied on the natural overhang of the buttress, which extends far enough to create a space perhaps four meters deep and eight meters long that is merely *very windy* rather than uninhabitable. Inside, the walls are carved. Not decorated — carved functionally. There are niches for storage, a shallow basin cut into the floor that collects condensation drip from the overhang, a series of holes in the wall at regular intervals that might have held wooden beams or poles. In one corner, a hearth: a shallow depression ringed with fire-blackened stone, with a flue channel cut into the wall behind it that angles upward and exits through the rock face in a way that would create a draft even against the prevailing wind. Someone understood airflow here. Someone understood it intimately.

There are other structures. Scattered across the plateau, always in wind-shadows, always using the natural architecture of the eroded rock rather than fighting it — you find walls, partial enclosures, stone-lined paths that traverse the worst wind corridors by keeping low in the fissures. Whoever built these things was practical beyond sentiment. There is no ornament. There is no inscription. The tools they left behind — and there are a few, wedged in cracks or buried in the shallow soil of the crevice-gardens — are simple: hammer stones, wedges, a scraping blade made from a flake of the plateau basalt itself, knapped with skill but without flourish. One of them has a handle made from the dense wood of the dwarf trees, bound with a cordage of twisted grass fibers. The binding has partially petrified. It is very old.

Whoever lived here understood that this place was not to be conquered. It was to be threaded, moved through, occupied in the gaps. The structures suggest a population that was never large — a few dozen, perhaps, using the whole expanse of the plateau's upper surface in a rotating pattern, moving with the seasons of the wind if such seasons exist, occupying one wind-shadow and then another as the turbulence shifted. They are gone now. Whether they left or died or simply became so integrated with the plateau's rhythms that they ceased to build, you cannot say.

---

The light is ordinary. That is perhaps the strangest thing about this place — the sun rises and sets like any sun, the sky is blue trending toward white at the horizons, clouds form and dissipate in normal ways (though at this altitude they sometimes form *below* you, filling the lower terraces with a white sea from which the plateau rises like a dark island). The normality of the light makes everything else feel more extreme by contrast. There is no alien sun to explain the screaming wind, no supernatural glow to justify the eerie vegetation. This is a natural place operating under natural rules, pushed to an extreme that feels impossible but isn't. The basalt is real basalt. The wind is real wind. The plants are real plants, doing what plants do, just faster, harder, with more desperation and more ingenuity than you thought plants could manage.

In the late afternoon, when the sun is low and western, the light comes *through* the standing rock formations and the wind-carved arches, and the shadows they cast extend for extraordinary distances across the polished stone. The wind picks up at this hour — some thermal effect of the lowlands cooling faster than the plateau surface — and the sound deepens, acquires new harmonics. The plateau sings. There is no other word for it. The arches and tunnels and hollowed columns become organ pipes, and the frequencies they produce are low enough to vibrate the stone under your feet, to make the condensation on the rock surfaces tremble and coalesce into rivulets that trace the erosion grooves like veins suddenly filled with blood. For perhaps forty minutes, as the sun sets, the plateau is wet — actually dripping, actually running with water that appears from nowhere and goes nowhere, drawn out of the air by resonance and pressure and the particular geometry of ancient stone. The crevice-gardens drink. The dwarf trees' leaves, curled tight all day against the wind, unfurl slightly. Something in the deep cracks begins to click faster.

Then the sun drops below the horizon and the thermal gradient reverses and the wind shifts and the water stops and the plateau goes dry again, just like that, in the space of ten minutes. The sound changes from a song to a howl. The temperature drops — not dangerously, but enough to notice, enough to make you pull closer to whatever shelter you've found. The stars come out, hard and bright at this altitude, trembling in the disturbed air.

And the wind does not stop. Has never stopped. Will not stop until the plateau itself is gone, worn down to the level of the lowlands, which will take another few million years but which is, in geological terms, already well underway. The plateau is in its final form. Everything soft has already been taken. What remains is the hardest version of itself, the irreducible, the bones — and even the bones are slowly, slowly going.

The sound fills everything. You stop hearing it after a while. Then you hear it again, suddenly, as if for the first time, and it is the loudest thing you have ever experienced, and you wonder how you forgot.
