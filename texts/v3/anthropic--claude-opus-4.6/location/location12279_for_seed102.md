# The Singing Hollow

---

Below the surface — but not deep, not truly underground. Think of a depression, a sunken bowl in the earth, no more than fifteen feet down from the surrounding terrain, maybe thirty paces across. The kind of place you'd step into and realize, all at once, that the air had changed.

The walls are raw. Fresh fracture lines in a pale mineral substrate — something chalky and crystalline, like gypsum splitting along new planes, edges still sharp enough to cut. This hollow opened recently, in geological terms. Maybe decades ago, maybe centuries, but the rock hasn't had time to forget the shape of breaking. There are facets in the walls that catch what little light finds its way down, and they gleam with an interior luminescence, faintly, as though the mineral itself remembers being under pressure and releases that memory as a diffuse, parchment-colored glow. Not bright. Enough to see by, but only just. Enough to make everything look like it exists in amber, in old resin, in the light of a single candle reflected in milk.

There is no water here. None. The air is warm and utterly dry, the kind of dryness that pulls moisture from your lips and the corners of your eyes within minutes. You can feel it in your sinuses — a clean, mineral ache. The ground is powdery where it isn't stone, a fine pale dust that doesn't hold footprints so much as it absorbs them, settling back slowly into its own smooth surface as though it has an opinion about disturbance. The temperature sits in that range where you're aware of warmth on your skin but it isn't oppressive. Body-warm. Blood-warm. The hollow holds heat the way a cupped hand holds a coal.

And yet.

The vegetation is *impossible*.

They grow from the walls, mostly. From the fracture lines. From the places where the rock split and left gaps barely wider than a fingernail. Thick, pale stems — not green, not any of them, not exactly. They're the color of bone, of old ivory, of the inside of an almond. Some flush toward a dusty rose at their edges, or a sulfurous yellow where new growth pushes outward. They are succulent in form but not in the way of any surface plant. Their leaves — if that's the right word — are translucent, layered like scales on a pangolin or shingles on a roof, and inside them you can see structures that aren't veins. They look like crystalline lattices. Mineral scaffolding. As though the plants aren't drawing water from anywhere but are instead metabolizing the rock itself, pulling sustenance from gypsum and calcium and whatever trace metals thread through the substrate.

Some of them are enormous relative to the space. One plant near the center of the hollow — growing from a crack in the floor — has unfurled a canopy of overlapping translucent leaves nearly four feet across, each one no thicker than parchment, and they sway. They sway constantly, gently, though there is no wind. Not even a draft. The air in the hollow is perfectly still. The plants move anyway. It took you a while to notice this. Now you can't stop noticing it.

They are spaced out, these growths. Not crowded. Each one occupies its own territory with room to breathe around it — a cluster here, a single spindling stem there, a low rosette of pale leaves pressed flat against the wall like a hand. Maybe two dozen individual plants total, scattered across the bowl. Lush in their individual vitality but sparse in their distribution, so the overall impression is of a gallery — each specimen displayed with deliberate space around it, though no one arranged them.

Except someone *did* arrange something.

Against the eastern wall — if directions mean anything down here, and they might not — there are marks. Shallow notches carved into the mineral face with a crude tool, something harder than the gypsum but not by much. The cuts are rough-edged, overlapping, forming shapes that might be tally marks or might be a diagram of root structures or might be a map of something you've never seen. Nearby, a flat stone has been placed deliberately atop two smaller stones to create a crude shelf, and on it sit three objects: a piece of the crystalline substrate that's been worry-smoothed on one side by a human thumb (or something shaped like a human thumb), a coiled length of dried plant stem braided into a rough cord, and a small bowl chipped out of the wall-rock, empty, with a faint residue of pale dust in its bottom that smells, when you lean close, like electricity. Like the air before a storm, though no storm has ever touched this place.

Someone comes here. Or came here. Or will come here. The evidence is old and new at the same time, because nothing in the hollow degrades. Nothing changes. The dust is the same dust. The marks in the wall are as fresh as the day they were cut, whenever that was, because there is no force here — no wind, no water, no freeze-thaw cycle — to soften them.

This is the thing about the hollow: **nothing changes here**. Not visibly. Not measurably. The plants sway but do not grow. The dust settles but does not accumulate. The glow in the walls neither brightens nor dims. If you sat here for an hour and then stood up, you would find the place exactly as it was when you arrived — including, disturbingly, the dust where you sat, which would show no impression. Not because it filled in. Because it was never disturbed. Because the hollow doesn't accept change. It is an equilibrium so total it borders on the sacred, or the predatory.

And yet — it is *loud*.

This is the first thing you noticed and the thing you keep forgetting to think about, because the sound isn't something your mind categorizes as sound, exactly. It's a resonance. The plants produce it. Each one of them, each translucent leaf and mineral-threaded stem, vibrates at a frequency you can feel in your teeth before you hear it in your ears. Together, the scattered plants fill the hollow with a layered drone — a chord, if you want to think of it musically, though it's more complex than any chord. It's a sound with *architecture*. Low thrumming from the thick central canopy, so deep it's almost subauditory, felt as a pressure in the chest. A higher keening from the smaller rosettes on the walls, thin and sweet and slightly sharp, like a finger drawn around the rim of a crystal glass. Mid-range pulsing from the braided stems, a rhythmic wah-wah-wah that doesn't speed up or slow down, ever, has never sped up or slowed down, will never.

The sound doesn't echo. The walls should produce echoes — the mineral is hard, the geometry is concave — but the sound is *consumed* by the space as quickly as it's produced, absorbed and re-emitted in a perfect standing wave. It is exactly as loud at every point in the hollow. Stand near the wall, stand in the center, it doesn't matter. The volume is the same. The mix is the same. The sound occupies the space uniformly, the way a gas fills a container, and it has always been exactly this loud and will always be exactly this loud.

You feel it in your bones, literally. Your skeleton becomes a tuning fork. After a few minutes, you realize your own breathing has synchronized to some sub-rhythm within the drone, and that your heartbeat is trying to. This is not comfortable.

The fauna.

They live in the spaces between the plants. Small creatures — the largest are the size of a mouse, the smallest are barely visible. They have too many legs, or their legs are in the wrong places, or what you thought were legs are actually something else — cilia, perhaps, or sensory filaments. They are the same chalky white-bone-ivory palette as everything else in the hollow, and they move with a stuttering, deliberate quality, as though each movement is a considered decision. Some of them climb the plant stems. Some of them rest motionless on the floor for so long you mistake them for pebbles until one suddenly shifts, reveals legs, and picks its way to a new position. Their mouths — they have mouths, or at least openings — are pressed against the plant leaves, and you can see the faintest tremor where contact is made. They're feeding. Not on the plant tissue itself, but on the vibration. You're almost certain of this. They press themselves to the singing surfaces and they drink the sound.

There are perhaps forty or fifty of them. Enough to notice, scattered here and there, each one separated from its neighbors by a respectful distance. They don't interact with each other. They don't fight, don't mate, don't communicate in any way you can detect. Each one exists in its own small sovereignty, tending its relationship with the nearest plant, drinking its tone.

They don't acknowledge you. Nothing in the hollow acknowledges you.

One more thing. In the exact center of the floor, there is a crack. Not wide — a finger's width, maybe less. It descends into darkness that the wall-glow doesn't reach. If you kneel beside it and put your ear close, you hear the drone of the hollow change — not in volume, not in pitch, but in *depth*. As though the sound has a z-axis, a dimension that only reveals itself at this point, and down in the crack the chord extends further than it does at the surface, into registers that aren't audible so much as they are *spatial*, as though the sound down there describes a geometry, an architecture, a room below this room below the surface.

The shelf with its three objects faces this crack. The tally marks on the wall, if they are tally marks, might be counting something about it. The braided cord is exactly long enough, if you uncoiled it, to lower something small into the opening.

No one has done this. Or, in the logic of the hollow, someone is always about to. The potential hangs in the air, as stable and unchanging as everything else. A choice perpetually unmade, preserved in the amber light, vibrating at its own frequency among all the other frequencies, part of the chord, one voice in the ceaseless, beautiful, terrifying song.
