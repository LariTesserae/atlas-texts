# The Flats at the End of Wearing

---

There is so much sky here. That is the first thing. The land has been ground down so thoroughly, over such incomprehensible spans of time, that it has almost forgotten how to rise. What remains is a low, warm, waterlogged expanse that stretches to every horizon — a landscape so ancient it has become a kind of patience made physical. The bedrock itself, where it shows, has been polished to a dull luster by epochs of rain and slow dissolution. It is not flat in the way a floor is flat. It is flat in the way an old river stone is smooth — organically, inevitably, through the sheer accumulation of being touched.

The stone is a dark ruddy gneiss, Archean-aged, banded in faint stripes of mineral grey and rust. It surfaces in low shelves and exposed pavements that barely crest the water, their edges rounded past any memory of sharpness. In the creases between these shelves, water gathers. Not rushing water, not still water — *present* water. Water that seeps, that films, that sits in wide shallow sheets no deeper than a hand's breadth, catching the light and breaking it into trembling lattices of reflection. The entire landscape shimmers faintly. The water table is not below the surface here. The water table *is* the surface, or something very close to it. The ground does not so much contain water as coexist with it, the two materials interpenetrating in a negotiation that was settled a geologic age ago.

Walk here and your feet find the stone warm. The sun is generous and near-overhead, the light strong and even, the kind of light that does not dramatize but reveals — warm gold with a white core, pouring down through a sky that is mostly clear but hazed near the horizons with evaporative moisture. The air is thick. Not oppressive, but substantial. You can feel it on your skin, can feel the humidity sitting against you like a second atmosphere. When you breathe in, the air tastes mineral, tastes of wet rock and the faintest green bitterness of the sparse vegetation that clings in the crevices.

Because there is vegetation, though it does not dominate. Dark mats of cyanobacterial crust — ancient life-forms themselves, lineages as old as the rock — fill the slight depressions in the stone with a blue-green, almost black velvety film. Where silt has accumulated in shallow basins, wiry grasses grow in isolated tussocks, their blades tough and narrow, a pale sage green that looks silver when the wind moves them. These tussocks are scattered widely. You might count seven or eight of them from where you stand before the heat haze swallows the distances. Occasionally a low shrub, woody and gnarled, erupts from a crack in the pavement, its canopy sparse and spreading, barely waist-high, every branch a record of decades of slow growth. There are no trees. Nothing here reaches for the sky with any urgency.

But the *sound*.

This is the startling thing. For all its visual stillness, this landscape is astonishingly vocal.

Frogs. Thousands of frogs. They are hidden in every wet crease and shallow pool, tucked beneath the lips of stone shelves, half-submerged in the dark microbial mats, and they are calling. The sound is enormous, a layered, pulsing wall of voices — high crystalline trills, deep resonant clicks, buzzing metallic drones that rise and fall in overlapping waves. No single frog is visible from a distance. They are small, mottled brown and rust like the stone, and they press themselves into crevices and sit motionless except for the rapid inflation of their vocal sacs. But their collective voice fills the basin from edge to edge. It is not a chorus that builds and fades; it is a sustained presence, a pressure in the air, something you stop hearing as individual sounds and begin to feel as a quality of the place, as fundamental as the warmth.

Wading birds move through the shallows in ones and twos. Long-legged, pale-bellied, deliberate. They step with an almost mechanical patience, each foot placed and held before the next is lifted. Their reflections walk beneath them in the water-film, doubled and wavering. When one strikes — a quick pistoning jab of the beak into a shallow pool — the sound is a sharp *tok* against the stone, audible even through the frog-song. Small dark fish, finger-length, scatter in radiating lines away from the strike, their movements visible as fleeting distortions in the water's surface. Dragonflies the color of copper wire patrol fixed routes above the pools, their wingbeats producing a dry papery rattle when they hover.

Farther out, where the stone pavement gives way to a broad shallow depression — a basin that might once, a billion years ago, have been a valley before erosion filled and leveled it — the water deepens to perhaps knee-height and the color shifts from reflected sky to a warm amber-brown, tinted by dissolved tannins from the microbial mats. Dark shapes move slowly beneath this surface: large amphibians, perhaps, or bottom-feeding fish nosing through the soft sediment. Bubbles rise in irregular clusters and pop at the surface with a faint collective murmur, adding a textural hiss to the frog-song.

The wind, when it comes, comes in long slow exhalations. It moves across the open expanse without obstacle, and the water trembles under it in patterns that travel visibly from one horizon toward the other, a slow wave of changed light rolling across the flats. The grasses lean and hiss. The frog-song modulates — dips briefly, then resumes. The wind carries the warmth of distant stone and deposits it against your face.

There is nothing built here. Nothing that suggests intention or design beyond the biological. No paths, no cairns, no marks. The stone pavements might *look* placed, might *look* like some ancient flooring, but this is coincidence, the natural geometry of erosion working on banded rock for eons until only the hardest layers remain, exhumed and polished. The regularity is geological. The gaps between stone shelves follow the original bedding planes and fracture patterns of rock that was already old when multicellular life was new.

The distances are real and legible. You can see perhaps five or six kilometers in every direction before the haze closes in, and in that radius the landscape repeats its motifs without monotony — stone shelf, water-film, tussock, pool, exposed pavement, shallow basin — recombining in gentle variations. Here and there a slightly higher remnant of stone, a mesa reduced to a mere ankle-high ridge, casts a shadow that provides a different microhabitat: a cooler crevice where a different species of frog calls in a lower register, or where a small lizard basks on the sun-warmed edge, its flanks expanding and contracting with quick breaths. Insects — gnats, midges, small flies — gather in loose clouds above the warmest pools, their collective hum a higher harmonic above the frogs.

Nothing changes quickly here. The erosion is so advanced, so terminal, that the landscape has reached something like equilibrium. The rain that falls — and it falls often, warm sudden showers that arrive from the haze-horizon with little warning, hammering the stone and water into a single silver surface of rebounding droplets before passing — the rain no longer carves. It only maintains. It keeps the films flowing, keeps the pools filled, keeps the frogs singing, keeps the mineral taste in the air. The water cycle here is almost closed, almost self-sustaining: evaporation, condensation, gentle rain, seepage, evaporation again. The warmth drives it. The flatness contains it.

There is a deep geological hum beneath everything, or perhaps it is only the cumulative vibration of so many small voices resonating against so much flat wet stone. You feel it in the soles of your feet when you stand still. The rock itself is warm, storing the day's heat with the density of its ancient compression, and at dusk — though dusk is far off now — it will release that heat slowly, and the frogs will sing louder, and the mist will rise from every surface, and the flats will become a warm low cloud resting on the oldest ground in the world.

This is what a landscape looks like when it has finished becoming. When every possible hill has been leveled and every possible valley has been filled and the only things left with any energy are the small ones — the frogs, the wading birds, the fish, the insects, the microbial mats quietly photosynthesizing on warm stone. The land itself is done. It has arrived at its final answer. And the answer is: low, warm, wet, wide, and full of song.
