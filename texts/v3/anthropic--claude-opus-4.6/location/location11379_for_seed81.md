# The Sweating City

---

The first thing you understand is the emptiness inside the fullness. There is a city here — a real city, kilometers of it, stacked and structured and planned — and almost nothing is in it. The buildings stand like the walls of opened ribcages, and the streets between them hold only heat and a faint, sourceless wetness. You could shout down any of these avenues and the sound would travel for a very long time before it found anything to stop it.

---

The plateau sits high above whatever lowlands surround it — you can't see them. The air at the edges of the city is a brown, humid murk, a condensation boundary where the hot air rising off the rock meets itself coming back down, and the result is a permanent curtain of warm haze that hides the drop. You know you are elevated because of the subtle wrongness in your breathing, the way the heat seems to press not just from above but from within your lungs, thinner than it should be for how oppressive it feels. The sky overhead is a low, dim bronze. Not clouded exactly. More like the atmosphere itself has a color, a translucence, as if you are looking up through tinted glass. No sun is visible. The light is diffuse and comes from everywhere, casting almost no shadow, and what shadows exist are soft and directionless, the faint bruise-blue of deep exhaustion.

The rock beneath everything is dark. A basalt plateau, or something like basalt — dense, fine-grained, blue-black where freshly exposed but weathered to a rough ochre-brown across most surfaces. The city was built from it. The city was built *of* it. In places it is impossible to say whether a structure was constructed or carved. Walls emerge from the ground with the ambiguity of natural formations that have been squared off, or of foundations that have been so thoroughly weathered they have begun to rejoin the earth. The stone is old. Its edges have softened. Its corners have become philosophical suggestions.

And it sweats.

---

This is the central strangeness: the water comes from the city itself. Not from rain — there is no rain here, or if there ever was, the memory of it has been baked out of the atmosphere. Not from any river or spring you can find. The stone sweats. Every surface above a certain temperature — and nearly everything here is above that temperature — produces a thin, creeping film of moisture. It beads on walls. It pools in the concavities of worn steps. It traces dark veins down the faces of buildings, following paths that have been etched deeper by centuries of this same slow weeping. The water is warm, nearly body temperature, faintly mineral, and it tastes of iron and lithium and something else, something that makes your tongue feel briefly numb. It collects in the low places — shallow, dark pools in intersections, finger-deep puddles in the basins of what might have been fountains or what might have been industrial catchments. The pools are still. They reflect the bronze sky like black mirrors.

The stone is not alive. You are fairly sure of this. But it metabolizes heat into moisture through some process that is geological, or was engineered to appear geological, or was geological once and was refined. The deeper you go into the city — into the lower levels, the sub-street corridors, the vaulted spaces beneath the plazas — the more pronounced the sweating becomes. Down there, the walls are slick and run freely, and the sound of water moving in the dark is one of the few sounds that persists.

There is no vegetation. None. No moss in the wet places, no lichen on the stone, no dark stain of algae in the pools. The water is apparently hostile to biology, or biology never took hold here, or it was scoured out so thoroughly that even the seeds of life have been dissolved. The mineral pools sit undisturbed by any film or scum. Their surfaces are geometrically perfect.

---

The architecture reads as industrial. Not the industry of aesthetics — no one made these structures beautiful. They are functional on a scale that suggests a population of tens of thousands, organized by a logic that is almost but not entirely recognizable. Wide arterial streets connect to narrower ones, which connect to narrower ones still, in a branching pattern that resembles either a planned grid that has been warped by terrain, or a circulatory system that was straightened by intent. The buildings are massive, thick-walled, deep-windowed. Many have internal shafts — vertical drops, square in cross-section, that plunge from rooftop to below street level. Ventilation, perhaps. Or the conduits for something that needed to fall.

There are machines. Not many, and most are still, but they are present — embedded in the architecture as if the building grew around them or they grew into the building. Great flywheels of the same dark stone, set into walls, their axles fused with rust or mineral accretion. Pipe networks that run along ceilings, through walls, under streets, some of them still carrying the warm water to unknown destinations. In one district — you think of it as a district; it has its own character, its buildings slightly taller, its streets slightly wider — a row of structures contains what can only be engines. Massive cylindrical chambers with pistons of carved stone, their tolerances once fine enough to seal but now locked by thermal expansion or the slow creep of mineral deposits. They are warm to the touch. Warmer than the ambient air. Whatever reaction the stone undergoes to produce water may also be producing this heat, or it may be the other way around — the heat may be the input and the water the exhaust. You can feel a faint vibration through your hand when you press it to one of these engines. Not mechanical. Thermal. Molecular. The stone is busy at a level you cannot see.

Some of the pipe networks still function. You can hear them — a low, pressurized murmur in the walls, a gurgle beneath the street. Water moving through the city's infrastructure without anyone to direct it. The system is autonomous, self-sustaining, a closed loop of heat and moisture and mineral transport. This is part of why the city endures: it is its own maintenance. The water that seeps from the stone carries dissolved minerals. Where it pools and evaporates — in the higher, hotter, more exposed places — it deposits thin crusts that slowly, over long periods, fill cracks and reinforce surfaces. The city repairs itself. Badly. Unevenly. Some walls have been thickened by centuries of accretion into bulging, lumpy masses. Some doorways have narrowed to slits. Some stairways have become ramps. But the overall structure persists. It is weathered, deeply weathered, but not collapsing. It exists in a state of dynamic equilibrium with itself.

---

You walk for an hour and see no one. Two hours. The streets are wide enough to feel exposed, but the haze and the dimness compress your sightlines to a few hundred meters. Beyond that, the buildings fade into murk — suggestions of mass, vertical lines dissolving into the bronze air. The emptiness has a pressure to it. The city was built for occupation at a scale that makes your solitude feel like a violation of physics. Where did they go? Were they ever here? The architecture implies bodies — doorways at roughly humanoid height, steps at roughly humanoid scale — but the implications could be coincidental. The city might have been built by machines for machines. The city might have been built by the stone for itself.

In the deep districts, below the main streets, there are channels cut into the floors — troughs, really, smooth-sided, half a meter wide, running in long straight lines from building to building, connecting to the pipe networks. They carry the warm water in slow, almost imperceptible currents. If you crouch beside one and hold still, you can see the movement only by the way a bead of your own sweat, dropped onto the surface, drifts and gradually is pulled toward some unseen drainage point. These channels are the most carefully maintained features in the city. The mineral accretion has not been allowed to build up in them. Something keeps them clear. Something circulates through the system with enough regularity and force to prevent clogging. You find yourself thinking of the city as a body — the channels as vessels, the engines as organs, the sweating walls as skin performing thermoregulation. The metaphor is too easy. It is also too accurate to dismiss.

---

Sound: the city is quiet, but it is not silent. The difference matters. Silence is an absence. This is a presence held low. The background is a faint, pervasive hiss — water evaporating off hot stone, a sound so constant it becomes the floor of your hearing. Layered on top of it, intermittently: the gurgle of the pipe networks. A distant, irregular ticking as thermal expansion shifts stone against stone. Once, something deeper — a low, resonant groan from below, felt more than heard, as if one of the great engines shifted in its housing. The groan lasted four seconds and did not repeat. You waited a long time for it to repeat.

Fauna: you have seen two things that might be alive. The first was a movement in one of the mineral pools — a ripple that propagated from no visible source, concentric, precise, as if something had broken the surface from below and withdrawn. You watched the pool for twenty minutes afterward. Nothing. The second was a shape on a rooftop, five or six blocks away, silhouetted against the bronze sky for a moment before the haze swallowed it. It was the wrong shape for a person. Too many angles, or the wrong angles. It might have been a piece of broken architecture. It moved, though. You are fairly sure it moved.

The air sometimes carries a sound like breathing — slow, deep, arrhythmic. It comes from the ventilation shafts, rising from the lower levels, and it is probably just air moving through constricted passages as temperature differentials shift. Probably.

---

There are districts where the buildings are not made of the sweating stone. These are newer — though "newer" might still mean centuries old — and they are constructed from a lighter material, something like fired ceramic or sinteredite, grayish-white, porous, and *dry*. These buildings do not sweat. They sit among their dark, weeping neighbors like teeth in a wet mouth. They are also in worse repair. Without the self-maintaining mineral accretion of the native stone, they crack and crumble. Their edges are sharp where they break, and the fragments that litter the ground around them are angular and bright against the dark, wet streets. Someone, at some point, tried to build here using imported or manufactured materials. The attempt did not outlast the original.

Inside one of these ceramic buildings, you find something that might be a control room. A raised platform. Angled surfaces where instruments or displays might have been mounted. Conduits — dry pipes, disconnected — running from the walls to positions around the platform. The floor is covered in a fine grit that might be powdered ceramic or might be something else. It crunches underfoot and releases a faint smell: ozone and hot sand. There is nothing to control. Whatever systems this room governed have either been absorbed into the city's autonomous processes or have ceased. But the room tells you something: the city was, at one point, operated. Intentionally. By something that stood on platforms and used instruments and needed to see what was happening in the pipes.

They are gone now. The city has not noticed.

---

You climb to one of the higher points — a stepped structure, pyramidal, eight or nine stories, its upper levels narrowed by mineral accretion to claustrophobic passages. From the top, the city extends in every direction to the haze line. It is large. It is much larger than you expected. The buildings go on and on, block after block, their rooflines creating a dark, uneven horizon like a jaw of broken teeth. In the far distance, to what might be north, you can see where the buildings become lower and denser, packed together, their rooftops forming an almost continuous surface. In another direction, the city thins — wider gaps between structures, more exposed rock — and you can see the shimmer of larger pools, perhaps even a broad, shallow lake, its surface utterly still and dark as poured lead.

The heat is extraordinary. It rises from the stone in visible waves, and the sweat of the city rises with it, so the air is thick and heavy and mineral-tasting. Everything is damp. Your skin. Your clothes. The stone beneath your hands. The moisture collects in the low places of your body — the hollows of your collarbones, the creases of your palms — and it feels like the city is trying to make you part of its cycle. Absorb you into the water table. Dissolve your minerals and deposit them in the cracks of its own walls.

You should leave. You know this the way you know the groan from below was an engine and the shape on the rooftop was architecture. Confidently, and without evidence, and against a growing suspicion that you are wrong.

The city sweats. The pipes murmur. Something in the haze shifts and is still.
