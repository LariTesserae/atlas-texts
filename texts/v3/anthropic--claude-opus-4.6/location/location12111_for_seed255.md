# The Underhalls of the Ceramic Dark

---

You hear it first. Before anything else, before you understand where you are or what kind of place this is, you hear the sound: a deep, arrhythmic percussion that rises through the soles of your feet and into your chest cavity, like someone is striking the walls of a vast kiln from the outside. The blows come in clusters — three, then seven, then two, then silence for almost long enough to forget — and then the whole structure around you shudders and rings and the sound propagates through every surface, every wall and floor and vaulted ceiling, because everything here is ceramic, everything here is *made*, and fired clay carries sound the way water carries salt: dissolved into it, inseparable, everywhere.

This is a constructed place. Not a building exactly — not a single building — but an entire terrain of buildings, or rather a terrain that has *become* building, where the concept of ground and the concept of architecture have merged so completely that the distinction no longer applies. The floor beneath you is glazed tile, enormous slabs of it, each one slightly convex, fitted together with seams ofiteiteite darkite morite mortar the width of your smallest finger. The ceiling is somewhere above you — fifteen feet, twenty — a series of interlocking barrel vaults, also ceramic, their undersides textured with a spiraling pattern that was pressed in by hand. You can see the fingerprints. Thousands and thousands of fingerprints, preserved in the clay before firing, each one belonging to someone who reached up and dragged their fingers through the wet surface, and the spirals they made are not decorative so much as structural, channels that guide condensation toward specific points where the moisture collects and drips.

Because it is damp here. Not wet — there is no standing water, no streams, no pooling — but the air holds moisture the way a sponge holds the memory of water hours after wringing. The temperature is cool, just below what your skin considers comfortable, and this coolness meets the faintly warmer air that rises from the deeper levels through ventilation shafts (also ceramic, also handmade, also bearing fingerprints) and the meeting produces condensation. It gathers on the vaulted ceilings. It traces the spiral grooves. It converges at the lowest points of each vault where small ceramic nipples protrude downward — like inverted thimbles — and from these, the water drips. Not constantly. Intermittently. A drop here, then silence, then three drops in rapid succession from a nipple twenty feet to your left, then silence again, and the dripping becomes part of the percussive architecture of this place, a high counterpoint to the deep structural booming that continues to arrive from some unidentifiable source, some engine or process or ritual happening elsewhere in the complex.

The light is scarce. What exists comes from bioluminescent paste — no, not bioluminescent, that implies biology. This is a *mineral* luminescence, a phosphorescent slip painted onto specific tiles at irregular intervals, so that the darkness is broken not by any consistent illumination but by isolated patches of cold blue-green glow on the walls and floors, each one roughly the size of a spread hand. They do not light the space so much as they mark it, the way cairns mark a trail. You navigate between them. Your eyes adjust, but they never fully adjust, because the distances between the glowing tiles vary — sometimes three are clustered within arm's reach, and the light is almost enough to read by, and sometimes the next glow is forty paces away across an absolute blackness that you must cross by feel and by the sound of your footsteps ringing off the ceramic surfaces and returning to you with information about the shape of the space.

And the space is large. Not immense — not cavernous in the geological sense — but *extensive*, extending in every direction through connected chambers and corridors and halls, a dense lattice of rooms that share walls and share ceilings and share acoustics, so that you can hear activity happening in five or six adjacent chambers simultaneously without being able to see into any of them. The layout has no grid, no organizing axis. It grew the way coral grows, chamber by chamber, each one built against and upon the last, so that the whole complex is a single continuous mass of fired clay — walls four inches thick, floors six inches thick, ceilings that are also the floors of the level above. There are two levels in most places, three in some. You are on the lower level, which means you are beneath the weight of it all, and you can feel this, not dangerously, not oppressively, but as a presence, an awareness that everything above you was shaped by hands and placed by hands and fired somehow — and this is the mystery you keep circling back to, how was any of this fired? The kilns would have had to be immense. Or perhaps the firing process is different here. Perhaps the whole thing was built wet and raw and then something happened, some event, some sustained heat from below, and the entire complex was fired at once, was turned from shaped earth into something ringing and permanent and resonant in a single catastrophic burning.

There is no plant life. Nothing grows. The surfaces are too vitrified, too smooth or too deliberately textured, and whatever ecosystem might have colonized this place has been denied purchase. But this is not a dead environment — it is a *pre-life* environment, or perhaps a *non-life* environment, a place where biological colonization has simply not been part of the operating logic. Instead, there are mineral processes. The condensation carries dissolved calcium and silicates from the ceramic body itself, and where the water drips and evaporates, tiny formations are beginning — nascent stalactites of calcium carbonate, none longer than a child's finger, growing from the ceiling nipples. Mineral crusts at the edges of the glowing tiles, where the phosphorescent slip is slowly being buried under a thin crystalline scab. The ceramic is beginning to *weather*, but so slowly, so recently, that this weathering reads as emergence rather than decay. The place is still being born. The geological clock started recently. The formations are embryonic, the erosion barely a suggestion, and you get the sense that in ten thousand years this will be a very different place — crusted and mineralized and perhaps unrecognizable — but right now it is still mostly what it was made to be.

The percussion continues. You move toward it, because it is the only directional signal in this labyrinth, and as you move through chamber after chamber — each one slightly different in dimension, some low enough that you must duck, others tall enough that the ceiling vanishes into the dark — you begin to notice the fauna. They are sparse. A pale arthropod the size of your thumb, flattened like a trilobite, motionless on a wall, its segmented body the exact color of the unfired mortar between tiles. You almost miss it. Then, rooms later, you hear a faint papery rustling and look up to see something with too many legs moving along the joint between wall and ceiling, fast, disappearing into a crack where two vaults meet imperfectly. The creatures are adapted to the ceramic, or perhaps they were always here, perhaps they are what remains of whatever community built this place, reduced or transformed, finding the crevices and the drip-points and the patches of mineral crust where something microscopic grows — some film, some bacterial equivalent that feeds on the slow dissolution of the glaze. You never get a clear look at any of them. They are presences more than creatures: a sound, a flicker at the edge of the phosphor-glow, a sense of being observed by something small and numerous and patient.

The booming grows louder. It resolves, as you approach, into something more complex than simple percussion. There are harmonics in it now — the resonance of the ceramic chambers acting as a vast instrument, each room a resonating body, each corridor a tube, and the original sound being multiplied and transformed as it travels through the architecture so that what reaches you in any given room is unique to that room's geometry. The builders must have known this. The rooms are not arbitrary. They were *tuned*. Not in any way you can describe with musical terminology — this is not melody, not harmony — but the shapes were chosen so that the sound would propagate in specific ways, so that standing in one chamber you hear a thrumming bass that makes your vision blur, and stepping through an archway into the next you hear the same source sound transformed into a sharp crackling overtone that seems to come from inside your own skull. The architecture is an instrument. Or a machine. Or a temple. The distinctions feel less meaningful here.

You pass through a low crawlway — hands and knees on the cool glazed tile, the ceramic smooth and slightly slick with condensation — and emerge into a space that is different from the others. It is the largest room you've encountered, perhaps fifty feet across, roughly circular, its ceiling a single unbroken dome. The phosphorescent tiles are denser here, clustered in a band around the base of the dome, and for the first time you can see a space fully, can perceive its total shape. The walls are covered in hand-pressed patterns — not the drainage spirals of the corridors but something else, dense overlapping impressions of palms and knuckles and elbows, the clay pushed and shaped into a topography that makes the walls look almost organic, almost muscular, like the inside of a throat. At the center of the room, a structure rises from the floor: a ceramic column, five feet tall, hollow, its walls perforated with hundreds of small round holes. The sound is coming from it. Or rather, the sound is being *channeled* through it, rising from somewhere below, from some deeper level you haven't reached, and the perforations are shaping it, each hole a different diameter, each one emitting a slightly different pitch, so that the column sings — roars — vibrates — in a chord so complex it approaches noise.

The air near the column is warmer. The condensation here is heavier, and moisture beads on its surface and runs down in slow rivulets that catch the phosphor-light. You can feel the vibration in the air itself, a physical pressure against your face. Several of the flat pale arthropods are clustered near the base of the column, motionless, oriented toward the perforations. Feeding on something? Listening? Both? The distinction may not apply.

Something is happening below. Something is being made, or is making itself, or is being struck and shaped the way the clay of these walls was once struck and shaped, and the sound it produces is the sound of a world still under construction, still being fired, still hardening into whatever it will become. The halls carry the news of this process outward in every direction, through every vault and chamber and corridor, and the whole ceramic dark rings with it, and the condensation trembles and the tiny nascent stalactites vibrate and the pale creatures freeze and listen and you realize that this place is not old. It is not ancient. It is not a ruin. It is *setting*, like clay sets, like mortar cures, like a glaze finds its final crackle-pattern in the cooling — and you are standing inside it while it happens, inside a made-world that is still in the process of deciding what it is.

The booming shifts. A new rhythm, or the absence of rhythm. You press your palm against the wall and feel the structure speak through your bones. The fingerprints of the builders press back against your skin, a perfect negative contact across an unknowable distance of time, and the ceramic is cool and damp and utterly, irreducibly *present*, and the sound goes on, and on, and on.
