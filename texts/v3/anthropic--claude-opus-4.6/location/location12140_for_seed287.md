# The Cyst Works

---

Below the surface, below even the basements of the city that forgot it—though whether the city came first or this place did is a question with no comfortable answer—there is a cavity in the earth shaped like a held breath. Maybe forty meters across at its widest, roughly ovoid, its long axis tilted fifteen degrees off true. The ceiling is low in some places—you'd duck, if you were here—and vaults unexpectedly in others, opening into domes slicked with condensation that never quite becomes rain but never fully dries. The air is hot. Not desert-hot, not sun-hot. Hot like the inside of a body. Hot like stone that has been remembering heat for geological time and will not stop.

The walls are old. That's the first thing you understand about them. Not old like a ruin. Old like a riverbed. Old like something that erosion has not destroyed but *authored.* The original rock—some kind of pale volcanic tuff,ite seems, or something with that same compacted-ash quality—has been shaped by centuries of mineral-laden moisture into forms that no longer resemble geology. Folds. Pleats. Long curving ridges that look like the frozen wakes of enormous slow animals. In places the walls have developed shelves and overhangs so thin they're translucent, and the light that passes through them—we'll get to the light—turns the color of weak tea, of amber, of old iodine.

---

The vegetation is wrong. Not wrong like dead or wrong like alien. Wrong like *too successful.* Something is growing in here—several somethings—and they have had a long time to figure out how to do it.

The primary species, if species is the right word, is a root system. That's all it is. There is no tree above. There may have been, once, on the surface, but whatever canopy it fed has been gone for longer than anyone has been counting, and the roots have continued. They fill the cavity in slow, arterial tangles, pale as bone where they're exposed to air, flushing to a deep rust-red where they press against wet stone. They are thick—the main trunks of them as wide as a human torso—and they have grown into the walls, *through* the walls, become structural. If you removed them, parts of the ceiling would come down. They have accepted this responsibility without being asked.

Between the root-trunks, smaller vegetation has found its niches. Mosses so dark green they're almost black, thriving on the heat and the thin films of condensation that slide down every surface. Fungal shelves in stacked formations, their edges frilled and luminous—a faint bioluminescence, barely green, barely there, the kind of light you see better when you're not looking directly at it. And something else: a vine-like growth with no leaves, just tendril after tendril of smooth, rubbery stem, pale yellow, weaving through everything, connecting root to wall to machine to root again, as if trying to stitch the whole space into a single organism. It might have already succeeded.

---

The machines.

There are machines in here. They were not grown. They were brought, though "brought" implies a purposefulness that the evidence doesn't fully support. They sit in the cavity like organs in a body cavity—not arranged, exactly, but *placed* with an inevitability that makes arrangement feel beside the point.

The largest is a pump. Or it was a pump. It occupies the lowest point of the ovoid, where the floor dips into a shallow basin that holds, at any given time, a few centimeters of warm mineral water, rust-orange and still. The pump is cast iron, massively flanged, its housing cracked along one seam in a way that has allowed a root to enter it and grow through its impeller chamber. It does not function as a pump anymore. It functions as something else. Every nine or ten minutes—the interval is not regular, not quite—the pump emits a deep percussive *thunk,* a sound that travels through the stone floor and into your feet and sternum. Then a hiss, long and aspirated, like a steam release but wetter. Then silence, except it isn't silence. It's the space between the sounds filling back up with all the smaller sounds: the ticking of cooling metal that has been cooling for decades and still ticks, the slow creak of root-wood under its own weight, the faint wet sound of condensation gathering and releasing, gathering and releasing, on the underside of fungal shelves.

There are other machines. A panel of gauges mounted to a steel frame that has been bolted—with evident difficulty, at odd angles—directly into the tuff wall. Half the gauges are dark. Three of them still have needles, and two of those needles still move, twitching in tiny arcs that correspond to nothing visible. The frame has been colonized by the yellow vine, which has threaded itself through every bolt-hole and seam, and in one place has actually cracked the glass face of a gauge to enter the mechanism behind it. The needle in that gauge still moves. It moves differently from the others.

Against the far wall—far being relative; maybe fifteen meters—there is a generator of some kind, a squat cylindrical thing on a poured concrete pad that was clearly built *in situ.* The concrete is old enough that the mineral water has begun toite, depositing thin calcite ridges across its surface in concentric patterns, like the rings of a very patient tree. The generator hums. Not loudly. Steadily. It provides the power for—well. For whatever is still powered. The gauges. A single caged bulb mounted high on the wall near the ceiling's apex, which emits a sulfurous, brownish light that does more to create shadows than eliminate them. A box on the wall near the pump that might be a relay or a controller, its face dark except for one amber indicator that has been lit, continuously, for so long that the plastic lens over it has yellowed and begun to warp from its own small heat.

Who built these things? Who carried a generator down here, mixed and poured concrete in this hot darkness, bolted gauges to stone? The evidence says: someone who intended to monitor. Someone who intended to manage the water, perhaps—the pump suggests an effort to drain or control the shallow pool, the gauges suggest an interest in pressure or flow or temperature. But the evidence also says: that was a long time ago, and the cavity has had its own opinion about what these machines are for.

---

The sound. The sound is the thing you cannot escape.

It's not one sound. It's a layered drone that the space has been composing through its own acoustics for as long as the generator has been humming and the pump has been thunking and the roots have been creaking. The ovoid shape of the cavity focuses sound strangely. Stand in one spot and the pump's periodic exhalation is a felt thing, subsonic, a pressure change. Move two meters to the left and it becomes a sharp bark, almost painful. The generator's hum finds resonant frequencies in the stone shelves and amplifies itself into chords—not harmonious, not dissonant, just *there,* hovering at the edge of music the way the bioluminescence hovers at the edge of light. The roots contribute. On the hottest days—though how would you know, down here, when it's hotter above?—the roots expand minutely and groan against the stone, a sound like a ship's hull under stress, and the whole cavity seems to tighten and then release.

There is no animal life. This is notable. In any other warm, damp, enclosed space this rich in organic matter, you would expect insects at minimum. Spiders in the corners. Millipedes in the moss. Pale cave-adapted things scuttling along the root surfaces. There is nothing. The yellow vine grows over surfaces where beetles should be walking. The fungal shelves fruit without being grazed. The shallow pool holds no larvae, no worms, nothing. The ecosystem is incomplete in a way that should make it collapse, but it hasn't collapsed. It has been like this for a long time. The absence of fauna is not a failure of the system. It is a *feature* of it, or a *rule,* and the distinction between those two words matters here but cannot be resolved.

---

The strangeness.

You feel it when you stop cataloguing the physical details and let the place simply be around you. Something about the relationship between the machines and the roots is not parasitic and not symbiotic. It is something else, something for which the biological vocabulary is insufficient. The root that has grown through the pump has not killed the pump. The pump's periodic convulsion has not damaged the root. They have reached an accommodation that looks, from the outside, like a single system—a system that pumps nothing, monitors nothing, generates power for no external purpose, and grows toward no light. A system whose only output is its own continuation, its heat, its sound, its slow patient process of becoming more itself.

The yellow vine connects everything. Follow any strand of it far enough—carefully, in the dim sulfur light, stepping over root-trunks and ducking under calcified stone shelves—and it will lead you from organic to mechanical and back, threading through both with equal indifference to the distinction. Where it enters the cracked gauge, the needle moves in a pattern that the other needles do not share. If you watched it long enough—hours, days—you might begin to suspect the pattern is responsive. To what? To the vine's own growth? To some signal traveling through its tissues from the roots, from the fungus, from the warm stone itself? The gauge was designed to measure something. It is still measuring. The thing it measures may no longer have a name.

The condensation on the ceiling gathers and does not fall. It sits in fat trembling lenses on the underside of stone overhangs, vibrating faintly in sympathy with the generator's hum, and in the bioluminescent glow each droplet holds a tiny inverted image of the space below—roots, machines, moss, the amber eye of the indicator light, all of it miniaturized and suspended overhead in a constellation of warm water. Occasionally one drops. The sound it makes when it hits the pool is a clear, sharp note, startlingly pure against the drone, and for a moment after it falls the space seems to listen to itself.

The dynamic is slow. The changes here are measured in root-growth and mineral deposition, in the gradual calcification of concrete and the imperceptible warping of plastic under decades of gentle heat. But they are *changes.* The root system is still expanding, still pressing deeper into the walls, still slowly reshaping the cavity's geometry in ways that alter its acoustics. The vine is still threading into new crevices, new machines, new seams. The pump's interval is—maybe—growing longer. Eleven minutes now, where it was nine a year ago? Five years ago? There is no one here to have noticed. The gauges might know, but they are reporting to no one, in a language that has drifted from its original calibration into something private and untranslatable.

This is the Cyst Works. It was named by whoever installed the machines, or by whoever sent them to do it, and the name survives on a stamped metal tag wired to the generator's housing, the letters still legible beneath a film of mineral deposit: **CYST WORKS — SUBLEVEL MONITORING STN. 7.** Station seven of how many? Are there others? Other ovoid cavities, other pumps and generators, other root systems growing through other machines in other hot dark spaces beneath the city? The tag does not say. The machines do not say. The roots, which may by now know more about the subsurface geology than any survey ever conducted, are not telling.

It is hot. It is dim. It is loud in the way that a body is loud when you press your ear to it and hear the whole improbable system working. Nothing here is waiting for you. Nothing here is waiting for anything. It has settled into its own state, its own long process, and it will continue—the pump thunking, the generator humming, the roots growing, the vine threading, the water gathering and not quite falling—for as long as the heat lasts, which is to say, for as long as the earth lasts, which is to say, for longer than the question matters.
