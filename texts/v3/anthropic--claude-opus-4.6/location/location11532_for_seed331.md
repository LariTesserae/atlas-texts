# The Settling Pools of Ahn

---

The ground here has been ground down so long it has forgotten what it was. It remembers only water and the weight of ages, and it has become a kind of living concavity — a shallow depression no wider than a village commons, sunk into terrain so anciently eroded that the bedrock itself has the texture of worn soap. The stone is pale ochre, almost cream, smoothed into undulating bowls and shelves by a geological patience so extreme that even the concept of "hill" has been negotiated away. What remains is a low, warm, luminous basin — a place that has arrived, finally, at its own floor.

Water is everywhere, but gently. It weeps from the exposed rock in films so thin they seem painted on, collecting in dozens of shallow pools that range from the size of a dinner plate to the breadth of a small room. The pools are connected by the faintest of overflows — water migrating across the stone in sheets barely a millimeter deep, following channels so shallow they might have been etched by a fingernail. The water is warm, blood-warm, heated by something beneath the stone or perhaps by the bright, saturating light that falls on everything here without apparent source. The sky — if it is a sky — is a diffuse and sourceless white-gold, the color of light passing through alabaster, and it makes the wet stone gleam as though lacquered.

There is no sun. There is no shadow. The light comes from everywhere, and yet it has a warmth to it, a physical quality, a pressure almost. Objects here don't cast shadows so much as they sit in slightly dimmer versions of the same radiance, as though the light has simply agreed to be a little less enthusiastic behind them. It makes the whole basin feel like the inside of a lantern — bright, enclosed in some atmospheric sense, intimate.

---

The vegetation grows in the cracks and along the pool edges with an unhurried lushness, thick but never crowded. Mosses — deep emerald, almost black at their densest — upholster the stone lips where water runs. Low, succulent-leaved plants with waxy surfaces cluster in the shallow soil pockets, their leaves the size of coins, arranged in rosettes that overlap and intermingle but never quite form a continuous mat. There's space between each colony. You can see the stone between them, can trace the water's path. Some of the plants have produced small, fleshy flowers — pale violet, a washed-out coral — that sit close to the ground and don't so much bloom as simply *be open*, as though they've been open for a very long time and have no intention of closing. Thin filaments of something between lichen and algae trail in the warmer pools, forming slow, curling shapes beneath the water like calligraphy being written and erased.

A single structure of some kind rises near the basin's eastern edge — though "rises" is generous. It is perhaps waist-high, a plinth or platform of the same pale stone, but its surface is too regular, too deliberatly flat. It is clearly placed, not formed. Its edges have been softened by erosion into something approaching the organic, and the mosses have claimed two of its sides entirely. On its upper surface, barely visible under a permanent film of water, there is a pattern — a lattice of lines that shifts subtly when you look at it from different angles. Not engraved. Not projected. The lines exist *within* the stone somehow, at variable depth, and they move — slowly, on the scale of hours perhaps — rearranging in ways that suggest computation or perhaps communication. If you rest your fingertips on the wet surface, you can feel a faint thermal variation along the lines, as though the lattice is thinking in heat. Whatever technology produced this predates or exceeds the modern by a considerable margin. It hums at no frequency. It simply operates, continuously, in a medium that hasn't been named yet.

Nearby, half-submerged in one of the larger pools, there is something like a ring — dark metal or ceramic, the width of a bracelet, standing upright in the water with no visible support. Water passes through it and emerges on the other side *cleaner* — not just filtered but somehow simplified, as though the ring has removed not particulates but *ambiguity* from the liquid. The water downstream of the ring is startlingly clear. Small organisms seem to prefer it.

---

And the organisms. This is the overwhelming fact of this place.

They are everywhere. Not in swarms, not in herds — but in variety so dense it saturates the eye. Every pool, every wet surface, every stone shelf hosts something alive and distinct.

In the largest pool, which is perhaps three meters across and no deeper than a hand's depth, pale creatures like translucent crabs the size of thumbnails pick their way along the bottom on legs too numerous to count easily — twelve, fourteen, some number that changes depending on their gait. They are not crustaceans. Their bodies have a geometry closer to origami than anatomy, angular and folded, and through their glassy carapaces you can see organs that pulse in colors — amber, lilac — rather than in any rhythm that maps to a heartbeat. They forage along the algae strands, clipping them with mouthparts that seem to rotate rather than bite.

On the dry stone rims between pools, larger animals rest — or exist — in postures of absolute stillness that could be sleep, could be a resting metabolism, could be something else entirely. One resembles a flattened amphibian the size of a cat, mottled in ochre and dull green, its skin so perfectly matched to the wet stone that you might step on it before seeing it. Its eyes, when they open — rarely — are horizontal slits of gold that track movement with a mechanical smoothness. It breathes, you think. Its flanks move. But so slowly that you can watch for a full minute and see only one cycle. It is not dead. It is operating on a different clock.

Others are less identifiable. Something like an eel, but furred in fine cilia, undulates through the shallowest overflow channels, its body barely wider than the water film it travels through. It moves between pools with a purposeful, unhurried grace, and wherever it passes, the moss leans slightly toward it, then relaxes. A parasitic relationship? Mutualistic? The eel-thing doesn't eat the moss. The moss doesn't trap the eel-thing. Something is being exchanged that isn't visible.

Birds — or things that fly, at least — visit. Small ones, iridescent, with wingspans the breadth of a thumb, that hover over the pools and dip long, needle-fine proboscises into the water. They make no sound. Their wings beat at frequencies that should produce a hum but don't, as though the air around them has agreed to be still. They come in pairs, always pairs, and they seem to drink not the water itself but whatever the ring-artifact has done to it, preferring the downstream pools with visible urgency.

In the moss colonies, if you look closely — if you kneel and bring your face near the stone — you can see things moving that are barely larger than the moss leaves themselves. Creatures that are mostly leg, walking carefully between the rosette plants like herdsmen tending tiny fields. They carry things. Specks of something pale. They deposit these specks at the base of certain plants and move on. Some of the plants that have received these deliveries are notably healthier, their leaves thicker, their flowers more vivid. An agriculture? A symbiosis so refined it resembles economy?

And beneath the water's surface of the deepest pool, clustered around the base of the ring-artifact, something larger breathes. You can't see it clearly — the refraction and the light conspire to make it shapeless — but you can see the water move in rhythms that aren't caused by overflow or wind. Something is down there, curled around the ring's base, and it is the size of a large dog, and it is patient, and the small translucent crab-things give it a wide berth without appearing afraid. They simply navigate around a known presence. It has been here. It has always been here. The pools are, perhaps, *its* in some sense that predates ownership.

---

The sound is the sound of water so slow it barely qualifies. A faint, irregular dripping where one pool overtops and feeds another. The occasional wet sound of the flattened amphibian shifting its weight. Otherwise: a held silence, warm and close, the acoustic signature of a space with soft boundaries and no wind. Your own breathing is the loudest thing here. If you speak, your voice doesn't echo — it's absorbed by the wet stone and the moss, swallowed into the basin's atmosphere like a stone dropped into felt.

And yet the place is not still. The lattice on the plinth rearranges itself. The cilia-eel completes a circuit of the pools and begins another. The tiny herdsmen make their deliveries. The thing beneath the deep pool breathes. The flowers remain open. The water weeps and settles and weeps again. Everything here is in motion — slow, warm, purposeful motion — and everything here has been in this exact motion for a very, very long time. You get the sense that if you left and returned in a thousand years, the scene would be indistinguishable from what you see now. Not because nothing changes, but because the changes cycle at a scale that contains millennia the way a clock face contains minutes.

This is a place that has finished becoming what it is. The erosion has taken everything that was going to go. The life has filled every niche it's going to fill. The technology — whatever it is, whoever left it — has integrated so completely into the ecology that removing it would be like removing a species: the system would collapse. The ring cleans the water. The lattice does something that the organisms need, or that the stone needs, or that the light needs. The plinth and the ring are not ruins. They are organs of the landscape, still functioning, still embedded, still warm.

You are the newest thing here. The basin notices you — the gold-eyed amphibian opens one slit and watches, the crab-things adjust their paths by a few millimeters, the flying needles hover a moment longer over a pool you're standing near, then move to one farther away. You are not unwelcome. You are simply unprecedented, in the way that any new variable is unprecedented in a system that has been solving itself for longer than your species has existed.

The light continues, sourceless and warm. The water settles. The stone, worn to its final shape, holds.
