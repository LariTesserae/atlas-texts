# The Throatlands

---

There is a mountain range that is still deciding whether to exist.

The peaks — and they are extreme, vertiginous, clawing upward into a dim amber murk that serves as sky — have not finished arriving. You can see this if you look carefully at the ridgelines. The granite is soft at the edges. Not eroded. *Pre-formed.* The stone has the quality of something being sculpted from the inside, pushed upward by forces that haven't yet committed to a final shape. Whole cliff faces shimmer slightly, not with moisture (there is no moisture here, none, not a trace, not a memory of it) but with ontological uncertainty. The rock shifts between states: sometimes it appears to be basalt, sometimes chalk, sometimes a dense crystalline something that has no earthly analogue — a mineral that refracts the dim light into frequencies you can feel in your teeth but cannot name. The mountains are being *drafted.* First draft. You are seeing the rough sketch of a world's skeleton.

And yet.

And yet there is vegetation everywhere.

---

This is the central impossibility of the Throatlands, and the first thing any visitor would notice if visitors were a category that applied here: the plants grow without water, without soil in any conventional sense, and in many places without what you would call *ground.*

They emerge directly from the still-hardening rock as though the stone and the green were one organism in two moods. Vast lichens, yes, but also things that look like ferns the size of oak trees, their fronds a deep blue-green so dark they appear black in the dim light, unfurling from cracks in cliff faces that are still forming around them. There are grasses — pale, silver-white, waist-high — that grow in loose meadows on the high plateaus, and they move constantly, not in wind (there is no wind) but in response to the *sound.*

We should talk about the sound.

---

The sound is overwhelming. It is the dominant fact of this place, more present than the stone, more insistent than the vegetation. It comes from below, and from within the mountains themselves, and from the spaces between the peaks where the air — if it is air — funnels and amplifies and *shapes* the noise into something architectural.

It is not a roar. It is not a hum. It is closer to a chord, but a chord played on an instrument the size of a continent, sustained without breath or bow or hammer, a chord that contains sub-harmonics low enough to make the still-forming rock vibrate visibly and overtones high enough to be felt as pressure behind the eyes. The Throatlands are named for this: the valleys between the peaks function as enormous resonating chambers, throats through which the sound passes and is given texture. Each valley has its own harmonic signature. Cross from one into the next and the pitch shifts, and your sense of gravity shifts with it, and the silver grasses lean in a new direction, and the fern-trees flex their dark fronds toward a different angle of the dim amber light.

The sound has been constant for as long as anything here could be said to have duration. It changes, but slowly — over what might be centuries, a new harmonic emerges, or an old one fades to the edge of perception before returning with a subtly altered character. The plants seem to track these shifts. In valleys where a particular low frequency has recently intensified, the vegetation is thicker, lusher, the fern-trees taller and their fronds more densely subdivided, as if the sound feeds them, as if they photosynthesize noise instead of light.

Which may be exactly what they do.

---

The light is dim and sourceless. There is no sun. There is no visible sky, exactly — just the amber murk, which brightens and dims on a cycle so slow it is nearly imperceptible. Full "day" and full "night" may be separated by what would feel like weeks, or months. In the deepest dim phases, the only illumination comes from the vegetation itself: the fern-trees emit a faint bioluminescence from the undersides of their fronds, a cold greenish glow that makes the still-forming cliff faces look like the walls of an unfinished cathedral lit by votive candles. The silver grasses do not glow but seem to *hold* whatever light exists, reflecting it back with an intensity slightly greater than what they receive, so that the meadows on the high plateaus are always marginally brighter than their surroundings in a way that makes no optical sense.

And scattered across these plateaus, occasionally, there are structures.

---

Small. Crude. Made from stacked stone — the same uncertain stone as the mountains, which means the structures themselves have a faintly unfinished quality, as though they too are being drafted. Low walls that might be windbreaks except there is no wind. Circular enclosures that might be hearths except there is no fuel, no ash, no char. Stone slabs balanced on stone uprights in configurations that might be tables or altars or simply the aesthetic preference of whatever built them. The craftsmanship is simple: rough-hewn, hand-shaped, no metal, no mortar. Some of the stones show marks that could be tool marks or could be the natural grain of this still-solidifying geology.

There are no builders present. There is no animal life of any kind — no insects in the silver grass, no birds (if they could be called birds) circling the peaks, no burrowing things in the not-quite-soil. The structures stand alone. Some appear very old, though "old" is a complicated concept in a landscape that hasn't finished forming — the mountains seem to have grown *around* some of the structures, incorporating them into cliff faces, half-swallowing them into still-soft stone. Others look recent, the stones barely balanced, the enclosures incomplete. It is unclear whether these are ruins or construction sites. It is unclear whether the distinction means anything here.

There are tools near some of the structures. Chisels made from a dark glassy mineral. Wedges. Things that might be levers. They are sized for hands, approximately, but no two are the same size, and some suggest hands with more fingers than you have, or fewer, or a different concept of "finger" entirely.

---

Now. The weirdness.

You've already felt it, if you've been paying attention. The unfinished stone. The waterless vegetation. The sound that seems to precede and perhaps *cause* the geology rather than emerge from it. But there is more, and it escalates as you move deeper into the Throatlands, toward the highest peaks, toward the places where the sound is loudest and the harmonic interference patterns between adjacent valleys create zones of devastating resonance.

In these zones, the landscape *stutters.*

A cliff face will present itself in two positions simultaneously — not blurred, not doubled, but genuinely occupying two locations at once, with the space between them accessible if you step into it, though what you find there is not space as you understand it but something more like a *draft of space,* a rough sketch of three-dimensionality that hasn't been assigned final coordinates. Walk through one of these stutters and you may emerge on a different peak, or at a different point in the slow light-cycle, or in a version of the same valley where the harmonic signature is shifted by a quarter-tone and the fern-trees are a different shade of black-green and the silver grasses lean in a direction that doesn't correspond to any of the other three.

Because there are more than three directions here, in the deep zones. Not always. Not consistently. But the geometry of the Throatlands is itself still forming, still being negotiated by forces that haven't settled on a final dimensional framework. Sometimes, briefly, turning to the left takes you upward. Sometimes distance compresses: a peak that appears to be miles away is reachable in a dozen steps, and then expands again behind you to its full apparent remoteness when you look back. The sound seems related to this — the deepest sub-harmonics correlate with the most severe geometric distortions, as though the noise is the mechanism by which space is being *tuned,* calibrated, argued into existence.

The vegetation responds to these distortions with an equanimity that suggests it has never known anything else. Fern-trees grow at angles that defy the local gravity, which itself is a negotiable quantity — their root systems grip stone that exists in two states, and their fronds extend into dimensional folds where light behaves differently, and they seem to thrive there, drawing nutrients (sound? geometry? raw potential?) from the seams between what is and what is still becoming.

---

In the highest places — the extreme peaks, the places where the mountains are still barely more than intention, where the stone is so new it is almost *theoretical* — the sound resolves.

This is counterintuitive. You would expect the highest, most unformed, most reality-unstable peaks to be the loudest. But the opposite is true. As you climb (and climbing is a provisional verb in a place where altitude may rearrange itself mid-step), the deafening chord that fills the Throatlands begins to clarify, to separate into its component voices, and then, at the very highest points, to become comprehensible not as sound but as *structure.* You are not hearing the mountains being formed. You are hearing the *instructions* for the mountains. The blueprint. The argument for their existence.

It is not language. It is not music. It is something prior to both — a set of propositions about what matter should do, how space should curve, where the boundaries of the possible should be drawn. And the mountains, the vegetation, the dim amber light, the stone structures with their mysterious tools — all of it is the ongoing, slow, patient response of raw material to those propositions.

The Throatlands are a world being spoken into being. You are standing inside the word.

And the word has not been finished yet.

---

The silver grasses sway. The fern-trees glow faintly in the deepening dimness. Somewhere in a valley below, a cliff face shifts slightly, committing to a new angle, settling a fraction of a degree closer to its final form. The sound continues. It has always continued. It will continue for a very long time. The tools lie where they were set down, waiting for hands that may not yet exist to pick them up and begin the fine work — the shaping, the detailing, the translation of cosmic acoustics into habitable stone.

Somewhere on a high plateau, a new structure has appeared. Three stones, balanced. It wasn't there before. It has always been there. Both of these things are true, and the grass around it leans inward, listening.
