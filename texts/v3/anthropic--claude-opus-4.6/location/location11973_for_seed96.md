# The Cisterns of Kav Morat

---

The light comes first. It comes from everywhere and has no shadow.

You are standing on a surface that is not ground. It is tile — pale, cream-colored, slightly concave — and it extends in every direction, a vast shallow bowl that stretches further than you expect, further than feels right for something made by hand. But made by hand it was. Every tile. You can see the thumbprints in the grout. You can see where a trowel was dragged, leaving a faint arc of texture, and where a different hand — smaller, more deliberate — smoothed the surface flat. Thousands upon thousands of these small signatures, pressed into the seams between tiles that have not yet had time to wear down.

This is new. All of it is new. Whatever Kav Morat is, it has barely begun to age.

---

The cistern complex spreads across a high plateau — or rather, it *is* the high plateau now, because there is no exposed rock left. Everything has been covered. Tiled over, mortared, bricked, channeled, shaped. The natural substrate has been so thoroughly entombed in construction that it has become theoretical. Somewhere beneath your feet there must be stone, but between you and it lie layers: drainage gravel packed in clay-lined beds, then a course of flat-laid brick, then a waterproofing layer of some chalite compound — chalky, dense, faintly blue where it shows at broken edges — then the tile surface you stand on. Someone built downward before they built up.

The plateau is high enough that the air is cool and thin. You can feel it in your lungs, a faint insufficiency that makes you breathe more deliberately. The sky is an unbroken white — not cloudy exactly, but luminous, a diffuse radiance without a visible sun. It casts no shadows. The light is bright and even and somehow *heavy*, the way light gets when it has bounced off water and tile and water again so many times that it has forgotten which direction it came from. Everything gleams. Everything is the color of wet bone, wet chalk, wet milk.

And everything is wet.

---

Water is the organizing principle here. Not flowing water — there are no rivers, no waterfalls, nothing dramatic. The water is *systemic*. It is present the way blood is present in a body: in every channel, behind every wall, under every surface, always close, always moving, but slowly, slowly.

The cisterns themselves are arranged in a dense, interlocking pattern across the plateau's face. From where you stand you can see dozens of them, and the topology suggests hundreds more beyond — open rectangular basins sunk into the tiled surface, each one roughly the size of a modest room, each one full nearly to the brim. The water in them is still, or almost still. It carries a faint mineral turbidity that makes it look like thinned cream. Not opaque — you can see into it, maybe a forearm's depth — but not clear either. Something calcium. Somethingite. The dissolved bones of the plateau itself, perhaps, leached upward through the construction and held in suspension.

Between the cisterns, the walkways are narrow. Barely wide enough for two people to pass each other, though there are no people here. There are no people anywhere. The walkways are tiled in the same pale ceramic, but with slightly raised edges — low curbs, ankle-height — and their surfaces are perpetually damp. Water seeps up through the grout lines, or condenses out of the saturated air, or both. The tile is never dry. When you step, there is a faint *tck* of moisture releasing and resealing under your foot.

Every few paces, a narrow channel crosses the walkway — a groove cut into the tile, no wider than three fingers, carrying a thin rill of water from one cistern toward another. These channels have been carved with evident care. Their edges are crisp, un-eroded, the cuts still fresh and sharp. Some of them branch. Some converge. The system has a logic to it, though the logic is not immediately legible. Water moves from basin to basin according to some principle you haven't grasped yet — gravity, possibly, since the plateau is not perfectly level, but the grade is so subtle that you can't see it. You can only infer it from the direction of the water's barely perceptible drift.

---

The density of construction increases as your eye travels outward. The cisterns are packed close, but between them, and around them, and sometimes rising from their centers on narrow brick pedestals, are other structures. Walls that seem to serve no architectural purpose — freestanding, waist-high or shoulder-high, built of the same pale brick, mortared thickly. Some of them are straight, some curved. They break the space into smaller pockets without ever fully enclosing anything. They create intimacy without rooms. You weave between them and the cistern edges and the channels and the low curbs, and the sensation is of navigating something very full — not cluttered, because everything is purposeful, but *packed*, saturated, a space where every available volume has been claimed by some element of the built environment.

There are collection surfaces — broad shallow dishes of tile, slightly angled, mounted on brick columns at varying heights. They gather the condensation from the air. You can see the moisture beading on their undersides, gathering into droplets, falling with a barely audible *tik* into smaller channels that feed the larger channels that feed the cisterns. The whole plateau is a machine for catching water and holding it and moving it and catching it again.

Some of the basins are deeper than others. You pass one that is fully six feet deep — you can tell because of the discoloration on its inner walls, a tidemark showing the slow mineral accumulation at different water levels. But even this deep basin is full to within a hand's breadth of the walkway edge. You could reach down and touch the surface without kneeling. The water is slightly warmer than the air, and where your fingertip touches it, a ring of disturbance spreads outward with unusual slowness, as though the dissolved minerals have given the water a faint viscosity, a reluctance.

---

The quiet is the thing you notice gradually. There should be sound here — this much water, this much construction, this much *presence* — but the plateau absorbs it. The tile and brick and mineral water conspire to deaden resonance. What sounds exist are small, close, and brief: the *tik* of condensation falling. The *tck* of your footstep on wet tile. A faint, faint susurrus — more felt than heard — of water moving through the channels, a sound below the threshold of hearing that registers instead as a kind of vibration in the soles of your feet. If you stand still and hold your breath, you might hear the plateau breathing. A slow tidal rhythm. Intake and pause. Something filling. Something settling.

No wind. The air is cool and utterly still and saturated to its limits. You can feel the moisture on your skin, on your eyelashes. The bright, shadowless light makes the moisture visible — a faint aureole around every edge, a softness to every contour. The air itself seems faintly luminous, as though the light has gotten tangled in the water vapor and can't find its way out.

---

There are no plants. Nothing green, nothing growing, nothing alive in any way that you can identify. The water holds no algae, no biofilm, nothing. The tile surfaces are clean — not sterile exactly, but *uncolonized*. The mineral content of the water may prevent biological incursion, or the water may be too new, or the conditions may be wrong in some way that you can't perceive. Whatever the reason, the cisterns hold their pale milky water in perfect lifelessness. The basins are not ponds. They are vessels. The water in them is not habitat — it is material, held in reserve.

No insects skim the surface. No birds cross the white sky. The plateau belongs entirely to itself, to its own densely packed infrastructure of basin and channel and wall and tile. It is a world made entirely of craft and mineral and water and light, and it does not appear to need anything else.

---

And yet someone built it.

The evidence is everywhere, once you begin to look. The thumbprints in the grout. The trowel marks. The places where a line of tile changes character slightly — different clay, different firing temperature — suggesting a different batch, a different kiln, perhaps a different season of work. The brick walls are hand-formed, slightly irregular, with the characteristic compression marks of having been shaped in a simple wooden mold. The mortar was applied with fingers and flat tools, not extruded or precision-placed. The channels were chiseled, not machined. Everything here was made with hand tools, with patience, with what appears to be extraordinary collective labor.

The scale is staggering when you consider the method. The plateau stretches in every direction — you cannot see its edges. The cisterns number in the hundreds at minimum, probably thousands. Each one is lined, sealed, connected. The drainage substrate beneath everything required moving and laying an incomprehensible volume of gravel and brick. And it was all done by hand. Not sophisticated hands — there is no metal visible anywhere, no iron reinforcement, no steel tools. The chisels that carved the channels may have been hard stone, or perhaps simple bronze at most. The kilns that fired the tile were likely wood-fed, or fueled by something else local that left no trace.

The construction shows no single plan, exactly, but it shows a consistent logic — a culture of building that understood its own rules deeply enough to expand them endlessly without contradiction. New cisterns could be added at the margins. New channels could be cut to incorporate them. The system is not designed so much as *grown*, but grown according to a discipline so thorough that the result is indistinguishable from design.

---

Where are the builders?

Not here. Not recently, you think — but not long ago either. The edges of everything are still too sharp, the grout still too pale, the tile surfaces still too uniform. Nothing has been repaired because nothing has needed repair yet. The construction has the quality of something just finished, or nearly finished, or perhaps still being finished at its distant margins, where you cannot see.

But in the space where you stand — among the packed cisterns and the low walls and the moisture-beaded collection dishes — there is no one. There is no evidence of habitation: no soot marks, no worn patches on the walkways, no accumulated debris. The builders built and then withdrew, or the builders built and are still building somewhere beyond the luminous horizon, extending the plateau's tiled surface outward in every direction, covering whatever raw world lies beneath with this dense, wet, purposeful quiet.

---

You walk for a while. The cisterns change subtly — some are square, some rectangular, some have internal dividing walls that create smaller chambers within. Some have stepped edges that descend into the water like miniature amphitheaters. Some are connected to their neighbors by channels wide enough to be called sluices, with fitted stone gates that could be raised or lowered by hand. The gates are all open. Water moves freely, finding its own level across the interconnected system.

Occasionally you encounter a structure that is not a cistern. A column of stacked brick, solid, rising to shoulder height, its top surface slightly concave and holding a small pool of collected condensation. A low bench-like form, tiled on top, wide enough to sit on but also wide enough to be a working surface. A series of niches set into a freestanding wall, each niche the size of a brick removed from the pattern, each one empty but shaped with evident intention. Storage? Display? Some function that has no name in your vocabulary?

The weirdness here is not dramatic. It is structural. It is the weirdness of a world that is entirely made — that has no ground, only substrate; no weather, only condensation; no ecology, only hydrology. The rules here are the rules of masonry and gravity and mineral dissolution and slow, patient water. Nothing in this place is natural in the ordinary sense, but nothing is unnatural either. It is simply *built*, totally and comprehensively, a landscape that is also an artifact, a plateau that is also a tool.

And it is waiting. You can feel it. The cisterns are full. The channels are flowing. The collection surfaces are gathering. Whatever purpose all of this water serves — whatever the builders intended when they began tiling over the raw rock of this high, cool, bright place — it has not yet arrived. The system is charged, primed, saturated. The water sits in its thousands of basins, milky and still and faintly warm, and the light presses down from the white sky without shadow, and the air is so wet it is almost rain that has forgotten how to fall.

Something is being stored here. Something is being prepared.

The plateau hums with it — a frequency below sound, below vibration, something you register only as a slow pressure against the underside of your thoughts. The patience of the place is immense. It was built by hand. It will fulfill its purpose on the same timescale: slowly, thoroughly, without urgency, without waste.

You stand among the cisterns in the bright, wet silence, and you wait with it.
