# The Woven Heights

---

It begins with the understanding that the trees built themselves into the sky, and then the sky forgot it was anything other than trees.

---

You stand on a branch. You have always been standing on a branch. The branch is four hundred meters wide and it curves gently upward toward a vanishing point lost in warm, amber murk, and its surface is bark — deeply furrowed, ancient, rain-dark bark — but the furrowing has been *shaped*. Channels run along it in deliberate patterns, catching the condensation that weeps perpetually from the dimness above. The channels feed into basins. The basins are carved — no, *grown* — into the wood itself, their lips smoothed by centuries of use, their interiors darkened by tannins. Someone drinks from these. Someone has always drunk from these.

But there is no one here. Not right now.

Look up. The light that reaches you is the color of tea held to a candle — a dim, suffused warmth that has been filtered through so many layers of living canopy that it has forgotten what star it came from. There *is* a star, presumably. Somewhere above the final leaf-layer, past the uppermost membranes where the crown-growth merges into something meteorological, there must be open sky. You have never seen it. The oldest carvings on the burl-walls — simple, deep, gouged with stone tools into heartwood — depict no sun. They depict *brightness*, a general condition, coming from everywhere above. The people here, the ones who are not here right now, may not have a concept of sky as distinct from canopy.

Look down. The branch you stand on emerges from a trunk you cannot see the full circumference of. It simply *becomes the horizon* in both directions — a curved wall of living wood, shaggy with epiphytes and bracket fungi the size of plazas, disappearing into the warm murk below and above and sideways. The trunk is so large that it has its own weather. Updrafts form along its sun-facing side (if there is a sun-facing side; orientation is theological here, not astronomical). Clouds — actual clouds, small ones, wet and clinging — form in the hollows where major branches diverge, trapped there, cycling through condensation and evaporation in perpetuity. This is where the water comes from. Not rain from above, but the breath of the tree itself, caught and re-caught in the architecture of its own growth, unable to fall because there is always another branch below, another cupped leaf, another basin carved by careful hands.

The scale is — 

It resists description by analogy. A continent of wood. A planet that decided to be a forest instead of a lithosphere. The "ground" is a concept that exists in the carvings but not in living memory: the oldest burl-inscriptions show something called *the root-dark*, depicted as a place of absolute density far below, where the trunks converge and merge and the wood becomes so compressed it might as well be stone. No one goes there. No one has been there in a very long time. Some of the carvings suggest no one *can* go there — that the tree-matter below a certain depth becomes hostile, not in an animate way but in the way that pressure and darkness and the sheer mass of living tissue becomes hostile. The grain closes. The passages seal. The wood *remembers* downward and will not allow it.

And yet — the emptiness.

This is the paradox that defines the Woven Heights. The vegetation is *total*. Every surface is alive. The branch beneath your feet is alive. The air carries spores and pollen in such density that breathing feels faintly sweet. Mosses upholster every northern face (if there is a north; again, orientation is theological). Orchids the size of rowboats hang from sub-branches, their roots woven into the bark in patterns that look — that *are* — deliberately trained, guided by hands that understood them. Ferns cascade from crevices in curtains so vast they create their own microclimates beneath them. Lichens illuminate certain surfaces with a faint bioluminescence, a blue-green foxfire that provides the only light in the deeper reaches where even the tea-colored glow cannot penetrate.

But the *space*. The space between the branches is cathedral. It is oceanic. You stand on your four-hundred-meter-wide branch and you look out into the gap between this branch and the next major branch — a branch belonging to the same tree, or possibly a different tree; at this scale, the distinction between individual organisms has become academic — and the gap is *kilometers*. Kilometers of warm, dim, spore-thick air. Open. Void. A great amber nothing in which small clouds drift and things fly.

The things that fly.

They move in the distance, mostly — dark shapes wheeling in the open space, riding the thermals that curl off the trunk-face. Some are enormous. Shadows that cross the gap in long, slow glides, their wingspans suggesting something between a condor and a manta ray. They make no sound you can hear; at this distance, in this hush, they are purely visual. Closer in, along the branch-surfaces, smaller creatures move. Things with too many legs that navigate the bark-furrows with the ease of water finding channels. Colonies of something soft and pale that cluster in the basin-edges, possibly feeding on the biofilm that grows where water collects. A creature you catch only in peripheral vision — a vertical flicker along the trunk-wall, ascending, gone before you can focus. The fauna is *present*, but it observes the same silence as the place itself. Footfalls are muffled by moss. Wings don't beat; they hold. Calls, if they exist, operate at frequencies below your attention or above it.

The quiet is the first thing you lose track of and the last thing you notice. It isn't silence — true silence would feel dead, and this place is aggressively, overwhelmingly alive. It's more that the living systems here have reached a collective agreement about volume. The condensation dripping into the carved basins: a faint, irregular *tok, tok-tok*. The hiss of a breeze moving through a fern-curtain a hundred meters away, so attenuated by distance that it arrives as a whisper's memory. Somewhere far below, resonating through the wood itself more than through the air, a deep tidal creak — the tree *settling*, adjusting to stresses that operate on geological timescales. You feel it in your feet more than hear it. The branch is shifting. It has been shifting for ten thousand years. It will shift for ten thousand more. The carved basins are occasionally re-carved, adjusted for the new grade.

---

Now: the *built* quality. This is where reality begins to lose its edges.

Everything here is constructed. But the construction and the organism are the same thing. The tree — the forest — the world — did not evolve and then get shaped by inhabitants. Or: the inhabitants did not arrive and then begin building. The relationship is older and stranger and more *dissolved* than that. The branches grow in patterns that are architecturally deliberate — arching to create sheltered underspaces, bifurcating to form frameworks that could hold (and do hold, under their moss and orchid coverings) stretched membranes of dried fungal tissue that serve as walls, as windbreaks, as the boundaries of rooms. But who taught the tree to grow this way? The carvings don't say. The carvings treat the tree's architecture and the inhabitants' modifications as the same thing, depicted with the same symbolic vocabulary, as though the distinction between "the tree grew a shelter" and "someone built a shelter from the tree" is not a distinction this culture recognizes.

The tools you find — and you find them, resting in bark-niches, hanging from pegs of trained woody growth — are simple. Stone blades, the stone itself a mystery (there is no stone here; it must come from the root-dark, or from some deposit ofite mineralite trapped in ancient heartwood). Bone awls, made from the skeletons of the great gliding things, light and strong. Cord woven from inner bark fibers so fine it could be silk. These are the tools of a people who *shape growth* rather than *cut and assemble*. They score the cambium. They redirect branches while the branches are still green and compliant. They inoculate specific fungi into specific locations to produce specific structural outcomes decades later. Their technology is patience. Their engineering is gardening. Their craft is a conversation with the tree conducted over centuries, and the tree — the tree *answers*.

This is where the weirdness lives.

The tree answers. Not metaphorically. The carvings are clear about this, and the evidence supports it, if you let yourself look.

You notice it first in the branch-paths. You are walking along the main branch, following the carved water-channels because they lead somewhere, and you come to a place where the branch forks. Both forks are walkable. Both have water-channels. But one fork has — how to describe it — *anticipated* you. The bark surface along that fork has been recently smoothed. Not carved; there are no tool-marks. The bark *grew* smooth in the last few days, producing a flat, almost polished walkway that extends into the dim distance. The other fork is rough, furrowed, mossy, wild. It is being *un-maintained*. The tree is letting it go feral because no one is expected there.

You take the smooth fork. Of course you do.

As you walk, the architecture elaborates. Overhead, branches arc to form a vaulted ceiling, and from that ceiling, aerial roots descend in curtains that are too regular to be random but too organic to be planted. They form a colonnade. They frame the view outward, where the amber void opens up and you can see, for the first time, a *distance* — another trunk-wall, maybe fifty kilometers away, barely visible, a dark vertical suggestion in the murk, and on its face, at roughly your elevation, a faint blue-green scatter of bioluminescence in a pattern too structured to be wild growth. Someone lives there. Or something does. Or the tree is performing the *idea* of habitation for its own reasons.

The temperature is warm. Not tropical, not steaming, but a steady, deep, biological warmth — the warmth of a compost heap, of a body. The tree is generating heat. Its metabolism, at this scale, is a furnace. The wood beneath your feet is warm to the touch. In the deeper hollows, where the heartwood has been carved or has *offered itself* into chambers, the warmth intensifies, and the air smells of sap and fermentation and something almost like bread. Things are growing in those chambers. Cultures of yeast, maybe. Fungal gardens. The inhabitants tend them, or the tree tends them, or the tending is a single process that cannot be divided into actor and acted-upon.

---

And then there are the places where the branch is not a branch.

You come to one now. The smooth path leads you to a widening — a plaza, almost, a hundred meters across, where the branch-surface flattens and the bark takes on a strange, almost fleshy texture. The water-channels converge here into a single shallow pool, no more than ankle-deep, warm as blood, perfectly still. The pool reflects the dim amber light and the blue-green foxfire from the bioluminescent lichens that ring the space, and in the reflection you see — 

Not what's above you.

The reflection shows a *different canopy*. A canopy that is denser, closer, its leaves a deep arterial red rather than the muted greens of your surroundings. In the reflection-canopy, things move — not the gliding shadow-creatures of the open air but something smaller, quicker, with luminous eyes that look *down* at you from *inside* the pool's surface. You blink. The reflection ripples even though nothing has disturbed the water. When it settles, the canopy in the reflection has changed again. Now it is sparse, skeletal, as if seen in a different season — a season this place does not have, for the temperature never varies, the growth never stops. You are looking at *another version* of the tree. Or the same tree at a different time. Or a place the tree is *remembering*, rendering in this still water like a living memory projected into the one substance that can hold it.

The carvings around the pool are the oldest you've seen. The stone-cut lines are almost erased by the growth of the bark itself, as though the tree has been slowly, lovingly, eating the inscriptions — not destroying them but *incorporating* them, drawing the information into its own tissue. What remains visible depicts a figure standing at the pool's edge, arms raised, and from the figure's hands, roots extend downward into the water and branches extend upward into the canopy and the figure is *becoming* the tree or the tree is *becoming* the figure and the carving makes no attempt to clarify which because the question does not apply here.

This is the reality breakdown. It is not dramatic. There are no tears in the fabric of space, no impossible geometries (or if there are, they look like wood-grain, like growth-rings, like the natural spiral of a climbing vine, and you cannot distinguish them from biology). The breakdown is *categorical*. The categories of natural and built, of organism and architecture, of self and environment, of present and past, of real and reflected — they do not hold here. They have been composted. They have been *digested* by something so vast and so patient and so alive that it has had time to dissolve every boundary you thought was structural.

You are standing on a built thing that is a living thing that is a world-thing. You are standing in a city that is a forest that is a single organism that may or may not be dreaming. The air is warm and sweet and almost silent. Somewhere far away, a great shape glides across the amber void, and its shadow passes over you like a slow, soft hand, and for a moment the light dims even further and the foxfire brightens in response and the whole space shifts color — blue-green, spectral, submarine — and then the shadow passes and the amber returns and nothing has changed and everything has changed and the water in the pool shows a canopy you have never seen before, full of fruit, heavy with something that might be ripeness or might be intention.

The branch beneath your feet creaks. Warmly. Settling. Going on.

A new water-channel is forming where there wasn't one yesterday. It leads further in.
