# The Plateau of Constant Engines

---

There is a sound here that has never started and will never stop.

It is not a drone exactly — it has too much body for that, too many overlapping textures. It is more like a chord played on an organ the size of a continent, each pipe a different diameter, a different alloy, a different century of corrosion, all of them sounding the same note they have always sounded. The sound does not echo because there is nothing close enough to bounce it back. It simply goes outward in every direction across the plateau and eventually becomes indistinguishable from the cold, wet air itself.

---

The plateau is immense. You understand this not because you can see its edges — you cannot — but because the weather here behaves as if it has forgotten there is anything else. Clouds form over one district of the plateau and rain on another district of the same plateau, never leaving, never referencing a sea or a continental shelf or a pressure system born elsewhere. The sky is a ceiling of pewter and zinc, close enough to feel architectural, and it has looked exactly like this for as long as anyone could have looked. The light that comes through is even and directionless, the gray-white of a frosted bulb, and it casts no shadows. Noon and morning and evening are the same illumination. You could not photograph a sundial here. There is no sun to dial.

The air is cold — not biting, not dramatic, but persistently, structurally cold, the way stone is cold, the way deep water is cold, a temperature that does not fluctuate because fluctuation would require some engine of change, and the only engines here have other purposes. The cold sits at a fixed point just above freezing, close enough that water can't decide what it wants to be. Moisture hangs in the atmosphere like a permanent ambivalence. It condenses on every surface — on the pipework, on the gantry rails, on the flat dark rock of the plateau itself — and forms a film of water that never evaporates and never freezes and never accumulates into anything that flows. The ground is always wet. The metal is always wet. Your skin, if you were here, would be wet within minutes, and it would stay wet, and you would eventually stop noticing.

---

The plateau itself is young rock. Basalt, or something like basalt — dark, fine-grained, still sharp at its fracture points, not yet weathered into the soft undulations that deep time would give it. It rose, or was raised, or appeared, and then nothing happened to it. Rain does not carve channels here because rain does not fall hard enough or long enough in any one place; it mists, it beads, it dampens, but it does not erode. There are no rivers. There are no gullies. The stone looks freshly broken, as if the plateau calved from some planetary interior last week, except that last week was identical to this week and to every week that can be remembered or projected. The edges of the rock are crisp. Joints and cooling fractures run in clean geometric patterns across the surface — hexagonal columns in some places, long parallel sheets in others — and they are filled with condensation but not with soil, not with sediment, not with the soft organic muck of decay. Nothing has had time to decay, or rather, time here does not include decay as one of its properties.

And yet.

Between the fractures, in the joints of the rock, in the shallow depressions where moisture pools a few millimeters deep — there is moss. There is lichen. There are things that are perhaps neither moss nor lichen but something else, something thick and jewel-green and low to the ground, something that grows in dense mats and felted cushions and does not seem to grow taller or thinner or die back or spread. It is simply present, the way the moisture is present, the way the sound is present. It is intensely, almost aggressively green — the only saturated color in the entire landscape — and it covers perhaps a third of the visible rock surface in irregular patches that look, from a sufficient height, like the map of a country that has existed too long to need borders. Where the moss grows, it is soft underfoot. Where it doesn't, the basalt is glassy and slick with wet. There are no trees. There are no grasses. There are no flowers or seeds or fruits or thorns. Just the moss, the lichen, the unnamed green things, holding to the rock like a thought that won't leave.

No animal has ever moved across this moss. No insect has burrowed into it. No bird has landed on it, because there are no birds, and there are no insects, and there are no animals of any kind — not underground, not in the water film, not in the air. The silence of absent fauna is total and complete and it has never been otherwise. The ecosystem here is two-tiered: rock and photosynthesizer, mineral and chlorophyll, and nothing has ever emerged to eat the green, nothing has ever evolved to parasitize it, nothing has ever crawled or hopped or wriggled across the wet stone. The moss grows. The moss does not grow further. The moss does not die. That is the entire biology of this world.

---

Now: the engines.

They rise from the plateau at irregular intervals — enormous, dark, industrial structures that are the source of the sound, or perhaps the sound is the source of them, it is genuinely unclear. They are not clustered. They are not evenly spaced. They are distributed across the plateau's vastness with the logic of a settlement — there are denser concentrations that might be called towns, and between the towns there are gaps of twenty, fifty, a hundred kilometers of empty wet rock and moss, and then another structure, alone, doing whatever it does.

The structures are built from iron and from something darker than iron. They are riveted. They are piped. They have chimneys that rise into the low cloud ceiling and disappear, and from the chimneys comes steam or vapor or some emission that is indistinguishable from the clouds themselves — perhaps the clouds *are* the emission, perhaps the entire sky is exhaust, perhaps the weather of this plateau is an industrial byproduct that long ago became the atmosphere. The structures have housings and turbines and flywheels visible through gaps in their cladding, and the flywheels turn, and the turbines turn, and they have always turned at exactly this speed, and the speed does not vary, and the sound does not vary, and nothing about the operation of these machines has ever changed.

They are not new. Condensation stains run down their flanks in long mineral streaks — orange and white and verdigris — and the rivets are swollen with oxidation, and in places the cladding has developed a surface that is more rust than metal, a kind of ferric fur. But they are not old either, not in the way that implies decline. The rust does not deepen. The rivets do not fail. The turbines do not slow. Entropy has visited these machines exactly once and then stopped, leaving them in a state of permanent mild corrosion that will not progress to collapse.

Between the larger structures, in the denser concentrations, there are smaller buildings — low, blocky, flat-roofed, made from the same dark iron or from the basalt itself, cut and stacked with visible precision. These have doors that do not open. They have windows of thick greenish glass behind which nothing moves. They have the proportions of workshops, of control rooms, of housing — yes, housing, dormitories perhaps, or barracks, or apartments, with the implied domesticity of plumbing and ventilation shafts and what might be, behind the glass, the shapes of furniture. But no one is in them. No one has ever been in them, or no one is in them now, and the distinction between those two possibilities has been erased by the absolute stillness of the interiors. Moss grows on their roofs. Condensation drips from their eaves. They are part of the plateau now, as geological as the basalt.

There are roads — or tracks — or cleared paths where the rock has been cut flat and smooth, connecting the structures to each other in routes that imply logistics, supply chains, purpose. Some of the roads have rails set into them, iron rails, narrow gauge, and on a few of these rails there are carts or wagons, stopped, laden with something — dark ore, or processed material, or machine components — and they are motionless and have been motionless and their wheels are welded to the rails by rust that grew exactly this far and no farther.

---

What are the machines doing?

They are working. That much is certain from the sound alone — the deep, harmonically rich, continental chord of mechanical effort. The turbines turn. Steam or something like steam moves through the pipe networks in vast arterial systems that connect structure to structure across kilometers of open rock, the pipes elevated on iron trestles that march across the moss-covered basalt like the vertebrae of unimaginable animals. Something is being processed. Something is being converted, or generated, or refined. Intake and output. Cause and effect. But the intake does not diminish and the output does not accumulate, and whatever transformation is occurring inside those housings has reached a perfect equilibrium in which the machines work exactly as hard as they need to work to continue working, and the product of the work is the work itself, and the sound of the work is the sound of a world that has found its single stable state and will remain in it.

The sound is loud. At the base of a major structure, it would be deafening — the bass thrum of the turbines resonating through the iron cladding, the hiss of the pipe networks, the rhythmic percussion of connecting rods and crank assemblies, all of it playing at a volume that the human body would feel before the ears could parse it. But between the towns, across the empty stretches, the sound reduces to something almost bearable — a vibration in the rock underfoot, a thickening of the air, a pressure behind the eyes. It never reaches silence. There is nowhere on this plateau where you could escape the sound entirely. It is in the ground. It is in the moisture. It is in the clouds. It is the fundamental frequency of this place, and everything else — the green of the moss, the gray of the sky, the cold, the wet — is a harmonic built on top of it.

---

Walk far enough in any direction and the plateau does not end. Or if it ends, the ending is beyond the reach of any journey that could be made on foot across wet basalt under a sky that provides no navigational reference. The towns thin out. The structures become solitary — a single engine house standing alone on a plain of moss and rock, its chimney feeding the clouds, its turbine audible from ten kilometers away as a low pulse in the otherwise undifferentiated roar. Then even the solitary structures disappear, and there is just rock and moss and wet air and the sound, always the sound, carried from somewhere, everywhere, the harmonic residue of a thousand thousand engines working for no one toward nothing in the permanent cold.

And then, very gradually, another structure appears on the horizon. Another concentration. Another town.

The towns are not identical. The architecture varies — one will favor cylindrical housings and spiral pipe arrays, another will be all right angles and stacked cubes and cooling towers like enormous thimbles. One town's road network radiates from a central engine in a star pattern; another's follows a grid; a third's seems to trace the hexagonal jointing of the basalt beneath it, as if the town is an expression of the rock's crystalline geometry, machinery growing from mineral logic. But they are all working. They are all sounding. They are all permanently, unchangeably, industrially alive without containing a single living thing except the moss, which does not care, and the lichen, which knows even less than the moss, and the green things that are neither, which know nothing at all and grow softly in the cracks of the machine-world like a prayer no one is saying to a god who was never there.

---

Here is what is strange — stranger than the emptiness, stranger than the machines, stranger than the absent operators and the frozen logistics and the self-sustaining engines:

The plateau is aware that it is a plateau.

Not conscious. Not thinking. But the way the moisture behaves — always condensing, never flowing — and the way the sound propagates — always outward, always even — and the way the machines work — always at equilibrium, always at exactly the effort required to sustain themselves — all of it suggests a system that has solved itself. Every gradient has been resolved. Every potential energy has been converted. Every thermodynamic question has been answered, and the answer is this: this temperature, this humidity, this speed of turbine rotation, this thickness of rust, this depth of moss, this volume of sound, this exact and permanent configuration of matter and energy across a landscape so vast it makes its own weather and so stable it has made the same weather forever.

Nothing here is waiting. Nothing here is becoming. The plateau is not a before-picture or an after-picture. It is the picture. The final, the total, the equilibrium.

The sound continues. The moisture condenses. The moss grows to exactly the height it has always been and will always be, soft and green and impossibly alive in a world that finished happening long ago and forgot to stop.
