# The Drowned Plateau

---

The water is not above you. The water is *everything*. You are already inside it. You have always been inside it.

This is not an ocean floor. This is a high place — a plateau, a mesa — that happens to exist entirely within a body of water so vast it has no surface anyone has ever confirmed. The pressure here is not the crushing abyssal weight of deep ocean. It's lighter than that, somehow, as though the water itself is thinner at this altitude, as though elevation means something even when there is no air. The plateau rises from unseen depths below, and the water extends upward into unseen darkness above, and the cold comes from every direction equally — a still, mineral cold, the temperature of stone that has never known sunlight, hovering just above freezing but never quite arriving there.

There is no light source. And yet you can see.

The kelp produces it. Or not kelp exactly — kelp is a word from a world with surfaces and tides. These are something else. They grow in vast, separated groves across the plateau's weathered top, each grove a loose cathedral of fronds rising thirty, forty meters into the black water overhead, and each frond carries a faint luminescence that is not bioluminescence because there is no *bios* here, no animal life whatsoever, nothing that swims or crawls or pulses or hunts. The light is vegetal. It is the slow, cold phosphorescence of chlorophyll working without sun, pulling energy from chemical gradients in the water itself, and it illuminates the landscape in a color that your eyes want to call green but that is actually something older, something that sits between green and violet and gray. The light does not travel far. Each grove is its own dim island of visibility, and between them the plateau stretches in near-total darkness, and you move from glow to glow like moving between campfires on a lightless plain.

The sound is the first thing that unnerves you.

Water transmits sound more efficiently than air, and the plateau is loud in a way that has nothing to do with life. There are deep harmonic thrums that come from below — the stone of the plateau itself resonating as thermal currents shift through its porous interior. There are high, glassy pings that seem to originate from the kelp groves when their fronds knock together in the slow currents, a sound like someone flicking crystal with a fingernail, multiplied hundreds of times and arriving from all directions with slight delays. There is a constant low roar — not deafening but present, always present — that is simply the sound of the water moving across the plateau's eroded edges, a sound like wind if wind were thick and heavy and moved with the patience of geology. You cannot locate silence here. The plateau has its own acoustic weather, and right now it is storming with vibration.

The terrain is old. Not ancient in the way of mountains — mountains are violent, still carrying the memory of their uplift — but old in the way of a thing that has found its shape and kept it. The plateau's surface is a rumpled stone plain, basaltic, dark gray-brown, worn into long smooth undulations by eons of current. There are no sharp edges anywhere. Every ridge has been softened into a curve, every crevice widened into a gentle trough. The erosion is not dramatic; it is *settled*, the kind of weathering that has reached an equilibrium with its environment, where the rate of dissolution exactly matches the rate of mineral deposition from the saturated water. In places, the stone has developed a rind of pale calcium carbonate, giving it the look of bone — old, clean bone, the skeleton of a continent.

The groves grow in the troughs and hollows where sediment has gathered enough to give root-analogs purchase. Up close, the vegetation is extraordinary. The main fronds are thick as your wrist at the base, tapering over their full height to filaments as fine as hair, and they move in the current with a slow, deliberate grace that looks almost like intention. Smaller plants — bladeless, just branching structures of tube and node — cluster at the bases of the large fronds, forming dense mats of interlocking growth that trap sediment and build their own soil. There are things like mosses on the rocks between groves, flat crusts of photosynthetic tissue that produce no light of their own but respond to the light of the groves by deepening in color, turning almost black at their edges where the glow fades. The lushness is real but distributed — each grove is a pocket ecosystem, dense with growth internally, separated from the next by hundreds of meters of bare stone and low crust.

No fish. No worms. No crabs, no jellies, no anemones, nothing. This is the strangeness that sits at the center of the place and does not resolve. The ecological niches that animals would fill — grazer, decomposer, predator — are simply empty. The kelp grows and dies and falls and is broken down by microbial processes so slow you could watch a dead frond for a year and see it only slightly more translucent than when it fell. The groves thin themselves by competition alone: where two fronds grow too close, one will slowly starve the other of chemical nutrients, and the loser will fade over the course of decades, its light dimming to nothing, its structure softening until the current pulls it apart. This is the dynamic of the place — not seasons, not predation, but the slow territorial war of plants in darkness, playing out across timescales that make animal life seem hysterical.

And yet, something built here.

Scattered across the plateau, mostly between groves, occasionally within them, there are structures. They are small — none taller than two meters, most smaller — and made of the plateau's own stone, cut crudely and stacked. Some are simple cairns, towers of flat stones balanced with evident care but no mortar. Some are enclosures: low walls forming rough rectangles, roofless, open to the water above, their interiors empty or filled with sediment. A few show evidence of more deliberate craft — stones with grooves cut into them, notches that once held crosspieces, surfaces scraped flat with what must have been hand tools, the marks still visible as parallel lines in the calcium-crusted surface.

They are not ruins exactly. Ruins imply a civilization that rose and fell. These imply something more minimal: a presence that was here, that needed to mark the stone or shelter within low walls for reasons that may not have had anything to do with habitation. Some of the cairns are positioned at the exact midpoints between groves, as if marking routes through the dark stretches. Others seem to have no spatial logic at all. In one place, near the plateau's eroded northern edge, a single wall stands alone — four meters long, perfectly straight, built of stones so carefully selected for uniformity that the courses are nearly level. It shelters nothing. It faces nothing. It stands in the current like a sentence interrupted mid-word.

Who built them. What built them. The kelp does not build. The plateau has no animal life to have evolved hands or intention. The structures show no signs of ongoing construction — they are as weathered as the stone around them, coated in the same calcium rind, colonized by the same crust-mosses. They are old. They are as old as the current state of the plateau, which means they were built when the plateau was already this — already submerged, already cold, already dark, already empty of animals. Something came here that was not from here, built small and simple things, and left.

Or something was here that is no longer recognizable as what it was.

There is one more strangeness. In the largest grove — a stand of several hundred fronds occupying a broad depression near the plateau's center — the kelp grows in a pattern. Seen from above (if above meant anything here), the fronds form concentric rings, each ring a slightly different shade of that green-violet-gray light, the innermost ring the brightest, the outermost the dimmest. At the center of the rings, there is a cleared space, maybe ten meters across, where no kelp grows at all but where the stone has been worn into a shallow bowl, perfectly smooth, as if something heavy rested there for a very long time and was eventually removed. Or dissolved. Or grew legs and walked into the dark.

The bowl collects sediment. The sediment is finer than the sediment elsewhere on the plateau — almostite powder, calcium-white, and when the current shifts it stirs into small clouds that catch the light of the innermost ring and glow, briefly, like cold sparks, before settling again. This happens regularly: the current shifts every few hours with the deep thermal rhythms of the plateau's interior, and each time the bowl breathes out its small cloud of light, and each time the light fades, and each time the silence that was never actually silence reasserts itself as thrumming stone and clicking fronds and the long low voice of water moving over the edge of a high place that has no sky.

You are cold. You have been cold since you arrived. The cold is not aggressive; it is simply the medium. It is the water. It is what everything here has adapted to, grown into, built within. The plateau does not want you to leave. The plateau does not want anything. It is a place that has been what it is for so long that intention has weathered away along with the sharp edges of its stone, and what remains is a vast, dim, thrumming, lush, empty, half-built, fully strange permanence that will outlast whatever question you came here to ask.

The current shifts. The bowl exhales. The cold light rises and falls.
