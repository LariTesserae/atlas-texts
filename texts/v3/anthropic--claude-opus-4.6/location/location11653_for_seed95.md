# The Plateau of Settled Bones

---

You stand on something that was once a mountain range. You know this not because anyone told you, but because the stone itself remembers — in the way it lays down in long, sighing undulations, in the way certain ridges still hold the faintest ghost of what was once a jagged spine, now worn so thoroughly that walking along them feels like running your hand across the skull of something impossibly old. The erosion here has not merely smoothed the land. It has *finished* it. This is stone that has nowhere left to go. Every crystal that could fracture has fractured. Every face that could weather has weathered. The plateau is what remains when gravity and time reach an agreement and stop arguing.

It stretches for what might be twenty miles in the direction you'd call east, though distances deceive here. The air is warm and dry — warm the way stone is warm after a long afternoon — and there is a faint haze that is not quite dust, not quite humidity, but something in between that turns the farther formations into blue-grey suggestions of themselves. The light is plain. Not dramatic, not golden, not dim — just the honest, unexaggerated light of a sky that is thinly overcast, clouds so high and so uniform they barely register as clouds at all. The sun is present but unnamed, diffused into a general brightening of the world that casts soft shadows and leaves no glare on the stone.

The plateau is high. You can feel this in your lungs if you move too quickly — a gentle thinning, a reminder that atmosphere is not infinite. To the west, the land does eventually fall away, but so gradually that the edge is more concept than cliff. Somewhere out there, miles below and miles away, there may be lowlands. From here they are simply a deepening of the haze.

---

What is dense here is not the vegetation, and not the structures. What is dense is *presence*. Living presence. The plateau is saturated with fauna to a degree that seems, at first, impossible given how sparse the plant life is.

The plants themselves are low, hard, and ancient in their own right — not trees, not bushes, but something closer to biological mineral formations. Lichen crusts in ochre and verdigris coat every surface that holds moisture for more than a day. In the shallow depressions where rain occasionally collects — and it does rain here, briefly, in events so infrequent they feel less like weather and more like the land remembering what water was — in those depressions, dense mats of something like moss grow in circular colonies, grey-green, feltlike, curling at their edges where they've dried. Occasionally you'll find a plant that reaches knee-height: a stiff, leafless thing with a waxy trunk and a single whorl of fibrous fronds at its crown, looking less like it's growing and more like it's been standing there since before growth was invented. These plants are sparse but not random. They follow the faint veins of moisture that seep through the stone, tracing networks invisible to the eye but obvious to root systems that have had eons to find them.

But the animals. The animals are everywhere.

---

They are strange, and they are quiet, and they are *so many*.

The first thing you notice — once your eyes adjust to the sameness of the stone — is that the stone is not same at all. It is moving. Slowly, and in pieces. What you took for cobbles are the domed backs of creatures the size of dinner plates, grey-brown, with shells so encrusted with lichen they are nearly indistinguishable from the substrate. They move at the pace of a minute hand. You can watch one and see no motion. You can look away and look back and it has traveled an inch. They are everywhere — hundreds within any given acre, fitting into the hollows and runnels of the eroded stone like they were carved to match, and perhaps in some sense they were, or the stone was carved to match them, the two having shaped each other over time beyond reckoning.

Above them — not much above, but above — are the crawlers. These are longer, segmented, somewhere between a millipede and a salamander in basic plan but not truly either. They are the color of dried blood, almost black in their deepest segments, and they flow through the crevices in the stone with a liquidity that seems wrong for something with so many legs. They are silent. They eat the lichen, or they eat something in the lichen, or they eat the tiny translucent mites that eat the lichen — the food web here is compressed and intimate, everything feeding on everything else's leavings in tight, efficient loops that waste nothing.

There are fliers, but they don't fly much. They perch. They are everywhere on the ridgelines — small, the largest maybe the size of a thrush, covered not in feathers exactly but in a dense fibrous integument that looks like felt or like the fronds of those sparse plants, grey and tan and the palest rose. They have large eyes, forward-facing, and they watch you with an attention that feels less like animal wariness and more like *interest*. They do not scatter when you approach. They sidestep. Sometimes they open their mouths — wide, triangular mouths — not to vocalize but perhaps to taste the air, or to thermoregulate, showing the startling wet pink of their inner membranes against all the grey.

And below everything, in the stone itself, there is something larger. You feel it more than see it. In certain places, the worn rock has openings — not caves exactly, but deep smooth channels, polished by something that was not water. From these channels comes a faint exhalation that is warm and humid, warmer than the ambient air, carrying a smell that is organic and old and not unpleasant — like the inside of a shell, like chalk and copper. Something breathes down there. Something large enough that its respiration creates air currents you can feel on your ankles. You never see it. You only see the evidence: the polished stone, the warmth, the way the shelled creatures cluster near the openings, the way the felt-birds orient their perching to face toward the nearest channel as if attending to a sermon delivered in a frequency below hearing.

---

This is the strangeness of the place, and it deepens the longer you stay.

The animals do not behave as animals should. There is no predation you can see — or rather, no *dramatic* predation, no chasing, no killing that registers at a human timescale. The food web is a slow one, operating at the pace of lichen growth, of mineral seepage, of the decadal erosion of stone that is already as eroded as stone can be. Death happens here — you find shells, empty, turned upside-down in the runnels, their undersides pale and smooth — but it happens the way sunset happens. Gradually, and without argument.

The creatures seem to exist in a density that should require more resources than this stripped-down plateau can provide. But then you begin to understand — or begin to suspect — that the plateau *is* providing. The warm exhalations from the deep channels carry moisture, carry minerals, carry something nutritive. The lichen grows fastest near those openings. The moss mats are thickest there. The entire surface ecology of the plateau may be, in some sense, the outgrowth of whatever lives below — not a parasite system but a *dependent* one, the way a coral reef is the outgrowth of the coral. The plateau is not merely inhabited. The plateau is *tended*. Or the plateau is a body, and everything on its surface is the flora of its skin.

There are structures. You almost miss them because they are so worn, so settled into the terrain, that they register first as geological features. Low walls — knee-high at most, made from the same stone as the plateau itself but *stacked*, clearly stacked, in a way that weathering would not produce. They form partial enclosures, arcs and half-circles, open to the sky, open at their ends. No roofs. No right angles. Some of them have been colonized so thoroughly by lichen and by shelled creatures that they are more biology than architecture. In one, you find a shallow basin — carved, you think, though the carving has been nearly erased by time — that collects the occasional rain and holds it a little longer than the surrounding stone. A drinking place. A gathering point. Around it, the felt-birds are especially numerous, sitting shoulder to shoulder on the basin's rim, not drinking — the basin is dry today — but present, as if the habit of gathering outlasted the reason for it.

Who built these walls? There is no evidence of toolmaking anywhere on the plateau. No cut marks sharp enough to read. No refuse piles, no fire-scarring, no paths beyond the ones the shelled creatures have polished through millennia of passage. The builders may have been the animals themselves — a previous behavior, now vestigial, the way a species might lose the instinct for migration but still orient restlessly southward in autumn. Or the builders may have been something else entirely, long gone, absorbed back into the ecology, their bones powdered andite incorporated into the lichen crust. The walls remain. They mean nothing now. They collect rain.

---

The sound. There is so little sound.

You would expect, with this many living things in this much proximity, a cacophony. Instead there is a hush that feels almost willed. The felt-birds do not sing. The crawlers make no sound that human ears detect. The shelled creatures, in their infinitesimal motion, produce nothing. What you hear is: the wind, when it comes, which is rarely, a low and fluting passage across the channel openings that gives the plateau a voice like someone blowing across the mouth of a bottle, deep and hollow and gone before you can be sure you heard it. The sound of your own footsteps on stone so old and so smooth it barely answers your weight. And beneath that, if you are still, if you let the quiet become a thing you listen *into* rather than *through* — a rhythm. Not a sound so much as a periodicity. The warm exhalations from the channels come and go in intervals that might be eight seconds, might be twelve, not mechanical but organic, and you can feel the plateau breathe.

That is the moment the strangeness settles fully into you. Not as fear, not as wonder, but as a kind of recognition — the understanding that you are standing on something alive, or on the surface of something alive, and that everything here — the lichen, the crawlers, the felt-birds, the shelled multitudes, the half-dissolved walls, the rain that comes once a season and is held in carved basins by builders no one remembers — all of it is one system, one organism, one breath, and it has been here longer than you have language to describe, and it will be here when the last flake of stone finally gives way and the plateau sinks back to the level of the plains, and even then the breathing will continue from somewhere deeper, patient and warm and utterly unconcerned with your passage across its back.

You walk on. The felt-birds watch you go. The shelled creatures do not notice. Below, something exhales.
