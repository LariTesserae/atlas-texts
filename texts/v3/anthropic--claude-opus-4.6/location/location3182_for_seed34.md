# The Plateaus of Gathered Stone

---

There is a city here, but no one built it.

That is the first thing you must understand. The walls are limestone and slate, drawn up from the earth by frost-heave and erosion over uncountable millennia, split into forms that resemble doorways, corridors, stacked dwellings — a metropolis of accident. The plateau stretches in every direction, hundreds of miles of flat-topped highland fissured into a labyrinth of passages, chambers, overhangs, and courtyards, all of it roofless, all of it open to a sky the color of wet cement. The resemblance to architecture is so persistent, so detailed, that the mind keeps insisting someone *meant* this. No one did. The rock simply broke this way, along the same bedding planes, at the same angles, over and over, until the plateau became a city plan drawn by geology and read by whatever creature wanders through it.

The scale is the thing that undoes you. Stand on one of the higher remnant mesas — a flat-topped column of stone that rises above the surrounding maze like a watchtower — and you can see the labyrinth continuing to the horizon in every direction, gray-brown and blue-gray and the pale ochre of exposed chalk layers, all of it wet, all of it darkened by the moisture that saturates this place. There is no single center. There are districts — areas where the passages narrow to shoulder-width and the walls rise thirty feet, areas where the stone has collapsed into broad plazas of rubble, areas where the bedding planes tilted before they froze and the walls lean at disconcerting angles, creating the impression of a neighborhood in the act of falling. Each district has its own acoustic signature, its own drainage patterns, its own particular shade of lichen on the north-facing walls. You could walk for a week and still be inside the city. You could walk for a month.

---

The water is everywhere but rarely pooled. It seeps. The plateau sits at an elevation where clouds form against its edges and drag themselves across its surface, so the stone is perpetually damp, perpetually darkened at its seams. Water weeps from horizontal cracks in the walls — not flowing, just emerging, bead by bead, tracing thin mineral trails down the stone faces. The floors of the passages are often slick with a film of moisture too thin to see but thick enough to make footing uncertain. In the broader chambers, shallow puddles collect in depressions worn into the bedrock, never more than an inch deep, their surfaces trembling faintly from some vibration transmitted through the stone — the plateau flexing under its own weight, perhaps, or distant rockfalls propagating their tremors through the labyrinth.

There are places where the seep-water gathers enough courage to become a trickle, threading its way down through the maze toward the plateau's edges. These trickles follow the passages like pedestrians, turning corners, passing through doorway-shaped gaps, sometimes disappearing into cracks and re-emerging twenty yards further on. They carry dissolved calcium with them, painting the stone with pale mineral blooms, scalloping the edges of every surface they touch. Over the centuries, they have carved grooves into the passage floors — gutters, you might call them, if this were a real city. The water has its infrastructure. The water knows the way down.

After rain — and rain comes every few days, blown in from whatever lowland sea feeds this latitude — the labyrinth fills with a quiet rushing. Not loud. The passages are too narrow and too many for any single stream to grow powerful. It is a distributed sound, coming from everywhere, the sum of ten thousand tiny watercourses all running at once, all taking different paths through the maze. It lasts for hours. Then it fades. The seeping resumes.

---

The air is cool. Not cold enough for frost — not now, not in this season — but cool enough that your breath thickens slightly when you exhale in the deeper passages where the walls block whatever warmth the muted daylight carries. The stone holds its temperature with enormous inertia. It was cool yesterday. It will be cool tomorrow. The clouds passing overhead are low and unhurried, their bases flat and gray, their upper surfaces catching light from a sun that is present but not emphatic — a white-bright region in the overcast, a diffuse glow that casts no hard shadows. The light that reaches the labyrinth floor is even, almost clinical. Every crack and texture in the stone is visible. Nothing is hidden by shadow or romanticized by golden hour. The place simply is what it is, evenly lit and damp and vast.

---

Vegetation is sparse but persistent. Mosses colonize the joints between strata — dark green, almost black when wet, reviving to a brighter jade when the rare sun breaks through. Small ferns uncurl from horizontal cracks, their fronds extending out into the passages like hands reaching from windows. In the broader plazas where rubble has broken down into coarse gravel, wiry grasses grow in tufts, pale green going to silver at the tips, bending under the weight of condensation. Nothing is tall. Nothing has managed to become a tree here — the soil is too thin, the stone too close to the surface, the wind through the upper passages too persistent. The tallest plants are knee-high shrubs that cling to south-facing walls, their roots prying into the same cracks that the water uses, their branches hugging the stone as if for warmth. Everything photosynthetic here is small, tough, and patient. Everything has found a crack and committed to it.

Lichen covers enormous areas of the walls in slow, circular colonies — some of them three feet across, meaning they are centuries old. Pale green, rust orange, chalky white. They are the dominant life-form of the city, if dominance is measured by surface area occupied. They grow so slowly that their edges appear motionless across a human lifetime, but they are in competition with each other — you can see it at the boundaries where two colonies meet, the subtle warfare of chemistry, one species darkening and dying back where another's metabolic acids reach it. The city's longest-running conflict. The city's most patient citizens.

---

Animals are rare. There are small birds — pipit-like things, dun-colored, that nest in the deeper crevices and emerge to forage along the passage floors, pecking at insects too small to see. They are quiet. An occasional thin call, barely audible, bouncing between the walls before it dies. Their nests are made of moss and grass, wedged into cracks just wide enough, and in certain passages you can find their pale droppings streaking the stone below a nest site, the only obvious sign of their presence. Higher up, on the mesa tops, larger birds — raptors of some kind — ride the updrafts that form where wind hits the plateau edge. They are visible only as silhouettes against the overcast, circling, unhurried, watching. What they hunt is unclear. Perhaps the pipits. Perhaps something in the lowlands below the plateau rim.

Insects are more common than vertebrates but still not abundant. Small beetles navigate the wet stone. Pale moths shelter in the deeper alcoves during the day. In the puddles, you might find the larvae of something — gnats, perhaps — wriggling in the shallow mineral water, completing a life cycle that depends entirely on the plateau's refusal to ever fully dry out.

If there are mammals, they are nocturnal and small and you will not see them. But you might find their scat — tiny dark pellets — on the flat tops of rubble boulders where something has sat and surveyed its small territory in the dark.

---

The labyrinth is active. That is the paradox: a place that looks so still, so ancient, so settled-into-its-form, is constantly in motion. Not fast motion. Not dramatic motion. But motion.

Somewhere in the city, a wall is calving. A slab of limestone, undercut by centuries of seep-water dissolving the softer layer beneath it, has finally lost its argument with gravity. It peels away from the wall face in a single piece the size of a door, falls six feet, and shatters on the passage floor. The sound — a dull crack followed by a cascading rattle — propagates through the labyrinth for a surprising distance, arriving at your ears as a muffled report, sourceless, directional only in the vaguest sense. Then silence again. The labyrinth has rearranged itself. A passage has narrowed slightly. A new surface of pale, unweathered stone has been exposed to the air. The mosses will reach it in a decade. The lichen in a century.

The clouds are cycling. Their shadows — diffuse as they are — move across the maze in slow waves, dimming one district while brightening another. The seep-water adjusts its output in response to atmospheric pressure changes that precede each weather front. Before rain arrives, the walls weep more freely, as if the stone can feel the low pressure drawing moisture from its pores. The puddles rise. The trickles thicken. Then the rain comes, quiet and steady, and the entire plateau exhales.

Wind moves through the passages in complex patterns. The labyrinth is essentially a vast wind instrument that no one is playing. Most of the time the wind is light and the sound is barely there — a low, almost subliminal hum in certain narrow passages, a faint whistle at a gap between two leaning walls. But when a front moves through, the wind increases, and the labyrinth speaks more audibly. Not loudly. Never loudly. But with a layered, chorded drone that seems to come from the stone itself, the sum of air moving through ten thousand corridors of different widths and lengths, each one a pipe tuned by erosion to its own particular frequency. You hear this and you understand why the mind insists someone built this place. The sound has structure. The sound has something that resembles intention.

---

There are places in the labyrinth that function as intersections — nodes where five or six passages converge on a single open space. These are the closest thing the city has to plazas, to gathering places, though nothing gathers there except water and wind and the occasional pipit. The stone in these spaces is worn smooth by drainage and foot-traffic that never happened — the water simply found these junctions the same way a crowd would, following the paths of least resistance. The walls around these spaces are often the most densely colonized by lichen, the most stained by mineral seep, the most textured. They have a quality that an architect would call *articulated*. Ledges. Recesses. Horizontal bands of contrasting stone. The geology performs ornament without knowing what ornament is.

In certain light — the flat, even light that this place usually provides — these intersection spaces are beautiful in a way that is difficult to name. Not picturesque. Not sublime. Something more structural than that. A beauty of convergence, of pattern, of the inevitable result of physical law applied to a specific set of materials over a span of time too long to imagine. The beauty of something that was always going to end up looking like this, given the conditions. The beauty of a solution.

---

The plateau has edges. You can walk for days and reach them — places where the labyrinth simply stops, where the last wall stands at the brink of a long slope down to whatever lowlands lie below. From these edges, you can see the true scale of the thing: the plateau extending behind you like a gray-brown continent, its surface fissured into the maze, clouds dragging across it, rain falling on its eastern districts while its western ones stand dry. Below, the terrain drops away into haze — green, perhaps, or brown, warmer certainly, a different world. The plateau exists above and apart from whatever surrounds it. It has its own weather. Its own ecology. Its own pace.

You can stand at the edge and feel the wind coming up from below, carrying warmth and the faint green smell of a more generous landscape, and you can turn and look back at the labyrinth — silent, wet, cool, patiently rearranging itself — and feel the pull of it. The desire to walk back in. To follow another passage. To see what the stone has made around the next corner.

No one built this city. But something about it — the repetition, the structure, the convergence of passages on nodes, the guttered floors, the lichen-decorated walls, the wind-tuned corridors — suggests that the distinction between *built* and *emerged* is less clear than you thought. The plateau doesn't care about the distinction. The plateau is busy. It has water to move, stone to break, lichen to feed, wind to channel. It has been doing this for longer than anything with hands has existed, and it will continue long after.

The next rockfall is already preparing itself somewhere in the maze. A hairline crack is widening. A mineral bond is failing. Gravity is patient. The wall will fall, and the sound will travel through the corridors, and the pipits will startle briefly from their crevices and then settle, and the labyrinth will have a new shape, barely different from the old one, and the water will find its way through.
