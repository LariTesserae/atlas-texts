# The Plateau of Thresholds

---

There is a city on a high plateau that has never known night, never known morning, never known a moment different from this one. The light is simply *there* — a diffuse, warm amber radiance with no source, no sun, no shadows that lean or lengthen. It comes from the sky and the sky is not a sky. It is a membrane of heated vapor, cloud-stuff compressed into a permanent ceiling maybe three hundred meters above the highest rooftop, glowing with its own interior luminosity as though the atmosphere itself is a filament. The air beneath it is heavy, wet, and very hot, the kind of heat that presses on your temples and makes your thinking slow, that turns the simple act of breathing into something deliberate. You inhale and the air has *texture* — a thickness, a taste of wet mineral and green rot, and something else, something that might be ozone or might be the smell of stone dreaming.

The plateau is enormous. You can see the city extending in every direction for what must be kilometers, though distance is unreliable here — I'll get to that — and it sits on a mesa of dark basalt so high above whatever lies below that the edges, where they are visible between buildings, drop into nothing. Not mist. Not darkness. *Nothing.* The plateau floats in an absence. If you lean over the lip and look down, your eyes simply stop working for a moment — not blindness, but the cessation of the concept of *seeing*, because there is nothing there for vision to interact with. Then you pull back and your sight returns, and the edge-stone is damp and moss-furred and perfectly ordinary.

---

## The City

The buildings are made of the plateau itself. They rise from the basalt as though they were always there — as though the city is not built *on* the rock but carved *from* it, or grown *out of* it, a forest of columnar structures with walls of dark stone that have been worn smooth over spans of time that feel geological. The architecture is dense and layered: buildings crowd against each other, lean into each other, share walls and archways and buttresses that may be structural or may be the city holding its own hand. There are streets, narrow and winding, floored in the same basalt but grooved with drainage channels that run permanently with warm, clear water. The water comes from everywhere. It seeps from the stone walls. It beads on every surface. It drips from the undersides of arches and pools in the cupped hands of carved figures whose faces have been softened by millennia of mineral dissolution into expressions that are no longer specific — not joy, not grief, just the general condition of *having once been*.

There are no tools here. No mechanisms, no wheels, no pulleys, no glass in the windows (the windows are just openings, round and irregular, like the blowholes of enormous stony lungs). The infrastructure is sophisticated — aqueducts, bridges, stairways that wind up the sides of towers, plazas with recessed basins that collect rainwater or condensation or whatever the wet ceiling above continuously exhales — but all of it is carved or shaped or *persuaded* from the living rock by means that were never mechanical. The joints between stones don't exist because there are no separate stones. Each structure is continuous with the plateau beneath it, as if someone with unimaginable patience simply *removed* everything that wasn't a city.

And it is a city. It has districts. It has what might be a market square — a broad hexagonal depression surrounded by columnar buildings with wide arched openings at ground level, the interior walls carved with niches of varying sizes, shelving, counters, the unmistakable spatial grammar of commerce. There are residential warrens where the rooms are small and stacked, connected by internal stairways worn concave at the center of each step. There is something that might be a temple, or a reservoir, or both: a massive open cylinder carved into the plateau's surface, maybe forty meters across, its walls terraced with descending rings of seating or kneeling-ledges that lead down to a pool of water so perfectly still it looks solid, its surface exactly level with the bottom-most terrace, so that the water and the stone seem to share a single plane of existence.

There are no people here. There have never been people here. This is important and disorienting. The city was not abandoned. It was not emptied by plague or war or slow decline. It was shaped by no hands. It simply *is* a city, the way a canyon is a canyon — a thing that emerged from the interaction of forces over deep time, except the forces here are not wind and water alone. Something else is at work. The plateau grew a city the way a skull grows a brain: because the shape was always implicit in the material.

## The Vegetation

Everything is green and it is all *wrong*, in the way that things are wrong in places where reality has started to unravel at the seams. The vegetation is lush — aggressively, oppressively lush — and it occupies every surface that the water touches, which is every surface. Mosses coat the walls in layered pelts of emerald and chartreuse and a deep, almost black green that appears purple when the light catches it at certain angles. Ferns erupt from cracks in the architecture — not damaging it, not forcing the stone apart, but emerging from it as though the stone had always intended to produce ferns at these specific locations. Broad-leafed plants with rubbery, succulent stems grow in the drainage channels, their roots trailing in the warm running water, their leaves so dark and glossy they look lacquered.

But here is where the wrongness begins: the plants don't grow. They don't wilt. They don't reproduce. There are no seeds, no spores, no flowers, no fruit. No leaf here has ever unfurled; no frond has ever been a fiddlehead. They are simply present in their current state, as fixed as the stone, as permanent as the light. If you were to tear a leaf — and you could, it would feel like tearing a leaf, wet and fibrous and alive-seeming — the torn leaf and the torn-away piece would both remain exactly as they were at the moment of separation, indefinitely, without browning, without curling, without decay. You could line the fragments up and they would fit together perfectly, and in a way that you cannot articulate, you would understand that the tear *doesn't matter*. Not because it heals but because change itself is a foreign concept here. The tear would simply become another permanent feature of the landscape, as eternal as the architecture.

Vines — if they are vines — climb the towers and thread through window openings and drape themselves across bridges in heavy curtains. Some of them are as thick as a human torso. They are not parasitic; they do not strangle the stone. They decorate it. They complete it. In places, the vine and the basalt are difficult to distinguish, as though the plant has become geological or the geology has become botanical, and the boundary between the two kingdoms has softened into suggestion.

There are no animals. No insects. No birds. No things that swim in the warm running water. The ecology here consists entirely of stone and plants and water, and none of these three is entirely itself.

## The Sound

This is the thing that defines the plateau more than anything else. It is *deafening*.

Not in the way of an explosion, not in the way of a machine. It is deafening in the way that standing inside a cathedral organ would be deafening if every pipe sounded at once and the building were the size of a city. The sound is the water — all of it, the seeping, the dripping, the running, the pooling — but amplified by the architecture into something that has crossed the threshold from noise into *environment*. Every drainage channel is a throat. Every archway is a resonating chamber. Every hollow tower is a pipe. The city is an instrument that plays itself, and the note it plays is a chord of such complexity that it sounds simultaneously like a roar and a hum and a whisper and a scream.

It is not painful. It does not damage (nothing here is damaged; nothing here *can* be damaged in any lasting sense). But it is total. It fills every cubic centimeter of air. It vibrates in the basalt and in the water and in the thick wet leaves of the plants. You feel it in your sternum. You feel it in your teeth. It makes your vision tremble very slightly, or rather, the things you are seeing tremble very slightly, because they are all vibrating — the walls, the water, the moss, the light itself seems to shiver.

And within this oceanic drone, there are harmonics. The city's geometry creates interference patterns in the sound: stand in certain doorways and the roar suddenly contains a high, sweet singing, almost melodic. Cross a particular plaza and the bass frequencies cancel and for three steps there is a pocket of near-silence so startling it feels like deafness. Climb one of the towers — the stairways spiral upward through chambers of different dimensions and the sound changes with each floor, shifting in timbre and pitch, so that the ascent is also a journey through a musical scale that belongs to no human system of tuning.

This sound has always been here. It has always been exactly this sound. The water has always been falling at exactly this rate through exactly these channels into exactly these reservoirs. The chord has never changed. Not once. Not even in the smallest particular.

## The Breakdown

Reality here is *tired*. That is the closest word, though it's not right. The plateau exists in a state of advanced ontological fatigue — it has been exactly itself for so long that the concept of *being* has worn thin, like a word repeated until it loses meaning.

You notice it first in the geometries. The streets of the city are not quite Euclidean. You walk straight down a lane between two rows of buildings, making no turns, and arrive back where you started. Not because the lane curves — it doesn't; you can see its straightness the entire time — but because *straightness* here does not preclude return. In the same way, a tower may have more interior rooms than its exterior volume could contain. A stairway may ascend for two hundred steps and deliver you to a level that is below where you began. The cylindrical temple-reservoir, seen from across the city, appears to be simultaneously in three different locations, none of them the location where you will find it if you try to walk to it. You will find it somewhere else. It will be the same one.

The water participates in this. Follow a stream in a drainage channel and it will branch, and both branches will carry more water than the original stream contained. Follow them further and they converge again, and the combined flow will be less than either branch. No water is created or destroyed; the issue is that *quantity* has become a matter of perspective, or context, or narrative position.

The plants know something about this. (Can plants know? Here: yes, or at least, the distinction between knowing and being is even more eroded than the distinction between vine and stone.) In certain corners of the city — always corners, always the places where three walls meet and the architecture creates a triangular pocket of space — the vegetation becomes *dense* in a way that defies the available volume. You look into one of these corners and see depth upon depth of green, layers of leaf and frond and moss receding inward farther than the walls behind them should allow, a compressed jungle occupying a space the size of a closet. The light in these pockets is greener, wetter, and the sound changes — the great chord drops to a lower register, becomes more felt than heard, and there is a sense of *pressure*, as though something is trying to emerge from the corner into the regular space of the city but cannot because the regular space is already full and neither space will yield.

The light participates too. It is even and sourceless and warm, as I said, but in certain corridors — always the long, straight ones, the ones that should offer clear sightlines — the light develops *grain*. It becomes almost visible as a substance, a golden particulate suspension, and in these corridors the far end is always slightly out of focus, not blurred but *undecided*, as though the architecture there has not yet committed to a specific arrangement and is hovering between several possibilities, all of them equally valid, none of them quite resolving into the one that you will see when you get closer. When you get closer, it resolves. But the place you left behind, if you could turn and look (you can; nothing stops you), would now be undecided in exactly the same way.

The city is not haunted. Haunting implies something that was here and left and now persists as an echo. Nothing has ever left this plateau. Nothing has ever arrived. The city exists in a single frozen moment that has lasted forever, and the strangeness is simply what happens to *space* and *matter* and *logic* when they are held in place too long — when entropy is not permitted, when nothing moves and nothing changes, when the only dynamic force is the water and the sound and the vegetation and even these are locked into perfect static loops. Reality here has not broken down so much as it has *crystallized* into something too ordered, too complete, too permanent, and in that excess of stability it has become paradoxical. The plateau is a place where *is* has been going on so long that it has begun to rhyme with *isn't*.

Stand in the great hexagonal market square. Feel the heat pressing on your skin. Feel the sound in your bones. Watch the water bead on the ancient, faceless carvings. Look up at the amber ceiling of cloud-light. Notice that the moss on the eastern wall is exactly the same shade of green as the moss on the western wall. Notice that the pattern of water-drops falling from the nearest arch has a rhythm, and the rhythm does not vary, and has never varied, and that each drop strikes the pool below at exactly the same point with exactly the same sound and exactly the same splash-pattern, and that this has been happening since before *time* was a coherent concept here, and will continue happening after *happening* itself has forgotten what it means.

The plateau is patient. It is not waiting for anything. Waiting implies a future, and the future is a direction that does not exist here. There is only the permanent, roaring, green, wet, hot, luminous *now*, vibrating at its single eternal chord, a city with no citizens dreaming itself into being over and over in the same instant, the stone and the water and the vine and the light all holding each other in place, all refusing to be the first to change, all locked in a mutual agreement that *this* — exactly this, this temperature, this sound, this arrangement of moss on stone, this particular drop of water on this particular carved hand — is the final and only state of the world.

And it is. And it always has been. And the sound is deafening.
