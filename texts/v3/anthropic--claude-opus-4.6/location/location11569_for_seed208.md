# The Flats of Unceasing Thunder

---

The first thing is the sound. Before you can parse what you're seeing, the sound has already colonized your skull—a vast, rolling, overlapping percussion that comes from everywhere and nowhere, that seems to rise out of the ground itself. Not a single thunderclap but a *continuum* of them, staggered across the horizon, so that the end of one merges into the beginning of another, and another, and another, until the silence between them is a memory your ears have already forgotten. The sky flickers without rhythm. Sheet lightning pulses behind cloud layers so thick and low they look like a second ceiling, a grey-violet membrane pressed down over the world, and every pulse illuminates the underside of that ceiling in branching veins of pale blue-white, and then the dark folds back over it, and then another pulse, elsewhere, and the thunder from the first one arrives and mingles with the thunder from something that happened twenty seconds ago three miles to the east, and the whole acoustic field is a living, shifting, inescapable roar.

You are standing on stone. Everything here is stone—or was.

---

## The Terrain

The land is so old it has forgotten what shape means. Whatever mountains once existed have been ground to their roots and those roots have been ground further, leaving a vast, undulating expanse of exposed bedrock that extends in every direction for what must be miles, though the dim light and low cloud ceiling make it hard to know for certain. The surface is not flat, exactly. It rolls in long, imperceptible swells, like the frozen memory of ocean waves scaled up to geological size—amplitudes of a few meters spread across hundreds. In the troughs between these swells, water collects.

The rock itself is dark. A deep iron-grey streaked with veins of something paler—quartz, maybe, or some mineral you don't have a name for, exposed by incomprehensible ages of wearing-down. The surface has been polished by time into something almost glassy in places, then pitted and roughened in others where the composition was softer, where the rain found purchase. Because rain is constant here. Not always falling at your exact location, but always falling *somewhere* nearby, and the water finds its way across the stone in braided sheets that are barely a finger's width deep but dozens of meters across, sliding over the rock with a whispering hiss that you can only hear in the rare moments when the thunder lulls enough to let your ears reach for quieter things.

There are no rivers. The erosion is too complete, too ancient, for rivers. The land has been leveled past the point where water can carve channels—there is not enough gradient left. Instead the water *wanders*. It spreads across the stone in shallow, migrating films that shift with the wind and the rain, pooling in depressions that might be a handspan deep and the size of a town square, then draining away as the storm pattern shifts, then pooling somewhere else. The whole landscape is wet, filmed with moisture, gleaming dully under the lightning. Where the water stands long enough, it takes on a reddish-brown tint from dissolved iron, and these temporary pools look like old blood spread thin on glass.

There is no soil. Whatever soil once existed has been washed away over timescales that make human history look like a held breath. There is only rock, water, and the minerals the water carries.

## What Lives Here

And yet—the animals.

They are everywhere.

Not packed together, not swarming, but *distributed* across this vast barren expanse in a way that suggests a complex and functioning ecosystem operating on principles that take time to understand. The first ones you notice are the crawlers: low, flat-bodied creatures the size of large crabs, with pale shells that look almost ceramic, moving in slow deliberate paths across the wet stone. They seem to be licking the rock. Their undersides are equipped with something like a rasping tongue or a series of fine bristles, and they work their way across the surface with mechanical patience, processing the mineral film that the water deposits. They are the grazers here, feeding on dissolved iron and manganese and whatever trace elements the endless rain leaches from the ancient stone. Their shells are patterned with concentric rings, like tree rings, like tide marks—growth bands laid down over what might be very long lives. Some of the largest ones have shells the size of dinner plates, and their rings are too numerous to count.

Above them—or rather, among them—move things with legs. Longer-limbed, darker, faster. They pick their way across the stone on six articulated limbs that end in hard points, and they hunt the crawlers. You watch one stalk a large crawler for several minutes, circling it, testing approaches, before darting in and flipping it with a precise lateral strike. The crawler's pale underside is exposed, soft and working, and the predator begins to feed. The sound of this is lost entirely in the thunder.

But the most striking fauna are the ones that live in the water-films themselves. When lightning flashes and you look at the right angle, the shallow water comes alive with movement—ribbons of something translucent and muscular, each perhaps a foot long, undulating through water that is barely deep enough to cover them. They move in loose schools, dozens of them, navigating the shifting waterscape with apparent purpose, migrating from pool to pool as the rain fills and empties them. When a pool begins to shrink, they sense it—somehow—and begin to move upslope toward the next depression, sliding across wet stone in a frantic, writhing rush, like living water themselves. Some don't make it. You find their dried bodies occasionally, translucent husks curled on the rock where a pool vanished too fast or where the distance to the next one was too far. The crawlers converge on these husks. Everything here is currency.

There are flyers, too, though "flyers" isn't quite right. In the thick, humid air beneath the low ceiling, things drift. They have no wings in any recognizable sense—they are more like buoyant sacs, pale and faintly luminous, trailing filaments below them. They move with the wind, which gusts unpredictably as storm cells shift and collide, and their trailing filaments brush the wet stone and the water-films as they pass, collecting whatever they collect. Minerals. Microorganisms too small to see. The dissolved essence of the crawlers' waste. When two of them collide—which happens, in the gusting air—they merge briefly, exchange something, then separate. Sometimes after a merging, one of them deflates and falls, a crumpled membrane on the stone, and the crawlers begin their slow convergence.

The food web here runs on minerals, not photosynthesis. The light is too dim, too inconstant, the cloud cover too permanent. Whatever primary production exists must be chemolithotrophic—organisms that derive energy from the oxidation of iron, sulfur, manganese, the slow chemical bounty that the rain extracts from stone that has been weathering since before anything here was alive. The crawlers graze on microbial films. The ribbon-swimmers filter the mineral-rich water. The predators eat the grazers. The drifters scavenge what the water carries. And the thunder rolls on over all of them, indifferent, enormous, shaking the stone beneath your feet so that you can feel it in your joints.

## The Structures

Scattered across the flats, at intervals of maybe half a mile, there are cairns.

They are not natural formations—the stone here has been worn too flat, too smooth, for anything to stack itself by accident. Someone, or something, gathered stones and piled them. The cairns are rough, rarely more than chest-high, built from flat pieces of the local bedrock that have been pried up from wherever the erosion fractured them. No mortar. No shaping beyond what was necessary to make the pieces sit on one another without falling. They are old. Some of them have partially collapsed, their component stones spread around their bases like skirts, and the crawler-things climb over them and graze their surfaces with the same blind patience they bring to everything else.

But they are not *equally* old. Some are sharper-edged, more recently assembled. And when you look closely at the newest ones, you find marks—scratches in the stone surface that might be tally marks or might be something else entirely. Five parallel lines, then a diagonal through them. A circle with a dot in the center. Repeated patterns that have the unmistakable quality of *intention*, even if you cannot read them.

Nearby, in a shallow depression where water has collected in a broad, rust-colored pool, you find a tool. It is a piece of the pale quartz-vein material, knapped into a blade with a practiced hand—the flaking is deliberate, symmetrical, the edge still sharp enough to cut. It is sitting on a flat stone at the pool's edge as if placed there and not yet retrieved. The water in the pool is alive with ribbon-swimmers, dozens of them, circling in what might be a feeding pattern.

You do not find whoever placed it there.

But on the next swell of stone, perhaps a quarter mile away, there is another cairn, and this one has been recently added to—a fresh stone sits at its top, paler than the others, not yet stained by mineral rain. And on the stone, scratched deep enough to last a while before the weather takes it: that circle again, with its central dot, and below it, a row of marks you suddenly realize might be counting the days since the last time the thunder stopped.

## The Light, the Air, the Feeling

The light never changes much. It exists in a narrow band between dim and dimmer, the cloud ceiling occasionally thinning enough to suggest the existence of a sun somewhere above it—a brighter region that moves slowly across the grey expanse, providing the only real way to track time. When it is directly overhead, the landscape achieves its maximum illumination: a flat, shadowless, pewter-colored clarity that makes everything look both closer and less real. When it is near the horizon—assuming there are horizons behind that ceiling—the light drains to near-darkness and the lightning becomes the primary illumination, and the whole world strobes.

The air is cool and saturated. Breathing it feels like breathing through a damp cloth—not suffocating, but heavy, full of moisture and the faint metallic taste of dissolved minerals. The rain, when it reaches you, is fine and almost warm compared to the air, and it smells like wet iron, like blood without the organic sweetness. It coats everything. Your hands, if you touch the stone, come away with a faint ochre residue.

The thunder is not just sound. At this intensity, this duration, it becomes a physical medium, a pressure that fluctuates against your eardrums and your chest. You feel it in your sternum. The crawlers seem unbothered. The ribbon-swimmers seem unbothered. The drifting sac-creatures ride the pressure waves like ships on gentle swells. Everything here has evolved in thunder, has never known its absence, has built its biology around a world where the air is never still and never quiet. You are the only thing here that finds it remarkable.

And still, every few minutes, you catch yourself straining to hear beneath it—some pattern in the storm, some rhythm, some moment of structure in the chaos. And occasionally, for a second or two, the overlapping thunder-rolls align in a way that produces something almost tonal, a deep resonant hum that vibrates in the pooled water and makes the ribbon-swimmers go momentarily still, and then it passes and the chaos resumes.

You wonder if the cairn-builders hear it too. You wonder if that's what the circle with the dot means. You wonder if they are counting toward something, or away from something, or if counting is simply what you do in a world this old, this worn, this loud—you make marks on stone to prove that someone is still here, still listening, still waiting for the thunder to say something back.
