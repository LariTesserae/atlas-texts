# The Roost at Shale's Tooth

---

There is a pinnacle of stone that has no right to hold what it holds.

It rises—a single dark spire of basalt, young and sharp-edged, thrust up from a massif you cannot see because you are already on it, already near the top, standing in the one concavity near the summit where the rock cups inward like a cracked molar. The tooth. That is where you are: inside the tooth, in a hollow maybe forty feet across and twenty deep, open to the sky on one side where the wall has fractured away. The rock is new. You can tell because its fracture planes still shine like wet graphite, because there are no rounded edges, because the vertical grooves scored into the inner walls look like claw marks but are really cooling joints, places where the stone contracted as it hardened not so long ago—a few centuries, maybe less. Volcanic. The basalt columns are hexagonal in cross-section, packed tight and jutting at slightly different heights, forming a kind of irregular floor of dark pillars, some waist-high, some ankle-high, some flush. Walking across them requires stepping from pedestal to pedestal. In the gaps between the columns, the darkness goes down a long way.

The elevation is severe. The air here is thin and tastes of iron and altitude—that particular flavor of cold that isn't quite cold enough to freeze you but never lets your body believe it's warm. Your breath doesn't steam, quite, but your fingers ache. A persistent dampness hangs in the hollow, condensation more than rain, as if the clouds that pass through at this height leave a residue, a mineral film of moisture that beads on every surface. The walls of the tooth glisten. Small pools collect in the concavities of broken column-tops, each one a dark lens no bigger than a cupped hand, each one trembling faintly.

Trembling because of the sound.

---

It begins below hearing and arrives as pressure in the sternum. A thrumming. Somewhere between a purr and the lowest note of a pipe organ, resonating in the stone columns themselves, traveling up through the soles of your feet. It does not stop. It modulates—swells, recedes, layers over itself in slow interference patterns—but it does not stop. This is the sound of what lives here.

They are everywhere.

The hollow is full of creatures. Not full the way a jar is full of stones, but full the way a cathedral is full of bats—every vertical surface, every overhang, every crevice and seam in the dark basalt is claimed. They cling to the walls in overlapping clusters. They drape over the broken lip of the open side. They crowd the gaps between the column-tops so densely that what you first took for shadow is actually bodies, compressed together, shifting against one another in small unconscious adjustments. The ones on the walls are motionless except for breathing. The ones in the gaps between the columns extend downward into the dark like living stalactites, their limbs interlocked, chains of them descending into the unseen interior of the spire.

They are not bats. They are not birds. They are something that has aspects of both and properties of neither.

Each one is roughly the size of a large cat, though their proportions are wrong for mammals—the torso too deep, the limbs too numerous. They have six limbs total: four for gripping, articulated with an extra joint that lets them fold flat against stone, and two that are something else, something between wings and fins, membraned and dark, usually held pressed tight against the body like collapsed umbrellas. The membrane is not skin. It has the translucency of wet mica, and when one of them shifts in its sleep and a wing-fin partially unfurls, you can see the architecture inside it—not veins, not bones, but a branching fractal lattice that looks almost crystalline, almost geological, as if the creatures are growing their own basalt inside their flesh.

Their heads are broad and flat, eyeless, dominated by a structure you can only call a tympanum—a wide oval plate of taut membrane stretched across the front of the skull, slightly concave, like a drum head. This is what makes the sound. Each creature's tympanum vibrates at its own fundamental frequency, a low drone just below or at the threshold of hearing, and in the aggregate—hundreds of them, maybe a thousand packed into this small hollow—the frequencies combine into the thrumming that fills the tooth. It is not communication, exactly. Or rather, it is not *only* communication. You suspect the vibration is also thermoregulation, a way of generating warmth through sustained muscular oscillation of the tympanum. The temperature inside the hollow is fractionally warmer than the open air, a barely perceptible envelope of creature-heat trapped in the stone concavity.

They are sleeping. Most of them. You think.

---

The light is almost nothing. Whatever time of day it is—and at this elevation, with clouds threading through the open fracture in the wall, time of day feels approximate—the sky beyond the tooth's broken rim is the deep bruised indigo of altitude twilight, neither day nor night but that liminal register where the atmosphere is too thin to scatter light properly. Stars might be visible if the condensation cleared. It doesn't clear. Inside the hollow, away from the opening, the darkness is near-total. The creatures are darker than the dark stone. You know they are there because of the thrumming, because of the faint wet sound of membrane against membrane as they breathe, because occasionally one shifts its grip on the wall and there is a tiny click of claw on basalt, precise as a jeweler setting a stone.

But there is one other source of light, and it is wrong.

In the deepest corner of the hollow, where two walls of columnar basalt meet at an acute angle, the condensation that trickles down the stone feeds a patch of something that glows. It is not bioluminescence—or if it is, it belongs to nothing that has a clear kingdom. A smear of pale blue-green light, roughly the size and shape of a spread hand, coating the stone in a thin living film. Closer, you can see that it has texture: a furred surface, like mold, but the filaments are rigid and glassy, each one a tiny antenna vibrating at a frequency too high to feel. It pulses. Not rhythmically—arrhythmically, in clusters and hesitations, like a thought failing to form. The creatures avoid it. There is a clear margin of bare stone around the glow, a perimeter no creature crosses. Whether this is avoidance or reverence or simply that the glow's vibration interferes with their tympanic droning, you cannot tell. But the empty space around it is conspicuous in a hollow where every other surface is claimed.

The glow illuminates almost nothing. It is just bright enough to confirm the darkness around it, to let you see the nearest sleeping bodies in silhouette—the dense-packed mounds of them, the folded wing-fins, the slight rhythmic dilation of tympanums like dozens of mouths silently opening and closing. In the faint blue-green wash, the condensation on the walls becomes visible as a moving film, slow and ceaseless, feeding the glow, feeding whatever the glow is.

---

There is no built thing here. No tool, no mark, no path. But the creatures have shaped the space through sheer habitation. The basalt where they cluster most densely is polished to a glass-smooth finish by generations of gripping feet, the cooling joints widened fractionally by the slow chemical action of their bodies against the stone—some faint acid in their skin, perhaps, or just the relentless patience of contact. In the column-gaps where they hang in chains, the basalt edges are rounded in a way the rest of the stone is not. The rest of the tooth is sharp and young. These contact points are the only aged surfaces, prematurely worn, and they map the colony's habits as precisely as a blueprint: where they sleep, where they hang, where they don't go.

They don't go near the open side. Or rather—they are sparse there, clinging only to the underside of the broken lip, oriented so their tympanums face inward, away from the sky. What comes from the sky is not predators, not weather. You don't know what it is. But the colony's architecture turns its back to the opening as unanimously as a congregation facing an altar, and the altar is the dark interior, the downward gaps between the columns, the depths of the tooth where the chains of creatures extend into blackness and the thrumming grows so dense it becomes tactile, a pressure, a standing wave in the stone itself.

Something lives deeper inside the spire. Or something is the spire. The thrumming doesn't originate with the creatures—they contribute to it, sustain it, elaborate it, but the fundamental, the root note beneath all their overlapping frequencies, rises from below, from the volcanic throat of the pinnacle itself. Whether it is geological—some deep harmonic of cooling rock, of thermal contraction still ongoing centuries after formation—or whether it is something else, something the creatures came here to be near, something the glow in the corner is a surfacing symptom of—you cannot determine. The creatures seem to listen with their whole bodies. Their gripping limbs press flat against the stone not just for purchase but for contact, maximum surface area, as if they are trying to absorb the vibration through their skin.

One of them, near you—close enough to touch, though you do not touch it—opens its tympanum wider than the others. The membrane stretches taut and you see, for an instant, that it is not opaque. It is translucent, like the wing-fins, and behind it there is no skull, no brain cavity, just more lattice, more of that crystalline fractal branching, and at the very center of the lattice, a single bead of condensation, trapped and trembling, vibrating in sympathetic resonance with the note from below.

The creature holds this posture for a long time. Then the tympanum relaxes, contracts, and the bead of moisture is reabsorbed into the lattice, drawn inward, and the creature is still again, indistinguishable from the rest.

---

The air smells of wet mineral and something else—a faint ozonic sharpness, like the smell after lightning, though there has been no lightning. It comes from the creatures, you think. From the collective vibration of a thousand tympanums ionizing the thin damp air inside the tooth. Your skin prickles. Your hair moves. The static charge is not dangerous but it is persistent, and it makes the condensation on the walls behave strangely—the water film flows upward in places, drawn along the columnar joints by electrostatic gradients, rising toward the clustered bodies, feeding them, feeding the process.

Everything here is slow. The condensation moves slowly. The creatures shift slowly. The glow pulses in its own unhurried arrhythmia. Even the rare creature that moves—detaching from the wall, crawling across sleeping bodies with careful deliberate steps, finding a new position, reattaching—does so with a patience that suggests time works differently inside the tooth, that the thin air and the thrumming and the darkness conspire to thicken duration, to make each moment last longer than it should.

You are a visitor here. Nothing acknowledges you. The tympanums do not turn in your direction. The gripping feet do not flinch from your footsteps on the column-tops. You are irrelevant to the process, whatever the process is—this dense communal listening, this slow mineral harvesting, this dark patience at extreme altitude on a spire of young stone, oriented inward and downward, attuned to a frequency that began before the creatures arrived and will continue after they are gone.

The thrumming goes on. The condensation beads and trembles and flows upward. The glow pulses its uncertain light in the corner. And in the gaps between the basalt columns, the chains of creatures extend down and down and down into the dark throat of the tooth, and you cannot see where they end, and perhaps they don't.
