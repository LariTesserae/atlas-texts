# The Hollows Beneath the Rootfloor

---

You are inside something that was once a city. Or the city is inside something that was once a plant. The distinction dissolved so long ago that even the architecture has forgotten which came first.

The darkness is total. Not the darkness of night — night implies a sun that will return. This is the darkness of depth, of enclosure, of a space that has never known light and has arranged itself accordingly. You are beneath. Beneath the canopy, beneath the secondary canopy, beneath the tertiary growth, beneath the matted dead-fall layers, beneath the root mesh, beneath even the subterranean crown of the oldest taproots. You are in the negative space left behind when root systems the width of aqueducts slowly, over millennia, shifted their grip and moved on.

The air is cool. Not cold — the sheer mass of living matter above and around acts as insulation, a planetary blanket dozens of meters thick — but cool the way deep caves are cool, a temperature that has settled on its final answer and will not be argued with. It sits against the skin like a cloth that has just been taken from a stone shelf. There is no wind. There has never been wind here. The atmosphere is still and faintly sweet with the exhalations of slow decomposition, the sugary off-gassing of lignite compounds relaxing their chemical bonds over decades.

And yet: it is **loud**.

---

The sound is the first thing. Before you understand the space, before you can construct any mental image in the absolute black, you hear it.

A deep, pervasive groaning — not mechanical, not animal, but structural. The sound of wood under compression. The sound of growth itself, translated into audible frequencies by the unimaginable mass pressing down from above. Root-beams the diameter of halls are bearing loads that shift incrementally, and when they shift, the sound propagates through the solid medium of the plant-matter substrate like a voice through bone. It is not a creak. A creak is brief. This is a sustained, resonant *utterance* that travels through the floor beneath your feet, up through your skeleton, and settles in the hollow of your chest like a second heartbeat. It lasts fifteen, twenty seconds. Then silence — two breaths of it — and then another tone, a different pitch, from a different direction, answering.

Between these great structural voices, smaller sounds. The ticking of fibers pulling apart under tension. A papery rustle that could be bark splitting. And everywhere, constantly, a faint sibilant hiss: the collective respiration of fungal networks processing dead cellulose in the walls, the floor, the ceiling — everywhere there is surface, there is metabolism. You are inside a body that is digesting itself and regrowing simultaneously, and the process is not silent.

---

Now the space.

You reach out. Your hand finds a wall — not flat, not curved in any architectural sense, but *shaped*. Someone has worked this root. You can feel the marks: shallow gouges left by a blade, probably stone, possibly bone. The cuts are old. The edges have been softened by decades of slow fungal colonization, a fine fur of mycelium filling each groove until the tool-marks feel like something between carving and corduroy. But the intention is clear. This root-wall has been hollowed, its interior scooped and widened to create a chamber. The ceiling is low — you could touch it if you raised your arm — and it follows the natural arc of what was once the underside of a root as thick as a house.

This is how the city was built: not constructed but *excavated*, carved from the living and dead root-mass the way cliff-dwellings are carved from sandstone, except the medium is wood, and the medium is alive, and the medium moves.

It moves slowly. That is the central fact of habitation here. The great roots shift over years, over generations. A corridor that was passable when a grandmother was young may have closed to a crawlspace by the time her grandchild explores it. A chamber that was carved from a dying root may find itself compressed as neighboring growth expands to fill the void. The architecture is not permanent. It is negotiated — an ongoing, glacially slow argument between the intentions of the carvers and the intentions of the plants, in which neither party fully wins.

The city, then, is not a fixed map but a slow-motion wave. Habitation migrates through the root-mass over decades, following the paths of least resistance, occupying the voids that open as old roots die and retreat, abandoning spaces that the living network reclaims. Behind, the city fills in. Ahead, new chambers are gouged open with hand-tools of sharpened root-wood and knapped stone, the chips left in place because the fungal networks will reduce them to powder within a season.

---

There is almost no water. This is perhaps the strangest thing. Above — far above, in the canopy world you will never see — the plant-mass is photosynthesizing, transpiring, cycling moisture through vascular systems of impossible scale. But down here, in the root-dark, the air is dry. The plants have claimed all available moisture for themselves. It moves through the xylem channels sealed within the root-walls, inches from your hand but utterly inaccessible, pressurized and jealously held. The inhabitants have learned to harvest condensation from the thermal boundary where cool deep air meets the marginally warmer surfaces of metabolically active roots — a thin dew that forms on certain root-types at certain stages of their growth cycle. They collect it with woven fungal-mat sponges pressed against the right surfaces at the right time. It is enough. Barely. The dryness of the air is part of why the darkness feels so absolute; there is no mist, no humidity to scatter even the faintest hypothetical photon. The dark is *desiccated*.

The inhabitants. There must be inhabitants — the tool-marks attest to them, the shaped chambers, the narrow passages widened at their bases to allow passage. But the city feels **empty**. Vastly, echoingly empty, and you realize this is because most of it is abandoned. Not in crisis, not in catastrophe, but in the natural course of the city's migration. You are in an old quarter, a district the roots have nearly finished reclaiming. The walls are closing in — you can feel it in the way the carved surfaces have begun to deform, the careful gouges bowing inward under pressure from growth on the other side. In a decade this chamber will be a crevice. In a generation it will be solid wood again, undifferentiated, the carving marks compressed into the grain like fossils pressed into shale.

Somewhere — perhaps a hundred meters through solid root-mass in a direction you cannot determine — the inhabited quarter persists. You would hear them if you pressed your ear to the wall: a vibration that might be rhythmic, might be deliberate, transmitted through the wood the way everything here is transmitted through the wood. The inhabitants learned long ago that the root-network carries sound beautifully. Their communication is percussive, tapped and struck against the walls, patterns of rhythm that propagate through the substrate. From a distance, filtered through meters of living fiber, it would sound like the roots themselves speaking — indistinguishable from the groaning and ticking of the natural structure.

Maybe it is. Maybe some of what you are hearing is language, and you lack the grammar to parse it from the noise.

---

The ecology is almost entirely vegetal and fungal. No animals. No insects, even — the air is too dry, the environment too sealed, the chemistry too dominated by the terpenes and phenolic compounds that the roots exude as preservatives and defenses. What lives here is what grows here: the root-mass itself, in various stages of life and death and decomposition; the fungal networks that colonize every dead surface within hours of its dying; a few species of pale, rubbery lichen that have adapted to absolute darkness by abandoning photosynthesis entirely, subsisting on chemical gradients at the boundary between root-species. These lichens are faintly bioluminescent in theory — they produce trace amounts of light as a metabolic byproduct — but the output is so far below the threshold of any eye's detection that it amounts to nothing. The darkness keeps its perfect record.

The plants above are not one species. They are many — dozens, hundreds, a consortium that competed itself into a stable equilibrium so long ago that their root systems have fused and separated and fused again, grafting across species boundaries, creating chimeric structures that are genetically mosaic throughout. The root-mass you stand inside is not a forest's roots. It is closer to a single organism that was once a forest and has since lost the distinction. Its age is measurable in geological terms. The erosion parameter — ancient, near-terminal — applies not to stone but to the *idea of individual plants*. Identity has eroded. What remains is substrate: a living mineral, functionally geological, which happens to metabolize.

---

There is something else. The strangeness.

In certain chambers — the oldest ones, the ones deepest into the reclamation zone — the tool-marks on the walls are not utilitarian. They are dense, overlapping, frantic almost, layered so thickly that the original root-surface has been replaced entirely by a texture of human making. Patterns, maybe. Or maybe just the accumulated marks of many hands over many years, each carver adding to the record of the last, until the wall became a palimpsest of intention so deep it reads as texture rather than text.

And when the roots reclaim these walls — when the slow pressure deforms the carvings and compresses the surface — the marks do not simply disappear. They are *incorporated*. The growth-wood that fills the closing chamber carries, in its grain, a ghost of the pattern that was carved into the wall it consumed. A faint irregularity. A ripple. Years later, when a new carver opens a new chamber in a new quarter and slices into a root-wall, they sometimes find these ripples — the compressed record of a previous habitation, preserved in the wood like a memory preserved in tissue.

They read them. Or they try to. The practice is somewhere between archaeology and divination. The root carries the record, but it carries it imperfectly, warped by growth, distorted by pressure, edited by the slow intelligence of the plant-substrate itself, which is not sentient but is *selective* — it preferentially preserves certain patterns and dissolves others, according to chemical and structural logics that the inhabitants have spent generations trying to decode.

The city, then, has a memory that is not its inhabitants' memory. It is the root-mass's memory — partial, biased, alive. The inhabitants live inside an archive that is actively rewriting itself, and their oldest stories are about the difference between what they carved and what the wood chose to remember.

---

You stand in the dark. The groaning swells, a deep chord from three directions at once, the sound of a root-beam somewhere adjusting its bearing by a fraction of a millimeter under a load of several thousand tons. Your hand rests on the furred wall. Beneath the mycelium, beneath the compressed tool-marks, you can feel the faintest pulse — not a heartbeat, nothing so animal, but a rhythm: the slow peristalsis of sap moving through the sealed vascular channels, pressurized, cyclical, driven by forces originating in a canopy you will never see, responding to a sun you have no evidence for.

The city breathes around you, closing slowly, growing. Somewhere ahead of its own collapse, its people are carving new rooms with stone blades, pressing damp fungal sponges to sweating roots, tapping messages into walls that will carry their words farther than they know and longer than they intend.

Behind you, the wood is already forgetting the shape of this room. Or remembering it wrong. Or remembering it in a language you were never taught.

The dark does not thin. The sound does not stop. The cool air holds you like a palm.
