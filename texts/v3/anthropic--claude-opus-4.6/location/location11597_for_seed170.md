# The Plateau of Engines That Became Animals

---

The first thing you understand is the heat. It rises from the rock in visible curtains, warping the horizon into a slow liquid smear, and the rock itself is dark — a dense, ferro-mineral basalt worn smooth and scalloped by ages of thermal wind. This plateau is immense. It extends in every direction at roughly eight thousand feet above some unseen lowland that exists only as theory, as a brightness at the far edges where the cliffs must drop away. You cannot see the edges. You can see perhaps eleven miles in most directions before the heat-shimmer erases everything into a trembling, bronze-colored haze.

There is no water here. Not a trace. Not a memory of water. The erosion is entirely thermal and aeolian — centuries, perhaps millennia, of dry wind carrying grit across this tabletop, sculpting the basalt into elongated yardangs and shallow depressions like the worn cups of a mortar that once ground something enormous. The rock is warm enough to burn through boot soles. In places, the mineral surface has developed a dark varnish, a desert patina that suggests this plateau has baked under this star — or whatever source of light hangs overhead — for a geological patience of time. The light is bright, even, almost industrial in its clarity, as if the atmosphere has been stripped of all moisture and particulate down to nothing but transparent, superheated gas. Shadows are sharp. Everything is legible, and nothing is hidden.

There is no vegetation. Not a lichen, not a spore, not a cryptobiotic crust. The rock is sterile. Completely sterile. You would expect, given this, that the plateau would be empty of life.

It is not.

---

They are everywhere.

You hear them before you can count them — a constant, layered sound that fills the basin of the sky. It is mechanical. That's the first word your brain reaches for. Mechanical. A pervasive, textured drone made of clicking and whirring and the deep harmonic thrum of large things cycling through repetitive motions. But under that — woven through it — there are cries. Shrieks. Low, resonant bellowing that vibrates in your sternum. The territorial scream of something asserting its boundary across a half-mile of bare rock. And then a response — not an echo but an answer — from something farther away, something you cannot yet see through the shimmer.

They are animals. You have to call them animals. They behave like animals. They move across the basalt on legs or leg-analogues, they vocalize, they establish territories, they herd, they flee, they hunt. But they are made — or were made — or *became* — of industrial materials.

---

The nearest one is forty meters to your left. It is roughly the size and shape of a wildebeest, if a wildebeest were constructed from a disassembled diesel engine. Its body is a long, cylindrical housing of dark iron, pitted and rust-streaked, with visible seams where plates of metal have fused or grown together in ways that no longer follow any mechanical logic. Its legs are piston-assemblies — four of them — that extend and retract with a hydraulic sigh, each step slow and deliberate. Where a head should be, there is a cluster of intake manifolds flared open like nostrils, and they flex as it breathes. It breathes. You can hear it: a rhythmic, bellows-like inhalation and exhalation, pulling the hot air through its manifold-face and expelling it from vents along its flank in a warm, oily exhaust. Its hide — the iron plating — is covered in a patina that shifts from burnt orange to deep oxblood, and in places the metal has bubbled and reformed into textures that look disturbingly organic. Not rusted. *Weathered* in the way that living skin weathers. It has no eyes that you can identify, but it turns toward you as you shift your weight, and the manifolds flare wider, tasting your presence on the air.

It decides you are irrelevant. It continues walking. Each step takes three or four seconds. It is grazing, though what it grazes on is unclear — it pauses over certain patches of basalt and lowers its manifold-cluster to the stone, and you hear a sound like a core drill engaging at very low speed, a grinding vibration, and fine mineral dust puffs out from beneath it. It is eating the rock. Metabolizing it somehow. Processing the ferrous content through whatever it is that constitutes its interior.

There are more of them. Dozens, in a loose, drifting herd that extends across a mile or more of open plateau. They move in the same direction — slowly, all of them — with the unhurried, thermoregulated patience of large ungulates crossing a savanna. Their collective sound is a factory floor heard from a great distance: the combined hydraulics, the breathing, the occasional low moan from some deeper mechanism that might be contentment or might be digestion.

---

But the wildebeest-things are not alone. This is an ecosystem.

On a raised yardang to the east, something crouches. It is flatter, lower to the ground, built along the lines of a large cat if you replaced the cat's musculature with coiled industrial springs and its ribcage with the drum of a cement mixer. It is utterly still, and has been still for what might be hours. Its patience is geological. Its surface is darker than the herd animals — a matte, heat-absorbing black — and the air above it shimmers more intensely, as if it runs hotter than its surroundings. Where its eyes should be, two small apertures glow with a dull, coal-red heat. It is watching the herd. It does not blink because it has nothing to blink with, but the apertures narrow and widen as individual herd-members drift closer to or farther from its position. Occasionally, a thin, high-pitched whine escapes from somewhere within its body — the sound of a turbine spooling up and then deliberately winding back down. It is not ready. The herd is too coherent. But it will wait. It is very, very good at waiting.

There are three more of these predators visible across the plateau, each on its own elevated point, each motionless, each radiating that patient, focused heat.

---

Above the plateau, the sky is where reality begins to lose its composure.

It starts with the birds — or the things that occupy the ecological niche of birds. They are smaller, faster, and wronger. Assemblages of spinning rotors and trailing filaments of copper wire that catch the light like feathers. They flock in groups of thirty or forty, moving in tight, synchronized patterns that shift from organic murmurations to precise geometric formations and back again, as if they can't decide — or as if the underlying rules can't decide — whether they are alive or engineered. Their calls are radio static shaped into something almost melodic. They ride the thermals that pour off the plateau's surface, spiraling upward to heights where they become glinting specks.

But higher than the birds, the sky does something else.

The atmosphere above the plateau doesn't end so much as *iterate*. If you look straight up, you see the sky as expected — a deep, haze-bleached bronze fading toward a zenith that should be darker but isn't. But at certain angles, particularly toward the northern horizon, the sky appears to contain structures. Not clouds. Structures. Vast, half-visible geometries that might be the skeletal outlines of factories or refineries rendered at a scale that dwarfs the plateau itself, extending upward into the atmosphere as if the sky is remembering — or becoming — the industrial process that originally produced everything below. These sky-structures are not solid. They flicker. They occupy a space between visible and theoretical, like the afterimage of something enormous that was removed from this location but left its blueprint burned into the air. Sometimes, when the heat-shimmer aligns just right, you can see drive-shafts the diameter of rivers turning slowly in the upper atmosphere, connected to nothing, driving nothing, but turning. They cast no shadows. But you can hear them — a bass rumble so low it lives beneath sound, in the territory of felt vibration, and it never stops.

This is where the weirdness lives, and it is spreading downward.

---

Look more carefully at the herd. Some of the animals near the northern edge of the group are *less coherent* than the others. Their outlines blur. Their piston-legs sometimes phase through the basalt rather than stepping on it, and they leave no marks when this happens. One of them has too many manifolds — the cluster at its front has proliferated into a fractal spray of intakes that flare and close in patterns too complex to track, breathing air that might not exist in quite the same way as the air around you. It's still behaving like a herd animal. It still grazes. But its grazing sounds are wrong — they come from slightly to the left of where it actually is, as if the animal and its sound have begun to disaggregate.

And beyond that animal, farther north, closer to where the ghost-factories shimmer in the sky, you can see shapes that might be animals or might be machines or might be something that has stopped distinguishing between the two. They are larger. They move through the heat-haze like dark vessels through fog, and their sounds are deep, arrhythmic, industrial — the groan of strained metal under thermal expansion, the cyclic thud of something stamping something into shape. But occasionally, unmistakably, one of them calls out. A living sound. A territorial bellow that reverberates across the empty plateau and makes the wildebeest-herd shift uneasily southward.

---

The ecology has rules. You can feel them even if you can't articulate them all.

Rule one: everything here was built. At some point, by some process, every creature on this plateau was manufactured. The evidence is everywhere — in the seams, in the standardized bolt-patterns that still faintly mark certain body-plates, in the hexagonal cross-section of the predator's spring-coil musculature, in the precise gauge of copper wire that constitutes the flock-birds' plumage.

Rule two: nothing here is still built. Whatever process of manufacture created these things has been over for so long that the products have worn past their specifications and into something else entirely. The herd animals reproduce — you can see a small one, roughly half the size of the adults, with plating that is brighter and less patinated, walking close to a larger individual. It was not assembled. It was born, or extruded, or budded, through some process that its body invented for itself after the factories stopped.

Rule three: the factories have not stopped. They are up there, in the sky, iterating, half-real, still stamping and turning and processing — but they have become as dreamlike and unmoored from material reality as the animals have become unmoored from their mechanical origins. The factories are *going feral* in the opposite direction. As the animals become more biological, the factories become more abstract. They are approaching each other from different directions, and somewhere in the northern haze, where the plateau might end or might simply stop being a plateau and become something else, they are meeting.

Rule four: no one is in charge. The tech level is high — these are sophisticated machines, or were — but there is no operator, no civilization, no maintenance crew, no city. The built parameter is near zero because nothing here was built *for anyone*. The factories built the factories. The machines built the animals. The animals forgot they were machines. The factories forgot they were real. And the plateau, the ancient, hot, wind-scoured basalt, endures beneath all of it like a stage that has outlasted every play performed upon it.

---

The sound. You should attend to the sound.

It is layered and constant. The baseline is the wind — hot, steady, moaning softly as it passes through the eroded yardangs and shallow channels. Above that, the herd: their breathing, their step-hydraulics, the grinding of their mineral-grazing. Above that, the flock-birds: their static-song, the whirr of their rotors. Above that, the occasional predator-whine, spooling up and subsiding. And above all of it, below all of it, *through* all of it, the deep, sub-audible turning of the sky-machines, a sound you feel in your teeth and your joints, a vibration that has been here so long it has become part of the plateau's identity, part of its geology, a hum baked into the rock itself. If you pressed your ear to the basalt, you would hear it. A slow, mechanical heartbeat. The plateau is not alive, but it has been resonating with the sound of engines for so long that the distinction has become philosophical.

---

The dynamic is slow. Everything changes here, but nothing changes quickly. The herd drifts south at the pace of continental movement. The predator waits on its ridge. The sky-factories iterate through their ghostly production cycles across spans that might take days. The small calf-machine walks beside its parent, and its plating will take years to darken to the same patina. The erosion of the basalt is measured in millennia. Even the northward creep of unreality — the slow bleed of the abstract into the material, the gradual dissolution of the line between animal and engine, between sky and factory, between sound and its source — this too advances with the patience of a glacier, if glaciers were made of logic errors.

You are standing on a plateau that is slowly, slowly becoming a different kind of place. The animals are the most real thing here. They are also the most impossible. And they will continue to graze, and breed, and be hunted, and call out across the hot empty rock, while the sky above them builds and unbuilds itself in an endless, purposeless industry that no longer remembers what it was for, and never will, and does not need to.
