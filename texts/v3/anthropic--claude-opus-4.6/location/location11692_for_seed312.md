# The Perch Cities of the Ketharn Plateau

---

There is a cold that lives in high places that is not the cold of winter. Winter implies a turning, a promise. This cold is architectural. It is the temperature of the world at this altitude, as fundamental as the stone itself, and everything that lives here has made its compact with it.

The Ketharn Plateau stretches for what might be a thousand miles in every direction — the distances are genuinely uncertain, because the air at this elevation plays tricks with the horizon, compressing far ridges into what looks like a near wall, then on clearer days revealing the wall to be range after range of flat-topped mesas stepping away into a haze that is not mist but simply the exhaustion of light across too much open space. The plateau is not a single surface but a vast archipelago of elevated shelves, connected by ridgelines and separated by canyons that drop a few hundred feet before hitting another shelf. It is high enough that the sky is a darker blue than it has any right to be — not quite the indigo of true altitude, but deep, saturated, the blue of a bruise healing toward purple at dawn and dusk. The sun moves across it in full honesty, nothing to obscure it, and the light falls generous and direct, though it seems to warm nothing.

The rock is young. You can tell because it still has opinions about its own shape. The plateau was pushed up — or perhaps the world around it subsided — recently enough in geological time that the edges of things remain sharp. Canyon walls show clean fracture faces, pale gray and cream-colored, with the lamination of sedimentary layers still legible as crisp horizontal lines rather than the melted, weathered curves of old terrain. Boulders that have calved off cliff faces still have corners. The shelves and mesas have not yet been rounded into domes; their tops are genuinely flat, their edges genuinely abrupt. Where water has begun to carve — and it has begun, just barely — the channels are narrow and V-shaped, incisions rather than valleys, as though someone had taken a chisel to the stone and pulled it toward themselves in long deliberate strokes.

Water. There is water here, but it does not flow with conviction. The plateau catches moisture from clouds that drag across its western edge, and this moisture expresses itself as condensation on stone faces in the early morning, as a thin rime of frost that melts by midday into films of dampness that creep down vertical surfaces and collect in the cracks and joints of the rock. Occasionally, every few days or perhaps once a week, a cloud will catch on one of the higher ridges and disassemble itself into something between fog and rain — not quite either, more like the air itself deciding to become briefly wet. When this happens, the stone darkens for an hour or two, and thin temporary streams find the chisel-carved channels and trace them downward. Small pools persist in shaded depressions, surfaced with permanent cold, tasting faintly of mineral. These pools are important. Everything here knows where they are.

---

The cities begin with the birds.

Not metaphorically. The Ketharn Plateau is, above all else, a place of animals. The cold bare rock and the sparse vegetation — low cushion-plants in the crevices, pale lichens the color of old bone on every south-facing surface, here and there a twisted shrub with leaves like leather coins — these would suggest a barren place. It is anything but. The plateau teems. The density of animal life is startling, almost uncomfortable, as though every ecological niche that vegetation left empty has been filled by something with legs or wings or both.

The birds are the most obvious. They exist in layered populations that divide the vertical space with the precision of social agreement. Closest to the stone surface, in the cracks and beneath overhangs, are the dust-runners — small, round-bodied, flightless or nearly so, moving in nervous clusters of twenty or thirty, their plumage the exact cream and gray of the rock. One shelf up, or simply a few feet higher on any cliff face, are the hook-beaks: solitary, patient, about the size of a man's forearm, with curved bills they use to pry insect colonies from crevices. They make no sound except a dry clicking when disturbed. Higher still, occupying the open air in enormous loosely organized flocks, are what might be called kites or might be called something else — long-winged, fork-tailed, riding the thermals that rise where sunlight heats one shelf-face more than its neighbor. They rarely flap. They process across the sky in vast slow gyres, dozens at a time, their shadows sliding across the stone below with the measured pace of sundial arms.

But there are also the crawling things, the scuttling things, the things that press themselves flat into the horizontal cracks in the rock and emerge at dusk in leggy multitudes. There are beetles with iridescent carapaces that form living crusts on the undersides of boulders. There are something like lizards but furred, quick, thermal-opportunists that bask in precise patches of sunlight and vanish the instant a cloud crosses. There are burrowing creatures whose tunnel openings pock the flat tops of mesas in such numbers that the ground takes on a honeycomb texture. Something larger — no one seems sure what — moves in the deeper canyons and leaves tracks like dragged rope in the thin mineral silt. The sound this abundance makes is not loud. It is a pervasive rustle, a background of small frictions, clicks, the whisper of wings adjusting, claws on stone, the almost-subliminal hum of many small metabolisms running simultaneously. The plateau is mostly quiet the way a library is quiet: not because nothing is happening, but because everything happening is happening carefully.

---

The people came because of the birds, and they stayed because of the stone.

The cities — and they are cities, genuine cities, not camps or villages or seasonal shelters, but permanent, dense, structurally ambitious, politically complex habitations — the cities are carved and stacked and wedged into the canyon walls and along the shelf edges of the plateau. They look, from a distance, like the stone itself has developed a rash: regular pockmarks, ledges that are too even, shadows that are too square. Up close, the scale resolves. These are buildings. Rooms. Streets — or rather, the steep switchback paths and ladders and ramps that serve as streets when your city is built vertically into a cliff face.

The technology is stone and bone and sinew and wood — what little wood there is, traded upward from lower elevations at significant cost, used sparingly, for structural lintels and tool handles and the frames of the large carrying-baskets. But the stonework. The stonework is extraordinary. Not because it is decorative or monumental in the way of lowland civilizations, but because it represents an almost total mastery of the material at hand. The plateau's young, well-laminated stone fractures cleanly along its bedding planes, and the builders have learned to exploit this with a sophistication that borders on the geological. They split slabs of uniform thickness using bone wedges and water-frost cycles. They dress surfaces with tools made from the harder nodules found in certain layers. They fit block to block without mortar, the joints so tight that a knife blade will not enter them, relying on the weight and precision of the fit and on the fact that in this dry cold, there is almost no thermal expansion to work the joints open.

The result is a built environment of extraordinary density. The canyon-wall cities are sometimes thirty or forty stories tall — not by the lowland meaning, where each story is a generous space, but in the plateau sense, where a story might be six feet of vertical rise, a shelf of rooms cut back into the rock and fronted with fitted stone walls, connected to the stories above and below by internal passages and external ladders. Each room opens onto a narrow ledge, and from a distance, the ledges stripe the canyon walls in horizontal bands, giving the cities a layered appearance that echoes the sedimentary banding of the stone itself. Thousands of people live in a single canyon-wall city. Tens of thousands. The population density is that of a tightly packed urban core, but oriented vertically rather than horizontally, and because the plateau has so many canyons and shelves, each within a day's walk of the next, the total population spread across the Ketharn is vast. It is a civilization. It simply doesn't look like one from any angle a lowlander would recognize.

Every surface is used. The narrow ledges that front the rooms serve as workshops, social spaces, drying racks for the thin-sliced meat that is the dietary staple. The roofs of lower rooms are the floors of paths serving upper rooms. Internal passages are storage, sleeping space, and corridors simultaneously. Where a natural crack in the canyon wall provides a vertical chimney, it has been incorporated into the ventilation system. Where a horizontal seam produces a natural shelf, it has been reinforced and extended with fitted stone to create a public plaza — sometimes no wider than an arm-span, but running the full length of the city, a street in the sky where people pass each other in single file, pressing against the stone, exchanging news.

---

The relationship between city and fauna is not one of separation. The birds live in the cities. The dust-runners occupy the lower levels in flocks that nobody owns exactly but everybody manages. They eat the scraps and the insects. The hook-beaks nest in purpose-built niches in the outer walls, and their eggs are collected — not all of them, never all of them, with a careful accounting of nests and seasons that has the weight of law. The burrowing creatures riddle the mesas where the plateau-top gardens attempt to grow the few edible cushion-plants that can be cultivated in this cold. They are pests, technically, but they also aerate the thin soil, and so they are tolerated, managed, their population kept in rough equilibrium by children assigned to watch the garden-fields and selectively trap individuals when the burrow-density exceeds a threshold everyone seems to know but no one has written down.

The larger animals — the furred lizards, the canyon-things — are hunted, but slowly, infrequently, as part of cycles that seem to match the animals' own reproductive rhythms. The kites overhead are not hunted at all. They are watched. They are read. Their flight patterns indicate weather changes, shifts in the insect populations, the presence of the canyon-things below. Certain individuals in the cities do nothing but watch the kites all day, sitting motionless on high exposed ledges, and what they learn from this watching is considered essential knowledge.

---

The cold does not relent. The people wear layers of felted fur — from the furred lizards, from the burrowing creatures, from an animal no one has described to outsiders — stitched with bone needles and sinew thread into garments of considerable sophistication. The garments are not beautiful by lowland standards. They are dense, practical, built for a world where exposed skin loses heat in minutes and where every movement involves climbing, squeezing through narrow passages, reaching for handholds. They fit close. They are repaired endlessly. A garment may pass through four or five owners over its functional life, each time re-stitched, patched, adjusted, until the original material is entirely replaced and only the pattern remains.

The cities change, too, but slowly. A new room is excavated over the course of a season. A wall is built over the course of a month. The stone is patient and so are the builders. There is no hurry because there is nowhere else to go. The plateau is the world. Below it, the air is too thick, too warm, too wet, too foreign. The people of the Ketharn have been here long enough that the lower world is not a memory but a myth, a place referenced in certain songs that are sung during the condensation-fogs, when the moisture beads on the stone walls and the city goes quiet — quieter than usual — and even the dust-runners settle into their corners and wait.

The sound of the city in those moments: the drip of condensation collecting and falling from ledge to ledge, each drop striking a slightly different surface and producing a slightly different note. The shift of bodies in small stone rooms. The distant, barely audible friction of kites adjusting their wings in the fog overhead. And beneath it all, felt more than heard, the enormous silence of the plateau itself — the silence of a vast high cold place that was recently born and is still deciding what it will become.
