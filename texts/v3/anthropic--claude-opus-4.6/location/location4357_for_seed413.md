# The Cistern Engine

---

You are inside a machine that has been running so long it has become geology.

The space is not large. You could cross it in forty steps, if you could cross it, which you cannot. It is a chamber — roughly ovoid, the ceiling perhaps nine meters overhead at its highest point, tapering down at every edge — buried somewhere in the deep infrastructure of a city that no longer exists above it. Or perhaps the city still exists. It doesn't matter. This chamber has no relationship with the surface anymore. It has been here longer than the buildings above it have had names.

---

The walls are ancient iron, but that word — *iron* — does no justice to what centuries of wet air have done. The original plates were riveted in place so long ago that the rivets have become part of the wall, swallowed by successive layers of mineral crust. Rust has not destroyed the structure so much as *replaced* it. The iron oxidized, and the oxideite calcified, and the calcificationite was infiltrated by mineral-rich water seeping through whatever stone lies beyond, and now the walls are a striated amalgam — deep red-brown and ochre and black, with veins of calcium white threading through like the marbling of meat. In places, where the original iron has completely surrendered its integrity, the wall has been taken over by stalactite formations that hang like melted candle wax, fat with deposited silicates. They are warm to the touch. Everything here is warm to the touch.

The floor is not flat. It may have been once. Now it is a series of shallow basins scooped by centuries of dripping, connected by runnels where the water has found its routes and committed to them absolutely. The basins hold water — dark, still, mineral-thick water, warm as blood and opaque with dissolved calcium and iron. The surface of each pool is perfectly motionless. There are no ripples. The water does not flow; it *weeps in* from the walls and it *seeps out* through unseen channels below, and the rate of entry and exit have been in perfect equilibrium for longer than anyone has been alive to notice. The pools are maybe a hand's breadth deep at their centers, some less. Their edges are scalloped with flowstone — smooth, undulating formations in shades of rust and cream that look almost biological but are not. Nothing biological has lived here in a very long time, if ever.

The air is wet. Not humid — *wet*. The condensation is immediate and total. The moment you breathe in, you taste it: iron and ozone and the faint alkaline bite of calcium carbonate. Droplets form on every surface that isn't already submerged. Your skin films over. Water beads on your eyelashes. The warm air holds so much moisture that the boundary between air and liquid feels theoretical. There are no plants. No moss, no slime, no lichen. Whatever minerals saturate this water, they are hostile to life or simply uninteresting to it. The pools are sterile. The walls are sterile. The condensation on your own skin is sterile. This is a mineral world, wet and warm and utterly empty of biology.

---

And then there is the sound.

The sound is the first thing. It will also be the last thing. It is the reason you cannot think clearly in this place, the reason description comes in fragments, the reason your eyes wander without settling.

The chamber is dominated — *saturated* — by the noise of a machine.

Not machines, plural. A machine. One. It occupies the center of the chamber like an altar or a heart. It is a pump. Or it was a pump. Or it is what a pump becomes after it has been pumping for so long that it has accreted purpose beyond its original design.

The pump is a vertical cylinder of the same ancient iron as the walls, perhaps two meters in diameter, rising from the deepest of the floor basins to within a meter of the ceiling, where it connects to a massive pipe that disappears into the rock above. Around its base, a system of intakes — six of them, equidistant, mouths wide and crusted with the same flowstone as the pools — drink from channels in the floor. The water enters the machine. The machine sends it up. This has been happening for longer than the rust on the walls, because the machine *predates* the rust. The machine is the oldest thing in the room.

It runs on something. There is no visible fuel source, no electrical conduit that hasn't been consumed by mineral growth, no steam, no combustion. Deep inside the cylinder, something turns — you can feel it through the floor, through the water, through your teeth — a massive rotor or piston or cam, something heavy and relentless, turning or cycling with a period of about four seconds. The sound it produces is extraordinary. It is not a clang or a hum or a drone. It is a low, thick, *pressurized* throbbing — a sound that is felt as much as heard, that fills the ovoid chamber and reflects off the curved walls and compounds with itself until the air is simply *solid with noise*. It is the sound of dense metal moving inside dense metal, transmitted through warm water into warm stone into warm air. The reverberations overlap. The harmonics stack. The chamber is an echo chamber of perfect, terrible efficiency.

**WHUM.** Pause. **WHUM.** Pause. **WHUM.**

The pause is not silence. The pause is the decay of the previous stroke failing to fully die before the next one begins, so what you hear in the gap is a kind of pressurized hush, the ghost of the last **WHUM** still pressing against your eardrums. Your chest vibrates. Your vision blurs at the edges with each stroke, not from the sound exactly, but from the way the saturated air *shudders* — the condensation in the atmosphere momentarily disturbed into micro-droplets that catch whatever faint light exists and scatter it.

Because there is almost no light. The darkness is not absolute but it is *thick* — a deep umber gloom that seems to emanate from the iron walls themselves, as though the rust were a source of anti-light. What illumination exists comes from two sources: a faint thermal glow from the pipe above, where the ascending water heats the junction to a dull, barely-visible red; and a pale phosphorescence from the mineral deposits in the pools, a cold blue-white luminescence that is chemical, not biological, and so faint that your eyes must adjust for minutes before it resolves into anything more than the suggestion of light. In this almost-darkness, the pump is more felt than seen — a massive vertical presence, a column of sound, its outline traced only by the way it interrupts the faint glow of the pools.

---

The whole system is in equilibrium. You understand this the longer you stand here, your feet in the warm runnels, the water seeping through whatever you're wearing, the noise filling your skull. Nothing is changing. The water enters the chamber at the same rate it has entered for centuries. The pump moves it upward at the same rate. The mineral deposits grow so slowly they might as well be static. The temperature does not fluctuate. The sound does not vary. Each stroke of the pump is identical to the last — the same force, the same duration, the same resonance. You could come back in a hundred years and find every pool at the same depth, every stalactite the same length to the millimeter, the same droplet forming at the same point on the same wall and falling into the same basin at the same angle.

This is not stasis. Stasis implies nothing is happening. Here, everything is happening — the pump is working enormously, the water is cycling, energy is being expended at industrial scale — but it is all happening at exactly the rate required to maintain the current state. The system found its final form a long time ago. It settled. It is now just *being itself*, permanently, with immense effort and immense noise, in the dark, in the warm wet mineral air, beneath a city that may have forgotten it exists.

There are other pipes. You notice them slowly, as your eyes adjust. Smaller ones, branching off the main column at various heights, disappearing into the walls — some corroded shut, some still carrying flow, their routes through the surrounding rock unknowable. The pump feeds a network. The network feeds something above. What it feeds — reservoirs, fountains, heating systems, other machines — is beyond your reach. You are in the root system of an infrastructure whose canopy you will never see.

On the far wall, partially buried under a cascade of flowstone, there is a hatch. It is closed. The mineral deposits around its edges are unbroken. No one has opened it in a century at minimum. Whatever access corridor lies behind it has likely suffered the same slow mineral invasion as everything else. This chamber was meant to be serviced. Inspected. Maintained. It hasn't been. The machine has not needed maintenance. Or it has needed it and continued anyway, indifferent to need.

You realize you have been here for some time. The sound has not become bearable; you have simply stopped resisting it. It has replaced your heartbeat. It has become the tempo of your thoughts. **WHUM.** You notice the water around your feet is the same temperature as your skin, and you can no longer feel where your body ends and the water begins. **WHUM.** The faint phosphorescence in the pools seems brighter now, or your pupils have dilated so fully that you are seeing by light no human eye was meant to use. **WHUM.** The pump turns. The water rises. The minerals grow. Nothing changes.

You are standing inside a heartbeat that will never stop.
