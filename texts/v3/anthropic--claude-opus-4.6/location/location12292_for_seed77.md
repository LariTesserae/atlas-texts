# The Laying of the Floor

There is a world being built, and no one is building it.

---

That is the first thing you would notice, if you were here — the sheer absence of builders. The ground beneath you is not ground. It is a surface, flat and deliberate, extending in every direction farther than atmosphere allows you to see. It is made of something like fired clay, but immense — tiles the size of football pitches, each one a slightly different shade of ochre or rust or pale calcium-white, fitted together with seams so fine you might walk for an hour before your foot found one. The seams are warm to the touch, warmer than the tiles themselves, as though something beneath is still curing.

This floor — and it *is* a floor, unmistakably a floor, not a plain, not a plateau, a *floor* — stretches to every horizon. There are no walls yet. Maybe there will never be walls. But the floor is sure of itself. The floor knows what it is.

---

The heat comes from below and above simultaneously. The sky is a hazy off-white, the sun visible through it as a brighter region rather than a disc — diffused, the light of a kiln room, the light inside a ceramic before it's opened. The air temperature sits in that range where stillness becomes a physical presence, where the heat doesn't assault you so much as lean against you, constantly, from all sides. You breathe it and it feels thick but not wet. Dry heat, mostly. But not entirely.

Because there is moisture here. It comes in events.

Every few hours — though time is hard to fix in a place this featureless — a section of the floor will darken. Not from above. From within. Moisture seeps upward through the tile-body, appearing first as a slight change in color, then as an unmistakable damp sheen, then as actual condensation sitting on the surface in a film so thin it seems to hesitate about whether to exist. It lasts perhaps twenty minutes. The heat takes it back. The tile dries from its edges inward, returning to its original shade of burnt earth, and the event is over. These wet-blooms, as you come to think of them, do not happen randomly. Watch long enough and you begin to sense a pattern — a vast, slow pulse moving through the floor from what might be the southeast, a wave of dampness that takes the better part of a day to cross the visible distance, darkening tiles in sequence like the shadow of a cloud, except there are no clouds.

There are no clouds at all.

---

The sparseness of this place is not emptiness. It is *spaciousness*. The difference matters. Emptiness implies something missing. Here, the space between things feels intentional, a material in its own right. Walk for half a day and you will encounter a feature: a low ridge in the floor, perhaps three feet high, where a row of tiles has been set at a slight angle to the horizontal, creating a long, gentle step that runs north-south for miles. Or a shallow basin — a circular depression no deeper than your ankle, dozens of meters across, where the tile color shifts to a deep terracotta and the seams are slightly wider, admitting thin lines of that upwelling moisture which linger here longer than on the flat expanses, sometimes for an hour or more, forming a brief, vanishing film of water that catches the diffused light and holds it.

These basins are where the sparse vegetation lives.

Not plants as you know them. Something more like the idea of lichen arrived at independently by a world made entirely of ceramic. Pale green-grey crusts that colonize the basin seams, feeding on the periodic moisture, growing with geological slowness. They have no roots — nothing here has depth; the floor does not *have* a below that anything living can access — but they grip the seam-edges with a mineral stubbornness, and in the brief windows when the wet-blooms reach them, they soften perceptibly, their edges going faintly translucent, photosynthesizing in a rush before the drying comes. Between basins, the floor is barren. The distances between these scattered colonies might be ten miles, might be forty. Each one is an isolated nation of quiet growth, unaware of the others.

No animals visit them. Nothing moves here but the moisture and the heat and you.

---

The sound is the sound of scale. Which is to say: almost nothing, but not quite. Your own footsteps on the tile produce a faint, clean tap — satisfying, precise, the acoustic signature of a well-made thing. Beyond that, there is a low, nearly subsonic hum that you feel more than hear, rising from the floor itself. It is not mechanical. It has no rhythm. It is more like the sound a large structure makes simply by existing — thermal expansion, the micro-settling of vast masses, the flex of a continent-sized surface responding to temperature gradients. Occasionally, from a great distance, a soft *tock* — a single percussive note, clear and clean, as if one tile has shifted a fraction of a millimeter against its neighbor. These sounds might come once every few minutes, from any direction, never repeating from the same bearing twice. They are the only evidence, besides the wet-blooms, that this place is not static.

Because it isn't static. It's extraordinarily active. You're just too small, and it's too large, for you to see most of what's happening in real time.

---

The dynamism here operates at continental scale. Stand in one place for a week and you might notice that the seam nearest you has grown a millimeter wider. Or narrower. The floor is in motion — not shifting like tectonic plates, nothing so violent, but *adjusting*, the way a hand-built thing adjusts as it settles into its final form. New tiles are appearing at the edges of your perception, or rather, they are *becoming*. Walk far enough in any direction and you will reach a zone where the tiles are less finished — their surfaces rougher, their colors less resolved, their seams wider and still warm with that curing heat. Go farther still and the tiles become slabs, thick and approximate, their edges unshaped. Farther: you find raw tile-material, something likeiteite slip clay in massive formations, not yet cut, not yet fired, but already flat, already oriented horizontally, already *aspiring* to be floor. The world is assembling itself from its margins inward, or perhaps from everywhere at once, and the zones of completion simply migrate as new material comes into readiness.

There is no quarry. There is no kiln. The material appears to emerge from some process below the surface — or the surface *is* the process, matter organizing itself into flatness and fit and pattern with no agent directing it. The seams find their neighbors. The colors sort themselves. The firing happens in place, the tiles darkening and hardening as you watch, over days, the heat of their self-transformation radiating upward and contributing to the general warmth of the air.

This is what the heat is. You realize it slowly. The entire floor is a kiln. The atmosphere is the exhaust of creation. You are standing on a world in the act of making itself into a room.

---

What is the room for?

There is no indication. The floor does not suggest furniture. The absent walls offer no clue about what they might eventually enclose. The basins might be decorative, or functional, or accidents of the process — though nothing here feels accidental. The moisture cycle might be purposeful, a feature of the design, or it might be a byproduct of the curing, water driven upward through tile-bodies that haven't fully set. The lichen might be intended — welcomed inhabitants — or they might be opportunists colonizing a world that didn't anticipate them.

You walk. The tap of your feet on fired clay, clean and regular, is the most complex sound for miles in any direction. The heat leans. A wet-bloom passes beneath you, darkening the tile under your feet for a few minutes, and you feel the faintest coolness rising, and then it's gone, and the ochre returns, and you walk on across the vast, purposeful, unpopulated floor of a building that is building itself for reasons it has not disclosed, in a silence that is not empty but *patient*, the silence of a thing that knows exactly what it's doing and is in no hurry to explain.
