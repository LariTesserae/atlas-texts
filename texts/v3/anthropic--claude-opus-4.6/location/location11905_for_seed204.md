# The Mantling District

---

It is a single room, though the word "room" does violence to what it actually is.

Roughly eleven meters across. Hexagonal. The walls rise perhaps six meters before curving inward to a dome that never quite closes — there is a gap at the apex, maybe thirty centimeters wide, and through it you can see a sliver of grey sky that looks impossibly far away, the way the sky does when you are very high up and the atmosphere has thinned to something translucent and unconvincing.

You are, in fact, high up. Whatever structure this room is nested inside — and it is nested, embedded, one module among many — sits at an elevation where air pressure is perhaps sixty percent of sea level. You can feel it in the slight labor of each breath. The cold confirms it: not bitter, not wind-driven, but a patient, mineral cold that radiates from the walls and floor as though the building itself remembers the mountain it was built upon.

Or built *from*. That's the first thing that complicates the room.

---

The walls are not made of stone. They are not made of metal. They appear to be made of something that decided, at some molecular level, to be both. A dark graphite-colored material with a faint crystalline grain, like gabbro, but when you press your palm against it the surface yields by a fraction of a millimeter and then firms again — responsive, load-distributing, warm where your hand was and cold everywhere else. A phase-material. The kind of engineered substance that has no fixed internal structure but rearranges itself along gradients of pressure, temperature, and — you suspect, though you have no way of proving it — intention.

There is moisture on the walls. Not dripping. Condensing. The room is cold enough and the air carries just enough humidity that a permanent dew clings to every surface below about two meters. It gathers in the fine grooves of the hexagonal floor tiles — each tile perhaps forty centimeters across, each one slightly concave, so the condensation migrates to the edges and collects in the seams. The seams are channels. The channels run toward the center of the room, where a shallow basin about half a meter wide sits flush with the floor. The basin is never empty and never full. The water in it is maybe two centimeters deep, perfectly clear, and very still. It reflects the disc of grey sky visible through the oculus above.

You can hear the water if you hold your breath. A faint capillary sound, too quiet to be called trickling, more like the room slowly exhaling liquid. The walls weep. The floor collects. The basin holds. Something — some process you cannot see, embedded in the material itself — prevents overflow. The water level does not change.

---

There is no plant life in this room. None. The walls are bare. The floor is bare. The basin grows nothing. Whatever city or settlement this chamber belongs to, it exists in a biome that has either not yet developed vegetation or has long since moved past the need for it. The stone of the mountain — visible through the oculus, visible in glimpses through two narrow slit-windows on opposite walls — is raw, sharp, recently upthrust. Dark basalt and pale feldspar in jagged alternation, the kind of exposed geology that says *I am new, I was deep, I was not meant to see light yet.* No lichen. No moss. No soil. The mountain is too young, too cold, too high.

And yet the room is part of a city.

---

Through the slit-windows you can see *out*, and what is out there is not wilderness. Other structures — hexagonal, domed, dark-walled, identical in material if not in size — cluster along the ridgeline in a sparse, deliberate pattern. Not packed together. There are wide gaps between them, thirty or forty meters of bare rock and condensation-slick stone, crossed by narrow walkways made of the same phase-material as the walls. The walkways have no railings. They gleam with moisture. The wind at this altitude should make them treacherous, but the walkways seem to manage wind somehow — you can see currents of fog deflecting around them, curling aside, as though the air has been persuaded to move differently in their vicinity.

The settlement extends in both directions along the ridge and down the steep flanks of the mountain. Maybe two hundred structures visible from here, maybe more hidden by the curve of the terrain. Sparse. A city by function if not by density. The distances between buildings are purposeful — each structure allotted its own margin of emptiness, its own share of the mountain's silence.

There is infrastructure. Thin conduits, the same dark material, run along the ground between buildings, occasionally diving beneath the rock surface. They carry something — water, energy, data, some medium you don't have a word for. Where a conduit enters a building, there is a faint hum, subsonic, felt more in the sternum than heard. The conduits pulse. Not visibly. Not rhythmically. But there is a sense of transmission, of activity distributed across the network at a pace that is organic and mechanical at once.

---

The city is busy. Not with people — you have seen no one since you became aware of this room — but with *process.* The walls change temperature in slow waves. The condensation pattern on the floor shifts, the channels rerouting themselves subtly over the course of minutes, responding to something. The phase-material is thinking, or something like thinking. The basin in the center of the room brightens slightly and then dims — not light exactly, but a change in the water's transparency, as if its refractive index is being modulated. Data, maybe. Communication. The room is a node in a network and the network is in constant conversation with itself.

Outside, through the slit-windows, you can see one of the conduits detach from the ground, arch upward like a bridge, and reconnect to the rock surface ten meters further along the ridge. It does this over the course of perhaps ninety seconds. The rock where it previously rested shows a faint dark stain that fades as condensation washes over it. The city is reconfiguring. Not urgently. Not dramatically. In the way that a sleeping body shifts — continuous micro-adjustments to pressure, temperature, load, need.

On the ridge opposite — separated from this settlement by a deep notch valley where fog pools like grey milk — another cluster of structures is visible. Smaller. Newer. Some of them are still emerging from the rock, the phase-material spreading outward from what might be seed-points, crystallizing into walls and domes in real time. You watch one structure develop the beginning of a slit-window: a vertical stress-line appears, darkens, thins, and then opens, revealing the dim interior of a room not unlike this one. The whole process takes about four minutes.

---

Now and then, something moves across the walkways. Not human-shaped. Small — perhaps the size of a closed fist — and low to the ground, moving in an unhurried straight line from one structure to the next. Dark against the dark walkways, visible mainly by the way condensation parts around it. You see one pause at the junction of two conduits, hold still for ten or fifteen seconds, and then continue on its way. Maintenance fauna, or autonomous sensors, or something else. They are rare. You might see three or four in an hour of watching.

Once, something larger moves through the fog in the notch valley below. A shape, pale against the grey. It is there for perhaps two seconds before the fog closes. It did not look like a machine. It did not quite look like an animal. It moved with a lateral undulation, like something swimming through air that was thick enough to swim through. At this altitude, in this cold, in this wet — maybe the air *is* thick enough for something. The ecology here, such as it is, may not follow rules you recognize. There is no way to confirm what you saw.

---

Sound: mostly the room. The faint wet respiration of condensation finding its channels. The subsonic hum of the conduit where it enters the wall to your left. Occasionally, from outside, a sound like a struck bell — except muffled, deep, and sourceless. It happens two or three times over the span of an hour, always from different directions. It might be structural: the mountain settling, the phase-material resolving some internal stress. It might be the city signaling itself.

The light through the oculus is steady. Grey-white. It doesn't feel like overcast — it feels like the sky at this altitude simply *is* this color, that there is not enough atmosphere above to scatter light into blue. The light makes the condensation on the walls glitter faintly. It makes the basin in the center of the room look like a coin of mercury. It casts no strong shadows. Everything is evenly, softly, completely visible.

You are standing in a room in a city on a young mountain that still smells of deep earth. The city was not built by hands. It may not have been built *for* hands. But the room has slit-windows at human eye-height, and the basin is at a comfortable distance for kneeling beside, and the door — which you have not yet tried, a hexagonal opening in the wall behind you, currently sealed by a membrane of the same phase-material — is exactly the right size for you to walk through.

Whether the city made itself this way because it knew you were coming, or because it simply makes rooms this way, on the off chance that someone might arrive — that is a question the room is not answering.

The water in the basin shifts its clarity again. The walls exhale. Something small moves along a walkway outside, pauses, and moves on. The city on the ridge is young, and cold, and wet, and very much alive, and it is not waiting for you, but it is also not ignoring you, and the difference between those two things is the entire atmosphere of this place.
