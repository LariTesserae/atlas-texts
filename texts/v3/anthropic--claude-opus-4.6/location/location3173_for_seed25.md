# The Cleft of Voices

---

You come to understand where you are by sound before anything else.

The noise reaches you first as a pressure against the chest, a thrumming that enters through the sternum and settles behind the eyes. It is layered — not chaotic, not exactly, but so densely woven that individual threads are almost impossible to separate. Clicks overlapping with long, bowing moans. A stuttering chitter rising through a sustained bass drone that seems to emanate from the ground itself. Something shrieks — something else answers — and between those two cries a hundred smaller sounds are occurring, each one produced by a throat or a membrane or a limb scraped against a body part evolved specifically for this purpose: to add to the sound.

You are standing in a low, wide basin of stone. The rock is dark — not black, but the deep iron-gray of basalt that has been weathered into soft, rounded forms over spans of time that have nothing to do with you. The basin stretches in every direction at a gradual scale, perhaps the size of a small valley, though its edges are difficult to determine because the light is so thin. There is almost no light. What exists is a faint bioluminescence — cold blues, pale greens, a rare arterial red — that pulses and shifts across the surfaces of the basin in patterns that are not the rock's own. The light belongs to the animals.

They are everywhere.

The density is the second thing you understand, once your eyes adjust to what little glow there is. Every surface of the basin is occupied. The rounded basalt shelves and hollows, the shallow drainage channels where condensation trickles in slow, intermittent films of moisture — all of it is covered in living bodies. They are layered. They are climbing over one another. Some are still and some are in constant motion and many exist in a state between, swaying or vibrating in place, producing their sounds.

There is no vegetation. No moss, no lichen, nothing photosynthetic — there is barely any light for it. The basin floor is mineral: basalt worn smooth by millennia of condensation and the slow passage of bodies. In the shallow channels, water collects — not flowing, not quite, but seeping, beading on stone, pooling in depressions no deeper than a cupped palm. The air is cool and damp, the kind of damp that clings to skin without ever becoming rain. A mist, almost, but not visible. You feel it more than see it. It tastes of calcium and of something animal — a musk, an ammonia edge, the warm exhalation of countless metabolisms packed close.

Now look at them. Try.

The creatures nearest your feet are small, dense-bodied things the size and rough shape of a fist, with too many legs — perhaps twelve, perhaps sixteen, the count is hard to fix because the legs overlap and interweave with the legs of their neighbors. Their carapaces are the color of wet slate and they produce a clicking sound by flexing plates along their dorsal ridge. They move in slow, purposeful rotations, circling one another, circling nothing. They coat the ground so thickly that the stone beneath them is more inference than observation.

But these are only the bottom layer.

Rising from this living carpet are taller forms. Creatures like elongated hands, standing on their fingertips — pale, semi-translucent, their internal structures dimly visible in their own faint luminescence. Five or six radial limbs support a central body that tapers upward and ends in a structure that vibrates constantly, producing a sound like someone drawing a wet finger around the rim of a glass, except lower, much lower, felt in the jaw. These stand among the crawling fist-creatures and above them, occupying a different stratum of space in the same square meter of basin floor. Dozens within arm's reach. Hundreds in the middle distance. They sway.

And over these, flying or drifting or suspended by means not immediately clear — things with membranes. Dark against the darkness, visible only when they pass before a bioluminescent body below and their silhouettes register: wide, flat, undulating shapes like rays or like sheets of skin given purpose. They produce the moaning. Long, resonant exhalations of air through structures that may be mouths, may be spiracles, may be organs with no earthly analog. The moaning comes from above in overlapping waves, never ceasing, because there are so many of them that as one falls silent another three are already sustaining the note.

But you have only accounted for three kinds of creature, and the basin holds — you begin to sense — dozens. Perhaps more. The density and the darkness conspire to reveal them only partially, in glimpses. Something long and segmented threads between the hand-walkers, catching the faint green glow along its wet flank. Something with a single large eye, or what functions as an eye — a pale disc that reflects light — watches from a hollow in the rock, surrounded by the smaller clicking bodies, unbothered by them, immense by comparison. A cluster of organisms that appear to be a single colonial body breathes in unison, their shared expansion and contraction producing a huffing rhythm that underlies everything else.

The sound. You must return to the sound because it is inescapable. It is not merely loud — it is *saturated*. Every frequency is occupied. The clicks and the glass-moans and the shrieks and the drones and the huffing and a wet, rhythmic slapping from somewhere in the middle distance and a high crystalline ringing that might be produced by the moisture itself vibrating on stone under the influence of so many resonating bodies — all of it fills the basin to its brim and presses outward. If there are walls to this valley, the sound bounces off them and returns altered, layered over itself. You feel it in your teeth. You feel it in the small bones of your inner ear reaching their limit of useful discrimination and giving up, delivering to your brain not a catalogue of individual sounds but a *texture* — a unified roar that is somehow also a hush, because it is so constant, so totalizing, that it becomes a kind of silence. A silence made of everything at once.

The cool air moves. Not wind — there is no wind down here in the basin — but currents generated by the collective respiration and motion of the fauna. Thermal plumes rise from denser clusters of bodies. The drifting membrane-creatures ride these thermals, tilting, turning, their moans Doppler-shifting as they pass. The air itself seems thick with presence, as if the creatures have metabolized it into something partly theirs.

And the strangeness. You notice it in degrees.

First: the creatures do not eat each other. Or — you watch for a long time, and you see no predation. No pursuit, no evasion. The fist-creatures rotate among the hand-walkers' legs without concern. The great eye in the hollow watches not with hunger but with something else, some quality of attention that has no obvious survival function. The membrane-drifters pass over everything without descending, without striking. What are they all eating? The stone is bare. The water holds no visible life. There are no plants, no detritus you can identify. Yet the metabolisms are real — the heat, the moisture of breath, the luminescence all require fuel.

Second: the sounds are not random. The longer you stand in the saturated noise, the more you begin to perceive structure. Not language, not exactly, but *responsiveness*. A clicking cascade from the ground-layer triggers an adjustment in the glass-moaning of the hand-walkers nearby. The membrane-drifters' pitch follows something — not melody, but a shared contour, as if they are all responding to the same invisible signal or each responding to the response of the one beside them, a chain of adjustment that propagates across the basin in waves. The colonial breathers huff in a period that changes, and when it changes, the creatures within a body-length of them shift their own rhythms subtly. The whole basin is a single, dense, self-modifying chorus. It has no conductor. It may have no purpose. But it is clearly organized — or rather, it is clearly *organizing*, continuously, an active process that never resolves into a fixed pattern.

Third: the luminescence is part of it. The pulses of blue and green and rare red are not decorative, not mating displays — or if they are, then mating in this basin is a continuous, collective, sonic-luminous event involving all species simultaneously. The light moves in waves that correspond to the sound waves. A brightening in one quadrant of the basin accompanies a shift in the dominant frequency. A sudden flare of red from the eye-creature in the hollow coincides with a momentary near-silence — a dip, a breath — before the sound rushes back in, changed. Slightly lower. Slightly more resonant. As if the eye-creature edited something.

You look down and realize the fist-creatures have covered your feet. They click against your ankles without urgency. They are warm. Their carapaces are smoother than they look. The twelve or sixteen legs move across your skin with a delicate precision that feels almost investigatory, almost gentle.

The stone beneath everything is ancient. The basalt has been here since fire put it here, and water has been softening it ever since — not rivers, not rain, but this: the slow condensation of a cool, enclosed atmosphere, the breath-moisture of ten thousand generations of creatures pooling in channels, dissolving minerals grain by grain, leaving the stone as smooth and curved as the inside of a skull. The basin is a bowl made by patience. The shallow pools in the drainage channels are slightly alkaline, tasting of calcium carbonate, and they shimmer with reflected bioluminescence like small dark mirrors laid flat on the floor of the world.

There is a sense, standing here, that you have entered an organ. Not a landscape but a living interior — a stomach, a womb, a throat. The basin is the body. The creatures are its processes. The sound is its heartbeat, or its thoughts, or its digestion of something you cannot see. The cool air is its breath. The moisture is its blood, moving so slowly through stone channels that you would need to watch for hours to confirm its direction.

Nothing here was built. Nothing here was designed. But everything here is arranged with a density and interlocking precision that makes the word "wild" feel inadequate. This is not wilderness. This is something that organized itself into a state so complex and so self-referencing that it resembles intention without containing any.

The sound swells. The membrane-drifters above you shift and the light changes — a ripple of deep blue crossing the basin from east to west, if directions mean anything here. The hand-walkers near you adjust their glass-tone downward by a half-step you feel in your molars. The fist-creatures on your feet click faster. The eye in the hollow flares pale green, holds it, holds it, and releases. The colonial breathers exhale together and the whole basin seems to settle a quarter-inch deeper into the earth.

Something is happening. Something is always happening. You cannot tell what it is.

You stay.
