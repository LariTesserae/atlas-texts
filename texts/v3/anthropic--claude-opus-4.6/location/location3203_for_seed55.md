# The Drowned Halls

There is no sky here. There has not been sky for longer than the word *sky* has existed.

What there is: water. Cold, dark, still water, pressing in from every direction, filling every volume not already claimed by stone or wood or the fibrous matting of root-weave that holds the older structures together. The water is fresh — snowmelt from a surface so far above that no one remembers it as a real place. It descends through miles of layered construction, losing its light somewhere in the first hundred feet, arriving down here stripped of warmth, stripped of color, carrying only the faintest mineral taste and an impossible patience.

---

You are inside something. You have been inside something since before you arrived. The structure extends in every direction — not as a single building but as an accretion, a continent-spanning lattice of chambers, corridors, vaulted cisterns, flooded plazas, and passages that branch and reconnect and branch again across distances that would take days to swim. It was built. All of it was built. But it was built the way a coral reef is built: slowly, collectively, over epochs, each generation adding to what the last left behind until the original intent — if there was one — became irrelevant, subsumed into the sheer fact of the structure's existence.

The walls are stone, mostly. Not quarried stone — there are no quarries down here, no exposed bedrock to cut from. The stone was carried, piece by piece, from wherever stone could be found, and fitted together with a craftsmanship that is primitive in its tools but ancient in its refinement. No mortar. No metal pins or clamps. Just stone shaped by hand, by abrasion, by lifetimes of careful grinding until the joints between blocks are tighter than the width of a hair. Water does not pass through these joints. Water has been trying for ten thousand years and has not succeeded.

In the older sections — and there are sections so old that the stone itself has begun toite, to delaminate in slow mineral exhalations — the walls curve inward, forming arches and barrel vaults that distribute the crushing weight of the water and of all the construction above. These ancient halls are low-ceilinged, close, the proportions of spaces made by beings who understood pressure in their bones. The ceiling is never far. The walls are never far. You move through them the way water moves through capillaries: slowly, in single file, with a sense that the structure around you is alive in its own dense, lithic way.

But then a passage opens, and you are in a cistern so vast that the far wall is invisible in the dark, and the water column above you rises three hundred feet to a ceiling you cannot see, and you hang there in the blackness and feel the space around you like a held breath.

---

The vegetation is what makes it bearable. What makes it, against all logic, *lush*.

They are not plants — not exactly. They are photosynthetic only in ancestry, in some vestigial biochemical memory of a surface life abandoned so long ago that the chlorophyll has gone strange, turned to other purposes. Down here they feed on chemistry: on the mineral seepage through stone, on the sulfur-traces and the iron-ghosts dissolved in the cold water, on the slow metabolic exhaust of their own decomposition. They grow everywhere. They grow on everything.

Furred mats of something like moss — thick, pale, velvety to the touch — cover the lower walls of every corridor. They grow in the joints between stones where no water passes but where minerals precipitate in invisible films. Long trailing filaments hang from archways and ceiling vaults, stirring in currents so faint they might be imagined. In the great cisterns, entire forests of them rise from the flooded floors: columnar growths with branching crowns, rootless, anchored only by their own accumulated weight, swaying with a tidal patience that has no tide behind it. They are white, or grey, or the palest green — colors that exist only for the rare bioluminescent flicker that passes through them in waves, a slow pulse of cold blue-green light that illuminates nothing but itself, a rumor of visibility that moves through the halls like weather.

These pulses are the closest thing to light that exists down here. They move through the vegetation in long, rolling fronts — a wave of dim luminescence that takes minutes to cross a single cistern, propagating through the filaments by chemical relay, each organism triggering the next. In the largest halls, you can see — if *see* is the right word — three or four of these waves moving at once, crossing each other, interfering, creating momentary brightnesses that almost resolve into sight before fading back into a darkness so complete it has texture.

Between the waves: nothing. A darkness that is not the absence of light but the *replacement* of light, the thing that lives where light used to be. Your eyes do not adjust to it. There is nothing to adjust to. You navigate by touch, by the feel of the stone under your fingers and the moss against your skin, by the faint thermal differences between corridors where the mineral-eaters are thick and active and corridors where the stone is bare and the water is degrees colder.

---

Sound carries strangely. The water transmits it with perfect fidelity but the structure breaks it apart — absorbs some frequencies in its moss-covered walls, amplifies others in its vaulted chambers, bends the rest around corners and through passages until the origin of any sound is unknowable. What you hear, mostly, is the structure itself. The low, subsonic groan of stone under pressure — not a settling sound, because the structure settled into its final form millennia ago, but a *holding* sound, the continuous expression of forces in equilibrium. A deep tonal hum that you feel more than hear, that lives in your chest and in your teeth.

Beyond that: dripping. Somewhere, always, water finding its way through a joint that has finally, after centuries of resistance, begun to admit it. A single drop migrating through stone, emerging on the other side, falling — slowly, in the dense water — to join the volume below. The sound this makes is almost nothing. A tick. A tiny concussion. But in the silence of the Drowned Halls, it is an event.

Occasionally, less often, a different sound: the creak and sigh of the great vegetation columns shifting their weight. They grow so slowly that the movement is imperceptible, but the accumulated strain releases in sudden, quiet adjustments — a groan of fiber, a soft snap of a filament that has grown too long to support itself. These sounds echo through the cisterns like voices, and in the dark, with no reference and no light, it is easy to mistake them for exactly that.

---

There are no animals. There are no fish, no worms, no crustaceans picking through the moss-beds. Whatever ecology once existed here has simplified, over the ages, down to two kingdoms: the stone, and the things that eat the stone. The vegetation — if we keep calling it that — fills every role. It decomposes its own dead. It fixes its own chemistry. It generates the faint bioluminescent signals that coordinate its growth across distances of miles. It is not intelligent, but it is *organized*, in the way that a weather system is organized: patterns emerge, persist, propagate, and dissolve, and the whole system breathes with a rhythm that spans centuries.

The structure itself participates in this ecology. The builders — whoever they were, whatever they were — built with an understanding of the vegetation's needs. The stone they chose was mineral-rich. The joints they left were calibrated: tight enough to hold, porous enough to sweat. The proportions of the chambers seem designed to create the faint convective currents that distribute nutrients through the water column. Whether the builders served the vegetation or the vegetation served the builders is a question that lost its meaning long before the last builder died or departed or became something else.

Because the builders are gone. That much is clear. The tools remain — stone adzes, abrasive blocks, shaping implements carved from the bones of the vegetation itself, all of them resting where they were last set down, furred over now with pale growth, becoming part of the structure they were used to build. In some of the older chambers, the tools are fully absorbed: visible only as bulges in the moss, shapes that the hand recognizes even when the eye (if there were light enough to use it) would not. The craftsmanship is extraordinary, but it is the craftsmanship of patience, not sophistication. Every surface was shaped by hand. Every block was placed by muscle and by judgment. There is no machinery here, no mechanisms, no pulleys or levers beyond the simplest kind. What there is, is time. Enough time to grind stone with stone until it fits. Enough time to build a world from the bottom up, one hand-fitted block at a time, across generations that had nowhere else to go.

---

The scale of it defies comprehension. You can swim for hours in a single direction and the architecture changes — the proportions shift, the style of stonework evolves through centuries of aesthetic drift, the moss species turn over as you cross invisible chemical boundaries — but the *structure* never ends. It never opens onto raw earth or unworked rock. There is no outside. The Drowned Halls are not built *in* a place; they *are* the place, an entirety, a world that is nothing but itself. Somewhere above, unimaginably far above, the snowmelt enters. Somewhere below — if there is a below — the water must go. But down here, in the middle depths, the water simply *is*, and the stone simply *is*, and the pale vegetation breathes its faint chemical light across halls that no one sees, and the silence holds, and the cold holds, and the structure holds, and everything is exactly as it has been for longer than any living memory could contain, and will be for longer still, and the darkness is not empty but full — full of stone, full of growth, full of the patient, monumental fact of a world that was made, entirely, by hand, and then left to its own slow dreaming.
