# The Forges of Thaal Kethis

---

You come up over the last ridgeline and the world drops away beneath you — not into a valley, but into a rising. What you see shouldn't make sense at first. It takes your eyes time to parse it.

A plateau, impossibly high — the kind of elevation where the atmosphere should be thin and biting, except here the air is thick and hot and tastes of copper filings and something older, something mineral and sulfurous that sits on the back of your tongue. The sky is a pale amber, not from dust but from the altitude's distortion of light through layers of thermal convection, the sun appearing as a flattened white disc slightly too large, slightly too close, as though this elevation has brought you nearer to it than geography should allow.

The plateau extends for miles. And it is *full*.

---

## The Terrain

The ground beneath everything is fresh. That's the word that keeps asserting itself — *fresh*. Not ancient rock, not eroded sediment, but geology still deciding what it wants to be. The basalt is weeks old in places, months in others, extruded in slow dark tongues from vents that glow a dull and patient orange at their deepest points. In other places the stone is something not quite basalt — a glassy, blue-black mineral with an internal crystalline structure that catches light and refracts it into colors you don't have names for, colors that exist in the gap between ultraviolet and some other band. This mineral fractures in hexagonal columns, and where it fractures, the faces are mirror-smooth, and warm to the touch.

There is no soil. No dust, even. The stone is too new to have weathered into particles. Where you walk, your boots ring against surfaces that have never been walked on, that formed in silence and heat and have been waiting — not metaphorically, you'll come to feel, but in some patient literal way — for pressure.

The plateau is not flat. It buckles and ridges, the new stone pushing up in asymmetric folds, creating shelves and terraces and sudden drops of twenty or forty feet where a sheet of rock has calved away to reveal the glowing seams beneath. Steam rises from these wounds. The elevation is staggering — you are higher than almost anywhere, high enough that the horizon curves perceptibly, and the few clouds that exist sit *below* you, thin smears of white trapped in the middle atmosphere, visible through gaps in the terrain where the plateau's edge falls away into depths you cannot measure.

And built across every surface of this raw and still-forming world: the city.

---

## The City

Thaal Kethis — the name comes to you not from any sign or marker but from the low resonance of the infrastructure itself, a kind of harmonic identity embedded in the metalwork, readable if you press your palm against any girder and hold still long enough.

It is not a city that was planned and then constructed. It is a city that is being *extruded*, growing in tandem with the geology beneath it, and this is the first strange thing you must accept: the buildings are being manufactured by processes that are partly industrial and partly geological, and the boundary between the two is not clean.

Massive frameworks of dark iron rise from the basalt — scaffolding, you think at first, but no. The scaffolding *is* the architecture. Open lattices of riveted metal, forty and fifty feet tall, connected by walkways and gantries and enclosed corridors sheathed in plates of that blue-black mineral, cut into panels and bolted to the iron with fasteners that still radiate heat. The structures are dense, packed close, sometimes nearly touching, their girders interlocking where adjacent frames have grown toward each other like — not like trees, there are no trees here, nothing grows in the way you understand growing — like crystal formations competing for the same space.

Between the frames, at ground level: foundries. Not foundries with walls and roofs but foundries as *features* — open pits where the new stone's heat is captured and channeled through ceramic-lined troughs into crucibles the size of houses. Metal collects in these crucibles. Not mined metal. Metal that separates from the cooling stone of its own accord, drawn out by temperature gradients and some property of the blue-black mineral that acts as a kind of metallurgical filter. Iron, copper, something silver-white and very dense, something else that is liquid at temperatures where it shouldn't be and flows through specially carved channels with a viscosity like heavy oil.

The foundries feed assembly structures — long, low buildings (these have roofs, corrugated plates of the dark iron, and they tick and ping in the heat) where mechanical arms, powered by steam vented directly from geological fissures, bend and shape and rivet components whose purpose is not immediately clear. The components travel along rail-mounted conveyances, slow, almost stately, to other structures where they are incorporated into — more city. The city is building itself. The foundries produce the material, the assembly structures shape it, the rail systems deliver it to the expanding edge where new scaffolding rises from freshly cooled stone.

There is a pervasive, structural logic to it, an industrial metabolism. But no one is operating it.

---

## The Silence

This is the second strange thing: the quiet. A city of this density, this much industry, should roar. Hammers should ring, steam should shriek, conveyances should clatter and grind. But Thaal Kethis operates in something approaching silence.

Not true silence. You hear the tick of cooling metal. The low, almost subsonic hum of the geological processes beneath your feet — a vibration more felt than heard, a basso continuo that lives in your bones. Occasionally, the soft *shush* of a steam-powered arm completing a motion. The distant chime of a component settling into place, a single clear note that travels through the iron frameworks and arrives at your ears with a purity that seems intentional, almost musical. Once, a long groaning sound from the east, where a new fold of stone is pushing upward through an existing structure, the metal bending to accommodate it with a slow inevitability.

But these sounds are sparse. They exist within a larger quiet that is the quiet of patience, of processes so slow and so assured that they need not announce themselves. The conveyances move at walking pace. The mechanical arms cycle through their motions over minutes, not seconds. The stone rises imperceptibly. The city grows the way a stalactite grows — visibly, if you know where to look, but only just.

You stand on a gantry and listen, and what you hear most is the heat itself: the particular thick silence of air too warm to carry sound efficiently, the way extreme temperature creates a kind of acoustic insulation, muffling everything into a padded, cotton-wrapped hush.

---

## The Water

It rains here, sometimes. Not from clouds — you are above the clouds — but from condensation events within the city's own thermal architecture. Where hot air from the foundries meets cooler air in the upper reaches of the scaffolding, moisture collects on the iron and the blue-black mineral panels and gathers into droplets that grow fat and fall. It rains indoors, in the enclosed corridors, more often than it rains outside. The water traces bright lines down dark metal, collects in rivulets that follow the grooves between rivets, pools in the concavities of warped plates.

Some of this water reaches the ground and immediately begins to steam, a thin constant mist that haunts the lower levels of the city, softening edges, diffusing the glow of the geological vents into a lambent haze. Some of it is captured — the infrastructure includes narrow channels and cisterns worked into the framework, not large, not prominent, but present, as though the city anticipated its own condensation and built collection into its design.

There are also the thermal pools: where new stone has formed a depression and geological heat keeps the collected water just below boiling, small pools of mineral-rich water that glow faintly from the light of the rock beneath them. These pools are the closest thing to a natural feature in Thaal Kethis, and they feel anachronistic, almost sacred, nestled between the iron legs of enormous scaffolding structures, steaming quietly, their surfaces trembling with the vibrations of the industrial processes above.

---

## The Living Things

You will not see many. But you will see some, and they will trouble you.

On the third day — you've been walking for three days, you realize, the city is that large, and you haven't reached an edge — you notice movement on a girder above you. Something arthropod, many-legged, about the size of your spread hand, with a carapace that is unmistakably metallic. Not metal-colored. Metal. It moves slowly, deliberately, following the seam where two iron plates have been riveted together, and as you watch, it pauses, extends a proboscis-like appendage, and appears to *taste* the rivet. Then it moves on.

Later you see another, larger, clinging to the underside of a walkway. And then a cluster of smaller ones in one of the condensation channels, motionless, either feeding on the mineral-laden water or simply resting. They make no sound. Their legs grip the iron with a precision that suggests they were made for these surfaces specifically.

Are they fauna? Are they components? You cannot tell, and you begin to suspect the question is not well-formed here. Thaal Kethis does not observe the boundary between manufactured and alive. The arthropods move with purpose. They have something like behavior. But their carapaces bear the same rivet-marks as the panels of the city, and when one dies — you find a husk in a thermal pool, slowly dissolving — it releases trace metals back into the water, which flows back into the channels, which feed back into the foundries.

Once, on a high gantry, in the amber light of mid-afternoon, you see something larger. A shape moving through the frameworks several hundred yards away, too far to make out clearly through the thermal shimmer. It is the size of a large dog, or a small person, and it moves vertically, climbing the scaffolding with a fluid, unhurried grace. You watch it for ten minutes as it ascends toward the upper reaches of a structure still under construction, where the mechanical arms are slowly positioning a new girder. It reaches the top, and — you're not sure. It either merges with the structure or passes behind it. You wait, but it doesn't reappear.

---

## The Rules

You begin to understand:

**The city is an organ of the geology.** The plateau is not a place where a city was built; the city is what this plateau *does*. The fresh stone rises, hot and mineral-rich, and the city precipitates from it the way salt crystals precipitate from a cooling solution. The foundries are not extracting resources — they are *expressing* them, the way a gland expresses a secretion. The iron frameworks are the plateau's exoskeleton, its way of organizing its own growth, giving structure to the formless upwelling of new stone.

**The city is not finished because the geology is not finished.** Thaal Kethis has no final form. It is embryonic, perpetually. Every structure is provisional, destined to be incorporated into a larger structure, or folded into the next upwelling of stone, or disassembled by the slow arthropods and fed back into the foundry cycle. The construction you see is not progress toward completion but a continuous process of becoming, a metabolic steady-state operating on geological time.

**The heat is the engine.** Everything runs on thermal differential — the contrast between the molten seams below and the (relatively) cooler air above. This gradient drives the steam mechanisms, the condensation cycles, the metallurgical separation, the slow conveyances. If the plateau ever stopped rising, if the heat ever dissipated, Thaal Kethis would freeze in place, a dead city on a dead rock, and the arthropods would consume it over centuries, returning every atom to the stone.

**The silence is structural.** The city is quiet because it is efficient. No energy is wasted on noise. The mechanical arms are lubricated with the dense silver-white metal, which has friction properties that border on the impossible. The conveyances ride on that same liquid metal, gliding rather than rolling. Even the riveting is done by a thermal-expansion process — a component is heated until it grows into its socket, then cools and contracts into a bond so tight it produces no vibration. The quiet is not peaceful. It is the quiet of a system so well-integrated that it has eliminated the inefficiencies that would produce sound.

---

## What It Feels Like

Standing on an upper gantry, looking out over Thaal Kethis in the pale amber light, sweat tracing lines down your face and neck, hands gripping iron that is warm but not unbearable, you feel — small, but not insignificant. *Witnessed.* The city does not react to your presence, but you have the persistent sense that your weight on its structures is noted, that your footsteps are registered by the same systems that monitor the thermal gradients and the mineral flows and the positions of the slow arthropods.

You feel the age of the place — not in the structures, which are young, raw, still bearing the sheen of recent manufacture — but in the *process*. This has been happening for a long time. The current city is just the latest expression of something that began when the plateau first started to rise, perhaps thousands of years ago, perhaps longer. Layer upon layer beneath you, older cities, consumed and recycled, their metals reforged, their forms forgotten, their structures compressed into the very stone that feeds the current foundries.

You feel the heat as a presence, not just a condition. It is the animating force, the metabolism, the will of the place if the place can be said to have a will. It presses against you from below and from all sides, carried by the iron, radiated by the stone, shimmering in the air between the dense-packed frameworks. Your water — you brought water, you must have — is warm within the hour. The condensation channels are the only source of anything approaching cool, and even they are tepid, mineral-heavy, tasting of iron and that unnamed blue-black crystal.

And you feel the slow, implacable momentum of it all. The city does not hurry. The stone does not hurry. The arthropods do not hurry. You are the fastest thing in Thaal Kethis, and even you have begun to slow, to match your pace to the patient rhythm of a place that is building itself, atom by atom, rivet by rivet, geological epoch by geological epoch, toward something it may never reach and does not need to.

You stand there, and you watch the mechanical arms make their long, slow arcs, and you listen to the silence between the chimes, and far below, through the lattice of iron and stone, you see the deep orange glow of the newest rock, rising.
