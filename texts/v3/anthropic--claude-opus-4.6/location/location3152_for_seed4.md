# The Walled Hollow

---

You have to know where to look.

From above—if there were an above worth speaking of—you would see only the canopy. A low, continuous mat of interlocking mosses, club-mosses, liverworts, and things that have no name in the languages you carry, pressing together so tightly they form a living skin over the depression in the earth. The hollow itself is perhaps nine meters across at its widest. Maybe five deep. A dimple in a cold, wet lowland, its walls still sharp-cut and recent, as though the ground simply parted one season and never closed back.

It is almost entirely dark.

---

The light that enters does so through three gaps in the canopy membrane—punctures, really, each no wider than a closed fist, where the mosses have failed to seal over exposed root-lattice. What comes through is not sunlight. It is the thin, grey, pre-dawn non-light of a world under permanent overcast, filtered further through the canopy skin until it arrives below as something more felt than seen—a faint relaxation of the blackness in three irregular cones, just enough to give shape to the nearest surfaces if you hold still and let your eyes open all the way.

The rest is dark. Not void-dark. Breathing-dark. The darkness of the inside of a cupped hand.

---

The walls of the hollow are entirely alive.

Not soil-with-plants-on-it. Plants. Plant-as-substrate. The walls are woven root systems—enormous mats of pale, cold-adapted rhizomes from the canopy species above, intertwined with the finer lacework of hanging fern roots and the grasping tendrils of a creeping vine that grows downward instead of up. These roots have been trained. Guided. You can see the marks if you press close—cuts made with a stone edge, small wedges of shaped wood hammered into the root mass to redirect growth. Crude but patient. The work of hands that understand that a root, redirected, will in three seasons become a wall. In six, a shelf. In ten, a load-bearing arch.

Someone has shaped this hollow into rooms.

Not rooms exactly. Alcoves. The root walls curve inward at three points around the circumference, creating recesses deep enough for a body to sit inside with knees drawn up. The floors of these alcoves are padded with something—compressed layers of dried sphagnum, it feels like, cool and slightly damp, giving under pressure like old bread. In the largest alcove, the roots have been trained into a crude frame, a rectangular opening like a doorway that leads deeper into the root mass, though what's back there is lost to the dark.

There are other constructions. A stone-lined basin set into the floor near the center collects the water that seeps perpetually through the walls. The stones are uncut, fitted by hand, sealed with a paste that smells resinous and sharp—some plant product, some sap boiled down and worked. A set of pegs made from antler or dense wood are driven into the root wall at varying heights. Things hang from some of them: bundles of dried stems, a bladder of cured hide, something that rattles faintly when the air moves.

No fire. No soot marks, no ash pit, no char. Whoever uses this place either cannot make fire or will not.

---

The water is everywhere and nowhere in particular. It doesn't pool except in the basin. It doesn't flow. It weeps. The entire root-wall system is damp, and in the deeper darkness you can hear it if you stop breathing—a faint, pervasive *tick tick tick* of individual drops finding their way down through the lattice of roots. The air is saturated. Cold and saturated. Your breath would fog if there were light to see it by. The temperature sits just above freezing, that particular cold that feels colder than frost because the moisture carries it into you, through cloth, through skin, into the joints of your fingers.

The canopy above holds the rain when it comes—and it comes often, you can tell, because the sphagnum floor is not just damp but *has always been damp*, has structured itself around dampness, and the root walls have the pale, swollen quality of tissue that has never fully dried. When rain falls on the canopy skin, the hollow would fill with a hushed, even drumming—not loud, but total, pressing in from every surface of the ceiling at once. Between rains, the condensation continues. The hollow breathes moisture in and out, a slow transpiration cycle that fogs the air in invisible waves.

---

Now: the fauna.

You don't see them at first. You hear them, maybe. Or you feel them. A shift in the air near your face. A texture against your hand that you did not expect. A sense, accumulating, that the darkness is *occupied*.

Then your eyes adjust further—or they don't adjust, but your other senses open—and you begin to understand what lives here.

The root walls are threaded with silk. Not spider silk, though something here makes silk—a thicker, wetter filament laid down in sheets and tunnels through the root lattice, pale enough to catch the faintest light from the ceiling punctures. Within the silk tunnels, things move. Soft-bodied, many-legged, ranging in size from a grain of rice to a child's hand. They flow through the wall architecture in constant, unhurried transit, tending the root surfaces, clearing channels for water, carrying fragments of decomposing plant matter deeper into the wall. They are not insects exactly—too many legs, too flexible, no apparent exoskeleton but rather a muscular, almost liquid body plan. They are cool to the touch. They move over your skin without alarm if you hold still, exploring the heat differential, then moving on.

But they are the small ones. The maintenance fauna. The substrate.

In the floor sphagnum, larger things wait. Something that approximates a salamander—eyeless, broad-headed, its skin roughened with tubercles that match the moss texture precisely—sits motionless for so long you mistake it for a root knot. Then it strikes. A blur in the darkness, a wet sound, and one of the silk-tunnel creatures is gone. The salamander-thing settles back. The silence re-establishes itself around the event like water closing over a stone.

Overhead, hanging from the underside of the canopy skin, there are colonies. Clustered bodies, dozens strong, packed into the damp hollows where the canopy mosses are thickest. Warm-blooded, furred—you can feel the faint collective heat of them when you stand beneath a cluster, a barely perceptible warmth falling from the ceiling like a rumor. They are sleeping now, or doing whatever passes for sleep in a world without true day or night. When they wake, they unclamp from the canopy, spread membranes that are more leaf than wing—thin, veined, photosynthetic? or is that something growing *on* them—and they drop into the hollow's airspace. Dozens of them. The air fills with soft rustling. They navigate by sound, by temperature gradient, by some sense you don't have a name for. They eat the silk-tunnel creatures. They eat the fruiting bodies of fungi that emerge from the root walls in slow, overnight eruptions. They eat each other's dead. Their droppings fall to the sphagnum floor and are incorporated within hours by the root system and the decomposer fauna beneath.

Then there are the ones in the basin.

The water in the stone-lined basin is not still. It moves. Something lives in it, or several somethings, coiling slowly in the shallow dark water, their bodies bioluminescent in a wavelength just below what your eyes can reliably catch—you see it, then you don't, a green-blue flicker at the absolute threshold of perception. They are long and narrow. They breathe air, surfacing in slow rolls that break the water with a sound like a whispered syllable. They seem to serve a purpose. The basin water, unlike the walls, is clear. Whatever waste the colony produces, whatever organic matter accumulates, the basin creatures process it. They are a living filter. And whoever built the basin knew this, because the stones are fitted to give the creatures shelter underneath—gaps, crevices, a constructed habitat integrated into the water collection system.

The entire hollow is an ecology in miniature, layered and cycling and self-sustaining, and the human constructions within it are not set *against* this ecology but woven *into* it—using the fauna, accommodating the fauna, drawing the fauna closer. The antler pegs in the walls are pocked with small holes, and silk-tunnel creatures move freely through them, aerating the structures, preventing rot. The sphagnum bedding in the alcoves is alive with mites that keep it from compacting. The cured-hide bladder hanging from its peg has something small and luminous resting on it, casting its own faint, impossible glow across the surface.

---

Who lives here? Someone who knows cold. Someone whose hands are calloused from shaping wood with stone, from splitting roots, from fitting unshaped rocks together by feel. Someone who navigates entirely by sound and touch and the slow warmth of living things. Someone who has—and this is the strange part, the part that nags—*chosen* this. The lowland above is open. There is space, sky, however grey. But someone came down into this dimple in the earth, this fresh young wound in the ground, and began to grow the walls inward. Began to court the dark.

Maybe the canopy creatures told them something. Maybe the basin creatures did. There is a relationship here that exceeds utility—the way the alcoves are positioned to face the hanging colonies, the way the basin sits at the center like an altar or a commons. The bladder on the peg contains liquid, and when you tilt it, the luminous thing on its surface shifts and pulses brighter, and from the hanging colonies above there is a response—a collective rustling, a slight increase in the heat falling from the ceiling, as though the creatures have noticed, as though the glow means something to them.

You are in someone's home. You are in the dark, in the cold, in the damp, in the overwhelming company of creatures who outnumber you by thousands, and someone has arranged all of this into a kind of life. The pegs hold provisions. The root-walls hold warmth, barely. The basin holds water, cleaned by something alive. And when whoever lives here returns—down through one of the alcove passages, or through the root-doorway in the largest recess, or lowered through a canopy puncture on a braided-root rope—they will know the state of things by listening. By feeling the air. By the pattern of the bioluminescent flicker on the water and the warmth from the colonies above.

It is dark. It is quiet. It is alive in a way that makes the word *alive* feel thin.

Something moves in the passage behind the root-door. A sound like wet cloth sliding over stone. Then nothing.

You hold still. You wait. The silk-tunnel creatures flow over your hand. The salamander strikes again. The colony above rustles and settles. A drop of water falls from the canopy skin and strikes the basin, and for one instant the creatures in the water flare bright enough to see by—the walls, the roots, the pale silk, the thousand small moving bodies, all of it illuminated in blue-green, then gone.

The dark returns. The dark is the medium. Everything here lives in it.
