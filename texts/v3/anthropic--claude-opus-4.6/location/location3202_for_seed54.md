# The Drowning Tiers

---

The first thing is the sound. Not sudden — it has always been here, older than anything that listens. A deep, pervasive roar like the inside of a mountain's throat, layered with clicks and keening and a constant wet static that presses against the bones of your skull. It takes minutes to separate it into sources. You cannot, in fact, fully separate it. The sound is the place.

---

You stand on an upper terrace of a city built into the shoulder of a massif that has no name you can pronounce, because nothing here speaks in any human way. The elevation is considerable — high enough that the air should be thin, but the atmosphere is strange, denser than it should be, soupy with moisture that won't quite become rain. It clings. It beads on every surface in fat, trembling droplets that catch no light because there is almost no light to catch. The sky is a sealed vault, black and low, not clouds exactly but something more structural — a ceiling-membrane stretched across the world, featureless, emitting nothing. What little illumination exists seems to come from below, from the city itself, from trace phosphorescences in the wet stone and faint thermal signatures bleeding through surfaces in spectra you can almost but not quite see. Your eyes adjust. They keep adjusting. They never fully arrive.

The cold is absolute and patient. Not the biting cold of wind — there is no wind — but the slow, marrow-deep cold of wet stone in permanent darkness, the cold of deep caves and ocean trenches. It settles into you by degrees. The moisture on your skin doesn't evaporate. It accumulates.

---

The city — if it is a city — extends across and down the mountainside in a series of broad, irregular terraces connected by ramps and channels and sheer drops. It is dense, astonishingly so, structures packed tight against one another with only narrow seams between them, and those seams run with water. Water is everywhere. It weeps from the rock face above, it sheets across the terrace floors in thin, creeping films, it pools in basins that may be intentional or may be erosion, it fills channels that are definitely engineered — precise troughs cut into the dark stone, spiraling down through the tiers in logarithmic paths, splitting and rejoining, carrying water down to lower levels where it vanishes into grated openings that hum with resonance. The water is not clean. It is mineral-heavy, slightly viscous, leaving pale deposits on everything it touches — thin crusts of something crystalline and faintly luminescent, a blue so deep it's almost not a color.

The structures themselves are made of the mountain. Not built *on* the mountain — made *of* it, carved and shaped and extruded from the native basalt in ways that don't correspond to any excavation technique you understand. The walls have a grown quality, a pulled quality, as if the rock were convinced to become architecture through some process more intimate than cutting. Edges are precise but not sharp. Surfaces are smooth but not flat — they undulate subtly, like the interior of an organ. There are no right angles. The geometry is all tangent curves and nested arcs, structures fitting against one another like the chambers of a nautilus, and the whole mass of it is still *becoming*. In places you can see where new structures are emerging from the rock face — half-formed chambers still fused to the cliff, their interiors not yet hollowed, their walls still thickening, growing outward at a rate measurable only across weeks or months. The city is not finished. The city may never be finished. It is constructing itself with the patience of geology, and whatever is directing that construction is not in a hurry.

No vegetation. None. Not a lichen, not a film of algae on the wet stone, not a spore. The mineral world here is total. The pale deposits in the water channels are inorganic. The faint glow is chemiluminescent, catalyzed by trace metals in the groundwater reacting with the basalt. The terraces are barren of anything green or growing or photosynthetic, and this makes the density of *fauna* all the more staggering.

---

They are everywhere.

The first ones you notice are the large ones — because they are loud. Creatures the size of cattle, maybe larger, hunched and slow-moving on the middle terraces, their bodies low-slung and heavily armored in overlapping plates of something that looks like the same dark stone as the city. They are not stone. They are warm. You can feel it from here — faint thermals rising off their backs in the freezing air, the only warmth in this place besides whatever hums inside the walls. They move in groups of eight or twelve, following the water channels downslope, and the sound they produce is extraordinary: a continuous low thrumming from deep in their barrel chests, layered with sharp percussive clicks from their forelimbs striking the terrace floor. Each group's rhythm is distinct. Where two groups pass near each other, their rhythms interact, syncopate, briefly merge before diverging. The collective sound of dozens of these groups, spread across the visible terraces, produces the deep roar you heard first — a city-wide polyrhythm, structured and enormous.

But the large ones are not the majority. The majority are smaller, and they are *packed in*. Every surface that isn't actively flowing with water supports clusters and colonies of creatures ranging from fist-sized to barely visible. They cling to walls in dense mats — flattened, multi-limbed things with translucent carapaces through which you can see pale organs pulsing. They fill the narrow seams between structures in stacked columns, hundreds deep, their bodies interlocking like living masonry. They carpet the floors of the chambers you can see into — the open-faced rooms and galleries that honeycomb the lower tiers — moving over and under one another in slow, purposeful currents. Some are smooth and dark. Some bristle with sensory filaments that wave in the moisture-thick air, tasting it. Some glow faintly, the same deep blue as the mineral deposits, and you realize with a crawling certainty that the chemiluminescence you attributed to the water chemistry may be partly *them* — organisms so small and so numerous that their collective bioluminescence reads as a property of the environment.

There are flying things too, or rather gliding things — membranous, flat-bodied creatures that launch from the upper terraces and descend in wide spirals through the dark air, catching the thermals off the armored beasts below, their wings making a high, papery whisper that cuts through the deeper sounds. They land on the wet walls and fold themselves flat and become almost invisible, just another layer of life pressed against stone.

And in the water channels themselves: long, sinuous, segmented things, moving against the current with undulating contractions, their bodies barely wider than the channels that carry them, sometimes dozens visible in a single trough, packed head-to-tail, moving upslope with a collective determination that suggests migration or feeding or something with no earthly analogue.

None of these creatures seem to eat plants, because there are no plants. Some eat each other — you see the evidence, the slow engulfment of a smaller creature by a larger one on a wall nearby, unhurried, almost courteous. Some seem to feed on the mineral deposits themselves, scraping the luminescent crusts with specialized mouthparts, metabolizing the stone's chemistry directly. Some may feed on the water. Some may feed on the heat. The ecosystem is closed and lightless and entirely animal, or entirely *something*, and it is so dense that the city feels less like a settlement and more like a reef — a structure built by and for the organisms that saturate it.

---

Which raises the question: who built this?

The technology is evident in the water management system — those precisely calculated channels, the grated drains that regulate flow, the spiraling paths that seem designed to maximize mineral deposition along their lengths. It is evident in the architecture's organic geometry, which is too regular to be natural and too strange to be human. It is evident in the way new structures emerge from the rock face — not carved, not printed, but *induced*, as if some field or signal persuades the basalt to restructure itself at the molecular level, crystalline lattices realigning over months into load-bearing walls and vaulted ceilings and the nested chambers that house the teeming life.

You suspect the large armored creatures. Their rhythmic communication is too complex for simple herd behavior. The way they follow the water channels is too purposeful — they aren't just drinking, they're *patrolling*, and when one group pauses at a section of channel where the flow has shifted, they cluster around the disruption and their clicking intensifies and, over the course of hours, the channel corrects itself. The stone moves. Slowly, imperceptibly, but it moves. They are not builders in any way you'd recognize — they carry no tools, manipulate nothing with their limbs — but their presence, their sound, their heat, their rhythmic percussion against the terrace floor, seems to be the mechanism by which the city grows. They are the technology. Their bodies are the instruments. The engineering that shapes this place operates through vibration and resonance and thermal gradients and processes that sit just beyond the edge of what physics as you know it can comfortably describe.

The smaller creatures may be symbiotes, or parasites, or citizens, or building materials, or all of these. The boundaries between infrastructure and ecology are not boundaries here. The city is its inhabitants. The inhabitants are the city. The water is the circulatory system. The sound is the organizing principle.

---

You stand on the upper terrace in the dark and the cold and the noise, and you feel the moisture collecting on your skin, and you watch the slow emergence of a new wall from the cliff face twenty meters to your left — a process so gradual it looks like stillness, but isn't. Below you, the tiers descend into deeper darkness, denser populations, louder sound, and the faint blue glow intensifies in the lower levels until the deepest visible terrace shimmers like something alive, which it is, all of it, every surface, every sound, the whole mountain humming with the work of becoming what it is not yet, slowly, without urgency, in the permanent night of a world that has never seen the sun, or has forgotten it so completely that even the memory has crystallized and been eaten by something small and luminous and patient in the dark.
