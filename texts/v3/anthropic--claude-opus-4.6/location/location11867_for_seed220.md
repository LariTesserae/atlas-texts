# The Underhall of Vask

---

You descend through a bore-shaft barely wide enough for shoulders, and then it opens—not gradually, but all at once, like stepping through a membrane into a held breath. The space below is a single vast room, perhaps three hundred meters across, perhaps forty meters high, and almost completely dark. But it is not empty of meaning. It is empty of *mass*.

The chamber is a geological cavity—old, old, the kind of void that limestone karst dreams about over millions of years—but it has been *worked*. Not filled. Worked. The walls have been carved back and reinforced with a material that resembles black glass but behaves like something else entirely: it absorbs sound at certain frequencies and reflects others, so the acoustic space of the room is profoundly strange, selective, alive. You can hear your own breathing amplified into a slow tide. You cannot hear your footsteps at all.

What fills this underhall is infrastructure. Not buildings—not exactly—but the *skeleton* of a city turned inside out and pressed flat against the walls and ceiling of the cavern, leaving the interior volume almost entirely hollow. Walkways, conduit bundles, junction housings, thermal exchangers, data-runs that pulse with a light too dim to illuminate anything but themselves—all of it adhered to the rock surfaces like the circulatory system of some vast organism pinned open for study. The engineering is dense at the boundaries and absent at the center. You stand in the middle of the room and look up, and it is like being inside a ribcage, if the ribs were pipework and signal-carrying filament and structural members made of alloys that your hand tells you are warm—warmer than the air, warmer than blood—though you can see no source of heat.

The air itself is warm and close. Not humid, exactly—but *intermittently damp*. Every few minutes, something shifts in the thermal regulation of the space, and condensation blooms across the nearest surfaces in a rapid, sweating pulse. It beads on the black glass walls. It collects in hairline channels etched into the floor—channels too precise to be natural, too organic in their branching pattern to look engineered, as though the architects had studied river deltas and then miniaturized them. The water runs toward collection points along the perimeter: small reservoirs, each no larger than a cupped hand, that glow faintly with bioluminescent culture—green-blue, the only light that isn't purely infrastructural. These tiny pools are the brightest things in the room, and they are barely bright enough to see by.

So you navigate by sound.

And there is *so much sound*.

The underhall roars. Not chaotically—it roars the way a cathedral organ roars when someone leans on every key in the bass register. A low, pervasive, structural hum rises from the conduit networks along the walls: the sound of energy being moved through systems whose principles you cannot fully identify. It is not electrical hum. It is not mechanical vibration. It is something between the two, a drone that shifts in pitch as processes cycle through phases you can feel in your sternum but not describe. Layered over this is a rhythmic percussive pulse—maybe once every four seconds—that seems to come from below the floor, from deeper cavities you can't see, as though the facility continues downward and what you're standing in is merely its attic. And threading through all of it: a high, intermittent singing, almost tonal, almost linguistic, that comes from the data-runs themselves as information moves through them. The filaments resonate. They were *designed* to resonate. Someone built this place to be heard.

The floor is worn smooth. Not polished—*worn*. Thousands of paths have been walked across it, and the stone remembers. The most-trafficked routes are visible as shallow depressions, lighter in color where the dark glass coating has been abraded down to the pale calcium carbonate beneath. But no one is walking them now. The space is almost uninhabited. You can see, far across the chamber, a single figure seated on a low bench near one of the bioluminescent pools—motionless, possibly asleep, possibly interfacing with something you can't perceive. They are too far away to make out details. Their silhouette is human-shaped but *approximate*, as though they are wearing equipment that softens their outline, or as though the darkness is being kind to them.

The vegetation is sparse and deliberate. Not decorative—*functional*. Along the upper walls, where condensation is most persistent, patches of engineered moss cling in neat colonies: dark, almost black, photosynthesis abandoned in favor of chemosynthesis, feeding on trace compounds in the moisture that sweats from the thermal exchangers. The moss is tended. You can see where it has been trimmed back from critical junctions, where its growth has been guided along specific paths using tiny wire frames. It serves a purpose you can partially guess at—atmospheric regulation, maybe, or filtration—but it also seems to be *tolerated* in a way that suggests the inhabitants of this place find it companionable. Here and there a colony has been allowed to grow into a rough, asymmetric shape that might be ornamental. Might be accidental. The line between intention and emergence seems thin down here.

Once—just once, in the twenty minutes you stand listening—something moves along the upper conduit network. It is small, maybe the size of a rat, and it moves with the fluid, stop-start gait of something that has adapted perfectly to navigating pipework. You catch a glimpse of segmented limbs, a body that reflects the dim filament-light with an oily sheen. It could be biological. It could be mechanical. It could be a maintenance drone that has been running its loops so long it has taken on the behavioral character of an animal—pausing at junctions, seeming to *consider*, choosing a path, accelerating away. It makes a sound as it moves: a rapid clicking, like a geiger counter, like claws on glass. The sound bounces wildly through the selective acoustics of the chamber, arriving at your ears from three directions at once before fading.

The technology here is not alien, but it is *beyond*. The thermal exchangers along the north wall operate without visible moving parts; heat simply migrates through them in ways that suggest engineered thermodynamic gradients at a material level, as though the atomic structure of the exchanger housings has been tuned to conduct energy in one direction only. The data filaments carry information as resonance, not electricity—physical vibration in substrates that have been fabricated at scales you cannot inspect with the naked eye. The black glass reinforcement of the walls may be structural, or it may be computational; you press your palm against it and feel warmth, feel the faintest tremor, and have the unsettling sense that the wall is *doing something* with your presence, integrating the thermal signature of your hand into some process. Nothing responds. Nothing changes visibly. But the hum shifts, just slightly, for a moment, before settling back.

The erosion is everywhere and it is at peace with itself. This place was built long ago—how long, you can't say, but the wear patterns suggest generations, possibly centuries, of continuous low-level habitation. The floor depressions are deep enough to pool condensation during the damp cycles. The conduit housings show micro-fractures that have been repaired and re-repaired in layers, each repair slightly different in technique, as though the maintenance knowledge has evolved over time, or as though different hands with different training have attended to the same junction across decades. Some of the bioluminescent pools have calcite rims, mineral deposits built up drop by drop from the moisture that feeds them. The place is not decaying. It is *settled*. It has found its form. The systems that run through its walls have been running long enough to have worn grooves in the very physics of the space—the acoustics, the thermal patterns, the paths of condensation all shaped by infrastructure that shapes itself by running.

And the darkness. The darkness is not oppressive but *chosen*. There are no lighting fixtures anywhere in the chamber. The design assumes either that inhabitants can see by other means, or that sight is not the primary sense employed here. The bioluminescent pools, the faint pulse of the data filaments, the rare green glow of a status indicator on a junction box—these are concessions, landmarks, the equivalent of stars in an otherwise black sky. You orient by the hum. By the rhythm of the percussive pulse from below. By the way the condensation cycles announce themselves with a faint thermal shift you learn to feel on your skin. After ten minutes in the underhall, you realize you have stopped trying to see and started trying to *listen to the shape of the room*, and that this is what the room was built for.

The lone figure across the chamber has not moved. The clicking creature in the conduit network has not returned. Below you, the deeper systems pulse and pulse and pulse, patient as geology, warm as blood, loud as a world that has learned to speak entirely in sound.
