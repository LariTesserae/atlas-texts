# The Nursery Floor

---

You stand on metal. Everything is metal, or something that behaves like metal — alloy grating, riveted panels, welded seams that run to every horizon. The world is a floor. That's the first thing you understand: this is a floor, and it was built, and it extends in every direction farther than weather can reach. There are no walls. There is no ceiling you can see. But the floor — the floor is everything.

It is low. Not flat, exactly, but low — a vast undulating plain of constructed surface that dips and crests in gentle, purposeful grades, like the floor of a warehouse designed by someone who thought in continental terms. The depressions collect water. The rises shed it. This was planned. Every drainage channel, every perforated grate, every weeping joint where condensation beads on the underside of an I-beam and drips, slowly, into a catch basin below — it was all designed, fabricated, installed. The water is warm. It has no source you can find. It simply appears: condensing from a humid atmosphere onto cooler metal surfaces, pooling in the low places, filling the troughs and runnels that crosshatch the floor in a grid so vast its geometry curves with the horizon.

The air is thick and warm and close, like the inside of an engine room, but there is no engine. There is no hum of machinery. That's the second thing you notice: the quiet. For something so enormous and so thoroughly made, there should be noise — the drone of ventilation, the clank of moving parts, the subsonic throb of whatever powers all this. But there's almost nothing. A faint ticking, thermal expansion maybe, metal adjusting itself by fractions of a millimeter. The occasional soft plip of a water droplet finding its pool. The sound your boots make on the grating. That's close to all.

The light is steady and warm, amber-industrial, the color of sodium lamps but without visible sources. It simply exists in the air, as though the atmosphere itself has been engineered to luminesce. It casts no sharp shadows. Everything is legible, everything is clear, and nothing is illuminated from any particular direction. The light has always been this way. You can feel it — not as knowledge, but as bone-deep certainty. This light has never changed. This temperature has never shifted. The water has always been precisely this warm, this present, this carefully distributed. The Nursery Floor is in equilibrium, and it has been in equilibrium for longer than you have a frame of reference to comprehend.

---

Now. The animals.

They are *everywhere.*

That's not hyperbole. Every surface that isn't actively streaming with water hosts something alive. They cling to the undersides of grate panels. They nest in the junction boxes. They crowd the drainage channels in shimmering, mucilaginous clusters. They perch on the railing-like ridges that run along the tops of the larger structural members, packed so densely that from a distance the railings look furred, and they are — furred with bodies, with legs, with the slow synchronized breathing of hundreds of organisms pressed flank to flank.

None of them are plants. None of them photosynthesize (what would they photosynthesize with? The light is not sunlight). They are animals — or the word "animal" is the closest you have. They move. They consume. They breathe. They have interiors that are different from their exteriors. But they are strange, in the way that things are strange when they've been shaped by a world with rules you haven't fully learned yet.

The most common — the ones you can't avoid stepping around, and eventually can't avoid stepping on, which they seem not to mind — are the **mat creatures**. They are flat, flexible, roughly circular, and they tile the floor like biological coins, each about the diameter of your palm. They are warm to the touch, warmer than the metal, and their upper surfaces are textured with spiraling ridges that channel water across their bodies and off their edges. They are a dull coppery brown, and when you look closely you can see a slow peristaltic ripple moving through their flesh, a wave that passes from one edge to the other over the course of about thirty seconds. They feed on something in the water — or something in the metal — or something you can't detect. They do not move from place to place. They simply are where they are, tiling the surface, breathing faintly, processing whatever it is they process. There are billions of them. There might be trillions.

Above the mat creatures, in the shallow warm pools that gather in the depressions, live things you can only call **eel-lace**. They are translucent, threaded with fine dark lines that might be circulatory or might be structural, and they weave through the water in loose braids, dozens of individuals tangled loosely together, never quite touching, never quite separating. They have no visible eyes, no visible mouths. They taper at both ends. They move with a languid, spiraling motion that stirs the water just enough to keep it from going stagnant, and you wonder if this is their function — if they were designed for this, if they are part of the system the way the drainage channels are part of the system. The thought sits uneasily.

In the dry spaces — the higher ridges, the structural members, the bolted flanges where no water collects — there are **clingers**. These are the ones that make the railings look furred. They are small, about the size of your thumb, and shaped like elongated teardrops with six flexible gripping limbs arranged radially around their bases. Their bodies are a dark gunmetal gray, almost indistinguishable from the metal they cling to, and they cluster in packed formations with their blunt heads all oriented in the same direction, though there's no wind, no current, no obvious reason for the alignment. When you get close, you can hear them — not individually, but as a collective: a faint, high, papery sound, like static, like the white noise of a thousand tiny metabolisms happening in concert. This is the sound of the Nursery Floor, you realize. This is its ambient noise. Not machinery. Life.

And there are larger things. You see them in the middle distance, moving slowly along the floor — **grazers**, if that's the right word, though they graze on mat creatures or on something the mat creatures produce. They are bulky, low-slung, roughly the size and shape of overturned bathtubs, and they progress across the metal surface on wide, flat pseudopods that grip the grating with a sound like wet rubber on glass. Their bodies are segmented, armored in plates that look disturbingly like the alloy paneling of the floor itself — the same color, the same faint brushed texture. Whether they grew this way or incorporated the metal into themselves, you can't tell. They leave clean trails behind them, paths where the mat creatures have been scraped away or consumed, but even as you watch, the mat creatures at the trail's edges are already budding, dividing, filling the gap. The grazers do not hurry. Nothing hurries here.

High up — high being relative, maybe twenty meters above the floor where a structural truss arcs over a wide drainage basin — there are **drifters**. They are pale, almost white, and they hang in the warm air on membranes so thin they're visible only as a faint iridescence, like soap film. They are enormous. The largest you can see has a wingspan — membrane-span — of maybe fifteen meters, and it barely moves, riding some thermal gradient you can't feel from down here. Others are smaller, clustered in loose formations, and occasionally one will fold its membrane and drop a meter or two before catching the air again. They never land. You never see one on the floor. You wonder what happens when they die. You wonder if they die.

There are others. There are always others. Something dark and multi-legged picks its way carefully along the inside of a drainage channel, half-submerged, its carapace beaded with warm water. Something small and quick — the only quick thing you've seen — darts between two junction boxes and is gone before you can focus on it. Something that might be colonial, a linked chain of identical nodules draped over a pipe like a living garland, pulses with a rhythm that has nothing to do with the thermal ticking of the metal.

The density is oppressive and wondrous. You cannot look at any surface, any volume of air, any body of water, and see emptiness. Life has saturated this place. It has filled every niche the constructed environment provides, and the constructed environment provides *many* niches — the Nursery Floor is all edges, all surfaces, all joints and crevices and thermal gradients and moisture differentials. It is an infinity of microhabitats, and every one is occupied.

---

And none of it is changing.

That's the thing that settles over you slowly, the thing that transforms wonder into something more like awe, or more like vertigo. You watch the grazers for a long time, and they move, yes, but they move in the same paths. The mat creatures they consume are replaced at exactly the rate they're consumed. The eel-lace weave in the pools, but the pools don't grow or shrink. The drifters hang in the air, and the air doesn't shift. The water condenses, collects, and — you have to assume — evaporates somewhere, in some warm recess of the structure, at exactly the rate it condenses. The ticking of the metal is metronomic. The papery hush of the clingers doesn't swell or fade.

This isn't a world in stasis. It's a world in *perfect equilibrium*. Every birth is matched by a death. Every drop of water that falls is returned. Every calorie consumed is replaced by — what? Something in the metal? Something in the water? Something the floor itself provides, through mechanisms sealed beneath the grating, designed and installed and calibrated and then left, left to run, left to sustain this exact state forever?

You walk for hours and the landscape changes — wider basins, narrower ridges, a place where the structural members rise in dense clusters like an industrial forest, every surface packed with clingers, the sound of them here almost a whisper, almost a voice — but it doesn't *change*. The temperature is the same. The light is the same. The density of life per square meter is the same. You are crossing a continent-sized constructed surface, and every part of it is performing the same function with the same fidelity. The Nursery Floor is one environment, one system, one machine, and the animals are part of the machine, and the water is part of the machine, and you are walking through it and it does not notice you and it does not care, because you are a variable it has already accounted for, or you are too small to perturb it, or perturbation is simply not something that happens here.

The fresh edges of the metal — the sharp seams, the recently-cast bolts, the paneling with its crisp factory finish — tell you this is young. Built recently, or built to stay new, or built from a material that doesn't age. The structure has not had time to weather. The animals have had time to fill it, utterly and completely, but the metal is still sharp, still bright where the water hasn't dulled it, still bearing the marks of fabrication. This world was made, and then it was populated, and then it was *balanced*, and then it stopped. Not frozen — running. Running perfectly. A clock that will never need winding because it was wound exactly right the first time.

You find yourself lowering your voice when you speak aloud, though no one is listening. It seems wrong to be loud here. The Nursery Floor has its own sound — the ticking, the static-hush, the water — and your voice doesn't belong in it. You are a guest in a system that has no guests, in a place that was built for the things that live in it, and for nothing else, and for no other purpose than to *be what it is, exactly as it is, without end.*

You keep walking. The floor extends. The animals breathe. The water pools and weeps and pools again. Nothing changes. Nothing has ever changed. Nothing will.
