# The Cistern

---

It is the size of a closet. Maybe smaller. The kind of space you find behind a wall panel nobody was supposed to remove, or beneath a hatch in a floor that doesn't appear on any blueprint. You have to crouch to fit inside, and even then your shoulders brush the walls on two sides. The ceiling is close enough to feel against the top of your skull if you straighten at all.

Everything here was made. There is no natural surface. The walls are a dense composite — some ceramic-metal hybrid whose grain is too fine to see, warm to the touch, warmer than it should be, as though the material itself is metabolizing something. The warmth is steady and pervasive, the kind that makes you aware of your own skin, that softens the border between body and environment. The floor is the same material but textured differently, slightly concave, with a shallow depression at its center where water has gathered — not much, maybe two inches deep, maybe three, a dark little pool the color of cooling tea. The water is warm too. It condenses on every surface. The walls weep. Slow beads form along hairline seams you can barely see, tracking downward in paths that have been followed so many times they've left faint mineral traces, pale veins in the dark material, a record of ten thousand identical journeys from ceiling to floor.

The light is dim but not absent. It comes from within the walls — not a glow exactly, more like a luminous potential, as though the composite remembers being bright and hasn't entirely forgotten. It's enough to see shapes. Enough to see the water. Not enough to read by, not enough to dispel the feeling that this space exists in permanent dusk, a twilight pocket embedded somewhere deep inside a larger structure you can only infer.

---

And the sound.

The sound is immense. That's the first thing — the thing that makes this closet-sized space feel impossible, because no room this small should be able to contain this much noise. It roars. A deep, complex, layered roaring that comes from everywhere at once, transmitted through the walls, through the floor, through the warm water, through the bones of your jaw and the fluid in your ears. It's not painful in the way a siren is painful. It's painful the way a waterfall is painful when you stand behind it — the sheer pressure of sound as a physical medium, sound as a substance filling the room to capacity and then overfilling it. You feel it in your chest. Your teeth hum.

The roar is not uniform. It has textures. There are bass throbs so low they are more felt than heard — structural vibrations from whatever vast systems operate in the walls beyond this pocket. There are mid-frequency surges that come in waves, cycling every few seconds, like the breath of something colossal and mechanical. And threaded through it all, a higher-register sound, almost biological, a chorus of overlapping clicks and trills and whistling oscillations that seem to emanate from the water itself, or from just beneath it, or from somewhere in the layered spaces behind the walls where conduit meets conduit and fluid passes through channels no wider than a capillary.

You cannot hear yourself think. You can feel yourself think, but the thoughts arrive without their usual interior narration, stripped down to impulse and image. The sound is the atmosphere here. It is the weather.

---

Now: the fauna.

They are everywhere. The pool, small as it is, is dense with life — not the kind of life that evolved in sunlight and open water, but the kind that was designed for exactly this: for warm, dim, saturated pockets inside a structure that extends in every direction farther than you can know. They are small. The largest might be the length of your thumbnail. Semi-transparent bodies, segmented, moving through the shallow water with quick lateral undulations. Some cling to the walls just above the waterline, their pale limbs — six, eight, it's hard to count in this light — gripping the condensation trails. Others cluster at the seams where wall meets floor, their mouthparts working at something you can't quite see, some biofilm or mineral deposit or engineered nutrient substrate laid down in the composite itself.

They are not insects. They are not crustaceans. They are something designed to occupy the niche that this space provides — warm water, darkness, vibration, mineral-rich condensate. Their bodies have a faintly geometric quality, as though their morphology was constrained by a design language: the segments are too regular, the joints too precise, the way they move too coordinated to be fully wild. But they are alive. Unmistakably alive. They jostle each other at the feeding seams. They scatter when you shift your weight. Some of them produce the clicking sounds — you can see it now, a rapid flexion of a specialized plate along their ventral side, a tiny mechanical snap amplified by the water and the resonant walls into something that, multiplied by hundreds, becomes part of that vast layered roar.

Among them, smaller things. Motes. Nearly invisible except when they catch the wall-light at a certain angle and flash like suspended mica. They drift in the water column — such as it is, two inches of column — and sometimes land on the surface tension and skate there, propelled by movements too small to track. They are grazers, or filters, or something else entirely. They are abundant beyond counting. The water, looked at closely, is not still at all. It seethes with them. A whole trophic layer in a puddle.

And in the corners, where the concavity of the floor meets the walls at their tightest angles, you can see something else: pale threads, gossamer-fine, stretched between surfaces. They vibrate in the sound-field, trembling constantly, and along their lengths, tiny motile bodies traverse back and forth — harvesters of vibration, perhaps, or organisms that use the threads as highways between micro-territories. The threads are sticky. Condensation beads collect on them and are wicked along to junctions where small clusters of organisms gather and seem to drink.

There are predators too. You notice them because the smaller creatures avoid certain patches of wall with a consistency that implies knowledge. In those patches, something slightly larger sits motionless, its body pressed flat against the composite, nearly the same color as the mineral-vein staining. It waits. It has been waiting since before you arrived. When a wall-crawler strays too close, there is a movement almost too fast to register — a strike, a folding-in, and then stillness again, with one fewer crawler on the wall.

---

The erosion is not geological. Nothing here was shaped by wind or river. But the space has aged — profoundly, in its own way. The composite walls, whatever they were designed to be, have been altered by the slow chemistry of condensation, by the metabolic byproducts of generations of organisms, by the ceaseless vibration that works at seams and micro-fractures over timescales you can't estimate. The mineral veining is erosion. The faint roughening of the floor texture around the pool's edge is erosion. The way certain seams have widened just enough to admit the smallest organisms into the interstitial spaces beyond — that is erosion. The system is in balance. The organisms maintain the surfaces they inhabit; their feeding clears deposits that might otherwise clog the weeping seams, and their waste products contribute to the mineral patina that gives the walls their particular warmth-retaining quality. They belong here now. The space was not designed for them — or maybe it was, in some oblique, second-order way — but they have settled into it so thoroughly that the distinction between structure and ecology has dissolved.

---

The vegetation is almost nothing. But almost nothing is not nothing. In two places — one on the ceiling, one low on the eastern wall — there are small patches of something photosynthetic, if the word still applies when the light is this dim and the photons are coming from an engineered composite rather than a star. They are dark, almost black, with a faint iridescence when the wall-light catches them. Flat against the surface, growing in the mineral stain, spreading perhaps a millimeter per year, perhaps less. They feel ancient. They feel like they predate the animals, like they were the first colonists of this warm wet darkness, converting wall-light into sugar with a patience that borders on geological. The organisms graze their edges. The patches persist. They have been in negotiation with their grazers for a very long time.

---

You are crouching in this space. The warm water touches the soles of your shoes. The sound fills you completely — it has stopped being something you hear and has become something you inhabit, a medium as tangible as the humidity. The walls are close. The creatures are close. The light is not enough and is exactly enough. You are inside something vast and built and functioning, some structure or engine or habitat that extends outward and upward and downward beyond any horizon you can access from this pocket, and this is its hidden margin, its interstitial wetland, its unplanned wilderness thriving in the seams.

You could stay. It is warm. The roar, after a while, becomes a kind of silence — not because it diminishes, but because it saturates, because it becomes the baseline, the floor of perception, and everything else floats on top of it: the clicks, the water-drip, the faint high singing of the gossamer threads. The creatures go about their lives around you, over you, ignoring you now because you are still and warm and not a predator.

Somewhere far away — above, below, laterally, through walls and conduits and engineered substrates — whatever the larger structure does, it continues doing. The vibrations shift subtly. A new harmonic enters the roar, sustains for thirty seconds, fades. The condensation rate increases slightly, and the pool gains a fraction of a millimeter, and the motes at the surface adjust, spreading outward to the new shore. The flat predator on the wall captures another crawler. A bead of water completes its journey from ceiling to floor, following the mineral path laid down by all the beads before it.

The cistern breathes. The cistern hums. The cistern is small and full and old and alive.
