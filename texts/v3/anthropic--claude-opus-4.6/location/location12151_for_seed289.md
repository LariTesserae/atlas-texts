# The Singing Cyst

---

It is a cavity no larger than a cupped palm, lodged three inches beneath the surface of a limestone tableland that has barely begun to exist. The rock around it is young — not young in the way mountains are young, but young the way a tooth is young when it first pushes through the gum. The limestone is still deciding what it is. Its grain has not yet fully committed to crystalline structure. In places it feels likeite pressed chalk; in others, like calcium paste that dried too quickly and caught air bubbles in its eagerness to become solid.

And in one of those bubbles — this one — everything is happening at once, and has been happening at once, and will continue to happen at once, without change, forever.

---

The cavity is roughly ovoid, slightly flattened on its lower surface, approximately the size of a large walnut. Its walls are dense with mineral bloom — not stalactites, nothing so mature as that. These are mineral buds. Calcite rosettes no bigger than pinheads, packed so tightly against each other that they form a continuous crust, each crystal pushing against its neighbor. There is no negative space. Every surface is claimed. The walls are frosted with these formations so thoroughly that the original void-wall is invisible beneath them. If you could magnify them, you would see that each rosette is itself composed of bladelets finer than eyelashes, and between those bladelets, still smaller crystalline whiskers have colonized every gap. Density upon density. A fractal of occupation.

The light has no business being here, and yet.

The cavity blazes. Not with heat — the air inside is cool, a stable twelve degrees Celsius that has never fluctuated — but with a luminescence that radiates from the mineral crust itself. The calcite rosettes are bioluminescent, or rather, they contain something bioluminescent. Threaded through the mineral matrix, too small to see without magnification, are filaments of an organism that is neither bacterium nor fungus nor anything that has a name. They are chemolithotrophic — they eat the rock as it forms around them, metabolizing the slow precipitation of calcium carbonate, and they glow as they do it. They glow *brightly*. The interior of this walnut-sized void is lit like a tiny surgical theater, every surface a source, the light bouncing between facing walls so that shadows simply do not exist. If you could somehow peer inside — crack the cavity open, which would destroy it — you would flinch. It would be like looking at a miniature sun embedded in chalk.

The light is white with a faint lavender cast, and it does not flicker. It has never flickered. The organisms produce it at a constant rate because they consume the rock at a constant rate because the rock precipitates at a constant rate because the geochemistry of this particular pocket of barely-formed limestone has reached a perfect equilibrium between mineral deposition and biological consumption. The cyst is neither growing nor shrinking. It was made once, by accident, and then the accident stabilized, and now it simply *is*.

---

The moisture: a film. Not enough to pool, not enough to drip. A condensation skin on every surface, replenished by the slow exhalation of water vapor from the porous limestone matrix. The rosettes are damp. The air inside the cavity is at exactly 100% relative humidity. Fog would form if there were room for it, but there is no room. The space is too packed, too intimate. Instead the water just *coats*. Everything is faintly wet and has always been faintly wet.

In this moisture, in this light, the fauna:

They are mites. Or they are related to mites in the way that a prayer is related to a scream — same general category, different intensity. They are white. They are eyeless. They are approximately the size of a period at the end of a sentence, and there are hundreds of them. They move across the mineral rosettes in dense, overlapping processions that look, at proper magnification, like rush-hour foot traffic on a city bridge. They graze on the glowing filaments. They graze constantly. They have always been grazing. Their populations do not fluctuate. Their paths do not change. A mite born in this cavity will walk the same routes its ancestors walked, because the topology of the rosette-crust has not changed since the system reached equilibrium, and the organisms the mites eat regrow at exactly the rate they are consumed.

The mites have parasites: smaller creatures still, clinging to the joint-membranes of their legs. The parasites have symbionts. This, too, is how the density works — life packed into life packed into mineral packed into more life, all of it luminous, all of it still, all of it loud.

---

Because it is loud. Unbelievably loud, for what it is.

The mites stridulate. Each one possesses a ridged plate on its dorsal surface and a tiny scraper-limb, and as it walks, it sings. The sound is ultrasonic — far above human hearing — but if you had the right instruments, you would detect a cacophony so complex and layered it would resemble white noise. Hundreds of mites, all singing at once, their songs bouncing off the close calcite walls, reflecting, interfering, amplifying in the small resonant chamber of the void. The cavity is a bell, and everything inside it is a clapper. The sound pressure, scaled to the size of the space, is extraordinary. It is the acoustic equivalent of standing inside a cathedral organ. The rosettes vibrate microscopically. The condensation film trembles. The sound is part of the equilibrium: the vibrations prevent certain crystal faces from growing preferentially, keeping the rosettes uniform, keeping the geometry stable.

If the mites stopped singing, the rosettes would begin to elongate. The cavity would slowly reshape itself. The equilibrium would fail.

They have never stopped singing.

---

There is a human trace. Almost too small to notice, but it is here: on the surface of the tableland, directly above the cyst, someone — some hand, at some point — pressed a mark into the limestone when it was still paste-soft. A thumbprint. Partial, smudged, barely a centimeter across, now hardened into the surface of the rock. Whoever made it is long gone. They did not know what was beneath their thumb. They may not have been human in any way you would recognize; the limestone is so young that the timeline is strange, and the tableland exists in a geology that does not correspond to any you have studied.

The thumbprint is the only artifact. It has not weathered — erosion has barely touched this place. It is as crisp as the day it was made, and it will remain crisp, because nothing here changes.

---

Some vegetation has found the surface nearby. Sparse, cool-weather ground-hugging things: pale rosettes (an echo of the mineral rosettes below, though neither knows the other exists) of fleshy leaves pressed flat against the stone, drawing moisture from condensation in the pre-dawn hours. A few wiry stems with seed heads that set once and have never dropped their seeds. The seeds are viable. They simply have no reason to fall. There is no wind at this scale, in this place. The air above the tableland is still. The plants photosynthesize in the bright ambient light — the sky above is a featureless, luminous white, like an overexposed photograph, unchanging, sourceless — and they transpire, and the water they release eventually percolates down through the young limestone and joins the film of moisture inside the cyst.

The plants are rooted in a skin of calcium-ite soil no thicker than a coin. Below that, the rock. Below the rock, the cyst. Below the cyst, more rock, all the way down to wherever "down" ends in this place, which may not be far. The elevation is effectively zero. The surface of the tableland is the surface of the world, and the world is shallow, and most of what matters is just below.

---

The strangeness:

If you could measure the cavity at two different times — say, a million years apart — you would find it identical. Not similar. Not "well-preserved." *Identical*. The same mites in the same positions on the same rosettes, their legs frozen mid-stride in the same phase of their gait. The same condensation droplets on the same crystal faces. The same sound, the same frequency profile, the same amplitude. The system has not reached a steady state that *resembles* stasis. It has reached actual stasis. Thermodynamic, biological, geological: all gradients are zero. Energy enters and leaves the system at identical rates through pathways that have become self-reinforcing to the point of permanence.

This should be impossible. Entropy should degrade it. Mutation should drift the organisms. Mineral impurities should accumulate. But the cyst has found — or been found by — a fixed point in the mathematics of its own existence. It is a solved equation. It will remain solved.

The mites do not age. The filaments do not evolve. The calcite does not recrystallize. The thumbprint does not erode. The seeds do not fall. The light does not dim.

---

Listen. If you press your ear to the limestone surface, directly over the thumbprint, and if you are very still, and if the bones of your inner ear are tuned to registers that human bones are not usually tuned to — you will hear it. A hum. Not quite a sound, more like the memory of a sound, conducted through the stone. The aggregate voice of hundreds of mites stridulating in a closed, bright, wet, packed, unchanging, tiny, ancient, newborn room.

It sounds like something that has always been singing and will always be singing and does not know it is singing and would not stop if it did.
