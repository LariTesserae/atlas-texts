# The Hollow Inside the Living Engine

---

It is a room — no larger than a cathedral nave, though it has no walls in any traditional sense. The walls are wood. The ceiling is wood. The floor, where you can feel it underfoot, is a weave of root so ancient and so compressed that it has the density of stone, the texture of something geological, though it is alive, indisputably alive, still conducting sugar and water through capillaries older than most civilizations. Everything here is plant. Everything here has always been plant.

You are standing inside a tree.

Not inside a hollow trunk — not in a cavity left by rot. You are standing in a deliberate void, an emptiness held open by the architecture of growth itself, the way a ribcage holds open a space for the lungs. The tree — if it can be called a single tree, and there are reasons to doubt this — has grown around this room over millennia so deep they cease to be meaningful as numbers. It grew inward and then stopped. It left this. A pocket. A low-ceilinged emptiness perhaps thirty meters across, the ceiling bowed and vaulted in places where great horizontal limbs have thickened into beams, their bark long since smoothed by age into something almost metallic, almost ceramic, a dense grey-green surface that reflects the light with a faint sheen.

The light enters from above through gaps in the canopy — not holes exactly, but places where leaves overlap less densely, where the chlorophyll-heavy mass of the crown thins just enough to let columns of diffuse, cool daylight angle down through seams in the ceiling-wood. These columns shift slightly with the movement of the upper canopy in winds you cannot feel down here, but they are stable enough — they have been stable for so long — that the patches of illumination on the root-floor have developed their own micro-ecologies: pale mosses, liverworts with fractal edges, tiny ferns that uncurl in the perpetual grey-gold light and never grow taller than a finger joint.

The air is cool. Not cold — there is no bite, no sharpness — but cool the way the inside of a stone jar is cool, the way a cellar is cool: a temperature that has found its equilibrium and will not leave it. There is moisture here, but it is atmospheric, a faint condensation that beads on the inner bark and occasionally, on no particular schedule, releases a single drop that falls with a resonant tap onto the root-floor. The whole room is damp the way skin is damp after a fog walk. Nothing drips steadily. Nothing pools. The root-floor absorbs what it receives. There are small pockets — depressions where roots have braided around each other — that hold a few milliliters of dark water, tannin-stained, cold, still. These tiny pools are ancient. They have existed in exactly this form for longer than you can usefully imagine.

---

The sound is enormous.

This is the thing that makes no sense until you have been here for a full minute and your mind begins to separate the layers. The sound is enormous and it comes from everywhere and it is alive.

Beetles. Thousands — tens of thousands — of beetles, borers, bark-dwellers, cambium-feeders, sap-drinkers, fungus-farmers, each one producing its own small industry of noise. The chewing is a constant, a white-noise wall of mandibles working through wood, and it comes from inside the walls, from inside the ceiling, from inside the floor beneath your feet. You are standing inside a living structure that is being eaten and regrowing simultaneously, and the sound of this process is a roar, a soft roar, a roar made of a hundred thousand tiny violences, each one no louder than a fingernail dragged across paper, but together they fill this hollow space with a vibrating, ceaseless hum that you feel in your teeth, in your sternum, in the bones of your feet where they press against the root-floor.

This is the engine. This is the industrial thing you sense but cannot see.

The beetles are not destroying the tree. The tree is not fighting the beetles. They have been locked in this arrangement for so long that the distinction between parasite and host has dissolved. The beetles chew channels through the wood that become vascular pathways. Their frass — their sawdust waste — is colonized by fungi that the tree has co-opted for nutrient exchange. The beetles' larvae pupate in chambers that the tree walls off with resin, and when the adults emerge, the resin-sealed chambers become air pockets that regulate the tree's internal temperature. It is a factory. It is a closed-loop production system operating at the scale of tens of thousands of individual workers, and it has been running without interruption, without change, without innovation or failure, for an amount of time that has flattened all the urgency out of it. It does not accelerate. It does not slow down. It runs.

The beetles themselves are visible if you look. Small black shapes moving in deliberate lines along the inner bark, disappearing into bore-holes the diameter of pencil lead, emerging elsewhere. Some of them are enormous by beetle standards — thumb-sized, heavy-bodied, their elytra patterned with ridges that interlock with the bark grooves so precisely that when they stop moving they become invisible, just another texture in the wall. Others are minute, dust-mote-sized, visible only as movement, as a shimmer across the surface of the wood when the light catches them at the right angle.

There are other animals. A bird — something small, drab, round-bodied — hops along a root-beam near the ceiling, tilting its head to listen for larvae beneath the bark, then strikes with a bill like a sliver of dark glass. It does this with the mechanical regularity of a piston. Strike, pause, swallow, hop, tilt, strike. It has been doing this for its entire life. Its parents did it here. Their parents did it here. The bird is as much a component of this system as the beetles are.

In one of the darker recesses where the root-floor humps upward and the ceiling dips down to create a crawl-space barely large enough for a fox — there is a fox. Or what functions as a fox: a small, russet, sharp-nosed mammal curled in a depression between roots, sleeping. Its fur is damp with condensation. Its breathing is slow and even. It smells like wet wood and musk and the faint sweetness of beetle frass. It hunts the beetles, or rather it hunts the beetle larvae, digging them out of the softer wood with claws adapted for the purpose, and in doing so it aerates channels that the tree uses, and its scat deposits nitrogen in places the root-floor can access, and it is part of the engine, it is part of the machine, every living thing in this room is part of the machine.

---

There is no wind down here. The air does not move except in the faintest convective currents caused by the difference between the cool floor and the slightly warmer ceiling, and these currents are so gentle they cannot stir the smallest cobweb — if there were cobwebs, which there are not, because there are no spiders here, only beetles and birds and the fox-thing and the tree. The stillness of the air contributes to the sense of permanence. Nothing is arriving. Nothing is leaving. The system closed itself a long time ago.

The erosion is not geological here. It is biological. The tree has been worn into its final form by time and by the endless, patient work of its own inhabitants. The bark surfaces are smooth not because water carved them but because ten thousand generations of beetle feet have polished them. The root-floor is flat not because sediment settled but because the roots grew to fill every gap and then compressed under their own weight over centuries until they became this dense, dark, almost mineral platform. The few places where the inner wood is exposed — where a great limb cracked and healed a millennium ago — show growth rings so tightly packed they are almost invisible, a record of slow, steady, unvarying growth in conditions that have not meaningfully changed since the rings began to form.

You could come back in a hundred years and find this room identical. The same columns of light. The same cool damp. The same vast white-noise roar of mandibles in wood. The same bird — not the same individual, but the same species, the same behavior, the same mechanical strike-pause-swallow performed at the same rate in the same location. The fox would be curled in the same depression. The tiny pools between the roots would hold the same dark water.

This is not stasis in the sense of death. Everything is moving. Everything is eating, growing, excreting, reproducing, dying, being consumed. The engine is running at full capacity. But it has reached a state of equilibrium so deep and so old that change is no longer a meaningful concept here. The system found its shape, and the shape is self-sustaining, and the shape is this: a hollow room inside a living thing, full of sound and emptiness, cool and damp, lit from above, populated by thousands of small workers performing the same tasks their ancestors performed, producing nothing, consuming nothing from outside, a closed loop, a living machine that runs on itself, forever, or at least for a duration so long that *forever* is the more honest word.

The room smells of tannin, of damp wood, of the faintly acidic breath of beetle colonies, of the green astringency of living cambium. It smells like the inside of a cello. It smells like time that has stopped being time and become a place instead.

You are standing in it. The roar of small mouths fills your skull. The light moves almost imperceptibly on the ancient floor. A drop of condensation falls from the ceiling and strikes a root with a sound like a clock ticking once, and does not tick again for a very long while.
