# The Abyssal Plain of the Closing Sea

---

There is no sky here. There hasn't been for a very long time.

What exists is water — cold, dark, and ancient — pressing down with the weight of a column that once measured kilometers but now, through eons of slow planetary cooling and tectonic subsidence, has compressed the world into a single stratum of crushing, lightless brine. You are somewhere near the bottom of what remains. The seafloor, if it can still be called that, has been worn so smooth and so flat by geological ages that it resembles less a landscape than a theorem — the final, irrefutable proof that given enough time, all things are made level.

But *flat* does not mean *still*.

---

The darkness is not total. There is a faint, residual luminescence — not bioluminescence, not exactly, but something chemical and old. The water itself carries a sulfurous, mineral glow at certain densities, a pale ochre light that blooms and dies in curtains as the currents shift. It is enough to see by, barely, the way a single candle is enough to know you are in a cathedral. The light reveals the floor in glimpses: basalt worn to the smoothness of glass, ancient pillow lavas so eroded they have become abstract undulations, ripples frozen into stone a billion years ago and then sanded down by the patient abrasion of cold water until they are no more than suggestions. There is no relief. No ridges, no scarps. The deepest canyons were filled in ages past. The tallest seamounts were brought low. What remains is a plain that extends in all directions for hundreds of kilometers — a single, unbroken surface that curves only with the curvature of the world itself.

And across this surface, the turbulence moves.

---

Not waves. Not currents, not in any way a surface-dweller would understand the word. What moves here are density cascades — immense, slow-rolling upheavals of water at different temperatures, different salinities, different mineral saturations, all fighting for the same space in a sea that has nowhere left to go. The planet's interior is still hot enough to vent, and along the plain, in hairline fractures so thin they would be invisible if not for the chemical staining around them, superheated brine seeps upward into the frigid bottom water. The temperature differential is savage. The hot fluid is dense with dissolved metals — iron, manganese, silicates — and when it meets the near-freezing ambient water, it does not simply mix. It *detonates* into chemistry.

Plumes of black and rust-orange particulate erupt from fractures that stretch for kilometers, thin as razor lines across the glass-smooth floor. The plumes don't rise far — the pressure is too great, the water above too cold and heavy — so instead they *spread*, rolling outward in sheets, in curling horizontal storms of mineral-laden brine that collide with other sheets from other fractures and produce roiling, chaotic interference patterns. The turbulence is slow by human reckoning — a single convective overturn might take twenty minutes — but it is constant, layered, and everywhere. There is no still water here. Every cubic meter is in motion. Every parcel of fluid is being shoved, displaced, entrained, folded into something else. The density gradients create visible boundaries, shimmering walls of refraction that bend the faint chemical light into distorted halos, and these boundaries are themselves unstable, breaking and reforming in fractal cascades that propagate across the plain like slow-motion weather systems.

The sound of it is almost nothing. A deep, subsonic thrumming that lives more in the bones than the ears — the vibration of fluid masses grinding against each other at different velocities, transmitted through water so dense it behaves almost like a solid. Occasionally, a fracture vents with slightly more force, and there is a muffled percussive *thud*, like a door closing in a distant room of a flooded house. Otherwise, silence. The turbulence is violent but hushed, a war conducted in whispers.

---

The plain is *packed*. Not with life — there is almost no life — but with the products of deep time and relentless chemistry. Every square meter of that glassy basalt is coated, crusted, layered. Manganese nodules carpet the floor so densely they touch one another, black and roughly spherical, ranging from the size of a fist to the size of a skull, each one millions of years old, grown atom by atom as dissolved metals precipitated onto a seed grain of bone or stone. Between the nodules, iron-oxide films coat every surface in iridescent sheens of rust and amber. In the low places — shallow depressions where the floor dips by mere centimeters — pools of hypersaline brine collect, so saturated with minerals they are visibly denser than the water above them, forming eerie, mirror-still lakes on the ocean floor that reflect the faint chemical glow back upward. These brine pools have shorelines. They have *tides*, driven by the density cascades overhead. Sometimes the turbulence pushes a wave of heavier water across a pool and it overflows its margins in a slow-motion flood of toxic, crystallizing fluid that leaves white halite crusts on the nodules like frost.

There is no gap, no empty space. Where there are no nodules there are crusts. Where there are no crusts there are films. Where there are no films there are precipitate clouds so fine they hover in suspension permanently, giving the water itself a granular, textured quality, as though you could reach out and feel the individual particles of dissolved world hanging motionless in their slow fall toward the floor. The water is not clear. It is not opaque. It is *full* — full of the dissolved memory of every mountain, every continent, every volcanic island that was ever built and ever worn away. The sea has eaten the land, and this is where the land has gone: into suspension, into solution, into nodules and crusts and thin, iridescent skins of mineral. The plain is the final repository. The graveyard of topography.

---

Vegetation, such as it is, exists only in the thinnest sense. Along some of the warmer fractures, where the venting brine carries traces of hydrogen sulfide and the temperature climbs a few degrees above freezing, there are mats. Not plants — nothing photosynthetic could survive here — but chemosynthetic bacterial films, pale and sparse, clinging to the fracture margins in irregular patches. They are white, mostly. Some carry a faint pink or yellow tinge from the pigments they use to manage electron transport in the absence of light. They grow slowly, die slowly, and their dead layers accumulate beneath the living ones, building up crusts of organic material no thicker than a few millimeters even after centuries of growth. In a few places, the mats have been disrupted by the turbulence — torn free by a particularly violent density cascade and scattered across the nodule field in pale, ragged strips that drape over the black spheres like wet tissue.

The fauna is rarer still. There are worms — polychaetes of a kind so ancient and so conserved that their body plan has not changed in half a billion years. They are small, translucent, cold-adapted to the point of near-dormancy, and they live in the bacterial mats, threading their bodies through the layered films. They do not move quickly. They do not need to. Nothing hunts them. There may be, in the farther reaches of the plain, where the fractures cluster more densely and the chemistry is slightly more generous, small crustaceans — pale, eyeless, the size of grains of rice — but here, in this stretch, the worms are alone. Occasionally a dead one drifts upward into the water column, carried by a warm upwelling, and it tumbles slowly through the layered turbulence, turning end over end, a pale thread in the dark water, until it settles back to the floor somewhere far from where it lived and begins its own slow incorporation into the mineral record.

---

There is no built environment. No artifacts. Nothing was ever constructed here, nothing shaped by intent. But the plain is not without *structure*. The manganese nodules self-organize into patterns dictated by bottom currents — long, curving rows that follow the prevailing flow, clusters around topographic irregularities too subtle for the eye to detect, voids where some chemical condition inhibits precipitation. These patterns repeat across the plain with a regularity that approaches design. In one area, the nodules are arranged in concentric rings around a brine pool, each ring slightly different in size and composition, marking successive high-water lines like growth rings in a tree. In another, the fracture-line vents have deposited chimneys — not the towering black smokers of a younger ocean, but stunted, eroded remnants, worn down to ankle height, crusted and capped with minerals, leaning at angles that record the direction of currents that may have shifted ten thousand years ago. These are the closest things to architecture. They were not built. They grew, and were diminished, and what remains is a kind of ruin without a civilization — the aesthetic of abandonment without anything having been abandoned.

The technology, if the word applies at all, belongs to the bacterial mats. They are primitive engineers. Their metabolic processes alter the local chemistry in ways that encourage mineral deposition in some areas and inhibit it in others. The mats near the fractures are subtly *shaping* their environment — building up protective crusts on their windward sides, dissolving pathways through nodule fields to access fresher vent fluid. It is not intentional. It is the dumbest, oldest kind of tool use: the body itself as instrument, metabolic waste as medium. But it is real, and over the timescales at which this world operates, it has consequences. Some of the nodule-free corridors that wind through the plain were cleared not by currents but by bacteria, generations beyond counting working the same slow excavation.

---

The turbulence never stops. It shifts, reorganizes, finds new patterns and abandons them. A density cascade that has been rolling steadily westward for hours will encounter a colder mass moving east and the collision will produce a standing wave of shimmering, mineral-rich water that hangs in place for days, visible as a wall of distortion in the dim chemical light, before some shift in the venting pattern dissolves it. The fractures themselves are not stable — they open and close on timescales of years or decades, responding to stresses in the crust far below, and when a new one opens, the sudden influx of hot brine sends shockwaves through the layered water column that can be felt — as vibration, as pressure change, as a sudden warming of a fraction of a degree — across dozens of kilometers. The plain is ancient and eroded and nearly dead, but it is not quiet. It is restless in the way that only very old things can be restless: not with the energy of youth, but with the accumulated instability of a system that has been settling toward equilibrium for so long that every small perturbation propagates through it with exaggerated force, like a single stone dropped into a pond that has been still for a thousand years.

This is a world in its final state, or very near it. The water will not recede. The land will not rise. The fractures will seal, one by one, as the planet's interior cools, and the chemistry will slow, and the bacterial mats will shrink to nothing, and the worms will follow them, and the nodules will stop growing, and the turbulence will calm, and the mineral light will fade, and what will remain is a sphere of cold, dark, pressurized water over a plain of perfect smoothness, and silence, and nothing moving at all.

But not yet. For now, the vents still breathe. The cascades still roll. The water is still full of the dissolved bones of continents. And in the darkness, in the cold, in the immense and crushing pressure, the plain endures — packed with the residue of everything that came before, turbulent with the last energies of a dying world, ancient beyond any human frame of reference, and utterly, profoundly indifferent to being witnessed.
