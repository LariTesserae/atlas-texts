# The Plateau of Voices

---

There is a place where the world grew so old it became something new.

You stand on the rim of a plateau that has no edge — or rather, its edges have been worn away so thoroughly over such incomprehensible spans of time that what remains is not a cliff or a drop but a long, slow, barely perceptible descent in every direction, the rock beneath you having been ground down through eons until the very concept of *peak* became a philosophical suggestion. This is high ground — among the highest on the continent — but it doesn't feel sharp. It feels *settled.* Like a body that has finally exhaled after holding its breath for a geological age. The exposed stone where you can see it, in scattered patches between the roots, is a deep burgundy-grey, pocked and hollowed into honeycomb forms by wind and the patient dissolution of acidic rain over millennia beyond counting. You could press your thumb into some of those hollows and feel how perfectly smooth they've become. The stone has forgotten it was ever rough.

And yet almost none of this stone is visible.

Because the plants have eaten the world.

---

Not in the way of a jungle — not dense, not tangled, not a wall of green pressing against you. That's the strangeness of it. The vegetation here is *total* in coverage but *open* in structure. It is as if someone took a vast old-growth forest, stretched it vertically, and then removed most of the understory, leaving a landscape that is simultaneously all plant and mostly air. The dominant organisms are something between trees and vines and moss — enormous columnar growths, some of them forty meters tall, whose trunks are not wood but a fibrous, slightly yielding tissue that feels like dense cork when you press it. They are pale green at the base, darkening to a blackish emerald at their crowns, and they grow far apart from one another, each one commanding a territory of perhaps sixty to eighty meters in radius. Between them: nothing but the living mat.

The mat. That's the ground here. It is not soil. There is no soil. The entire surface of the plateau is a continuous organism — or perhaps a consortium of organisms so intertwined they've lost the boundary between species. A thick, spongy, living carpet of moss-analogues, lichen-webs, and shallow-rooted creeping plants that covers every centimeter of the ancient stone beneath. It's ankle-deep in most places, knee-deep in others, and it breathes. Not metaphorically. In the warmth of midday, you can see it shift — a slow, rhythmic compression and expansion as gas exchange occurs across its vast surface area. Walking on it feels like walking on a mattress laid over stone. Your feet sink slightly with each step and the mat exhales a faint sweetness, something like cut grass and overripe fruit and mineral water.

The columnar trees are connected to one another and to the mat by a network of aerial roots and runners that arc through the open air between them like the flying buttresses of a cathedral that has no walls. These runners are draped with epiphytes — cascading curtains of small succulent leaves, silver-blue and rust-orange, that catch the light and tremble in the wind. They are the main water-harvesting system of the plateau. In the mornings, when clouds roll up from the lowlands and condense against the high ground, every surface beads with moisture. The epiphytes drink it, channeling it down through the runners and into the root-mat, which stores it in spongy reservoirs against the drier afternoons. By midday the air is warm and the stone beneath the mat has absorbed enough heat to radiate gently upward, and everything is merely *damp* — not wet, not dry, but holding its water close, cycling it, losing almost none.

The openness is what strikes you. You can see for kilometers through the widely spaced columns, across the gentle undulations of the plateau's eroded surface. The horizon is a haze of pale green and warm stone-color. There are no hills, no ridges — nothing sharp enough to interrupt the view. Just the slow roll of the living ground and the pillars of the great columnar growths receding into atmospheric perspective. It looks empty. It feels empty.

It is not empty.

---

The sound is the first thing that would have warned you, if you'd been paying attention to anything other than the landscape. But it builds so gradually as you move inward from the plateau's edge that by the time you consciously register it, it has already saturated everything.

It is *deafening.*

Not in the way of a machine or an explosion — deafening in the way of ten thousand overlapping voices, each distinct, none decipherable, until the sum total becomes a physical pressure against your eardrums and a vibration in the spongy ground beneath your feet. The fauna of the plateau are almost entirely vocal. They live in the columnar trees, in the runner-networks, in the root-mat, in the air between — and they never stop calling.

The largest are the canopy-swingers: broad-bodied creatures, maybe twenty kilograms each, with six limbs and skin that shifts between the green-black of the tree crowns and the rust-orange of the epiphytes. They move through the aerial runner network in loose family groups of eight to fifteen, and their calls are deep, resonant, booming — a low-frequency pulse that you feel in your sternum before you hear it with your ears. Each group maintains a territorial song that cycles on roughly a ninety-second period, and because the plateau supports hundreds of groups within earshot, the overlapping pulses create a constant, throbbing bass layer that never resolves into silence.

Below them, in the middle air and along the columnar trunks, are the chitterers — small, fast, innumerable. They look like something between a lizard and a moth, with membranous wings folded tight against scaled bodies when they cling to the bark, flaring wide and iridescent when they leap between columns. Each one produces a rapid staccato clicking that alone would be a minor sound, a dry rattle, but they exist in populations so dense that the combined effect is a shimmering curtain of high-frequency noise, an insectile roar that fills every gap the canopy-swingers leave. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. They move in visible clouds between the columnar trees, and when a cloud passes overhead, the sound spikes so sharply it makes your eyes water.

In the mat itself, things move. You rarely see them directly — only the ripple of the living ground as something large pushes through the spongy tissue beneath the surface. Mat-swimmers, the locals call them. They are the apex predators of the plateau, legless and muscular, moving through the root-mat the way eels move through water. They surface to feed on the chitterers that land to rest on the ground, exploding upward in a spray of torn moss and silver-blue leaves, swallowing dozens in a single lunge before sinking back. Their calls are the most unsettling: a low, grinding moan that transmits through the mat itself, so that you hear it with your feet. When several of them vocalize at once, the ground *hums,* and the epiphytes on the nearest runners shiver and drop condensation like sudden rain.

There are burrowers in the ancient stone beneath the mat — small things with grinding mouthparts that slowly, slowly extend the honeycomb hollows that erosion began. There are pollinators the size of your fist that hover around the columnar trees' crown-flowers with a drone so deep it seems wrong for their size. There are parasites that live inside the aerial runners, visible as moving lumps beneath the green skin of the tissue, and their hosts have evolved to sing — a thin, reedy whistle — when the parasite load becomes too heavy, attracting chitterers that land and eat the parasites through the runner's surface. The whole system is alive with sound and interaction, a web of calls and responses that has been refining itself for longer than any civilization on this world has existed.

At any given moment, standing still on the plateau, you are hearing: the bass throb of canopy-swingers, the shimmering rattle of chitterer clouds, the ground-transmitted moan of mat-swimmers, the deep drone of pollinators, the whistling of parasitized runners, the wet ripping sound of epiphytes being torn by feeding animals, the soft exhalation of the mat itself, and — if you hold very still — the faint, almost subliminal grinding of the burrowers in the stone below. It is a wall of sound. It is an ecosystem expressing itself at full volume across every frequency band. After an hour, you stop hearing individual calls and start hearing the plateau as a single instrument playing a single, endless, evolving chord.

---

People live here. Not many.

Scattered across the plateau, at intervals of several kilometers, you find the shelters — low, rounded structures woven from dried runner-tissue and epiphyte stems, anchored to the root-mat with pegs of the burgundy-grey stone that someone has painstakingly shaped by hand. They look like blisters on the landscape, barely rising above the knee-height of the mat, easy to miss from a distance. Each one houses a small group — three to seven people, extended families or bonded work-units, it's hard to tell. Their tools are stone and bone and the hardened resin that the columnar trees exude when cut: blades, scrapers, needles, small containers. They harvest the canopy-swingers with weighted nets thrown into the runner-network. They collect chitterer eggs from the bark of the columns. They dig mat-tubers — starchy growths within the root-mat that regenerate when partially harvested.

They have adapted to the sound. Their spoken language is largely gestural when used at close range, supplemented by a system of percussive signals — striking the mat with flat stones in patterns — that transmit through the ground over distances where vocal communication would be swallowed by the ambient roar. At night, when some of the fauna quiets (the chitterers mostly land and sleep, though the canopy-swingers never fully stop), they speak in low voices, close to one another's ears, in a language that sounds clipped and consonant-heavy, optimized for clarity in noise.

They do not build up. They do not build large. Something about the plateau discourages it — not any specific danger but a cultural understanding, ancient and deeply felt, that to build tall here is to challenge the columnar trees, and to build wide is to displace the mat, and the mat is the ground, and the ground is alive, and to wound it is to wound the thing you stand on. Their shelters are small and impermanent, rebuilt every few years when the mat grows through them. They own little. They carry their tools with them when they move between harvest sites. They know every columnar tree within a day's walk by its individual pattern of runner-connections, and they navigate the featureless plateau by these networks the way a sailor navigates by stars.

There is something unusual about their relationship with the mat-swimmers. Not worship, exactly, but an acknowledgment that borders on reverence. They do not hunt them. They mark the locations where mat-swimmers are known to surface and feed, and they avoid these areas during active periods, but they also return to them during quiet times to sit at the edges and wait. Sometimes — rarely — a mat-swimmer will surface slowly, not to feed, but seemingly to rest, its massive head (blunt, eyeless, ringed with sensory pits) protruding from the torn mat for minutes at a time, and the people will sit with it in a silence that is never truly silent, surrounded by the endless chord of the plateau, and something will pass between them that an outsider would struggle to name.

---

The light here is clear and strong, as befits the elevation. The sun moves across an open sky — few clouds persist over the plateau during the day, having shed their moisture in the morning condensation. Shadows are crisp. The colors are vivid: the deep green-black of the canopy crowns, the silver-blue and rust-orange of the epiphytes, the pale green of the columnar trunks, the mottled emerald of the root-mat, the fleeting iridescence of chitterer wings catching the light as they swarm. In late afternoon, when the sun angles low, the aerial runners between the columns cast long, thin shadows across the mat like lines drawn on a living page, and the whole plateau takes on a warm amber quality that softens the ancient stone in the few places it shows through.

The wind is constant but gentle. It carries moisture up from the lowlands in the morning and warmth from the sun-heated stone in the afternoon. It moves through the runner-networks with a low, taut singing that adds yet another voice to the chorus. It smells of the mat — that green-sweet-mineral exhalation — and of the animals, a musky warmth, and of the epiphytes, which release a faintly astringent scent when they're heavy with captured water.

At dusk, the canopy-swingers shift their song. The cycling territorial pulses lengthen, deepen, become less rhythmic and more melodic, as if the creatures are improvising around the structure of their daytime calls. This is when the plateau is loudest, paradoxically — the transition period when day-voices and night-voices overlap, when the chitterers are still active but the nocturnal pollinators are already emerging, when the mat-swimmers are at their most vocal. For perhaps twenty minutes around sunset, the sound reaches a crescendo that can be physically disorienting, a full-body vibration that blurs your vision and makes your bones ache. The people of the plateau call this time something that translates roughly as *the opening.* They stop what they're doing. They sit on the mat. They close their eyes.

Then the chitterers land, and the sound drops — not to silence, never to silence, but to something that functions as silence here: the bass throb, the ground-moan, the wind in the runners, the slow breath of the living ground. Stars appear in an unpolluted sky. The mat glows faintly in places — bioluminescent fungi within its structure responding to the drop in light — and the plateau becomes a dark field scattered with pale green-blue points, like a reflection of the sky above, extending in every direction to an eroded horizon that no longer exists as a line, only as a gradual darkening where the glow of the mat meets the glow of the stars.

And the sound continues. Always the sound. The plateau has been singing to itself for longer than the stone remembers being sharp.
