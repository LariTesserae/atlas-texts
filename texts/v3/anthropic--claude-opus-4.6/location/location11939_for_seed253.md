# The Hanging Gardens of the Abyssal Spires

---

There is no sky here, and there has not been one for a very long time.

What exists is water. Water above, water in every direction, water as the medium through which all sound and all darkness travel. But this is not the open ocean, not the featureless abyss. You are inside something. You have been inside something for as long as you have been descending, and the descent has taken—well. Time moves strangely when there is no light to mark it.

The spires rise.

They rise from depths you cannot see into heights you cannot fathom, and they are the oldest things in this world. Once they were mountains—the highest mountains, in fact, the kind that in some distant geological era scraped at a sky that no longer matters. Tectonic forces pushed them up, and then the water came, and the water never left, and the mountains became the bones of a drowned world. Millennia of submersion have done their patient work. The peaks are rounded now, softened into enormous columns and mesas and finned ridges, their granite and basalt worn into shapes that suggest intention—buttresses, arches, colonnades—though no hand carved them. Erosion is the architect here. Erosion working across timescales so vast that the mountains have been sculpted into something that reads, unmistakably, as *built*. As if the water itself decided what these peaks should become and then spent an age making it so.

The stone has been hollowed. Channels and chambers riddle the spires like the passages of a cathedral, carved by ancient currents that have long since settled into stillness. Overhangs curve into vaulted ceilings. Ridgelines have been etched into repeating crenellations. Where softer rock gave way between harder strata, the result is terracing—vast shelves stepping up the flanks of the spires, each one flat and broad as a plaza, connected to the next by smooth ramps of worn stone that could be stairways if you chose to see them that way.

And on every surface, in every hollow, along every terrace and arch and sculpted buttress—the plants.

---

They are not like surface plants. They have never seen the sun. What they are is something else entirely: a kingdom of vegetation that has made its peace with absolute darkness, that feeds on chemical gradients seeping from the ancient stone, on thermal whispers from deep geological processes far below, on nutrients suspended in the still black water. They grow slowly. They have always grown slowly. But they have been growing for so long that they *are* the spires now, as much as the stone is.

The dominant form is a kind of frond-coral hybrid—call it gorewood, for the deep red-black color of its tissues, visible only if you could somehow illuminate it, which you cannot. It grows in fans and shelves that extend outward from the rock faces, each fan meters across, layered one above another in tiers that mimic and exaggerate the terracing of the stone beneath. The gorewood is rigid, calcified at its base where it bonds to rock, but its outer edges are supple and finely divided into filaments that sway in currents so faint they are barely currents at all—more like the memory of water moving. Each frond is a filter, combing the water for particulate matter with a patience that borders on geological.

Between the gorewood fans, clinging to the vertical faces of the spires, are mats of something softer—a bioluminescent moss, but barely. It emits the faintest conceivable glow, not enough to see by, not even enough to register as light in any meaningful sense. More a suggestion. A thermal shimmer at the very lowest threshold of perception. In aggregate, across the full face of a spire, these mats create a kind of ghost-outline, a dim shape in the darkness that tells you *something is there* without telling you what. You navigate by these ghost-shapes. Everything does.

Higher up—and the spires extend so high that the water pressure decreases noticeably as you rise, the temperature dropping further into a cool stillness that feels like the inside of a stone jar—the vegetation changes character. Here the gorewood gives way to something more architectural: vast tubular structures, hollow-stemmed, that grow upward from the terraces in clusters of three and five and seven, reaching heights of fifteen or twenty meters before their tips divide into canopies of branching filaments. These are the tower-kelps. They are the tallest living things in this world. Their hollow stems channel water upward through simple osmotic pressure, and the sound they make—

The sound.

---

This is a loud place. Not loud the way a storm is loud, not chaotic. Loud the way a cathedral organ is loud: sustained, resonant, *structural*. The tower-kelps are the primary instrument. Water moves through their hollow stems, and the stems resonate. Each one produces a deep, continuous tone, a hum pitched so low it is felt in the bones more than heard by the ears. The pitch varies with the diameter and length of the stem, so a cluster of tower-kelps produces a chord—a fixed chord, unchanging, because the kelps do not grow anymore. They reached their full height long ago and have simply persisted, and their song has not changed in centuries.

The chords differ from spire to spire. One terrace hums a minor third. Another, a quarter-mile away across the black water, drones a perfect fifth. The intervals overlap and interact across the open water between the spires, creating interference patterns, standing waves of sound that you can feel thickening and thinning as you move through the space. There are places between the spires where the harmonics cancel and the silence is sudden and total, like passing through a curtain. There are other places where three or four chords converge and the resonance becomes so dense it is almost tactile, a pressure against the skin, a vibration in the teeth.

Beneath the tower-kelp song, there is the sound of the fauna.

---

They are everywhere. Not densely—there is space between them, great volumes of empty dark water—but *everywhere*, at every depth, on every surface, threading through the architecture of stone and plant with an ease that speaks of deep familiarity. This is their world. They have never known another.

The most numerous are the drift-eels: long, laterally compressed bodies, colorless and scaleless, moving through the water with a sinusoidal undulation so slow it appears meditative. They range from the length of a forearm to the length of a human body. Their eyes are vestigial—sealed shut behind translucent skin—but their lateral lines are extraordinarily developed, reading pressure changes in the water with a sensitivity that amounts to echolocation without the echo. They feel the tower-kelp harmonics. They navigate by them. A drift-eel can identify which spire it is near by the chord, can feel the interference patterns and use them as a map. When they school—and they do, loosely, in ribbons of ten or twelve—they move in unison through the harmonic landscape like a single thought threading through a vast mind.

On the terraces themselves, in the gorewood fans and along the moss-lit stone, there are the grazers. Creatures like enormous isopods, segmented and domed, each the size of a dinner plate, with dozens of feathered legs that serve double duty as gills and locomotion. They move across the plant surfaces methodically, rasping at the gorewood's outer filaments, which regenerate at exactly the rate they are consumed. This balance was struck long ago. It does not change. The grazers move in their patterns and the gorewood grows in its patterns and the two patterns interlock like gears.

Larger things move in the open water between the spires. You feel them before you are aware of them—a displacement, a change in the local pressure that your body registers before your mind does. These are the mantle-rays: broad, flat-bodied creatures with wingspans of three to four meters, their dorsal surfaces covered in a velvety integument that absorbs sound. They are nearly silent movers in a loud world. They feed on the drift-eels, folding around them with a sudden contraction of their wings, but the hunts are infrequent. Everything here is infrequent. Metabolisms are slow. The cool water and the chemical-based food web support life abundantly but not urgently. The mantle-rays spend most of their time gliding between spires on currents so subtle that their passage seems effortless, even voluntary, as if they choose to move not out of need but out of something like aesthetic preference for a particular harmonic zone.

And in the hollows of the spires—in the deep chambers where erosion carved out rooms the size of amphitheaters—there are the sessile colonies. These are animals, technically, though they look like plants: branching, fractal structures anchored to the chamber walls, each branch tipped with a polyp that filters water. They are colonial organisms, hundreds or thousands of individuals fused into a single architecture that, over the millennia, has grown to fill its chamber completely, branch interlocking with branch until the interior of the hollow is a dense lattice of living tissue. The chambers concentrate sound. The tower-kelp harmonics funnel in through the openings and reverberate, and the polyps respond to the vibrations by contracting and expanding in rhythm, which moves water through the colony, which feeds it. The colonies are, in a sense, powered by music.

If you were to enter one of these chambers—drifting in through a worn archway, the moss-glow outlining the entrance just enough to find it—you would feel the sound change. The open harmonics of the spire exterior would compress into something richer, denser, almost choral. The colony-lattice would be all around you, close but not touching, its branches swaying in micro-currents generated by its own feeding rhythm. In the near-total darkness, you would be aware of it only as a presence: a thickness in the water, a network of faint resistances if you reached out a hand, and everywhere the amplified drone of the tower-kelps transformed by the chamber's acoustics into something that vibrates in the sternum, in the skull, in the spaces between thoughts.

---

The distances between spires are considerable. This is where the sparseness lives—not on the spires themselves, which are lush with life and structure, but in the open water between them. Here there is nothing. Black water, cool and still, carrying only the fading harmonics of the nearest spire and the approaching harmonics of the next. A drift-eel might cross this space in an hour. A mantle-ray, riding a current, might take longer, content to let the water carry it. The void between spires is not hostile—there is nothing in it to be hostile—but it is empty in a way that has weight. You are aware, crossing it, of the sheer volume of water above you and around you, of the fact that these submerged mountains, tall as they are, are still deep beneath a surface you will never reach.

How many spires are there? From any given point, you can feel—not see, never see—perhaps four or five, each identified by its unique harmonic signature. The drift-eels know more; their maps extend further. The oldest eels, the longest ones, carry in their lateral lines a knowledge of dozens of spires, a harmonic atlas of a drowned mountain range that stretches for what, in surface terms, might be a hundred kilometers. Every spire different. Every chord unique. And every one of them ancient, stable, long past the point of change.

---

Nothing here is new. That is the defining quality of this place, more than the darkness, more than the water, more than the sound. Nothing is new and nothing is becoming new. The erosion that shaped the spires has reached its terminal form; the stone will not change appreciably for another million years. The gorewood fans have achieved their maximum extent. The tower-kelps have reached their full height and will not grow another centimeter; they will simply persist, humming their fixed chords, until the chemistry of the water changes—which it will not, because the geological processes that feed it are as stable as the stone. The grazers and the gorewood have reached their equilibrium. The mantle-rays and the drift-eels have reached theirs. The sessile colonies fill their chambers exactly, and have for longer than any living thing can remember.

This is a world that has finished becoming. It is not dead—far from it, it is rich with life, busy with feeding and swimming and the slow passage of bodies through dark water—but it is *complete*. Every niche is filled. Every cycle is closed. Every sound has been sounding for so long that silence would be the catastrophe, not noise. The tower-kelps hum. The chambers resonate. The drift-eels thread their courses through the harmonic map, and the mantle-rays glide between the chords, and the grazers rasp and the gorewood regrows and the moss barely glows, and all of it—all of it—has been exactly this way for longer than the concept of *long* has meaning.

You are here. You are the newest thing in this world by an incomprehensible margin. The water is cool against your skin. The nearest spire is a ghost-shape in the darkness, outlined in the faintest bioluminescent haze, and the sound of it reaches you through your bones: a chord, low and sustained, patient beyond all human measure, and you realize that it has been playing since before your species existed, and that it will be playing long after, and that the only appropriate response is to be still, and to listen, and to let the ancient music hold you in the dark.
