# The Cistern of Rooks

---

It is a room the size of a small orchard, sunk into the earth, and every surface of it was made by hands.

The walls are stone — not quarried stone, but stone that was shaped in place, chiseled and picked and worn smooth by touch over what must be generations. The ceiling is corbelled, each course of stone stepping inward and upward until it closes in a rough dome perhaps four meters overhead. There are no mortar lines. The stones hold themselves up by their own weight and the patience of whoever stacked them. Lichen has tried to grow on the interior walls and mostly failed. A few pale crusts cling where water seeps through joins in the masonry, but even these seem provisional, barely committed to life.

The floor is flagstone — broad, uneven slabs fitted together with the gaps packed with clay that has long since hardened into something like cement. The flags are worn into shallow bowls where feet or water or both have worked them down. In the center of the chamber, a rectangular basin has been cut into the floor, perhaps two meters by one, knee-deep. It is full of dark water. Not stagnant — you can see it moving, barely, a slow upwelling from some crack or channel beneath, fed by a spring or a seep that predates the construction and that the construction was clearly built to receive. The water brims almost to the lip of the basin, and in the dim light it looks black until you kneel close and see that it is merely tea-dark, tinted by minerals or by the stone itself. The overflow runs in a shallow groove cut into the flagstones, traveling toward the far wall where it disappears into a drain — a rough hole packed with gravel, hand-sorted.

The water makes a sound. A constant, glassy murmur. Not loud in itself, but the corbelled dome catches it and holds it, amplifies it, gives it a resonance like a voice humming inside a jar.

That sound is the quietest thing here.

---

The rooks came in through the oculus.

At the crown of the dome there is an opening, perhaps half a meter across, ringed with stones that have been blackened by centuries of weather and smoke and the oily passage of wings. It lets in the only natural light — a disc of gray sky, cloud-choked, the kind of overcast that could be morning or afternoon or neither. The light that falls through is weak, pewter-colored, and it strikes the surface of the basin water and dies there.

But through that opening, the birds.

There are dozens of them. Rooks, jackdaws, something between — corvids of several kinds, black and slate and gun-metal blue, and they have colonized this place utterly. They line the upper courses of the dome where the stone steps narrow, packed side by side on ledges that were either made for them or that they have claimed so thoroughly the distinction no longer matters. Their nests — rough, functional — are stuffed into every crevice and shelf in the upper third of the chamber, built from twigs and wire and what looks like dried grass hauled in from somewhere outside. The nests are old. Some are built on top of older nests, compressed into strata. The lowest layers have calcified, fused with droppings and dust into something almost geological.

The noise is enormous.

Not constant — it surges. A bird shifts on its ledge and jostles its neighbor, who screams, and the scream propagates around the dome in a wave of indignant croaking that peaks and subsides over five or six seconds. Then silence — or rather, the water-murmur underneath — and then it starts again from a different quarter. The acoustics of the corbelled ceiling do something unnatural to the sound, layering it, folding echoes back on themselves so that a single caw arrives at your ear three times in quick succession, each iteration slightly altered by its bounce off curved stone. When several birds call at once the chamber fills with a churning, guttural roar that is startling for its volume and then stops, clean, like a door closing.

The birds watch you. They are not afraid. Their eyes catch the oculus-light and hold it — wet, black, intelligent. Some of them are doing things. One drags a strip of something pale (cloth? skin?) along a ledge, working it under its foot, testing it. Another drops from its perch to the flagstones near the basin, walks to the water with a rolling gait, drinks, and flies back up in a single explosive motion that sends a gust of cold air across your face. Two others are fighting — or mating — or conducting some negotiation that involves locked beaks and spread wings, balanced on the narrow rim of a nesting shelf, and the sound they make together is like tearing fabric.

There are also smaller things. Beetles in the nest-strata. Pale moths that cling to the lower walls where the stone is damp. Something moves in the basin water — not a fish, something flatter, a newt or a larva, a dark comma that drifts and then flicks itself to a new position. The gravel drain at the far wall is home to something that clicks — a crayfish, maybe, or a water beetle with hard wing-cases. The ecosystem is real, layered, alive, built on the intersection of the spring water below and the corvid colony above, their droppings feeding the insects, the insects feeding the smaller birds, the smaller birds' eggs feeding the larger ones. It is a food web compressed into a single room.

---

Along the walls, at human height, there are niches cut into the stone. Dozens of them, rectangular, each about the size of two fists held together. Most are empty. Some hold objects: a smooth river stone, a coil of hand-twisted cord, a clay cup with no handle, a small iron blade so corroded it has become mostly rust and suggestion. One niche holds a bird skull, cleaned and dry, placed with care. Another holds what might be a candle stub or what might be a finger-shaped piece of chalk.

The niches are old. Older than the nests. The tool marks that made them are different from the tool marks on the basin — cruder, or simply made by different hands in a different era. Some of the niches have been partly blocked by nest material that the birds have stuffed in from above, and no one has cleared it. The objects in the niches that remain accessible have a quality of having been left rather than placed — not arranged, not displayed, but set down by someone who meant to come back, or who didn't, or who understood that leaving a thing in a niche in a stone wall is its own kind of sentence with no need of an ending.

The air is cool and tastes of mineral water and bird. It is humid without being warm — the kind of dampness that settles into cloth and stays. The walls weep in places, not dramatically, just a sheen of moisture on the stone that catches what little light there is and makes the walls seem to breathe. Where the water-sheen is thickest, the stone has developed a patina — not quite biological, not quite mineral, a dark glazing that might be iron deposit or might be some slow chemical dialogue between the water and the rock and the centuries.

Everything here was built. The floor, the walls, the dome, the basin, the drain, the niches, the oculus. Even the ledges where the birds roost may have been cut or widened by tools — it's impossible to tell now, under the accretion of nests and droppings and time. But the builders are gone, or they are the same people who occasionally leave a river stone in a niche and then walk back out through the low doorway you came in by — a passage just tall enough to pass through bent at the waist, framed by two massive lintels that have settled and cracked but not fallen, not yet, held in place by the weight of everything above them.

You notice, finally, that the bird-sounds have organized themselves. Not into music — into something more like weather. Swells and lulls, pressure systems of noise that move through the dome space in patterns you can almost predict. The rooks are responding to each other, to the small shifts in light through the oculus as clouds move overhead, to the movements of the beetles in the walls, to you. The chamber is a single instrument and the birds are playing it, not with intention but with the accumulated habit of generations who have lived inside its acoustics and shaped their calls to fit.

You are standing in a wet stone room that someone built around a spring, a long time ago, for reasons that may have included the birds or may not have. The birds are here now. The water is here. The dim light falls through the eye of the dome and touches nothing brightly. The sound fills the space the way water fills the basin — constantly, from below, with a slow pressure that does not overflow but never recedes.

It is not yours. You are a guest in it. The rooks know this. The water doesn't care.
