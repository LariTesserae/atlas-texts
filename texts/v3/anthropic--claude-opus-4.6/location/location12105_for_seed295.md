# The Nesting Terraces of Óramak

---

At first there is only the sound. A wall of sound, a living pressure against the eardrums so constant and so layered that the mind, upon entering it, briefly refuses to parse it — treats it as silence, or as wind, before the texture resolves and you understand that what you are hearing is *animals*. Millions of them. Tens of millions. Their calls overlapping into a single roar that rolls across the high plateau like something geological, like the voice of the rock itself.

---

The terraces extend for miles.

They are not natural. Nothing here is natural, not in the way that word usually means. Every surface you see — every ledge, every wall, every curved overhang, every pillar, every hollow — was built. But not by human hands, and not by any technology more sophisticated than a beak, a claw, a throat full of calcium-ite paste, the patient deposit of material over generations beyond counting.

The birds made this place. The birds and the things that live among them.

---

Imagine a high plateau, two thousand meters above the plains, where the air is warm but thinned, where clouds form below you in the valleys and the sky above is a deep indigo that rarely brightens past twilight. The sun does not reach here well. The plateau faces north and is shadowed by a higher ridge to the south, and the light that arrives is always slant, always grey-violet, always dim. At midday there is a bruised glow along the western rim. At other times there is near-darkness, and you navigate by the bioluminescence of the fungal mats that colonize every damp surface — a faint blue-green glow, the color of old copper, that gives the terraces their phantom architecture in the dark hours.

The plateau itself — whatever stone once constituted it — is almost entirely buried. What you walk on, what you see, what rises around you in tiered walls and scalloped galleries, is a substance called *óram* by the people of the lowlands, who do not come here. Óram is compacted guano,ite calcium excretion,ite feather-oil, insite digestite — no, let me be plainer. It is the accumulated biological product of an avian colony so old and so vast that it has literally rebuilt the landscape. The substance is hard where it has cured for centuries, chalite and pale, likeite calciumite calciumite — likeite boneite calcium —

Like calciumite limestone. Like paleite calcium — 

It is pale. It is hard. It is layered. Centuries of deposition have created a material that resembles sedimentary rock: cream-white where old, yellowish where recent, stained black or green where water has carried fungal spores through it. The birds have shaped it not with intention but with habit — the same species nesting in the same configurations, in the same structures, generation after generation, each generation adding its layer, each layer slightly modifying the architecture, until what was once a bare rock shelf has become a terraced city of alcoves, ledges, tunnels, buttresses, and overhangs that rises in some places forty meters above the plateau floor.

It is, in its way, the most completely built environment imaginable. Every surface is made. Every structure is constructed. And none of it was designed.

---

The terraces step upward in long curved ranks, following the contour of the plateau's edge. Each rank is perhaps three to five meters tall, and each is pocked with nesting hollows — thousands per terrace, hundreds of thousands across the full extent of the colony. The hollows vary. Some are simple depressions, scooped clean each season. Some are deep tubes that angle back into the óram substrate, warm and sheltered. Some have been expanded by generations of digging until they connect, forming warrens that riddle the interior of each terrace wall like the chambers of a coral head.

Between the terraces, the ground is covered — thickly, deeply — with vegetation. This is where the lushness lives. Not on the walls themselves, which are too coated with fresh excrement and too constantly disturbed by talons and wings, but in the sheltered valleys between terrace ranks, where runoff collects and organic material composts into a soil so rich it is nearly black. Here: dense mats of broad-leafed ground plants, their leaves dark and waxy, optimized for the low light. Ferns with fronds as long as a human arm, unfurling in slow spirals. Climbing mosses that ascend the lower portions of each terrace wall before the bird-traffic scours them away. Shelf fungi the size of dinner plates, pale underneath, dark above, growing from every crack where moisture seeps through the óram. Seed-grasses that have co-evolved with the colony — their seeds distributed exclusively by the birds, their roots anchored in bird-made soil, their entire evolutionary trajectory shaped by this place.

The vegetation is dense and close and wet. Water does not flow here in streams. It *seeps*. The plateau is above the water table, but the óram is porous, and the warm air rising from the valleys below meets the cooler air of the altitude and condenses. Every surface is faintly damp. Droplets gather on the undersides of overhangs and fall in slow, irregular drips. In the deeper hollows of the terrace valleys, standing pools form — shallow, murky, rich with nutrients, covered in films of algae that glow faintly in the dark. The whole place smells of ammonia and wet earth and the green-sharp scent of crushed stems, and under it all, a warm animal musk that is not unpleasant so much as *total* — it is the smell of a living system, the smell of a body, as if you had climbed inside some vast organism and were standing in its gut.

---

Now the fauna.

The colony is dominated by three species of large seabird — or what were once seabirds. The plateau is far from any coast, but these birds' ancestors, in some era before counting, followed an inland sea that has since dried to salt flats far below. The birds remained. They adapted. They fly enormous distances to feed — down from the plateau, across the scrublands, to the rivers and marshes of the mid-country, and back. Their wingspans range from one to nearly two meters. Their plumage is dark — black, slate-grey, deep brown — which makes them near-invisible against the dim sky, so that when a flock returns to the terraces, you hear it before you see it: a rising chorus of calls, a percussive thunder of wings, and then they materialize out of the gloom like a thrown handful of shadows, thousands at once, banking and wheeling and settling onto the ledges in a chaos of sound and motion that shakes loose dust and dried excrement in pale cascades down the terrace walls.

But the three seabird species are only the founders, the architects. Living among them — in the cracks, in the hollows, in the vegetation, on the birds themselves — is an entire dependent ecosystem of staggering density.

Small passerines nest in the gaps between the larger birds' territories, darting between the massive bodies with impunity, feeding on the insects that feed on the waste. Those insects are themselves uncountable: beetles with iridescent shells that process guano into soil, flies whose larvae riddle the upper layers of fresh óram, pale moths that emerge at the darkest hours and fill the air between the terraces in such numbers that walking through them feels like walking through a warm, fluttering snow. Mites live on the birds. Mites live on the mites. Parasitic wasps, so small they are barely visible, lay their eggs in the bodies of the flies. Centipedes — thick, rust-colored, as long as a hand — patrol the base of the terrace walls, hunting beetles with a mechanical patience.

There are lizards. Small, dark-scaled, sticky-footed, they climb the terrace walls at night and eat the moths. The lizards are eaten by the smaller raptors — kestrel-like birds that have carved out their own niche within the colony, nesting on the highest points where the óram has been sculpted by wind into thin spires and fins. The kestrels add their own excrement, their own architecture, their own thin screaming calls to the chorus.

There are bats. Not many, but present — roosting in the deepest tunnels, emerging at the same hours as the moths, competing with the lizards, navigating the dense air by echolocation that adds an ultrasonic layer to the soundscape, a frequency you feel in your teeth more than hear with your ears.

In the standing pools: larvae, tadpoles of a small frog species that breeds nowhere else, water beetles, nematodes in the millions per liter. The frogs themselves are thumb-sized and translucent and sit on the wet moss at the edges of the pools and call — a high, glassy piping that, when hundreds of them call together, cuts through even the massive bird-chorus like a bright thread through dark cloth.

And in the soil between the terraces, in the composting vegetation, in the deep layers of old óram: worms, grubs, burrowing beetles, fungal networks whose mycelia bind the whole structure together like hidden rebar, colonies of ants that farm the fungi, spiders that prey on the ants, pseudoscorpions smaller than a grain of rice that prey on the mites that fall from the birds above.

Everything eats. Everything is eaten. Everything is loud.

---

The sound deserves its own accounting.

Stand in the valley between two terrace ranks at any hour and you are inside a noise so thick it has texture. The base layer is the seabirds — their calls are guttural, resonant, somewhere between a bark and a moan, and when thousands of them vocalize simultaneously (which is always, because there are always thousands awake, always thousands arriving, always thousands departing, always thousands squabbling over nest sites or courting or warning or simply announcing their continued existence), the sound is a low roar that vibrates in the chest. It is the sound of a stadium. It is the sound of a waterfall heard from inside.

Layered over this: the sharper cries of the kestrels. The chattering of the passerines. The piping of the frogs. The hum of insect wings — not a single hum but a composite drone made of millions of individual flight-tones, rising and falling as swarms condense and disperse. The clicking of beetles. The occasional deep *thump* of a large bird landing on a hollow surface. The dripping of water, which you cannot actually hear but which you know is happening because you can feel it on your skin and see the ripples in the pools.

At certain hours — when the main flocks return from feeding, when the terraces are most active — the sound is genuinely painful. It is not a sharp pain but a pressure, a saturation, as if the air itself has thickened. Conversations would be impossible here, not because of any single loud noise but because there is no frequency band left unoccupied. The sound fills every register. It is a white noise made of living voices.

And beneath it all, so low it is felt rather than heard, a kind of structural groan — the óram itself settling, adjusting, the deep layers compressing under the weight of the new, the whole plateau-sized construction shifting by fractions of millimeters per year, the slow patient creak of a living building that has been building itself for longer than anyone has been alive to watch.

---

The changes here are slow. Seasons matter, but not dramatically — the warm air rising from the lowlands keeps the plateau temperate year-round, and the birds' comings and goings shift in pattern but never cease. A terrace wall might grow a centimeter in a year. A tunnel might deepen by a handspan in a decade. A new terrace rank begins to form where a critical mass of nesting activity deposits enough material to raise the floor, and this process takes centuries.

The vegetation grows and dies and composts and grows again in the valleys, cycling slowly, never clearing, never overtaking the terrace walls, held in check by the birds' constant disturbance above and fed by their constant output below. The fungal networks extend through the óram and quietly digest the oldest layers at the bottom even as new layers are added at the top, so the whole structure exists in a dynamic equilibrium — always building, always decaying, always the same.

Species come and go on timescales too long to observe. A niche opens; something fills it. A population crashes; its predators decline; its prey explodes; balance returns. The colony has survived droughts that dried the lowland rivers for years — the birds flew farther, many died, the terraces quieted but never silenced. It has survived storms that sheared away the upper spires and collapsed whole terrace faces — the birds rebuilt, because rebuilding is just living, just nesting, just continuing.

---

If you could stand at the western rim of the plateau at the hour when the dim light is strongest, and look out across the full extent of the terraces — the pale curving walls rising in ranks like an amphitheater, the dark vegetation in the valleys between, the constant motion of wings against the dark sky, the faint blue-green glow of fungal light beginning to emerge in the shadows — you would see something that looks like a city. An old city, unplanned, organic in its growth, shaped by no single intelligence but by the accumulated decisions of countless small lives over unthinkable time. A city with no streets and no doors and no windows, only ledges and hollows and the ceaseless circulation of wings. A city that is also a body, also a machine, also a compost heap, also a cathedral of sound, also just a place where birds have been nesting for so long that they have, without knowing it, replaced the mountain.

You would not be able to stay long. The sound would drive you out, or the smell, or the simple density of life pressing against you from every direction — the moths in your hair, the mites on your skin, the beetles underfoot, the birds wheeling so close overhead that you feel the wind of their passing. This place was not made for you. It was not made for anyone. It was made by the living of lives, millions upon millions of them, each one small, each one brief, each one adding its imperceptible layer to the structure that will outline them all.
