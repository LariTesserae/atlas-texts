# The Humming Shelf

---

There is a shelf of young basalt on the shoulder of a mountain that should not work the way it does.

It is perhaps the size of a chapel nave — forty paces long, fifteen wide — jutting from a steep alpine slope where the clouds sit low and cold and never quite leave. The rock is fresh, sharp-featured, only centuries old at most. You can see where it cooled in ropes and pillows, where gas bubbles froze mid-escape, leaving the surface pocked with tiny mouths. Nothing has had time to soften it. The edges of the shelf are crisp as broken chocolate. Below, the mountainside drops away into grey vapour. Above, more vapour. The shelf is a room with no ceiling, walled on one side by the mountain's flank and on the other three by altitude and cloud.

It is cool here. Not freezing — there is no frost, no ice — but the air sits against your skin like a damp cloth pulled from a cold stream. Everything is wet. Water beads on the basalt, collects in the little gas-pock mouths, trembles there. It seeps from a crack in the mountain wall in a slow, wide weep, not a spring exactly, more like the rock is exhaling moisture. The water runs in a thin film across the shelf's slight tilt and drips off the far edge into nothing. After rain — and it rains here often, soft and sideways — the entire surface gleams.

And the shelf is **green**. Absurdly, extravagantly green for a place this high and this cold and this young. Mosses of a dozen species have colonized every horizontal surface in thick, emerald cushions. Liverworts cling to the vertical face in overlapping fans, their reproductive structures like tiny green parasols. Ferns — cold-hardy, small, intensely vital — erupt from every significant crack, some of them knee-high, their fronds uncurled and trembling with collected water. There is a single rowan tree growing from a fissure near the back wall, barely taller than a person, ancient-looking despite its youth, its roots gripping the rock like fingers. It is heavy with clusters of orange berries. The berries do not fall. They have not fallen in anyone's memory. The tree does not grow. The mosses do not spread further than they have spread. The ferns hold their fronds at exactly the angle they hold them now.

Nothing here changes.

This is the first and fundamental strangeness. The water weeps at the same rate. The cloud cover neither thickens nor thins. The dim light — a pewter-grey luminescence that seems to come from everywhere and nowhere, sourceless, as if the cloud itself were a single enormous diffused lamp — does not brighten at midday or darken at dusk. If there is a midday. If there is a dusk. The rowan's berries are orange. They are the same orange. They will be the same orange. A fern frond, if you push it aside, will return to precisely its former position with the slow, deliberate certainty of a compass needle.

Now: the animals.

They are **everywhere**, and they are busy, and they are the loudest thing here, and they are impossible.

Start with the birds. There are perhaps thirty of them on the shelf at any given time — though "any given time" is a phrase that buckles under scrutiny in this place. They are pipits and wagtails and small thrushes, species you might expect at lower elevations, in meadows, near streams. They hop across the mossy basalt, they perch on the rowan, they flit to the fern-tips which accept their weight without bending one degree further than they are already bent. They call to each other in sharp chips and liquid trills that bounce off the rock wall. They do not seem distressed. They do not seem to be doing anything in particular. They do not eat the rowan berries. They do not hunt insects — there are no insects, or if there are, they are not visible. The birds simply *move*, in their quick avian way, full of apparent purpose that resolves into nothing.

Then: the frogs. At least a dozen, maybe twenty, tucked into the moss cushions, half-submerged in the film of water. They are small, dark, glistening. They call — a low, resonant, collective thrumming that is the basso continuo beneath the birdsong. The sound is disproportionate to their size. It fills the shelf, vibrates in the basalt, hums in your teeth. The frogs do not move from their positions. They have not moved from their positions. But they breathe, and they call, and their throat-sacs inflate and deflate with mechanical regularity.

*Mechanical.*

That word will keep surfacing.

There is a stoat that lives on the shelf — pure white, as if in winter coat, though there is no winter here, no season at all. It runs along the base of the rock wall in a fixed route, pauses at certain points, turns its head at certain angles, then continues. If you watch it long enough — and time here is strange, but you can feel the repetition in your body — you will see the route repeat. Exactly. The same pause. The same head-turn. The same angle of the ears at precisely the same positions along the wall. It is not a habit. It is a *program.*

A fox sits near the edge of the shelf, in the cloud, mostly obscured, its tail wrapped around its feet. Its eyes are open. They track nothing. Occasionally — at perfect intervals — it yawns. The yawn is identical each time. The teeth are the same. The tongue curls the same way. The jaw closes with the same soft click.

Voles move through the moss in tunnels you can hear but not see. The sound of their movement is a soft, continuous rustling, arrhythmic only in the way a complex machine sounds arrhythmic until you understand its cycle time.

And here is where the world begins to break down, or rather, here is where you begin to understand that it broke down long ago — or was never together — or that "together" and "broken" are not the right categories:

**The animals are warm.**

Not body-warm. *Warm* warm. If you hold your hand near the stoat as it passes — and it will let you, because you are not a variable it accounts for, or because you are exactly the variable it accounts for — you feel heat radiating from its fur like a banked furnace. The frogs, half-submerged, keep the water near them at a temperature that makes the surrounding cold water steam faintly at the boundary. The birds leave brief thermal ghosts on their perches. The fox, sitting at the edge, heats the air around it enough that the cloud visibly thins in a halo around its body.

This heat does not dissipate into the environment. Or — it does, but the environment does not warm. The system is in perfect, impossible equilibrium. Energy pours from the animals and the shelf remains cool and wet and dim and nothing changes. The energy goes *somewhere*. It must. But the somewhere is not here.

Put your ear to the basalt. You will hear it — beneath the frogs, beneath the birdsong, beneath the vole-rustle: a low, continuous, mechanical hum. Not geological. Not alive. It has the character of turbines, of dynamos, of vast rotational systems buried impossibly deep in young rock that is only a few meters thick above empty air. The hum is felt more than heard, a vibration at a frequency that makes your vision swim if you focus on it too long. It is always there. It has always been there. It is exactly the same at every point on the shelf, which should not be possible with any physical source — unless the source is the shelf itself, every atom of it, vibrating in lockstep.

The animals are engines. Or the animals are *driven by* engines. Or the animals are the visible portion of a process that is mostly invisible, a process that expresses itself as white stoats and damp frogs and orange berries that never fall, a process that requires the continuous input of energy on an industrial scale and routes that energy through living bodies that are also somehow mechanisms, clock-parts, elements in a device whose function you cannot determine because you are standing on it, inside it, and the device is the size of a chapel and it is disguised as a mountainside.

Or perhaps: the device is not disguised as a mountainside. Perhaps the mountainside is a *component*. The fresh basalt with its sharp edges and gas-pock mouths was not erupted by geological forces. It was *manufactured*. It was *extruded*. It cooled in this shape because this shape was needed — the precise tilt for water drainage, the precise crack pattern for root purchase, the pocked surface for condensation capture. The weeping crack in the wall is not a natural seep but a *coolant line*, or a *lubricant feed*, or the visible terminus of a hydraulic system that keeps some deeper mechanism from overheating.

The rowan tree is an antenna. The ferns are sensors. The moss is insulation.

No — that's too neat. The rowan tree is a rowan tree that is also an antenna. The ferns are ferns that are also sensors. Both things are fully true. The stoat is a stoat. It has stoat-bones and stoat-blood and a stoat's small fierce heart. It is also a repeating subroutine in a process that has been running at perfect equilibrium for longer than the rock has existed, which is impossible, which is exactly the kind of impossibility this place specializes in.

You could sit here for what feels like hours. You will not get hungry. You will not get colder. You will not need to urinate. The shelf will incorporate you, if you let it — not maliciously, not even intentionally, but the way a stream incorporates a stone. You will become part of the equilibrium. Your body heat will stabilize. Your breathing will find a rhythm. Your heartbeat will synchronize, slowly, with the hum in the rock, and you will feel — not peace exactly, but *consistency*. The birds will route around you. The stoat will adjust its path by exactly the width of your body. The frogs will account for the shadow you cast, if you cast one, which in this light is uncertain.

The cloud never lifts. The berries never fall. The fox yawns at its interval.

Somewhere deep in the basalt, the turbines turn.
