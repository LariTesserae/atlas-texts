# The Roaring Terraces of Geth-Ammon

---

It hits you first as sound.

Not sound as you understand it — not a wave approaching from a source — but a wall of noise so total it seems to have no origin, as though the air itself were screaming. A roar that fills the skull from every direction: the massed shrieking of ten billion wings, the crack and grind of something enormous always breaking apart and reassembling, a tidal drone underneath it all that vibrates the teeth and the sternum and the small bones of the inner ear. You feel it in your kidneys. You feel it in the gaps between your thoughts.

And yet somehow — within a minute, maybe less — the ear begins to make distinctions. Separations. The cacophony resolves, not into silence, but into a kind of grammar.

---

You are standing on one of the terraces.

The place is a bowl-shaped depression in a high plateau, perhaps two hundred meters across at its widest, ringed on all sides by ridges of pale mineral that catch the daylight and hold it in their grain like embedded quartz. The elevation is considerable — the air is thin and cool on the skin, carrying an almost alpine bite, and the sky overhead is a deep, saturated blue, the blue of altitude, cloudless and hard. The light is generous and even. It is midday on a world, or in a region of a world, that has no visible sun — just a diffuse luminance that pours from the sky as if the atmosphere itself is the lamp. Shadows exist but they are soft, smudged, directionless.

The terraces themselves step down toward the center of the bowl in a series of irregular shelves, each one between half a meter and two meters in height. They are mineral — dry stone, calcium-pale, threaded with veins of iron oxide and some darker compound that catches light in ways that suggest crystalline microstructure. These are old surfaces. Fantastically old. The edges are rounded by time into smooth lips. The faces of the risers are scalloped with erosion patterns that took centuries or millennia to form, each one a frozen record of winds that no longer blow in those particular directions. There is no water. There has been no water here for a geological age. The rock remembers rain the way a fossil remembers breath — as a shape, an imprint, a structural echo. The ground is dry in a way that goes beyond dryness; it is *post-aqueous*, a landscape that has moved entirely past the question of water, as though water were a language it once spoke and has since forgotten.

And yet.

Every terrace, every shelf, every cracked and weathered expanse of pale stone is *covered* — explosively, extravagantly, almost violently covered — in vegetation.

---

Not green. Or rather, not primarily green.

The dominant colors are deep indigo, violet, a dusty silver-blue, and a red so dark it reads as black until the light finds its edges. The plants grow in dense mats on the flat surfaces and cascade over the rounded lips of each terrace in curtains and braids. They are succulent, or something analogous to succulent — fat-leaved, turgid, swollen with whatever they use instead of water. Their tissues, when you look closely, have a faintly luminous quality, as though they contain a fluid that fluoresces just below the visible threshold. Some of them — the silver-blue rosettes that cluster in the crevices — are cool to the touch, almost cold, as if they are pulling heat from the air and storing it somewhere metabolic.

They do not photosynthesize in any way you recognize. They *resonate*. Each leaf, each frond, each plump and indigo pad vibrates at a frequency tuned to the ambient sound, and the vibrations drive something — some chemistry, some process — that feeds the plant. The louder the world becomes, the more they grow. The roar is their sunlight. The noise is their rain.

And the noise is extraordinary because the noise is *alive*.

---

The fauna.

Picture a swarm — no. Picture a dozen swarms, each one a different species, each one occupying a slightly different altitude above the terraces, each one moving in patterns that interact with and destabilize and reconfigure the patterns of all the others. The lowest stratum, skimming just above the vegetation, is a species of flat-bodied arthropod — or something that fills the arthropod niche — with translucent wings that beat at roughly two hundred cycles per second. Each one is about the size of your thumbnail. There are millions of them. Millions. Their wingbeats alone generate a collective hum that would be painful in any other context; here it is simply the bass note, the foundation upon which the rest of the soundscape is built.

Above them, in the zone between one and four meters, the fliers are larger and fewer — elongated creatures with membranes stretched between multiple articulated limbs, iridescent in the daylight, wheeling and diving and colliding and splitting apart in patterns that seem almost choreographed until you watch long enough to realize they are genuinely chaotic. They scream. Each one produces a high, sharp, continuous tone from some organ you cannot locate, and the tones shift and clash and occasionally, briefly, harmonize before shattering apart. When two of them collide midair — which happens constantly — there is a percussive crack, and both tumble, recover, spiral back into the mass.

Higher still, things move that are harder to see. Long, trailing shapes, gossamer-thin, riding updrafts generated by the collective thermal output of the swarms below. These are the quietest creatures, but "quiet" is relative. They produce infrasonic pulses that you feel rather than hear — deep throbs in the chest that arrive irregularly, unpredictably, adding a subsonic chaos to the cacophony.

And then there are the crawlers. On the stone itself, in the gaps between the mats of vegetation, things move with startling speed. Six-legged, low-slung, armored in chitin or something harder, the color of the rock itself. They erupt from crevices, cross an open stretch of terrace in a blur, vanish into another crack. Each emergence and disappearance is accompanied by a sharp *tik* — mandibles striking stone, perhaps, or some territorial signal — and there are so many of them, so constantly appearing and disappearing, that the *tik-tik-tik-tik* becomes a staccato layer woven through everything else, a texture of percussion that never resolves into rhythm because it is too many independent rhythms layered atop each other.

The bowl of the terraces acts as an amphitheater. The sound reflects off the mineral ridges and returns, amplified, phase-shifted, tangled with itself. Standing here is like standing inside a bell that is being struck from every direction simultaneously and continuously.

---

But the place is *empty*.

This is the paradox, the thing that takes longest to register: for all the life, for all the noise, for all the frantic motion, the space feels cavernous and hollow. The swarms, dense as they are, are concentrated in specific strata, and between those strata the air is simply air — clear, cool, open. The vegetation mats are thick but they cling to surfaces; the volume above them is unoccupied. The crawlers are numerous but they exist only in a thin layer at the interface of stone and plant. Step back and the whole scene has the quality of a decorated emptiness — life applied to surfaces, swarming in defined bands, leaving the great majority of the space bare and resonant and void. The bowl is mostly air. Mostly nothing. The life is a skin on it, a vibrating membrane stretched across the emptiness, and the sound fills the gap between, gives the void its substance.

---

Now. The structures.

There are three of them in the bowl, and they are why you are here, or why anyone would be here, or why the concept of "here" has any meaning beyond the ecological.

The first is on the western terrace, half-embedded in a thick growth of the dark-red cascade plants. It is a column, or was a column — roughly two meters tall, the diameter of a human torso, made of a material that is neither stone nor metal nor ceramic. It is warm to the touch regardless of the ambient temperature. Its surface is covered in a geometric pattern that shifts if you look at it from different angles — not an optical illusion, but an actual reconfiguration of the surface topology at a scale too fine to see. Run your fingers across it and the texture changes under your skin. The plants growing around its base are different from the others: larger, more deeply pigmented, vibrating at a frequency slightly out of phase with the ambient roar. They are feeding on the column, or the column is feeding them, or they are elements of the same system.

The second structure is near the center of the bowl, where the terraces flatten into a rough floor of eroded stone. It is a ring — an open circle of the same warm material, laid flat against the ground, three meters in diameter, perhaps fifteen centimeters high. Inside the ring, the stone is different. Smoother. Younger-looking, as though it is being renewed. Small crawlers avoid the ring's interior. The flat-bodied fliers, the millions that form the lowest stratum, will not cross its boundary. But the vegetation inside the ring is the lushest in the entire bowl — a single, unbroken mat of silver-blue rosettes, so tightly packed they form what looks like a living carpet, trembling in unison.

The third structure is the most difficult. It is on the eastern ridge, at the lip of the bowl, and from most angles it is invisible. You see it only when you stand in a specific spot near the ring — and then it appears suddenly, a tall, narrow arch of the warm material, framing a section of the deep blue sky. Through the arch, the sky looks *different*. Not a different color. A different *texture*. As though the sky seen through that frame has a higher resolution than the sky around it, contains more information per unit of visual field. Stare too long and vertigo sets in — not the vertigo of height, but the vertigo of depth, as though the arch opens onto a distance that the geometry of the bowl cannot contain.

These three structures are not ruins. They are not decaying. They are not old in the way the stone is old. They are *functioning*, though their function is opaque. The column reconfigures. The ring maintains its interior. The arch frames its impossible depth. They are doing something — something sustained, something intentional, something that operates on principles that resist analysis. The technology is not hidden; it is *exposed*, present, visible, tactile. It simply does not explain itself. It is engineering that has moved past the need for legibility — or engineering by something that never shared a common basis of legibility with you in the first place.

---

And underneath all of this, *the turbulence*.

Nothing here is stable. Nothing is still. The swarms reconfigure constantly — one species surges, another collapses, a third explodes outward from a point source in a radial burst that scatters the flat-bodied millions into temporary vortices. The vegetation responds to the shifting sound pressure, growing measurably in minutes in zones where the noise intensifies, retracting and curling where it drops. The crawlers erupt in waves, their territorial patterns disrupted by some signal you can't detect, and for thirty seconds a section of terrace that was bare stone becomes a solid mass of armored bodies before they scatter back into their crevices. The cascade plants on the terrace lips sway and whip in currents of air generated by no wind — only the collective downdraft of wings.

The column's geometric pattern accelerates, shifts, spirals. The ring hums at a frequency that makes the rosettes inside it pulse. The arch flickers — not visually, but *spatially*, as though for an instant it occupies two positions simultaneously before collapsing back.

It is all accelerating. Or decelerating. Or oscillating between states of acceleration and deceleration so rapidly that the difference becomes meaningless. The system — bowl, terraces, swarms, plants, crawlers, structures — is a single coupled mechanism in a state of permanent, roaring, dynamic instability, held together not by equilibrium but by the sheer velocity of its own fluctuations. Stability through chaos. Persistence through constant collapse and reformation.

The sound is so loud now that your body has stopped trying to hear it and started simply accepting it as a physical medium, like temperature, like gravity. You are immersed in noise the way a diver is immersed in water. It presses against you. It has texture and direction and current. It flows around the column and eddies behind the ridgeline and pools in the deeper crevices where the crawlers hide. It is not background. It is substrate. It is what this place is *made of*.

The air is cool. The light is even. The stone is dry and ancient and smooth under your hand.

Everything is screaming, and everything is alive, and the emptiness between the screams is vast, and the three structures hum with purpose you will never decode, and the whole bowl rings like a struck bell that will never stop ringing, and something — you cannot say what — is *paying attention*.
