# The Plateau of Leng-Katal

---

The darkness here is not the darkness of night. There is no night because there is no day. The sky above Leng-Katal is a permanent ceiling of dust so old it has forgotten what lifted it — a mineral haze fixed at some indeterminate altitude, particulate matter suspended in configurations that have not shifted in centuries, maybe longer. It does not swirl or drift. It simply *is*, a static ochre void pressing down above everything, faintly luminous in no identifiable spectrum, offering a sourceless, nearly useless dim glow — enough to distinguish shape from emptiness, edge from open ground, but never enough to cast a shadow.

Beneath that permanent sky, the plateau extends.

---

It is an immense elevated surface, high above whatever lowlands may or may not still exist beyond its eroded rims. The plateau's edge is theoretical — out there, somewhere to the south and east and in every direction, the ground must eventually descend, but you'd walk for days before finding the lip, and even then you might not recognize it. Erosion has worked this place for so long that the concept of a cliff has been sanded into a long, imperceptible gradient. Everything here slopes by fractions of degrees. The terrain is a masterwork of entropy: what were once mountains have been reduced to low, whale-backed ridges no taller than a few dozen meters, their forms so smoothed they resemble geological sighs. Between them, vast shallow basins stretch, not concave enough to hold water even if water existed here. The stone is a dense ferric aggregate, rust-colored tending to dark maroon in the low light, its surface covered in a fine alkite powder that hasn't been disturbed by wind — because there is no wind. There has been no wind for a very long time. The powder lies in undisturbed sheets across the ground, recording no footprints, no ripples, no evidence of atmospheric movement whatsoever.

The warmth is ambient, rising from the plateau itself. The rock retains heat from sources deep in the planet's mantle, conducted up through kilometers of compressed and ancient strata. The air — and there is air, thin but breathable in a way that leaves you slightly oxygen-hungry, as though each breath delivers nine-tenths of what you need — sits at a temperature that might register as a mild summer evening in a place that had summers. It is warm the way a long-cooled engine block is warm: residually, fundamentally, without any suggestion of increasing or decreasing. Thermal equilibrium was achieved here long ago.

No vegetation. Not a lichen, not a spore, not a crust of anything photosynthetic or chemosynthetic clinging to the rock. The stone is bare of biology. If life ever existed on this surface, it left no fossils that erosion has spared, or it was the kind of life that leaves no fossils — or it was never here at all. The ground has the sterility of a place that predates biology, except it doesn't predate it; it *post*-dates it, or sidesteps it entirely. The mineral world is complete unto itself.

---

And yet.

There is a city.

---

Leng-Katal proper occupies a region where several of the low eroded ridges converge, their remnant forms creating a kind of natural amphitheater — though "amphitheater" implies too much drama for these gentle, worn elevations. Call it a broad, shallow gathering of the terrain. Across this area, covering perhaps twelve square kilometers, structures stand.

They are built from the plateau's own stone, quarried from it — or possibly *extruded* from it, because the joints between building and bedrock are sometimes invisible, as though the structures grew upward from the ground by some process of directed lithic accumulation. The architecture is industrial in character but stripped of any aesthetic consideration to such a degree that it circles back around to a kind of severe beauty. Rectangular forms predominate. Walls are thick, load-bearing, featureless on their exteriors except where large circular ports — sealed with mineral shutters or open to darkness — punctuate them at irregular intervals. The buildings range from single-story warehouse-like structures stretching hundreds of meters in length to denser clusters of blocky towers, none taller than six or seven stories, their flat roofs all at slightly different heights, creating a low skyline that reads as a bar graph of unknown data.

Between the structures: space. This is not a dense city. It is a sparse one, a city that seems to have been designed with the expectation that the empty ground between buildings would serve some purpose that never materialized — or whose purpose is simply *interval*, the architectural equivalent of silence between notes. Wide, perfectly flat expanses of alkite-dusted stone separate building from building, crossed by rail lines.

The rails are the most prominent infrastructure. Narrow-gauge tracks, set into shallow channels cut or worn into the bedrock, run in long straight lines and sweeping curves across the plateau's surface, connecting structures, running between the warehouse buildings, converging at switching yards where dozens of tracks merge and diverge in patterns of cold geometric complexity. The rails themselves are made of a dull alloy — not quite iron, not quite anything identifiable — and they are worn smooth, their upper surfaces polished to a matte gleam by use, though nothing currently runs on them. At the switching yards, manual lever assemblies stand upright, their handles in various positions, frozen mid-configuration as though someone walked away from the controls in the middle of an operation and never returned.

Along the rail lines, at intervals, stand small mechanical structures: signal posts with rotating drum indicators rather than lights, their drums stopped at various positions displaying symbols in a notation system that might be directional or might be something else entirely. Beside them, pneumatic buffer assemblies — pistons and cylinders meant to absorb the momentum of approaching rail cars — wait in their extended positions, never compressed. Everything is engineered for function. Everything is built to last in a way that suggests the builders understood deep time, understood that their materials would need to outlast not years or decades but epochs. And they were right. The machinery, though still, is not rusted or degraded. The alloys have held. The mechanisms, if actuated, might still move.

---

There are other machines. Inside the larger warehouse structures — and you can see into some of them through the open circular ports, if you press close and let your eyes adjust to the deeper darkness within — rows of devices stand on the bare stone floors. Presses, maybe. Or mills. Or something for which the industrial vocabulary of other worlds has no precise term. They have the anatomy of machines meant to *process*: intake apertures, compression chambers, output chutes. They are scaled for bulk material handling, with hoppers large enough to accept cubic meters of raw mineral feed. But the hoppers are empty. The output chutes terminate over collection bays that hold nothing. The machines are complete, operational in principle, and idle.

Some of the structures contain different equipment — tall cylindrical tanks connected by manifold pipe systems, valves at every junction, pressure gauges with needles resting at zero. Distillation columns, perhaps, or fractional separators, designed to extract specific mineral compounds from processed slurry. But there is no slurry. There is no water to make slurry. Whatever liquid medium these systems were designed to work with, it is not present and shows no residue of ever having been present. The tanks are dry. The pipes are dry. The valves are clean.

And in one district — toward the northern convergence of ridges — a cluster of structures stands taller and more closely spaced than elsewhere, their walls thicker, their circular ports sealed with what appear to be pressure-rated mineral shutters bolted from the outside. These buildings hum.

---

The hum is the primary sound of Leng-Katal. It is deep, tonal, barely above the threshold of hearing, and it does not vary. It does not pulse or cycle or modulate. It is a single sustained frequency rising from beneath the sealed buildings in the northern district, transmitted through the bedrock so that if you place your hand on the ground anywhere within a kilometer of that cluster, you can feel it in your palm — a vibration so steady it seems less like the operation of a machine and more like a property of the stone itself, as though the plateau is resonating at its natural frequency and always has been.

The hum might be the only evidence that anything in this city is still active.

Beyond it, the soundscape is a study in almost-silence. No wind. No biological noise — no insects, no calls, no rustling. Occasionally, and so rarely that you might wait hours between instances, a faint *tik* sounds from somewhere in the rail-switching yards — a thermal expansion click, metal adjusting to infinitesimal temperature fluctuations in the planet's crust, a sound so small and isolated it seems to emphasize the silence rather than interrupt it. The alkite powder absorbs footfalls. Your own breathing becomes the loudest thing you've ever heard.

---

Fauna: almost none. But not absolutely none.

In the deeper darkness inside some of the warehouse structures, where the processing machines stand in their permanent readiness, something moves occasionally. You don't see it so much as sense its displacement — a marginal redistribution of the already-dim ambient glow, a shape passing between you and a far wall. They are called *threadwights* by no one, because no one is here to name them, but the word arrives in the mind anyway. They are elongated, filamentary, somewhere between solid and suggestion. They move slowly along the floors, occasionally ascending the sides of machines with a motion that implies adhesion rather than climbing. They appear to feed on — or at least orient toward — the mineral dust that accumulates in the seams and joints of the machinery. Whether they are organisms in any conventional sense is unclear. They might be mineral accretions that have achieved a rudimentary motility over geological time. They might be something else, something for which biology and mineralogy are both inadequate frameworks.

They avoid open spaces. They do not appear outside the structures. They make no sound.

---

Here is what is strange about Leng-Katal, beyond the obvious:

The city shows no evidence of habitation. Not past habitation — *any* habitation. There are no dwellings. No residential structures, no dormitories, no barracks, no spaces designed for biological comfort or rest. The buildings are all industrial — processing, storage, transport infrastructure. There are no doors scaled to a body. The circular ports in the walls are too large for doorways and too uniformly placed to be entrances; they are ventilation or observation ports, designed to be looked *through*, not walked through. The rail system connects machine-buildings to machine-buildings, switching yards to processing facilities, storage warehouses to output bays. It is a complete industrial infrastructure with no provision for workers, operators, inhabitants, or visitors. The lever assemblies at the switching yards are the only mechanisms that appear designed for manual actuation, and even they are ambiguous — they could be manually operated, or they could be calibration interfaces for an automated system that simply happens to use a lever-and-fulcrum format.

The city was not abandoned. It was never occupied. It was built — by something, at some point in the deep past — as a pure system, an industrial process laid out in stone and metal across twelve kilometers of eroded plateau, and it has stood here in its readiness ever since, waiting to process materials that have never arrived through machines that have never been started, storing outputs that have never been produced, transporting goods along rail lines that have never carried a load.

Except for the hum. The hum in the northern district. Something there is running. Something there has been running for longer than the erosion patterns on the surrounding ridges can account for, running so continuously and so consistently that it has become part of the plateau's identity, indistinguishable from geology.

---

Stand at the center of Leng-Katal, on the wide alkite-dusted ground between two warehouse rows, and look outward in any direction. The low buildings recede into the dim murk. Beyond them, the ancient ridges rise gently, their eroded forms barely visible against the static dust-sky. The warmth rises through the soles of your feet. The hum fills the bone behind your ear. A signal drum on a nearby rail post displays a symbol you almost recognize — a circle with a single radial line — and you realize you have been looking at it for a long time, longer than you intended, and nothing has changed, nothing is going to change, and the symbol means *ready*, has always meant *ready*, and the machines are ready, and the rails are ready, and the city is ready, and it has been ready for so long that readiness has become indistinguishable from permanence, and permanence has become indistinguishable from purpose, and you are standing in a place that is *complete* — not abandoned, not waiting, not dead, but complete — a finished sentence in a language that never needed a speaker.

The threadwights move in the dark of the nearest building. The signal drum holds its position. The hum continues. The dust does not settle, because it settled long ago. Everything is exactly as it is.
