# The Drowned Canopy

---

There is a world where the ocean rose and the forest refused to die.

Not refused — that implies effort, defiance, something that could be narrated as a story with a beginning. This is older than refusal. This is a place that simply *is*, and has been, and shows no memory of having ever become. The equilibrium is total. Nothing here is becoming anything else. Nothing here is decaying. Nothing here is growing. The world arrived at this state and stopped, the way a bell stops ringing but the shape of the bell remains.

---

Imagine a plateau — no, a chain of plateaus, high tablelands rising two thousand meters above a sea floor that no longer matters because the sea floor is ten thousand meters down and the water goes all the way up. The plateaus are submerged. Everything is submerged. The water begins at the crust of the world and ends somewhere above where the atmosphere should be, a gradient so slow from liquid to vapor that there is no surface, no bright mirror where water meets sky — only a thickening, a thinning, a place where water becomes more like water and eventually becomes water completely. Light enters from above as a broad, directionless glow, the color of old paper, yellow-white, diffused through hundreds of meters of increasingly dense atmosphere-ocean. By the time it reaches the canopy it is still generous, still legible, but it arrives from everywhere at once. Shadows here are suggestions. Nothing is backlit. Everything is simply *lit*.

The plateaus are covered in forest.

Not covered — *are* forest. The stone has long since been swallowed. The root systems of trees the size of stadiums have threaded through every fissure and joint in the basalt until the basalt is a kind of skeleton that the roots wear. In places, where the rock has dissolved entirely — two on a scale of erosion, old enough to have settled, weathered enough to have negotiated — the roots hold the shape of cliffs that no longer exist, cantilevers of living wood suspended over gorges that are themselves walled with root and trunk. The trees grow from the trees that grew from the stone that the trees dissolved. It is a closed system. The forest is substrate, architecture, geology, and ecology simultaneously.

And every branch, every root, every frond and tendril and vast horizontal limb exists underwater.

---

Here is what it looks like:

Rise up from the plateau floor — which is itself a mat of roots so dense and so ancient they have lignified into something harder than the rock they replaced — and you pass through a layer of enormous trunks. They are pale. Bark in this world is smooth, almost ceramic in texture, white and gray-green, because there is no wind to scar it and no seasons to crack it. The trunks rise in columns spaced far apart — *sparse*, relative to the canopy above, the distances between them measured in hundreds of meters. You could drift between them for an hour and touch nothing. The water in this understory layer is cold — cool, at least, the deep plateau chill that never warms — and dark relative to above, the color of jade, and utterly still.

Sound carries. That is the first strangeness.

Water is a better medium for sound than air, and this is a world of water, and the sound is *enormous*. It comes from above, from the canopy, from the region a thousand meters overhead where the branches finally spread and interlock and the forest becomes a single continuous organism stretching to every horizon. The sound is mechanical. It is rhythmic. It is the sound of vast biological engines.

---

Here is what the canopy is:

The trees, at their crowns, do not leaf. They *pump*. Each crown is a radial array of hollow tubes — imagine organ pipes the diameter of railway tunnels, fanning outward from the trunk's apex in a crown sixty, eighty, a hundred meters across. The tubes are living wood, their interiors lined with cilia the size of a human arm, and they move water. Continuously. They draw the cold, nutrient-poor water of the deep understory upward through the root system, through the trunk's vascular network, and express it outward through the crown tubes in vast slow jets that carry dissolved minerals, organic particulate, and heat — captured from somewhere deep in the plateau's volcanic bones — out into the mid-water column.

This is the engine. This is the technology.

It is not built. Nothing here is built. There are no structures, no artifacts, no signs of intention or design. But the trees are *industrial*. They operate at the scale of factories. Each crown processes thousands of cubic meters of water per hour, and there are millions of crowns, and the cumulative effect is a current system — a forest-driven ocean circulation — that spans the entire plateau chain. Warm, mineral-rich water rises from the canopy. Cool, clean water sinks along the plateau edges. Weather forms in the water column above: thermal plumes, vast slow vortices, regions of upwelling and downwelling that are as stable and permanent as trade winds. The forest has manufactured its own ocean climate, and the ocean climate sustains the forest, and neither is cause and neither is effect.

The sound of this — the deep, resonant thrum of millions of crown-tubes expressing water, the harmonic drone of cilia beating in synchrony, the subsonic pulse of thermal columns rising through kilometers of water — is the voice of the plateau. It is loud the way a waterfall is loud if you live inside it. It is not harsh. It is bassy, pervasive, felt in the chest, in the bones. It has overtones. In places where the crown-tubes of adjacent trees are tuned to slightly different frequencies by differences in diameter or wood density, the interference patterns create standing waves in the water — visible as shimmering curtains of vibration where suspended particles align into luminous sheets. These shimmer-walls hang between the canopy crowns like veils, permanent, unmoving, gorgeous, and completely incidental.

---

The fauna have adapted to the sound.

Schools of elongate, eel-like creatures — silver, translucent, their internal organs visible as dark shapes behind gill plates the size of dinner tables — navigate by the drone. They use the standing waves as landmarks, threading between the shimmer-walls on migration routes that never change because the shimmer-walls never move because the trees never change because nothing here ever changes. The eels are abundant in the mid-water, between canopy and understory, where the thermal plumes create rivers of warmth in the cold ambient water. They are not alone. Clusters of sessile animals — soft-bodied, filter-feeding, resembling anemones but scaled up to the size of weather balloons — cling to the undersides of the great horizontal branches, their tentacles trailing forty meters downward into the current, catching particulate. Small armored grazers, like crustaceans but with flexible calcite shells that ring musically when they scrape against bark, move in herds across the trunk surfaces, feeding on the biofilm that coats every square centimeter of wood. Their scraping adds a higher-frequency layer to the soundscape: a crackling, a chittering, a texture of small industry laid over the great drone.

Birds — not birds, but the analogue — are bladder-bodied, neutrally buoyant, trailing long ribbon-fins that undulate slowly as they maneuver between trunks. They are the size of automobiles. They are few. They hang in the understory gloom like lanterns, bioluminescent patches along their flanks glowing a faint amber that does not compete with the daylight from above but becomes visible in the shadows of the root-mat below. They eat the grazers when the grazers stray too far from the bark. They have been eating the same species of grazer in the same region of trunk for longer than any measurement could capture.

Nothing here is new. Nothing here is old. The system has no age because age implies change, and change is the thing this world has finished with.

---

From above — from the perspective of the light filtering down — the canopy is a continent of pale wood and dark tube-mouths, stretching beyond resolution in every direction, the crowns so regular in their spacing that from sufficient distance they resemble a textile, a weave, a fabric of living machinery laid across the drowned highlands. The spaces between crowns are the sparse places, the voids, where the water is open and the shimmer-walls hang and the eels pass in silver ribbons. The spaces are vast by any human standard — canyons of clear water hundreds of meters wide — but they are the minority. The canopy is the dominant fact. It fills the world. It *is* the world. The plateau beneath it is an afterthought, a scaffold that the forest has absorbed and replaced.

And the sound — the deep, permanent, world-filling sound — does not echo, because there are no walls, and does not fade, because the source is everywhere, and does not change, because nothing here changes.

The cool water moves through the white trunks.
The shimmer-walls hang in their ancient positions.
The crown-tubes pump.
The eels follow their routes.
The light comes down.

It has always been like this. There is nothing in this world that remembers otherwise. Even the word *always* is wrong — it implies a duration, a persistence through time, and time is not something this place experiences. It is simply in its state. Equilibrium is not a condition it achieved. Equilibrium is what it *is*.

You drift between the trunks. The cold water holds you. The drone fills every cavity in your body. Somewhere far above, the canopy breathes its thermal rivers into the deep water. Somewhere far below, the roots grip the ghost of stone.

The forest does not notice you. It does not notice anything. It is too busy being permanent.
