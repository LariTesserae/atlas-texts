# The Hydroponic Reliquary

---

You are standing inside a room that is also a mountain.

Not on a mountain. Inside one. Someone built a room inside a peak—or built the peak around the room, it's hard to tell which came first. The walls are concrete and volcanic glass, poured in alternating layers so that cross-sections shimmer with a dark translucence between bands of matte grey. The ceiling is perhaps nine meters overhead, slightly domed, and every surface sweats.

Water is everywhere. Not flowing—*present*. The air is so warm and so saturated that breathing feels like drinking something thin. Condensation beads on every hard edge, pools in every joint and seam. The floor, which is textured steel grating over deeper reservoirs, gleams with a perpetual wet film. If you press your palm flat against the concrete wall, it comes away with a cool print of moisture, and the wall's surface looks briefly different—darker, like skin after a bruise—before the water gathers again.

There are no windows. Light comes from panels recessed behind milky polymer casings bolted into the ceiling's curve, industrial-bright and even, the color of overcast noon. It erases shadows almost entirely. Things here don't cast darkness so much as suggest slightly less brightness on one side. This flatness of light makes the room feel both clinical and unreal, as though everything exists at the same visual distance.

Now: the plants.

---

Every vertical surface is claimed. The walls are fitted with columnar hydroponic arrays—powder-coated steel frames housing hundreds of interlocking pods, each pod a fist-sized cradle of engineered ceramic foam from which vegetation erupts with an aggression that feels almost hostile. These are not decorative plantings. They are dense, competitive, entangled systems that have exceeded their allotted geometries and begun to merge.

Vines with leaves the shape of lancet windows crawl between pods, binding one column to the next with living cable. Ferns—enormous, prehistoric-seeming ferns with fronds that uncurl in spirals wider than your arm span—lean outward from their cradles and droop under their own wet weight, their tips brushing the grated floor. Moss blankets every surface the hydroponic frames don't cover, and in places it has begun to cover those too, growing over the steel and softening its edges into something fibrous and uncertain. The moss is several species at once, a patchwork of textures—some velvety, some wiry, some encrusted with tiny structures like mineral deposits that catch the flat light and hold it.

Between the large arrays, smaller systems are bolted to every remaining surface: wall-mounted trays of dense ground cover, pendant baskets dripping chains of some succulent whose leaves are swollen and nearly translucent with stored water, vertical tubes where root systems have grown so thick they've cracked their housings and now spill outward in pale, reaching tangles. Roots hang in open air, collecting moisture directly from the atmosphere. They are white and faintly pink at their tips, and they move—not visibly, not in any moment you can catch, but if you look away and look back, they've shifted. Over hours, they creep toward the nearest water source with a slow, vegetable certainty.

At the center of the room stands the main reservoir: an open-topped tank of welded steel, perhaps three meters across, its interior walls green with algae. The water inside is warm—you can feel its heat radiating from a meter away—and almost opaque with dissolved nutrients, a murky jade color. Pumps hum somewhere below the grating, circulating this water through the walls, up into the arrays, recollecting it through drainage channels cut into the floor. The system is a closed loop. Nothing leaves.

---

There is almost no empty space. The room—perhaps fifteen meters across at its widest—is so filled with apparatus and growth that moving through it requires turning sideways, ducking under fronds, stepping over root-choked drainage channels. The air between surfaces is itself occupied: mist, humidity, the faintest aerosol of nutrients being atomized by nozzles recessed into the ceiling panels. You are breathing the room's circulatory system. The plants are breathing you back.

The density creates a strange acoustic. Sound doesn't echo—it dies immediately, absorbed by the biomass. Your footsteps on the wet grating make small, precise metallic ticks that vanish as soon as they're made. The pumps beneath the floor produce a vibration more felt than heard, a deep organ-note just below the threshold of perception. Occasionally, a drop of condensation falls from a leaf tip to the reservoir and makes a single clear note. Then silence reasserts itself. The quiet is not empty. It is *padded*.

And here is the strangeness: the room is high up. Very high. You know this—you *feel* it—though there are no windows to confirm it. The atmospheric pressure is subtly wrong. Your ears want to pop. The warm, wet air feels thinner than it should, as though the humidity is compensating for an altitude that would otherwise make this space cold and dry. Whatever engineering maintains this environment is working against the mountain, forcing a lowland tropical density into a place where it has no business existing. The concrete walls are warm to the touch because they are heated from within, a network of pipes carrying thermal fluid just beneath the surface, and the condensation that forms on them is being *manufactured*—the mountain's cold stone on the other side of those walls would freeze this water in minutes if the systems stopped.

The room is young. The concrete is unweathered, its edges still sharp where it meets the steel grating. The hydroponic frames still bear manufacturer stamps, legible beneath the moss—part numbers, load ratings, a logo you don't recognize. The welds on the central reservoir are clean and raised, not yet ground smooth by age. Whatever process of construction brought this place into being completed recently, perhaps months ago, perhaps a year. But the vegetation has moved faster than expected. The growth rate in this closed system, fed continuously, bathed in warmth and moisture and unbroken light, has outpaced the architecture. The plants are ahead of schedule.

---

There are no animals. No insects, no mites, no nematodes in the root systems. This is conspicuous once you notice it. A system this warm and wet and organic should be teeming—should hum with small life. But the air is sterile. The water in the reservoir, despite its murky color, is biologically simple: algae, dissolved mineral salts, the metabolic outputs of the plants themselves, and nothing else. Whatever filtration or sterilization process is running through those sub-floor pumps, it is thorough. The plants exist in a world without pollinators, without decomposers, without predators. They grow and they do not fruit. They spread and nothing eats them. When a leaf dies—and you can see a few, yellowed at the margins, curling—it falls to the grating and slowly dissolves into the water below, its nutrients recaptured and fed back through the system.

This is ecology reduced to chemistry. There is something severe about it, something monastic. The lushness is not wild—it is *manufactured* lushness, abundance generated by total control. And yet the plants resist the framing. They are exuberant beyond specification. They crack their housings. They bridge gaps they were not meant to bridge. They find moisture in places the system did not intend to provide moisture. The moss in particular has colonized surfaces that were meant to remain bare—access panels, valve handles, the underside of the ceiling panels where heat collects. It grows where it is not wanted with the same mute persistence as anywhere else.

---

You could stay here a long time and almost nothing would happen. The light would not change. No day, no night—just the flat industrial noon. The pumps would hum. The roots would creep their imperceptible creep. A fern frond would unfurl over a period of days, one more green arch in a room already saturated with green. The condensation would form and fall, form and fall. The reservoir would circulate. Everything moves, but on timescales that make human attention feel rushed, irrelevant.

The room does not need you. It barely registers you. You are the only warm-blooded thing in this wet concrete chapel bolted into the side of a peak above the clouds, and the room is not impressed. It has its water, its light, its slow green industry. You are a brief perturbation in the humidity levels, a shadow that the flat light nearly erases.

Somewhere beyond the heated walls, the mountain is cold and dry and high. Wind scours the summit. The air is thin and tastes of nothing. But in here, the air is thick as broth, and the walls drip, and the roots reach, and the pumps push warm water through a body that has no heart but does, undeniably, have a pulse.
