# The Growing City

It shouldn't be this quiet.

That's the first wrong thing. You stand at the edge of a valley — shallow, wide, stretching maybe nine or ten kilometers across — and what fills it is *dense*. Packed. A city, or the becoming of a city, crammed so tightly that from here the structures blur into a single mottled surface, like lichen on a stone. Rooftops, smokestacks, gantries, bridges between buildings, bridges between bridges. Condensation beads on every surface, a fine wet film that catches the flat, ordinary daylight and gives everything the sheen of something just-born. The air is cold — properly cold, a wet-wool-and-metal cold that sits in your lungs — but the valley floor steams faintly in places, and you can't tell whether it's warmth rising from below or just the sheer density of structures breathing their own moisture back into the sky.

But it's quiet. A city this size should roar. Instead there's a low, pervasive hum, more felt than heard, like the subsonic vibration of a transformer you can't find. Occasionally a sharper sound rises — a ratcheting click, a long metallic sigh — and then subsides. The silence isn't the silence of emptiness. It's the silence of concentration.

---

The city is building itself.

Not metaphorically. You watch a factory near the valley's eastern rim — a blocky thing, cast iron and riveted plates, three smokestacks venting something that isn't quite smoke but a pale, damp exhalation — and from its open bay doors a structure is emerging. Not being carried out. *Emerging.* Like a shoot from a seed. A framework of iron girders extends itself one joint at a time, each new node clicking into place with that ratcheting sound, and behind the skeleton comes the skin: brickwork that assembles in quick cascading waves, mortar appearing as a grey paste that weeps from the joints and then hardens, windows that grow from the center outward like ice crystals forming on glass. The building — it will be a building, a four-story something with a pitched roof and what looks like it might become a clock tower — is perhaps a third complete. It takes about ninety seconds for a new row of bricks to finish a course. The whole thing lists slightly to the left, as though the ground beneath it hasn't fully decided to be level yet.

Because the ground is new too.

The valley floor is raw geology in the most literal sense. Not ancient, not weathered — *recent*. The stone here has the quality of something that cooled last week. Basalt, mostly, dark and fine-grained, but seamed with intrusions of something paler, almost chalky, that crumbles when the city's foundations press into it. The terrain hasn't had time to develop a proper soil layer, and yet —

And yet the vegetation is extraordinary.

---

Every gap. Every crack. Every windowsill, gutter, rooftop margin, alley floor, chimney base. The city is lush with a cold-weather greenery that defies the rawness of the ground it sits on. Mosses dominate — great pillowy mounds of sphagnum in shades from acid green to rust, cushion mosses that coat entire walls in velvet, Grimmia clinging to the basalt in silver-grey rosettes. But there are ferns too, and they're large, disproportionately large, fronds a meter long unfurling from between bricks with a muscular slowness you can almost see in real time. Liverworts plaster themselves across factory floors in overlapping scales. Lichens — crusts and foliose fans in sulfur yellow and slate blue — colonize every metallic surface within days of its formation, if the patina of their growth is any guide. In the deeper alleys, where condensation pools in the basalt depressions, there are sedges and rushes, tight clumps of cold-tolerant grasses, and something that might be a horsetail if horsetails grew as thick as your forearm and had a faintly bioluminescent stripe running their length.

The vegetation doesn't compete with the construction. They occupy the same space simultaneously, interpenetrated, as though the city's blueprint includes the moss.

Maybe it does. You watch a wall assemble itself and notice that the bricks arrive already furred — a thin green nap on their upper surfaces, as though they were grown somewhere damp and warm before being slotted into place. The mortar, when it weeps, carries spores. The city seeds itself as it builds.

---

The fauna fits into the crevices. Literally.

Small things, mostly. Beetles with iridescent carapaces — blue-black, oil-slick green — that navigate the moss landscapes in purposeful lines. You see them carrying fragments of something pale and chalky, those crumbly mineral intrusions from the valley rock, transporting them to unknown destinations in the walls. Are they part of the construction process? They move with the unhurried determination of workers who know exactly what they're doing. Fat, slow moths — grey and brown, densely furred against the cold — rest on warm chimney surfaces with their wings spread, as though charging themselves. In the pooling condensation along the gutters, you spot amphibians: tiny, translucent-skinned salamanders the color of wet basalt, their bodies so still they might be mineral until one flicks its tail and vanishes into a drainpipe.

There are birds, but they're strange. Corvid-shaped, roughly, but too large in the head and too small in the wing, hopping along ridgelines and gantries in groups of four or five, watching the construction with what you'd swear is supervisory attention. One tilts its head at a misaligned beam. The beam adjusts. You're not sure whether correlation is causation, but you file it away.

No mammals that you can see. Nothing warm-blooded except the birds, if the birds are warm-blooded, which you're not prepared to assume.

---

The turbulence becomes clear once you stop looking at individual structures and watch the city as a whole.

It's churning.

The eastern edge grows. The western edge — you squint — dismantles. A row of buildings two kilometers away is folding inward, collapsing not in ruin but in *orderly retraction*, the bricks disaggregating in the reverse of how they assembled, the iron skeletons retracting joint by joint back into a factory bay that swallows them without ceremony. The vegetation on those retreating walls doesn't die; it peels away in mats and rolls itself inward, fungal threads drawing the moss back like a green tide receding. The ground left behind is smooth, washed by condensation, and already the pale mineral intrusions are rising through it in new patterns, marking where the next iteration will root.

The city is migrating. Or reforming. Or both. Its western face dissolves as its eastern face extends, so the whole mass creeps across the valley like a slow wave, like a slime mold exploring a petri dish. No — more purposeful than that. There's a pattern you can almost grasp if you watch long enough. Certain structures persist: the factories, the clock towers (there are several, you realize, and they all show different times), the wider streets that function as arteries. Everything else is substrate. Temporary. The city tries configurations the way a river tries paths through a floodplain, except rivers don't remember their failures.

This city does. You can see it in the ground. Faint outlines of previous foundations, the ghosts of walls, a palimpsest of floor plans layered on the raw basalt. The mosses grow thickest along these old traces, as though the memory of a building is itself a kind of nutrient.

---

You walk in.

The streets are narrow and packed — not with people, because there are no people, but with *presence*. Every surface is occupied, active, textured. Pipes run along building faces carrying fluids you can't identify — one is warm to the touch, another vibrates faintly, a third sweats beads of something oily and aromatic, like pine resin mixed with machine grease. Gears are visible through grated floors, turning slowly in substrata you didn't know existed. The buildings are taller than they looked from outside, four and five stories, leaning toward each other across the alleys until the sky is a crooked line of grey-white light far above. The cold is sharper here, concentrated by the shade, and the condensation thickens on your skin.

The quiet deepens too. That hum is omnipresent but directionless, and beneath it you begin to hear the smaller sounds: the tick of a brick finding its neighbor, the sigh of mortar curing, the tiny scritch of beetle legs on stone. A clock tower nearby strikes something that isn't an hour — a single low tone that resonates in the buildings and seems to prompt a shift. The street ahead of you is rearranging. A wall on the left is retreating by six inches; the wall on the right advances the same. The alley narrows, then widens, then narrows again, breathing. You stop walking and wait for it to settle. The mosses on the walls ripple like fur.

A corvid hops onto a windowsill above you and tilts its oversized head. Its eye is not a bird's eye. It's faceted, compound, insectile — a fly's eye in a crow's skull. It watches you with every facet.

---

The light is ordinary. That might be the strangest thing of all.

Daylight, flat and democratic, the kind of light you get under a high, even overcast, falls into this self-building, self-devouring, creeping industrial organism of a city, and it just looks like a city. If you took a photograph, cropped out the edges where buildings are half-formed or half-consumed, ignored the lack of people, you might think: some industrial town, northern somewhere, a bit overgrown, a bit quiet. A Wednesday. The mundanity of the light makes everything else more unsettling. There is no dramatic coloring, no unearthly glow, no darkness to hide the wrongness in. Just a cold, damp, ordinary day in a city that has no inhabitants, no history, no reason you can identify, and is building itself across a valley that came into existence so recently the rocks haven't learned to crack.

Somewhere behind you, the west side dissolves. Somewhere ahead, a new district congeals. The beetles carry their chalk. The salamanders wait in the drains. The mosses thicken. The clock towers disagree. The city moves eastward for reasons it hasn't shared, across geology that isn't finished yet, and the only sound is the hum, the click, the settling of cold stone against cold stone, and the flap of wings that belong to something watching.
