# The Thirstwood Plateau

---

There is no water here. There has not been water here for an age so long that the stone itself has forgotten what dissolution feels like, has settled into its final mineralogical confession: a plateau of compacted calcium silicate, smoothed by wind and root-pressure into undulating bone-colored shelves that extend in every direction under a bruised, coppery sky. The erosion that shaped this place was not recent. It ended. The rock stopped changing. And then the trees came, and the trees did not need water at all.

---

They rise from the dry stone in clusters and solitary towers, separated by wide lanes of bare mineral flat — a strange open parkland where the gaps between trunks measure thirty, fifty, sometimes a hundred meters of empty ground. The spacing gives the plateau a quality of intentional arrangement, as though someone planned sight lines, processional avenues, breathing room. And maybe someone did. But the trees themselves are the dominant fact of the world, and they are not trees in any way your body knows how to interpret on first encounter.

They are lignite-colored at the base, their bark an ashen gray-violet that cracks in tessellated hexagonal plates. Each trunk is massive — fifteen meters across at the root crown — and they taper only slowly, rising forty, sixty meters before they begin to branch, and when they branch, they *ramify* with a geometric enthusiasm that fills the upper air completely. The canopy is not a dome. It is a ceiling. A continuous, breathing, groaning ceiling that stretches from horizon to horizon, supported on these widely spaced columns of living wood, and between the columns the ground is open, bare, dimly lit. You are standing in a cathedral with no walls, a structure of pure roof, and the roof is alive.

The canopy is where the density lives. Down here, things are sparse and echoing. Up there — a hundred meters overhead — the branches interlock and fuse. Literally fuse: where two branches from neighboring trees meet, their cambium layers merge through a process that has nothing to do with grafting as botany understands it. The tissue at the junction points glows faintly with a cool violet bioluminescence, and the glow pulses in slow, rhythmic cycles, like a circulatory system visible through translucent skin. These fusion points are nodes in a network. The entire canopy is a single connected organism — or perhaps was always a single organism that chose to express itself as a forest, the way a coral chooses to express itself as a reef.

The leaves are black. Not dark green — black, with an iridescent sheen of deep blue-violet when what little light there is catches them at the right angle. They are photosynthetic, but not for any star whose light currently reaches this plateau. Their chemistry is tuned to a wavelength that doesn't dominate the local spectrum, which means they operate at the edge of starvation, drawing energy slowly, running the whole vast system on a trickle. This is why the canopy must be total — every photon must be caught. Not one scrap of usable radiation is allowed to pass through. The dim copper light that filters to the ground floor is only the wavelengths the leaves have rejected as unusable.

---

The air is cool and still at ground level, but above — you can hear it — the canopy roars.

It is a sound like a stadium. Like surf against a cliff face. A continuous, layered, modulating thunder that comes from the upper branches flexing and rubbing against each other in high-altitude winds that never reach the ground. The canopy acts as a total windbreak. Down here, the air barely moves. Up there, gales from the plateau edge drive through the branch network and the whole structure creaks and groans and sings with the friction of wood on wood, the pop-pop-pop of seed pods cracking under pressure, the resonant hum of hollow branch segments acting as organ pipes. The sound is enormous. It rolls and shifts. There are moments when harmonics align and a single bass tone emerges from the noise — a chord that vibrates in your sternum — before dissolving back into the general roar. At other times, a section of canopy will suddenly fall silent as the wind shifts, and the silence is almost more alarming than the noise, a gap in the world's breathing.

Occasionally, something *cracks* up there with a sound like a rifle shot, and a branch segment the size of a shipping container will tumble down through the column of open air between the trunks and hit the stone floor with an impact you feel through your feet. The wood splinters into fibrous shards that immediately begin to twitch and curl — not from residual tension, but with apparent purpose. Within hours, the fallen fragment will have crept several meters across the stone, probing for the nearest root crown, dragging itself by cilia-like fibers. If it reaches a trunk, it is reabsorbed. If it doesn't, it desiccates and becomes part of the thin, powdery, fibrous litter that coats patches of the plateau floor like dry snow.

---

The settlement is here, in the space between the trunks.

It is built on the ground-level shelves of ancient stone, and it is built *with* the trees, or perhaps *by* the trees, or perhaps *as an agreement* between the trees and the people who live here. The structures are platforms and enclosures made from shaped living wood — branches that have been coaxed or trained or instructed to grow horizontally, to flatten and widen, to form floors and walls and spiraling ramps that wrap around the great trunks. Some of the structures hang from the lower branches like pendant nests, swaying slightly with the vibrations transmitted down from the roaring canopy. Others sit directly on the stone, walled on three sides by trained root buttresses, roofed by woven branch lattice.

The technology is subtle and pervasive. You notice it first in the light: the bioluminescent nodes at the branch fusion points above have been — *encouraged* is the word people here use — to be brighter, more consistent, in the areas above the settlement. The violet glow is steady enough to navigate by, and it pools in the concavities of the living architecture like light in cupped hands. But that's the least of it. The trees themselves are computational. The fusion network in the canopy is a processing substrate — a distributed biological computer of staggering scale, running on electrochemical gradients through sap-analog fluids. The people here didn't build this system. They *discovered* it. They learned to interface with it using implanted mycorrhizal intermediaries — fungal filaments threaded into the root crowns that translate between human nervous systems and the tree network's signal language. The interface points are visible as smooth, opalescent calluses on the trunks, each the size of a dinner plate, each warm to the touch, each humming at a frequency just below audibility.

The settlement has the population and footprint of a small town — perhaps three thousand people, their structures scattered across a kilometer of plateau floor — but the *functional* scale of the community includes the entire canopy network for a radius that nobody has been able to fully map. When someone here speaks of their "city," they mean something that extends to the horizon and beyond, a municipality whose infrastructure is alive and whose processing power exceeds anything built by hands.

---

The fauna emerges at transitional hours — not dawn or dusk, exactly, since the light on the ground floor barely changes, but at moments when the canopy noise cycles through particular patterns. There are animals here that navigate by sound, that read the roar of the upper branches the way seafarers read waves.

The most visible are the *shellers*: hexapodal creatures, each about the size of a large dog, with carapaces made from hardened tree resin that they cultivate on their own backs by sleeping pressed against the trunks. Their shells are honey-colored and translucent, and the animals glow faintly from within, their metabolic heat caught and refracted by the resin into a warm amber light. They move in loose groups of eight to twelve across the open stone flats, feeding on the fibrous litter of fallen branch material, their mandibles whirring with a rapid clicking sound that adds a staccato counterpoint to the canopy roar. They are not afraid of people. They walk through the settlement as though it is simply another arrangement of trunks.

Higher up — clinging to the trunks in the first twenty meters above the ground — are things that might be amphibians if there were any water to be amphibious about. They are moist-skinned despite the aridity, sweating a glycerol-like fluid from their own tissues, pale and translucent and the size of human hands. They climb in spirals, eating the bioluminescent fungal films that colonize the bark. When startled, they leap and glide on membranous lateral extensions, and for a moment the dim space between two trunks will be full of dozens of them, pale and silent and spreading their bodies flat to the air, falling slowly in the stillness like living paper.

There are things in the canopy too. You can hear them — heavy bodies moving through the branch network, contributing to the general noise. Occasionally a face will appear at the edge of a branch-hole a hundred meters up and look down at the settlement with calm, reflective eyes. Nobody here seems to know exactly what they are. The tree network, when queried through the mycorrhizal interfaces, returns signals that the inhabitants interpret as *kin* or *co-process*, which settles nothing.

---

The plateau drops away somewhere out beyond the settlement, on all sides. You can walk to the edge in a few hours — past the last of the structures, past a zone of increasingly sparse trunk columns, to a place where the canopy thins and actual sky appears and the wind comes howling in across the bare stone and the temperature drops sharply. The plateau edge is a cliff — the ancient, smoothed, wind-polished remnant of an escarpment that was once a mountain range, worn down over geologic time to this single elevated shelf. Below, the land descends into a haze of dust and distance, treeless, empty, the color of rust. Nothing grows down there. Nothing moves. The forest exists only up here, on this one remaining height, rooted in rock, drawing its energy from light it can barely use, connected to itself across kilometers of continuous canopy, thinking its slow, arboreal thoughts, hosting its town of people who learned to listen.

The sound changes at the edge. The canopy roar is behind you, muffled by distance, and ahead is the open wind — thin and cold and carrying nothing. Between the two, for a few meters, there is a zone of strange acoustic interference where the sounds cancel and reinforce in unpredictable patterns. People sometimes come here to stand in the quiet spots, the nodes of cancellation where the world goes momentarily silent. They say it helps them think in their own voices, untangled from the network, alone with the hum of their own neurology. Then they turn around and walk back into the sound, into the dim violet light, into the dry forest that has outlasted every other living system on this ancient, waterless world, and they press their palms to the warm opalescent calluses on the trunks and rejoin the conversation that has been going on for longer than their species has existed.
