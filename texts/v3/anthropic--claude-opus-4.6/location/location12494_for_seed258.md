# The Grown City

---

It was built entirely by plants. That is the first thing you need to understand. There are no stones here, no metals, no fired clays. Everything you see — the arching spans that bridge the valley, the hollow towers that rise nine stories from the wet plateau, the streets, the stairs, the aqueducts, the walls — all of it is living wood and woven root and compressed bark and latticed vine. And none of it was shaped by hands.

---

The plateau sits at modest elevation, high enough that the air holds a warm dampness without becoming stifling, high enough that clouds sometimes drift through the upper canopy-spires and leave their moisture on every surface as they pass. Below the plateau's edges, the land drops away into haze. You cannot see the lowlands. You are not meant to. This place exists at its own altitude, self-contained, and it extends — valley and ridge and long curving basin — for the span of a small country's worth of terrain, all of it given over to this singular project.

The project being: the city.

Not a ruin being reclaimed by forest. Not a garden pretending to be architecture. A city that was *grown*, deliberately, by organisms that are not animals and are not thinking in any way you would recognize as thought, but which have — over what must be an extraordinary span of time — organized themselves into structures so purposeful, so interlocking, so clearly *civic* in their function, that calling this a city is not metaphor. It is the most accurate word available.

---

The main thoroughfares are corridors of packed and living root-mass, wide enough that twenty people could walk abreast — if there were people. There are not. The streets are empty. Not abandoned-empty. Never-occupied-empty. The buildings breathe gently, the walls transpire, moisture beads and trickles down surfaces of bark so tightly woven it resembles leather, and no one walks here, no one has ever walked here, and the city does not care. It is not *for* anyone. It is simply *being* a city, the way a termite mound is being a termite mound, except there are no termites either.

The spacing is what strikes you next. For a place of such total construction — where every surface, every square meter of ground, every vertical rise is made of plant tissue — the city is strangely, almost ceremonially sparse. The buildings stand well apart from each other. Great open plazas of moss-carpeted ground stretch between the tower-trunks. The bridges arc high overhead with nothing beneath them but warm air and the quiet rush of water moving through channels you cannot quite see. There is *room* here. Room to stand in the middle of a living boulevard and look up and see the sky through gaps in the canopy-vaulting, actual sky, dove-grey and luminous, the light coming through even and full and strangely without shadow.

The light. Yes. Normal daylight, or something very like it, but it doesn't behave quite right. It doesn't shift. The angle doesn't change. The shadows — few as they are — stay where they are. You could stand here for what feels like hours and the illumination would not brighten or dim or move. It is afternoon here. It has always been afternoon here. The sky is the color of a sky two hours before evening, and it will remain that color. The clouds drift but the light doesn't change. You begin to wonder if the sky itself is part of the construction — some vast canopy so far overhead that it only *appears* to be atmosphere.

You don't know. You can't get high enough to check.

---

The towers are the most unsettling structures. They are not trees. They are too regular, too hollow, too obviously *rooms stacked upon rooms* to be trees. But they are made of tree-stuff — great columns of heartwood that grew in spirals, their grain following a helical pattern that creates natural stairways up the interior walls. The windows are gaps where branches were redirected, leaving oval openings that look out over the plazas, and inside the windows, on the sills of living wood, small ferns grow in precise rows like someone's indoor plants, except no one placed them there.

Each tower is different — different species of wood, different patterns of fenestration, different heights — but they share a common vocabulary. Buttress roots flare out at the base like the feet of gothic cathedrals. Upper stories narrow and taper. The rooftops are crowned with dense flowering structures, enormous inflorescences in pale yellows and deep reds that bloom perpetually and never drop their petals. These flowers produce the sound.

---

The sound. It is loud here. Not oppressively so, but undeniably loud, and the sound is this: a constant, layered, harmonic droning that comes from the flowers. Millions of tubular blossoms channeling the warm wind that moves endlessly across the plateau. Each flower is a pipe. Each tower-crown is an organ. The pitch varies with the size and shape of the blossoms, and because the towers are spaced widely across the valley, the sound reaches you from multiple distances simultaneously, creating a reverberant, almost choral effect — low tones from the massive drum-shaped blooms on the nearest tower washing over you, while higher, thinner notes arrive a half-second later from towers further away. It is not music. It is not random. It is the sound of a city breathing through its architecture, and it never changes because the wind never changes, and the flowers never close, and the pitch never wavers. It is the equilibrium made audible.

Beneath the droning, if you listen carefully: water. Always water. It runs through the city in channels carved from living root, some as narrow as your finger, some wide enough to wade in. It emerges from the ground in places where the root-mass parts to reveal dark upwelling pools, and it disappears again into other openings. The plateau is saturated. Not flooded — the root-systems manage the water with extraordinary precision — but *wet*, fundamentally wet, the way a sponge is wet even when it isn't dripping. Every surface you touch is slightly damp. The air tastes green and mineral and alive. If you pressed your palm flat against the bark-wall of a building, you would feel the faintest pulse of moisture being drawn upward, a hydraulic heartbeat so slow it barely registers.

---

The geology beneath all this is newborn. That is the strangest part. You would expect a place this established, this architecturally complex, to be rooted in ancient bedrock, but the ground under the root-mat is *young* — recently cooled basalt, perhaps, or fresh sediment barely consolidated, the kind of substrate you find on new volcanic islands or freshly deposited alluvial fans. If you could somehow dig beneath the root-mass (you cannot; it goes down many meters, a dense tangle of rhizomes and mycorrhizal networks and compacted organic matter that has become its own kind of geology), you would find stone that has barely begun to weather. No soil horizons. No deep mineral profiles. Raw rock, embryonic and unformed, as though the plateau itself rose from the earth only recently and the plants — seized it. Claimed it. Built upon it before erosion could even begin to shape it.

This means the city is not old in the way you assume. Or it means the plants build faster than you think. Or it means something else, something about the relationship between biological construction and geological time that doesn't follow the rules you know.

---

Animals are rare. You see them only at the edges — a shape moving through one of the open plazas that resolves, when you focus, into something like a salamander, dark-backed and glistening, the size of a cat. It crosses the moss-floor in an unhurried diagonal and vanishes into a gap between roots. Later, you hear something that might be wings in the upper canopy, a brief papery flutter, and then silence — or rather, the return of the drone, which you realize you had stopped hearing for a moment. The fauna here is incidental. Tolerated but not integrated. The city has no ecological role for animals. They are visitors, like you, passing through a system that does not require them.

The insects, if there are insects, are invisible.

---

Walk further. Move through one of the long avenues where the root-floor rises gradually and the towers fall away and you enter a section of the city that functions — you begin to believe — as infrastructure. Here the architecture becomes less recognizably architectural and more like plumbing or wiring: vast braided cables of vine running horizontally between anchor-points, some of them vibrating slightly with the passage of water or nutrients or signals you have no way of detecting. Root-bridges span a wide depression in the plateau where the young basalt has cracked and subsided slightly, and in the depression, a lake has formed — shallow, warm, perfectly still, its surface covered in a continuous mat of floating plant tissue that is photosynthesizing so actively you can see tiny bubbles of oxygen rising and popping at the margins.

The lake is a reservoir. An organ of the city. Its edges are sculpted by root-walls into geometric shapes — not quite circles, not quite hexagons, something between — and channels lead away from it in six directions, carrying water to the tower-districts. You understand, standing at the lake's edge, that the city is not a collection of individual plants. It is one organism, or many organisms so interconnected that the distinction has become meaningless. The mycorrhizal network beneath your feet is the nervous system. The water channels are the circulatory system. The flower-pipes are — what? A voice? A respiratory system? An aesthetic choice by something that has no aesthetics?

You don't know. The drone continues. The light holds steady. Nothing here has changed in a very long time, and nothing here will change.

---

There is one more thing. At the center of the city — and it does have a center, a point toward which the avenues converge and the tower-density, while still sparse, reaches its maximum — there stands a structure that is not a tower. It is a dome. A smooth, unbroken half-sphere of interwoven branches so tightly grown together that they have fused into a continuous shell, the bark forming a seamless surface like the cap of an enormous mushroom. It is perhaps forty meters across and twenty meters high. There are no openings. No windows, no doors, no gaps where you might peer inside. The flowers that grow on its surface are different from the tower-blooms: white, silent, closed. They do not channel wind. They do not drone.

What is inside the dome? You circle it. You press your ear against the shell. Through the warm, damp wood, you hear — or imagine you hear — a deeper sound, not the wind-drone of the towers but something lower, more fundamental, something like the sound a root makes when it pushes through soil except magnified a thousandfold. A sound of growth. But growth that has reached its destination and now simply *maintains*. A sound of being exactly what it is, perpetually, without change.

The dome does not open. The city does not explain itself.

You stand in the warm light that does not move, in the loud harmonic air, on the young stone that was claimed before it could become anything else, in a place that was built by the living for no purpose you can name, and you understand that you are not unwelcome here. You are simply *irrelevant*. The city is complete. It was always complete. You can stay as long as you like. Nothing will happen. Nothing needs to.
