# The Floodworks

---

Imagine a world that drowned, and then imagine someone decided to build inside the drowning.

The ocean here has no floor. Or rather, it does—somewhere—but so far below that pressure and darkness have long since made it irrelevant. What matters is the middle zone, the vast inhabitable column of cold Atlantic-gray water stretching in every direction, and the fact that someone filled it with architecture.

They are called the Floodworks, though no one living remembers who named them, and they extend for what might be a thousand kilometers in any lateral direction and perhaps six vertical kilometers of active, functioning depth. Fabricated. All of it. Every surface your hand might touch was poured, riveted, welded, or bolted into place.

---

Begin with the obvious: the pylons.

They rise—or descend, depending on your orientation—like the trunks of inconceivable trees, columns of riveted steel and poured ferro-ite composite, each one forty to sixty meters in diameter. They are spaced far apart. Absurdly far apart. You can drift for twenty minutes in open water between one pylon and the next, surrounded by nothing but gray-blue murk and the sound of your own breathing, and then the next one materializes out of the haze like a wall assembling itself from fog. They do not taper. They do not branch. They simply continue, upward and downward, beyond the range of sight, beyond the range of sonar, beyond whatever you have available to perceive them. Their surfaces are textured with bolt-heads and weld seams and maintenance rungs and the occasional access hatch, all of it covered in a thin biofilm of bacterial slime that gives the steel a sickly amber tint under lamplight. They groan. Deep, tidal, tectonic groans that travel through the water and settle in your sternum. The pylons are always groaning.

Between the pylons, at irregular intervals of depth, run the platforms. Imagine floors without rooms, ceilings without buildings—horizontal planes of grated industrial steel extending outward from each pylon in broad aprons, some of them a hundred meters across, perforated with drainage holes the size of manhole covers through which water cycles endlessly. The platforms are where the machinery lives: pump stations the size of warehouses, desalination arrays trailing forests of intake pipes, turbine housings that shudder and whine as they convert thermal differentials into power. Catwalks connect platform to platform in long, sagging spans, their railings beaded with condensation that is indistinguishable from the ocean itself because the ocean is everywhere, pressing in from all sides, filling every gap and corridor and open space that the Floodworks have not explicitly sealed against it.

There is no air here. Not in the open. Air exists only inside the pressurized habitation modules bolted to the pylons like barnacles on a dock post—ovoid steel capsules with fogged portholes, connected by ribbed umbilical tunnels. But the Floodworks themselves are open to the water. The machinery operates submerged. The catwalks are for divers. The platforms are underwater plazas where figures in heavy suits move slowly between tasks, trailing streams of exhaled bubbles that rise and are absorbed into the gray above.

---

The light is unexpected. You would think—this far under, this much water overhead—that it would be dark. It isn't. The Floodworks generate their own illumination: sodium-vapor lamps bolted to every pylon at regular intervals, casting cones of sulfurous yellow-orange light into the water. Work lights on articulated arms. Strings of caged bulbs running along the catwalks. Floodlights—actual floodlights, the irony is not lost—mounted on the platforms and aimed outward into the open water, where their beams lose coherence after a hundred meters and dissolve into a milky, luminous haze. The cumulative effect is that the Floodworks exist in a state of perpetual overcast daylight. Not bright. Not dim. The light of a winter afternoon in an industrial port city, gray and even and slightly orange at the edges, the kind of light that makes distances hard to judge. You can see far enough to know there is more, always more, another pylon emerging from the murk, another platform below, another string of lights above, the geometry continuing past the point where the water swallows it.

It is loud.

Sound travels mercilessly in water, and the Floodworks are a civilization of machines. The pump stations produce a low, rhythmic thudding—*whump, whump, whump*—that propagates for kilometers and arrives at your body not as hearing but as pulse, as though the ocean itself has a heartbeat. The turbines add a higher frequency, a drilling whine that modulates as they cycle under load. Chains run through pulleys. Gantry cranes on the larger platforms swing loads of raw material—slabs of alloy, coils of cable, sealed drums of chemical stock—and their motors produce a grinding, ratcheting sound that echoes off the pylons and returns from multiple directions at once, creating a spatial confusion of noise. Somewhere distant, always somewhere distant, something is being welded or cut, and the hiss-crack of an underwater arc torch reaches you as a staccato popping, like ice fracturing in a glass. The habitation modules add their own layer: the muffled thrum of air recyclers, the clang of hatches, voices rendered unintelligible by steel and water. And beneath all of it, always, the groaning of the pylons, which flex in response to deep-ocean currents and thermal expansion and the sheer accumulated weight of everything they support.

---

The water itself is cold. Not freezing—the machinery generates waste heat, and the thermal output of a thousand platforms keeps the core temperature at perhaps eight or nine degrees Celsius—but cold enough that exposed skin goes numb in minutes, cold enough that the divers wear layered neoprene and heated undergarments and still shiver. Outside the Floodworks' thermal envelope, out where the lights end and the pylons fade to shadows and then to nothing, the water drops toward four degrees. The cold is patient. It leans against the Floodworks from every direction, pressing inward, and the machinery pushes back, and this negotiation—this thermal argument between industry and ocean—produces currents. Not gentle currents. The Floodworks have their own weather.

Convection cells form between the heated platforms and the cold open water. Columns of relatively warm water rise; sheets of cold water descend. Where they meet, turbulence occurs—spinning eddies the size of buildings that can catch a diver and tumble them end over end, that rip unsecured tools from carabiner clips, that set the catwalks swaying and the suspension cables singing. The workers call these events *washes*. A wash can last minutes or hours. The larger ones—the ones generated when a major pump station cycles down for maintenance and the thermal balance shifts—can persist for days, reshaping the current patterns across an entire sector of the Floodworks. Visibility drops. Sediment—a fine particulate of rust, biofilm, and organic detritus—gets stirred into suspension, and the world turns brown and opaque, and the sodium lights become smeared halos, and work stops, and everyone retreats to the habitation modules and waits.

When the washes subside, things have moved. Platforms haven't moved—they're welded to the pylons—but everything else has. Loose equipment has drifted. Supply caches have scattered. The bodies of small creatures pile in the eddies' dying corners like leaves after a storm.

---

Because there are creatures. The Floodworks are not sterile.

Life has colonized the infrastructure with the opportunistic persistence that life always displays. The biofilm on the pylons is the base layer—bacterial mats that feed on dissolved organics and the chemical runoff from the machinery, tinting the steel amber and green. In places where nutrient-rich water pools against structural features, the biofilm thickens into a rubbery mat that maintenance crews scrape away with long-handled tools, because it clogs intake valves and fouls sensor arrays. This is vegetation, of a sort—the only kind the Floodworks support. Strands of bacterial filament trail from bolt-heads and cable junctions like sparse, colorless hair. Occasionally, in a sheltered corner where the current is slow and the light is steady, something resembling a kelp analog takes hold: a rubbery, translucent ribbon anchored to the steel by a chemical adhesive it secretes, waving slowly in the current, photosynthesizing—or chemosynthesizing, more accurately—off the lamp light and the dissolved chemistry of the industrial water. These growths are uncommon. They are also, according to the workers, bad luck to disturb.

The animals are more numerous. Swarms of small, silver-scaled fish—engineered or adapted, no one is certain anymore—move through the open spaces between pylons in schools of thousands, turning in unison like a single liquid thought, their bodies catching the sodium light and throwing it back in rolling flashes. They feed on the biofilm and on each other. They are, in turn, fed upon by larger predators: sleek, dark-bodied things, a meter or so in length, that cruise the shadows at the edge of the light with the calm efficiency of animals that have never needed to rush. The workers call them blacks. Blacks are not aggressive toward humans, but they are territorial around the platforms where fish concentrate, and they have been known to strike at the gloved hands of divers who disturb their hunting grounds.

Deeper—or rather, further from the core of the Floodworks, out where the lights are fewer and the pylons are spaced even further apart and the cold is asserting itself—there are things that are harder to see clearly. Large, slow-moving shapes that appear on sonar as diffuse returns, as though their bodies are not entirely solid. Jellyfish analogs, perhaps, or something without easy analog. They drift through the deep margins of the structure, occasionally tangling in cable runs or draping themselves across unused platforms, and the maintenance crews give them wide berth not out of fear, exactly, but out of a practical respect for anything that lives comfortably in water that cold and that dark.

The ecosystem is simple but active. Things eat, and are eaten, and reproduce, and die, and their deaths feed the biofilm, which feeds the fish, which feed the blacks, which die and sink and are consumed by the bacterial mats in turn. The Floodworks did not intend to create an ecology. The ecology happened anyway. It pulses. It cycles. It responds to the washes and the thermal shifts and the output schedules of the pump stations with a biological rhythm that is, by now, deeply entangled with the industrial rhythm of the structure itself.

---

Who built the Floodworks? They are self-evidently built. Every surface is manufactured. The pylons were fabricated in sections and assembled—you can see the joints, the transition points where one massive segment was mated to the next, each joint reinforced with a collar of welded bracing. The platforms were lowered into place by cranes that must have operated from above, from a surface that may or may not still exist. The habitation modules were pressure-tested and installed according to engineering specifications that are still legible on faded placards bolted near their hatches: maximum occupancy, rated depth, maintenance intervals, emergency procedures in three languages, one of which no one speaks anymore.

The Floodworks were not built all at once. You can read the history in the materials. The oldest pylons—the ones at the geographic center, if center means anything in a structure this size—are made of a heavier, cruder steel, riveted rather than welded, their surfaces rough with age and corrosion despite the anti-fouling coatings that were once applied and have long since worn away. The newer pylons, toward the periphery, are smoother, lighter alloys, welded with cleaner seams, fitted with integrated conduit channels and sensor mounts. The Floodworks expanded outward over time. They are still expanding. At the margins, construction crews work in the cold water, assembling new pylon sections from prefabricated components delivered by slow, autonomous barges that navigate the open water between the existing structure and fabrication platforms somewhere further in. The construction is ongoing, methodical, industrial in pace—not fast, not slow, simply continuous, the way a coral reef grows, except that everything here is steel and intent.

The purpose of the Floodworks is, on one level, obvious: they are infrastructure. They pump. They process. They desalinate. They generate power. They sustain the habitation modules and the workers inside them. But the scale exceeds any reasonable interpretation of necessity. A thousand kilometers of pumping stations? Six vertical kilometers of turbines? The Floodworks produce more than they consume. Far more. The excess power goes—somewhere. Into cable trunks that descend into the deep pylons and vanish below the lowest inhabited platform. Into transmission lines that run laterally beyond the outermost ring of construction. The Floodworks are supplying something. Sustaining something. The workers do not know what. Or if they know, they do not say, and perhaps it doesn't matter, because the work is the work, and the shifts are the shifts, and the washes come and go, and the blacks hunt the fish in the sodium light, and the pylons groan, and the pumps beat their slow heartbeat into the water, and the Floodworks continue, and continue, and continue.

---

Stand on a catwalk. Hold the railing. Feel the vibration in the metal—not one vibration but dozens, layered, a chord of mechanical frequencies transmitted through the structure from sources near and far. Look up: steel and light and murk, pylons receding upward into haze. Look down: the same. Look out: the yawning, flooded space between pylons, dim and enormous, a cathedral of nothing except water and the slow patrol of fish and the distant glow of the next platform over, a smudge of orange light a quarter-kilometer away, and beyond it another, and another, the Floodworks repeating themselves into the fog of distance like a thought that won't complete itself.

It is cold. It is loud. It is built, all of it, every centimeter, and it is almost empty. The emptiness is the dominant feature. The structures are the punctuation; the water is the text. Kilometer after kilometer of cold, gray, inhabited ocean, stitched through with steel and light and sound, alive with small silver bodies and the slow drift of things at the margins, and all of it—all of it—humming.
