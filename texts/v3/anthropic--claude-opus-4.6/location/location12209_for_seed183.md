# The Weeping Tiers of Oshkaal

---

The first thing you understand is that the mountain should not be wet.

You are standing at extreme altitude — far above where clouds form in any ordinary atmosphere — on a series of vast, staggered plateaus that descend from a peak you cannot see. The air is thin and cool, not biting but persistently chill, the kind of cold that lives inside stone. The sky above is an unbroken brightness, not white, not blue, but a luminance that seems to come from everywhere and nowhere, as if the concept of a sun has been replaced by a generalized radiance saturating the upper atmosphere. Shadows are rare here. When they appear, they seem almost accidental, like errors in the light's otherwise total coverage. You squint, but there is nothing to squint at. The brightness is diffuse and inescapable. It makes every surface hyper-legible, every crack and mineral vein and wet streak visible with an almost surgical clarity.

And everything is wet.

Water runs in thin, persistent sheets across the stone terraces, with no apparent source. There are no glaciers above — you can see the ridgelines, dry and barren, razor-sharp where millennia of wind have sculpted them into fins and blades. There are no clouds to deliver rain. The moisture simply emerges. It beads on the undersides of overhangs. It pools in the countless shallow depressions that pit the plateau surfaces like thumbprints pressed into grey clay. It weeps — that is the only word — from vertical rock faces in curtains so fine they look like static, like a visual hum against the stone. The water is cool, clear, faintly mineral-tasting if you touch your tongue to a wet surface, with a finish like dissolved chalk and something sharper underneath, something almost alkaline and electric.

The stone itself is ancient. These plateaus have existed in their current configuration for a span of time that resists human comprehension. The rock is a dense metamorphic composite — slate-grey predominantly, shot through with veins of a pale mineral that might be quartz or might be something without a terrestrial name, something that catches the omnidirectional light and holds it a half-second too long before releasing it, so that as your eye moves across a rock face it seems to shimmer with a faint delay, the brightness chasing itself across the surface. Erosion has worked these terraces into their settled, resolved forms: every edge is rounded but not soft, every surface textured by aeons of water-flow and wind into a topography of shallow channels, ridges, and basins that repeat at multiple scales. Look at a ten-meter stretch of plateau and you see the same drainage patterns you'd see in a palm-sized fragment. The stone knows one shape and performs it at every resolution.

There is no plant life. None. Not a lichen crust, not a thread of moss in the wettest crevice, not a stain of algae in the standing pools. The water supports nothing. The mineral world here is total and uncontested. But — and this is where the place begins to slide — the stone is not inert. The plateaus are densely featured, every surface occupied by form and process. Mineral growths erupt from the wet faces: clusters of crystalline needles, some translucent, some opaque and dark as iron, growing in radiating bursts from points where the weeping water concentrates. In the shallow basins, concentric rings of deposited mineral build up slowly, visibly, forming structures like frozen ripples, and if you watch one basin long enough you can see the outermost ring extending — not growing in any biological sense, but *accruing*, molecule by molecule, in real time. The water is building things. It is dissolving the mountain at one elevation and reconstructing it at another, and this process is not passive. It has a pace, a rhythm, almost a will.

The sound of the place is considerable and strange. It is not the roar of a waterfall or the crash of surf. It is a layered, persistent, mid-volume hum composed of thousands of individual water-sounds: the ticking of drops falling from overhangs into shallow pools, the whisper of sheet-flow across stone, the faintly resonant *tink* of water striking the hollow crystalline formations, each one ringing at its own pitch like a glass bell barely touched. Together, these sounds build into a texture that fills the space between the terraces, reverberating off the rock faces, folding back on itself. It sounds, if you stop trying to identify its components, like a choir of small bells being played by rain in a cathedral with no walls. It is too loud to ignore, too harmonious to call noise, and it never stops. There is no quiet moment. The mountain is always speaking in this voice.

Now: the built things.

Scattered across the terraces, you find them. Small structures of stacked stone — not cairns exactly, but deliberate constructions, hand-sized rocks chosen and placed with care, mortared with the same mineral paste that the water deposits naturally. Some are knee-high pillars. Some are low walls, barely more than single courses of stone, tracing curves across the plateau surfaces that seem to follow — or perhaps redirect — the water's natural drainage channels. A few are more ambitious: chest-high enclosures, roofless, roughly circular, with narrow openings that face specific directions. Inside one, you find a shallow basin that has been manually carved into the bedrock, its interior smoothed by hand-tools — you can see the marks, the patient scraping of stone against stone, thousands and thousands of strokes visible in the faintly striated surface. The basin collects water. The water deposits minerals. In the basin's center, something is growing: a formation of pale crystal, thicker and more ordered than the wild growths elsewhere, shaped by the geometry of its container into something almost architectural. A miniature tower. A tiny city. It has been guided.

Whoever did this is not here. There is no sign of recent habitation — no fire-marks, no food remains, no tools left behind. The structures themselves could be decades old or centuries old; the mineral encrustation makes them look like natural features unless you crouch close enough to see the tool-marks underneath. But they are clearly the work of hands, of intention, of a primitive but deeply patient technology — a technology of *collaboration with the water's depositing process*, of shaping the mineral world by shaping the channels through which its building material flows. Someone understood this mountain. Someone came here, repeatedly or permanently, and participated in what it does.

And what it does is where reality starts to thin.

Because the mineral formations are not obeying consistent rules. On the eastern edge of the third terrace down — you can count them, the major plateaus, staggered like enormous steps descending a slope of perhaps thirty degrees — there is a cluster of crystal spires, the tallest nearly two meters, that cast shadows pointing in the wrong direction. Not wrong by a few degrees. Wrong by being oriented toward the light source rather than away from it. The shadows extend *into* the brightness, dark fingers reaching upward and outward, and they are sharp-edged and definite and undeniable. You walk around the cluster. The shadows do not move. They are fixed relative to the crystals, not relative to the light. They belong to the formations the way color belongs to them, as an intrinsic property rather than an optical consequence.

Elsewhere: a long, narrow channel of flowing water crosses a plateau in a perfectly straight line — too straight, unnervingly straight, laser-straight across forty meters of naturally eroded stone. The water in this channel moves at a constant speed and constant depth and does not ripple. Its surface is a perfect mirror, and what it reflects is not the bright sky above but a darker sky, a sky with stars in it, a sky that belongs to some other time of day or some other place entirely. You can lean over and look into it and see constellations you do not recognize arranged around a moon that is too large and too close and faintly amber-colored. The reflection does not change if you disturb the water's surface. Your hand passes through the flow — cold, mineral, real — and the stars remain undisturbed beneath your fingers, as if the reflection has a deeper claim to reality than the thing doing the reflecting.

On a higher terrace, you find a place where the stone has been deposited by the water into a structure of extraordinary regularity: a hexagonal lattice, each cell perhaps a handspan across, extending over an area the size of a small house. Inside each hexagonal cell, the mineral deposit has formed a slightly different shape — a spiral in one, a branching tree-form in another, a tight helix in a third, as if the water is running experiments, testing what geometry can do. And in the center of the lattice, one cell contains something that is difficult to look at: a shape that seems to occupy more dimensions than three, a mineral formation that your eyes refuse to resolve into a stable image. It is a knot. It is a flower. It is a staircase descending into the stone, except the stone is only centimeters thick here and there is nothing below it but more plateau. You blink and it is all of these things and none of them. The water beads on its surface and runs in directions that include, somehow, *inward*, toward a center that keeps receding.

The mountain is building something. The water is its instrument, and gravity is its patience, and the mineral world is both its medium and its message. Whatever process governs the deposition of crystals and precipitates has begun to exceed the boundaries of chemistry. The formations are not merely complex — they are *impossible*, in small specific ways that accumulate as you move across the terraces, as if the altitude itself is a factor, as if at this extreme elevation the rules are thinner, worn through by proximity to whatever that sourceless brightness is, and something else is leaking in through the gaps.

The cool air moves across your skin. The bell-choir of water sounds fills your ears. The light presses evenly against every surface, and the shadows of certain formations point where they should not, and the reflections in certain channels show places that are not here, and in the hand-carved basins left by absent builders, small crystal cities are assembling themselves one molecule at a time into architectures that do not belong to this physics.

You are standing in a place that is, slowly and with enormous patience, *becoming somewhere else*.

The water keeps weeping. The mountain keeps building. No one is here to see it except you, and you are not entirely certain that "here" is a concept this place still fully respects.
