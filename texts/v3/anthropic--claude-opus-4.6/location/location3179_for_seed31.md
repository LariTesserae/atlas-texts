# The Plateau of Still Waters

---

There is a town up here, though you would not call it alive.

The plateau extends for miles in every direction — a high tableland of dark basalt, young and sharp-featured, its edges still crisp where they cracked upward from whatever tectonic event pushed this shelf of rock two thousand meters above the lowlands. The cliffs at the margins are vertical and clean, not yet rounded by wind or softened by time. The stone remembers its breaking. Walk the plateau's edge and you can see where columns of basalt separated like the pages of a book being opened, the fracture planes still glassy in places, still showing the faint conchoidal ripples of a fresh split.

But the surface of the plateau is flat, and the surface of the plateau is wet.

Water is everywhere here. Not flowing — sitting. The basalt is dense and barely porous, and the atmosphere at this elevation is a permanent condensing engine. Clouds form below the plateau and rise to meet it, dragging moisture up the cliff faces, where it beads and weeps down fresh rock and then pools on top. The air is warm — warmer than you'd expect at this height, some geothermal generosity bleeding up through the young stone — and it holds humidity like a held breath. Every surface is filmed with water. The basalt is black and gleaming. Shallow lakes, none of them more than knee-deep, spread across the plateau in irregular shapes, their edges determined by the subtlest undulations in the rock. Between the lakes, the stone is wet, puddled, streaked with the slow migration of water that has nowhere to go. There are no rivers. There is no drainage. The water simply accumulates until it reaches the plateau's edge and slips over in thin, silent curtains that evaporate before they reach the ground far below.

The light is even and ordinary — a warm overcast, the sun visible as a bright smear behind a thin membrane of cloud. It gives the water a pewter sheen. Reflections are everywhere but muted, not mirror-bright, more like the plateau has been laminated in grey metal. The sky and the stone meet in the water and become the same color.

There are no animals. None. No birds cross the sky. No insects dimple the surface of the shallow lakes. No fish. No frogs at the marshy edges where water meets rock meets the sparse, determined vegetation. The silence this produces is extraordinary — not the silence of absence but the silence of a place that has settled into itself completely and has no reason to make a sound. If you stand still and hold your breath, you will hear water. Not rushing, not dripping, but the faintest possible sound of condensation gathering on stone, a sound more felt than heard, a thickening of the quiet.

The vegetation that does exist is stubborn and spread thin. Mosses colonize the permanently damp basalt in patches — dark green, almost black, hugging the stone so tightly they seem painted on. In the shallower margins of the lakes, a kind of sedge grows in loose, scattered tufts, each cluster separated from the next by several meters of open water or bare rock. The sedge is a muted olive color, its blades stiff and upright, and it grows to about thigh height. Here and there, in the cracks where the basalt plates meet, a low shrub has taken hold — woody, gnarled even though it can't be very old given the youth of this plateau. These shrubs have small, leathery leaves with a waxy coating that beads the condensation into visible droplets. They are scattered so sparsely across the landscape that each one feels like a landmark, a punctuation mark in a sentence that is mostly silence and stone and water.

And then there is the town.

It sits in the approximate center of the plateau, or at least the center of the largest contiguous area of relatively dry stone — a slight rise, perhaps two meters higher than the surrounding wetness, enough to keep the foundations from sitting directly in water. The buildings are made of the basalt itself, quarried from the plateau's edges where the columns broke clean and could be sectioned into rough blocks. The construction is simple, handmade, unmistakable in its craft even if that craft is early and unpretentious. Walls are stacked stone, fitted without mortar in the older structures, chinked with a crude calcite-ite mud in the newer ones. Roofs are flat slabs of thinner basalt, cantilevered slightly to shed the perpetual condensation away from the walls. The buildings are low — one story, occasionally one and a half where a sleeping loft is tucked under the roof slab — and they are arranged with a kind of deliberate, spacious geometry. Wide gaps between structures. Broad paths of laid stone, their surfaces worn slightly smoother than the surrounding rock from the passage of feet. A central clearing, roughly rectangular, that might be a market square or a gathering place, its stone floor kept meticulously clean of moss.

There are perhaps two hundred buildings. Maybe fewer. They spread across the rise in a loose grid that respects the terrain's subtle contours, bending where the ground dips, clustering slightly where the stone is highest and driest. Some buildings are clearly dwellings. Others are larger — workshops, storage, communal halls. A few structures at the town's periphery are partially submerged, their lower courses sitting in a few centimeters of water, and these seem to serve as cisterns or processing areas, their interiors open to the sky, their floors deliberately flooded.

The town has channels — shallow grooves cut into the basalt with hand tools, the chisel marks still visible, directing water from the plateau's condensation toward specific collection points. Stone basins, carved from single blocks, sit at intersections. The engineering is simple but thoughtful, the work of people who understood that they lived on a sponge of stone in a cloud and needed to negotiate with the water rather than fight it. Walkways are raised slightly on courses of stacked stone. Thresholds are stepped up. Every doorway is six inches higher than the path outside it.

There are hand tools left in places. Basalt chisels with wooden handles, the wood dark and swollen with moisture. Stone hammers. Flat scrapers for clearing moss from surfaces. Woven baskets of sedge, tight enough to hold water, sitting in rows inside one of the larger buildings. A grinding stone in the central square, its bowl worn smooth. The tools are primitive but not crude — they show skill, repetition, a tradition of making.

But no one is here.

The town is not ruined. The buildings stand solid. The channel system works — water moves through it in thin, purposeful sheets, arriving at the basins, which are full. The baskets are intact. The tools are where someone set them down. There is no decay, no collapse, no sign of catastrophe or abandonment. The town simply exists, populated by its own construction, maintained by the equilibrium of water and stone. The moss has not overtaken the cleared surfaces. The channels have not silted. The roofs have not fallen. Everything is exactly as it is, and it seems like it has been exactly as it is for a very long time, and will continue to be exactly as it is for a very long time more.

You can walk through it. Your footsteps on the wet stone are the loudest thing on the plateau — a soft, flat sound, absorbed quickly by the humid air. The buildings are cool inside, a few degrees below the warm ambient temperature, and the condensation on their interior walls runs in slow, fine trails to the floor and out through small drainage slots at the base of each wall. Everything is damp. Everything is clean. The stone has a mineral smell, faintly metallic, mixing with the green scent of wet moss.

Stand in the central square and look outward in any direction and you will see the town give way to the open plateau — the sparse sedge, the shallow lakes reflecting the overcast sky, the black wet stone extending to the sharp edges where the tableland ends and the cliffs drop away. The scale of the plateau makes the town seem small but not insignificant. It sits on the stone the way the moss sits on the stone: placed there, holding on, part of the surface.

Nothing changes here. The condensation gathers at the same rate. The lakes neither rise nor fall. The light holds its even, warm grey. The shrubs neither grow nor die. The channels carry their thin sheets of water to the basins, and the basins are full, and the overflow slips quietly to the stone and joins the general wetness, and the general wetness creeps toward the plateau's edge and falls silently into air.

The quiet is so deep it has texture. It presses gently against the ears. It makes the faintest sounds — the breath of condensation, the nearly inaudible trickle in a channel, the creak of your own weight on stone — into events.

This is a place that arrived at its final state and stopped.
