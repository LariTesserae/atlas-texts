# The Cistern of Hundred Hands

---

Below the surface of something — a hill, a town, a ruin so old it has become indistinguishable from geology — there is a cistern. But *cistern* is generous. It implies a single purpose, a single chamber. This is more like a warren of cisterns, interconnected, built over generations by hands that kept adding, kept extending, kept filling every available volume with *more structure*, until the space became almost solid with the fact of its own making.

You descend through a passage that is barely a passage — more like a gap between two walls that were built by different people in different centuries, neither aware the other's wall existed. The stone is warm. Not from any fire you can find. The warmth is architectural, accumulated, the thermal mass of stone that has been underground so long it has found its own temperature and refuses to negotiate. The air is heavy with it, close, like breathing through a wool blanket that has been left in the sun.

---

The first chamber opens around you — "opens" being relative, because nothing here truly opens. The ceiling is low, perhaps twice your height, but every surface is occupied. Shelves have been carved into walls, and then shelves have been built *in front of* those shelves, and then brackets and pegs have been driven into the built shelves, and things have been hung from the brackets, and more things have been leaned against the hung things. The materials are all hand-worked: clay bricks of irregular size, stones shaped by chiseling rather than cutting, timber beams darkened with age and fitted without nails — held by the pressure of their neighbors, by wooden pegs, by the sheer density of everything pressing against everything else.

There is no natural substrate here. Nothing you touch is earth, is raw stone, is ground. The floor is brick, uneven, relaid in patches where older floors sank or cracked. The walls are mortared stone faced with plaster faced with more plaster, the layers visible where chunks have fallen away — a stratigraphy of surfaces, each one someone's attempt to make the place *theirs* before the next person came and plastered over it. Even the ceiling is constructed: beams and stone slabs and packed clay, and you can see where additional support pillars have been wedged in over the decades — not because the ceiling was failing, but because someone couldn't bear to leave an open span of air unused.

---

The sound hits you before you understand it. It's coming from everywhere and from specific places simultaneously.

Dripping — irregular, percussive — from condensation gathering on the undersides of stone lintels and falling onto ceramic basins placed to catch it. This is the water here: not a river, not a pool, but a slow seeping through the architecture itself. Moisture travels through the layered walls by capillary action, finds its way to cool surfaces, beads, drops. Dozens of these drip-catches are scattered throughout the chambers, each one making its own rhythm. Some basins are nearly full, their surfaces trembling with each new drop. Others are dry, waiting. The water tastes of chalk and something faintly metallic — the taste of the walls themselves, dissolved and reconstituted.

But the dripping is only the substrate of the sound. Over it: the animals.

---

They are everywhere. This is what the density is *for*.

Small brown birds — something between a wren and a swallow — have colonized the upper reaches of every chamber, building mud nests in the angles between beams and walls, in the hollows of broken shelving, in the mouths of clay pipes whose original purpose has been forgotten. They are loud beyond their size. Their calls bounce off the hard surfaces, multiply, reinforce, until the air in each chamber vibrates with a bright, chattering, overlapping chorus that never fully stops. When one bird falls silent, three others are mid-phrase. The acoustics of the space — all those hard surfaces, all those strange angles and alcoves — create a reverb that makes it sound like there are thousands of them. There may be hundreds. It's impossible to count. They move too quickly, darting between chambers through gaps and broken ventilation shafts, and the sound of their wings is a constant papery shuffling layered under the singing.

In the lower spaces, in the crevices between walls, in the warmer and damper corners: lizards. Pale, almost translucent, with oversized eyes adapted to the half-light. They are languid in the warmth, draped over brickwork like ornaments someone placed and forgot. But when a moth or a beetle moves — and there are many moths and beetles, drawn to whatever draws anything to underground warmth — the lizards snap into motion with a speed that seems wrong for their lethargy. The click of their jaws is audible. A small, sharp punctuation in the bird-noise.

Beetles the color of old bronze navigate the floor bricks, following moisture lines that are invisible to you but apparently blazing highways to them. They make a clicking, ticking sound as their carapaces brush against the uneven surfaces. In one chamber, you find a basin that has overflowed slightly — a palm-width film of water on the brick floor — and it is alive with the larvae of something, small pale commas wriggling in the thin sheet of moisture. The lizards know about this place. Several are arranged around the water's edge like diners at a table.

And the spiders. In the corners, in the shelving, spanning the gaps between pegs with webs that catch the faint light and turn it silver. They are patient. They have been here a long time. Their webs are thick with the husks of consumed insects, and new silk is laid over old in layers that echo the human construction around them — structure built on structure, never cleared, only added to.

---

The light comes from above, barely, through gaps in the construction — places where bricks have been left out intentionally, or where old ventilation shafts connect to the surface. It arrives as shafts, as blades, as diffuse glows through translucent patches of thin-scraped stone that someone fitted like windows. The effect is uneven. One chamber might be bright enough to see every detail — the chisel marks in the stone, the thumbprints in the mortar, the individual barbules of a bird's breast feather caught in a spider web — while the next is a murky twilight where shapes are suggestions and the lizards' eyes catch what little light exists and hold it, twin sparks in the dark.

In the better-lit chambers, you can see the walls more clearly, and this is where the strangeness — the slight, persistent strangeness — of the place asserts itself. The shelves and niches carved into the walls are not storage. Or they were storage once, but they have been converted, over unknowable time, into something else. Each niche holds an object, placed with intention. A smooth river stone balanced on a fired clay disc. A bundle of sticks lashed with gut into a form that is almost but not quite a figure. A row of beetle carapaces arranged by size, glued to the shelf with the same mortar used in the walls. A bird skull, cleaned and white, nested in a cup made from a larger skull — some rodent, maybe — and the whole assemblage sitting on a bed of shed lizard skins.

Hundreds of these. Thousands, probably, if you could explore every chamber, every niche, every shelf-behind-a-shelf. They feel votive, but there is no altar, no center, no focal point of worship. The whole space is the altar. Every surface is a site for offering. And the offerings themselves are made from the materials *of* the cistern — the animals, the minerals, the moisture-deposited salts that form white crusts on certain stones. It is a closed system of devotion. The place worships itself with its own substance.

---

Move deeper — there is always deeper, another doorway half-blocked by a later wall, another passage that forces you to turn sideways — and the character shifts without changing. The chambers get smaller. The density increases. In one room barely large enough to lie down in, every surface has been covered with small ceramic tiles, each one hand-pressed, irregular, bearing a thumbprint or a scratch-mark, and the tiles have been grouted with a different-colored clay, creating a surface that looks almost like mosaic but has no image, no pattern, just the overwhelming *fact* of having been made, piece by piece, by someone's hands. The birds are louder here, somehow. A nest blocks half of one ventilation shaft, and the parent bird, arriving with an insect in its beak, scolds you with a rising trill that the tile walls amplify into something almost painful.

Condensation is heavier in the deeper chambers. The walls are slick in places. Where moisture has gathered and evaporated repeatedly over decades — centuries? — it has deposited mineral traces that form thin, branching lines on the plaster, like the ghosts of river systems. These lines are beautiful, and someone has noticed: in several places, they have been traced over with a dark pigment, emphasized, incorporated into the made surface. The place does not distinguish between the accidental and the intentional. Whatever appears is absorbed into the project of construction.

---

There is a sound under the bird-sound. You notice it only when you hold still and let your ears adjust to the layers. A low, intermittent groaning from the structure itself — thermal expansion, maybe, or the slow settling of walls against each other, or water moving through channels in the masonry that you can't see. It is the sound of the cistern *working*, doing whatever it was originally built to do — collecting, holding, distributing moisture — even though its purpose has been so thoroughly overwritten by the impulse to build, to fill, to make. The groaning comes and goes. It is almost tidal. The birds ignore it. The lizards ignore it. Only you, the newcomer, hear it as strange.

Sometimes a section of wall breathes. Not metaphorically. Air moves through the porous masonry in slow cycles driven by temperature differentials between the surface world and this buried one, and you feel it as a faint exhalation from the bricks themselves, warm and chalky, smelling of mineral dust and the ammoniac tang of bird droppings and the green smell of the sparse, pale moss that grows where moisture and light overlap — the only vegetation, clinging to bricks around the ventilation shafts in thin crusts, yellow-green, barely alive but stubbornly present.

---

A beetle crosses your foot. A bird lands on a shelf three inches from your face, regards you with an eye like a wet black seed, and departs. A lizard drops from the ceiling onto your shoulder and is gone before you can flinch, leaving only the memory of dry, warm feet.

The cistern is not old the way ruins are old — collapsed, emptied, finished. It is old the way a living thing is old: worn and repaired and adapted and still going. Something built it. Something *builds* it, present tense, though you see no builders. The fresh mortar in certain joints — pale against the darkened old — tells you that whoever tends this place was here recently. Days ago. Hours. The newest niche-offerings have bright, unchipped edges. A beetle carapace in an arrangement on the east wall still has a faint iridescence that the older ones have lost.

You could stay. You could add a stone to a shelf, press a tile, trace a mineral line. The place would accept it. The place accepts everything. That is its rule, its only law: nothing is taken out, everything is added. Layer over layer, hand over hand, until the cistern is more construction than void, more object than space, and the animals and the water and the sound move through what remains — the cracks, the gaps, the absences that even the most devoted builder cannot fill.
