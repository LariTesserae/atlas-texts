# The Boiling Plateau of Kesk

---

The first thing you notice is the light. It comes off everything—off the wet mineral flats, off the pale sky, off the standing water that pools in shallow depressions across the plateau's surface like mercury settling into a tray. The sun is high and white-hot, unfiltered by any canopy because there is no canopy, no trees, no scrub, nothing green. The plateau stretches in every direction at this elevation—perhaps fifteen hundred meters above whatever lowlands exist below, though you can't see them from here. The horizon is a shimmering band where bright stone meets bright sky, and the air between warps gently with heat.

The ground is a weathered mineral expanse. Not raw—not fresh volcanic glass or jagged crystal—but something that has been here a long time, settled into itself. The rock is a pale calcium color in most places, stained in long arcs of ochre and rust where mineral-laden water has seeped and dried and seeped again over centuries. The surface is smooth in broad stretches, worn by thermal expansion and the constant weeping of subsurface water through hairline fractures. In other places it buckles into low ridges no taller than your shin, where harder seams of rock resisted the wearing-down that softened everything around them. These ridges run in long parallel curves across the plateau, like the ribs of something enormous buried just beneath the surface.

Water is everywhere and nowhere contained. It rises from below through the porous stone, arrives at the surface already warm—sometimes hot—and spreads into films and shallow pools that evaporate under the glare only to be replenished by the next upwelling. The plateau sweats. There are places where the stone darkens in wide blotches as if something has just breathed on it, and if you kneel and press your hand to the surface, it is slick and warm and faintly trembling. Some of the pools are centimeters deep and meters across, barely visible until the light catches their edges. Others are narrow channels where the water moves with slow, observable purpose along mineral grooves it has carved for itself over decades. The air carries a faint sulfurous mineral tang—not acrid, but persistent, the way the smell of hot stone never quite leaves the inside of your nose.

There is a town here.

It doesn't announce itself the way a town should. You come upon it gradually, the way you come upon a geological feature—first a low wall, then something that might be a wall or might be a natural ridge, then a structure that is unmistakably built but built *with* the plateau rather than *on* it. The architecture is low and spread out. Buildings rarely exceed two stories, and most are single-level complexes with broad footprints, constructed from the same pale stone as the plateau itself, quarried from somewhere—perhaps from the plateau's edges where it drops away. Roofs are flat and slightly concave, designed to collect the water that condenses from the hot air at night and channel it into interior cisterns, though water is hardly scarce here. Maybe it's about *which* water. The upwelling groundwater carries minerals that stain and corrode. Rain and condensation are clean.

The town is sparse. That's the defining quality of its layout. There is space—tremendous, deliberate space—between structures, between districts, between one life and the next. You can stand at the edge of one building complex and see across two hundred meters of open wet stone to the next cluster of structures. The gaps aren't empty in the way abandoned lots are empty; they are occupied by the plateau itself, by its pools and seepages and mineral formations, and the town simply *defers* to them. Streets, where they exist, curve around geological features rather than cutting through them. Some of the low mineral ridges pass directly through the town, and buildings are built alongside them or straddle them, incorporating the natural stone into walls and foundations.

The industry is here but muted. At the town's eastern extent—a direction you orient to only because the mineral staining on the stone grows thicker, suggesting a prevailing flow—there are extraction works. Low, heavy buildings with riveted walls of the same pale stone reinforced with dark metal framing. Pipes emerge from the ground at shallow angles, some of them trembling faintly, carrying hot mineral water from deeper sources to separation tanks where whatever is valuable is precipitated out. You can see the tanks from a distance: rows of wide, open-topped basins, some dark with concentrated brine, others crusted at their rims with crystalline deposits in whites and yellows and one unsettling blue-green. Chimneys exist but are short and squat, more like vents than smokestacks. Thin vapor rises from some of them. The machinery runs on something—electrical cables sag between poles made of the same dark metal, connecting the extraction works to a generation station you can't quite identify but whose low hum you can almost feel in your teeth when the air is still enough. Almost feel. Because the defining acoustic quality of the Boiling Plateau is *quiet*.

Not silence—there is the faintest sound of water, always, a kind of subcutaneous trickling that never resolves into the clear noise of a stream or a drip. And there is the near-subliminal vibration of the industrial works. And very occasionally the thin, high cry of something living. But these sounds exist in a vast envelope of quiet. The plateau absorbs sound. The wet stone, the open distances, the heavy mineral-laden air—they conspire to swallow acoustic energy. Conversations across the open stretches become inaudible within thirty meters. The town has no echo. When the machinery pauses—and it does pause, in cycles you begin to recognize—the silence that fills in is so total it creates a pressure behind your eardrums, as if the plateau itself is listening back.

---

The fauna.

This is where the strangeness begins, or rather, this is where you begin to *notice* the strangeness, because it has been here all along, moving at the edges of your attention.

They are abundant. Surprisingly, almost unsettlingly abundant for a landscape this barren of plant life. They are what lives here instead.

The most visible are the **stone-skimmers**: low-bodied creatures, something between a crab and a salamander in basic morphology, with wide flat bodies the exact color of the pale mineral stone. They range from palm-sized to the span of a dinner plate, and they move across the wet surfaces in quick lateral bursts, pausing to press their ventral sides flat against the rock. They are feeding—on what, you realize after watching closely, are biofilms. Thin, invisible-unless-you-know-to-look layers of microbial life that coat every wet surface on the plateau. Not vegetation. Not even fungal, exactly. Something simpler, something that metabolizes the mineral upwellings and the relentless sunlight into a living skin that coats the stone the way tarnish coats copper. This is the base of the food web. The stone-skimmers graze it.

There are larger things. Knee-height creatures that move between the pools on stilt-like legs, their bodies held above the hot stone, dipping long curved mouthparts into the mineral water. They are solitary and widely spaced—you might see three or four at a time across a vista of several hundred meters—but they are *always* visible somewhere. They are dark-bodied, almost black, which seems wrong for a creature living under this hammering sun until you notice that their carapaces are beaded with condensation. They are using their own body heat differential, their dark shells absorbing solar energy and driving moisture from the air onto their surfaces, and somehow this is part of their biology, part of how they process the mineral water, though you can't guess the full mechanism.

Birds—or something that fills the ecological role of birds. They don't fly so much as glide on the thermals that rise constantly from the heated plateau, and they are almost never on the ground. Wide-winged, translucent-membraned, their bodies small and dense at the center of great spans of tissue. They cast moving shadows across the stone, and the stone-skimmers scatter when the shadows cross them, which suggests predation, though you never see a strike. The gliders circle in wide, patient arcs. Dozens of them, sometimes, turning slowly in the thermal columns above the extraction works where the vented heat creates the strongest updrafts.

And there are things in the water. In the deeper pools—the ones that don't fully evaporate, the ones fed by stronger upwellings—there are shapes. Slow-moving, sinuous, hard to resolve through the mineral-clouded water. They surface occasionally: pale, elongated, eyeless. They seem to navigate by the thermal gradients in the water, orienting always toward the warmer currents. The townspeople leave these pools alone. Some of the pools have low stone walls built around them—not to contain, but to demarcate. To say: *this belongs to something else*.

The ecology is active but unhurried. Predation happens. Reproduction happens. The stone-skimmers have seasons, or cycles, driven not by any change in the relentless climate but by fluctuations in the mineral content of the upwelling water, which shifts the composition of the biofilm, which shifts everything above it. The townspeople know these cycles. They have names for the phases, names that are also the names for economic seasons—when certain minerals precipitate more readily, when the extraction yields change, when certain pools become too biologically active to harvest from.

---

The strangeness isn't dramatic. It accumulates.

It's in the way the mineral ridges hum at certain times—a frequency you feel in your molars, not your ears—when the subsurface water pressure changes. The townspeople call this "the plateau talking" and adjust their extraction schedules accordingly, but no one can explain the mechanism fully. The hum doesn't correlate perfectly with water pressure. It correlates with something else, something the geological surveys haven't identified, or something the geologists identified and then quietly set aside.

It's in the pools with the walls around them. Not all the walled pools contain the pale eyeless swimmers. Some contain nothing visible at all—just clear, hot water, still as glass. But the walls are maintained. Repaired when they crack. The stone used to repair them is always freshly quarried, never salvaged.

It's in the behavior of the gliders. They circle the town in patterns that, seen from above—from the roof of one of the taller extraction buildings, say—describe geometries that feel intentional. Not random thermal-riding. Not hunting patterns. Something else. The townspeople don't look up much.

It's in the way the biofilm grows thicker around the foundations of the oldest buildings. Not because of runoff or shade—there is no shade worth mentioning—but because something about the structures, or something about what the structures were built on top of, encourages the microbial layer to flourish. The oldest district of the town, at the plateau's approximate center, has a faintly different color to its stone—greener, almost, though green is the wrong word. *Livelier*. The stone-skimmers cluster there. The buildings in the old district are inhabited but their purposes are unclear. They are not extraction works, not residences in any obvious configuration. They have deep interior rooms with no windows, rooms that go down into the stone rather than up from it, and the doors to these rooms are heavy and fitted closely and sometimes warm to the touch.

It's in the quiet itself. The quiet that is too thorough, too consistent, too *maintained* for a place with industry and wildlife and water always moving. As if the plateau is not merely absorbing sound but selecting which sounds to permit. The thin cries of the gliders carry for kilometers. The hum of the ridges propagates through stone. But footsteps die within paces. Voices carry only as far as conversation requires. Machinery operates in a sonic envelope that seems to end at the walls of the buildings that house it.

The plateau has been here a long time. The town has been here a long time—not as long, but long enough that the distinction has begun to blur. The extraction works process the minerals that the water brings up from below. The fauna graze the biofilm that the minerals and sun sustain. The townspeople maintain the walls around the pools. The gliders circle. The water rises. The light hammers down and bounces off everything—the wet stone, the still pools, the metal pipes, the pale walls of the low buildings, the crystalline crusts on the evaporation tanks—until the whole plateau seems to glow from within, as if the brightness is not arriving from above but *escaping* from below, surfacing like the water, like the heat, like whatever the plateau is slowly, silently, patiently bringing up into the world.
