# The Cistern Works

---

Below the city, below the foundations, below even the oldest sewer lines, there is a chamber that the city is still in the process of growing.

It is not large. You could cross it in a hundred paces if the footing were sure, which it is not. The ceiling is low in places, maybe three meters, and in others it opens into pockets where the earth has softened and sagged upward, creating domes of wet clay that hold their shape through some temporary agreement between gravity and moisture. The air is hot — deeply, persistently hot — the kind of heat that rises from the earth itself rather than descending from a sun. Down here there is no sun. There is only the pale industrial glow of caged work-lights strung on a cable that loops across the space in a careless drape, many of the bulbs dead or flickering at the end of their filaments, casting a brownish, uncertain light that makes the walls seem to breathe.

The walls are calcium-rich sediment,ite — calcareous sediment that is in the process of becoming limestone but hasn't committed yet. It is pale, damp, yielding to the touch. If you pressed your thumb into the wall, it would leave an impression that might last a thousand years, entombed eventually in rock. The floor is the same material in a wetter state — a pale slurry in some places, a firmer paste in others, crisscrossed with the imprints of boot treads and the ruts of narrow-gauge cart tracks. The whole chamber has the quality of a mold that hasn't set, a geological embryo being shaped by pressures from above and heat from below.

The infrastructure is what makes it a cistern works, though the name is old and possibly inaccurate. At the center of the chamber, two cast-iron pipes descend from the ceiling — each as thick as a man's torso — and plunge into the soft ground, their casings sweating with condensation. These are municipal water mains, and they are warm to the touch. Something about passing through this stratum heats the water. Around the pipes, a scaffold of iron bracing and wooden planking has been erected — a rough industrial cradle to keep the pipes steady as the ground beneath them continues its slow compression. Bolts have been driven into the soft rock; some of them are already beginning to be absorbed, the sediment closing around the bolt-heads like flesh around a splinter.

There is a pump station against the far wall — hand-operated, though its flywheel is connected by a slack belt to a small diesel engine that sits on a wooden pallet, silent now, its exhaust pipe snaking up through a crack in the ceiling. A workbench holds wrenches, pipe fittings, a tin cup with cold tea in it. A clipboard hangs from a nail. Someone works here. Someone comes down the iron ladder in the northeast corner — you can see the ladder, its rungs polished by use — and spends hours in this hot dim room, tending the pipes, monitoring the slow formation of the ground, maybe tightening the bolts before the earth swallows them entirely.

But that person is not here now.

What is here, overwhelmingly, are the moths.

---

They are everywhere. Not in frantic clouds but in a persistent, patient occupation of the space — resting on the walls, clinging to the underside of the scaffold, settling on the iron pipes where the condensation beads. They are pale, most of them — cream-colored, ash-gray, soft tan — as if they have been calibrated to the palette of the sediment. Their wingspans range from a thumbnail to a handspan. In the dim light their eyes catch the filament-glow and throw it back as tiny paired sparks, so the walls seem studded with faint copper stars.

They are not silent. The collective sound of their wings — not all flying at once, but enough of them shifting, resettling, lifting and landing in staggered rhythm — creates a papery, continuous rustle, like a room full of people turning pages. Beneath that, the deeper sounds: the ticking of the cooling engine, the slow drip of condensation falling from pipe-joints into the soft ground, the almost sub-audible groan of the earth settling. The heat makes the air itself feel thick with sound, as if noise has weight here and sinks rather than dissipates.

There are other creatures too, drawn by the moths or by whatever draws the moths. Spiders have strung webs between the scaffold struts — not elaborate orb-webs but messy, pragmatic sheet-webs heavy with caught wings. Pale geckos cling to the warm pipes, motionless for long minutes and then startlingly quick, snapping at moths that venture too close. You can count eight, ten, twelve geckos on the two pipes alone, their translucent bellies showing the shadows of recent meals. Beneath the workbench, a toad sits in a shallow depression it has worn into the soft ground — a fat, patient toad with pale, almost white skin and dark eyes, surrounded by the discarded wings of hundreds of moths, a litter of tissue-thin scales that stir when anyone moves through the room.

And there are beetles — small, dark, hard-shelled — that trundle across the floor in the ruts left by the cart tracks, feeding on whatever organic material the moths deposit. Their movements are so slow and deliberate that they seem mechanical, like tiny autonomous vehicles following predetermined routes. One of them has climbed the base of the diesel engine and is making its way across the flywheel, antennae twitching.

The ecosystem is dense, specific, and enclosed. It has the quality of an island — everything here depends on everything else, and the boundaries of the chamber are the boundaries of the world. The moths came first, probably, drawn by the heat and the light, finding their way down through the same cracks that carry the engine exhaust up. The spiders followed the moths. The geckos followed the moths. The toad followed the moths. The beetles followed the waste. And now they all live here together in the hot dim embryonic rock, a complete food web operating in a space the size of a modest apartment, ticking over with the slow patience of things that have found their niche and settled in.

---

The vegetation is almost nonexistent, but not quite. In the corners, where condensation pools and the light from the nearest bulb is weakest, there are thin films of something — algae or some relative of algae — that coat the wet sediment in a faint greenish sheen. A few threads of pale root have found their way down from above, penetrating the ceiling in hairline cracks and dangling a centimeter or two into the open air before giving up. They are dry and dead at the tips. Whatever plant sent them down was searching for water and found only heat.

The moisture in the room is particular. It is not wet in the way of a swamp or a rain. It is wet in the way of a body — an internal wetness, metabolic, warm. The air is saturated enough that your skin is damp within minutes of descending the ladder, but nothing drips dramatically. The water is *in* things rather than *on* them: in the walls, in the sediment, in the condensation film on the pipes, in the sweat on the wrenches. The ground squelches underfoot. Your boot-prints fill slowly with pale water that is slightly warm and faintly chalky. The water table is near — just below the floor, maybe a meter down — and the whole chamber exists in negotiation with it, the sediment wicking moisture upward like a sponge balanced on a shallow bath.

---

What is this place *for*?

The clipboard offers clues. The top sheet is a log — dates, readings, initials. Water pressure in the mains, temperature at three points in the chamber, a column labeled "SUBSIDENCE (mm)" with numbers that increase by fractions from entry to entry. Whoever monitors this place is tracking the slow sinking of the ground, the gradual compression of raw sediment under the weight of the city above. The pipes must be kept level, must be kept sealed, must be kept from being swallowed. It is maintenance work at the pace of geology — a few millimeters per month, a bolt retightened here, a brace repositioned there. The chamber itself is not finished; it is not a room that was carved and completed. It is a void that the earth hasn't closed yet, kept open by the infrastructure threaded through it, a space that exists only because something needs to be tended in it.

And yet the creatures have made it theirs. The toad does not know about water pressure readings. The geckos do not care about subsidence. The moths are drawn to the warmth and the light, and the rest follows, and the chamber that was meant to be a utilitarian access point — a node in the city's plumbing — has become a vivarium, a hot dim nursery full of pale wings and quiet feeding.

The light flickers. For a moment, half the room goes dark, and in that half-darkness the moth-eyes glow brighter, a constellation pressed against the soft walls of a world still deciding what kind of stone it wants to become. Then the filament catches again, and the light steadies, and the rustle of wings continues, and the ground settles another fraction of a millimeter, and the toad swallows, and the beetles trundle on.
