# The Overgrown Garrison

---

It must have been a fort once, or a watchtower compound — something military and stone-cornered, set on a cool plateau shelf where the ridgeline dips to form a saddle between two modest peaks. You can still see the bones of it: a perimeter wall, hip-high now, its dressed stones furred so deeply in moss and liverwort that they look like green loaves of bread. Corner posts. The ghost of a courtyard. A cistern, long since overrun. Three, maybe four structures whose rooflines collapsed decades ago and whose interiors have become planting beds for everything that seeds, spores, or creeps.

The place is perhaps sixty meters across. You can walk its boundaries in a few minutes. But you cannot walk them quickly, because there is nowhere to put your feet that is not alive.

---

## The Green

Vegetation here has passed the point of occupation and become *substrate*. The old walls are not walls with moss — they are moss that happens to contain stone. Ferns erupt from every joint and ledge, their fronds uncurling in slow fiddleheads or already spread wide, overlapping each other in layers three and four deep, each frond hosting its own cargo of smaller epiphytes. Where the original roofbeams fell and rotted, nurse logs now support entire garden-rows of saplings — mountain ash, some kind of small-leafed willow, a birch whose trunk has bent sideways to grow through what was once a window and now serves as a trellis for a climbing hydrangea so dense with white lacecap blooms it looks like the wall is foaming.

The ground is not ground. It is a mat — a woven, breathing, saturated mat of sphagnum, hair moss, liverwort, creeping jenny, wild thyme running feral, wood sorrel, and at least two species of tiny fern that grow flat like green lace pressed into a book. Step on it and water wells up around your boot. Step again and you might go ankle-deep. The water table sits just beneath the surface, held there by the plateau's clay underlayment and fed constantly by the mist that gathers along the ridgeline. Everything is wet. The stones weep. The moss drips. The leaves of the ferns carry permanent beadwork.

In the old cistern — a stone-lined rectangle maybe four meters by three — the water is open and dark, stained brown with tannins, and water plants have colonized it so aggressively that the surface is more leaf than liquid: duckweed, water starwort, a miniature water lily with leaves no bigger than coins, and beneath them, trailing forests of submerged pondweed moving in slow currents generated by the springs that still feed the basin from below. The overflow runs through a channel cut in the perimeter wall — the fort's builders knew drainage — and this channel has become a rivulet bed choked with watercress and brooklime and the brilliant emerald threads of algae that catch and hold any light that falls on them.

Trees lean inward from every side. The plateau shelf is not high enough for timberline to matter, but the exposure and the cool ridge-winds have kept the trees small, dense, wind-trained. They form a canopy over the compound that filters the daylight into a green-gold haze, shifting constantly as the branches move. Hazel. Rowan. Crabapple heavy with fruit the size of marbles, sour and hard but everything eats them — everything.

---

## The Fauna

This is where the place becomes overwhelming.

The compound teems. It is an island of shelter and water on an exposed ridge, and every creature for a mile in any direction seems to know it. The sheer *count* of animals visible at any given moment is difficult to process.

**Birds** dominate by volume. Wrens — there must be a dozen wrens, all of them apparently furious, ricocheting through the fern layers and the collapsed walls with a manic energy that never rests, singing at a volume that seems impossible for something that weighs less than a coin. Their songs bounce off the remaining stone surfaces and multiply. Then there are tits — coal tits, blue tits, working the moss on the walls for insects with the focused intensity of surgeons, hanging upside down, flitting, calling to each other in sharp chips and trills. A pair of redstarts nest somewhere in the eastern wall, the male a constant flicker of orange tail among the green. Blackbirds root through the ground-mat, flipping debris, finding worms, pausing to sing from the top of whatever height they can find — a broken wall-post, a leaning branch — and their song is rich and absurdly loud, a fluting that fills the entire space. A grey wagtail works the cistern overflow, its yellow belly flashing as it bobs and darts over the watercress. In the canopy, a woodpigeon coos its five-note drone endlessly, stupidly, a background metronome to everything else.

And the sounds *layer*. At this moment — this single frozen moment — you can hear at least seven bird species singing simultaneously, plus the constant chatter of movement: wing-rustle through ferns, the scratch of claws on stone, the thump of a blackbird's beak striking soil.

**Insects** are everywhere the warmth allows, and even the cool air doesn't suppress them entirely — they are just slower, more deliberate. Hoverflies work the hydrangea blooms, their flight a patient, precise suspension. Bees — small solitary bees and a few early bumblebees whose fur is still damp with mist — navigate between thyme flowers at ground level. Moths rest on the undersides of fern fronds, their wings folded like pale origami. Craneflies drift through the interior spaces in that aimless, thread-legged way they have, bumping into things. Beetles, too: a green tortoise beetle on a leaf, a ground beetle traversing the moss mat like a black pebble with purpose.

**Amphibians** own the wet margins. A common frog surfaces in the cistern, blinks, submerges. Another sits on the moss beside the overflow channel, throat pulsing. Under the collapsed stone slabs of what might have been a storehouse floor, you can hear — you can *hear* — the wet shifting of salamanders. Newts, too, in the cistern. Palmate newts. You catch a glimpse of one swimming beneath the duckweed, its body undulating, its tail a ribbon.

And something larger — a stoat, maybe, or a weasel — has slipped into a gap in the southern wall. You didn't see it clearly. Just the sinuous pour of a brown body into darkness, and then a small bird alarm-call, and then silence from that corner, and then the normal noise reasserts itself.

---

## The Turbulence

Nothing here is still. The compound exists in a state of perpetual churn, an ecological bazaar that never closes.

The mist moves through in waves. You will have five minutes of clear, green-gold light and then the ridge-cloud descends and everything goes grey-white and close and the dripping intensifies. The temperature drops a degree. The birds do not stop. Then the mist lifts and the light returns and the hydrangea blooms glow like lamps and the frog calls start up again.

The wind, too, is inconstant and gusty on this saddle between peaks. It comes from the west mostly, but it swirls in the compound, caught by the remaining walls, and it moves the canopy in unpredictable surges. Leaves spin down. Water drips from disturbed branches in sudden small showers. The ferns nod and recover. A rotted section of old roofbeam, balanced on a wall-edge for years, shifts in a gust and drops into the interior space with a damp thud, and the wrens explode out of the ferns in outraged alarm and then return almost instantly.

Animals chase each other. Tits squabble over a patch of insect-rich bark, fluttering up in brief vertical confrontations before returning. A blackbird chases another blackbird across the courtyard at speed, banking through the fern-filled window openings. The stoat — if it is a stoat — reappears on the far wall and two wrens mob it hysterically, diving at its head until it vanishes again. A frog leaps into the cistern with a small plop. Something is excavating a hole at the base of the northern wall: fresh earth scattered on the moss, the excavation ongoing, the digger — a vole? a shrew? — invisible but audible, a faint rhythmic scraping under the stone.

The plants themselves grow with visible urgency. Not that you can *watch* them move, but you can see the evidence of recent push — a fern frond that has uncoiled and straightened in the last day, its tip still curled, its tissues still pale and tender. The ivy on the eastern wall has sent new shoots a hand's-breadth past the rest of its leading edge, each shoot tipped with a pale searching nub. The hydrangea has blooms in every stage from tight green bud to full white parasol to brown-edged decline. Time moves quickly here. Growth and decay proceed at the same pace, interleaved, simultaneous.

---

## The Built

The human craft is all old. Hand-dressed stone. Lime mortar, mostly dissolved now, replaced by root and moss as binding agent. Someone cut drainage channels with a chisel. Someone fitted lintels over doorways that the vegetation has since reduced to green arches. There are iron fittings — hinges, a latch — rusted to the color and texture of the stone around them, bonded now by corrosion and moss into single objects that are neither metal nor stone nor plant but some fused category. In one corner, a hearthstone: flat, fire-blackened, cracked by frost, with a small rowan seedling growing from the crack, its roots gripping the soot-darkened surface.

Whoever built this place chose well. The saddle catches water. The walls break wind. The southern exposure, even on this cool ridge, gathers what warmth there is. They engineered a functional cistern that still works — still fills, still drains — decades after they left. The watercress in the overflow channel may even be descended from plants they cultivated. The crabapples may be feral descendants of a planted tree.

But they are gone, and the compound has been colonized so completely that their departure seems ancient. The moss grows in layers you could measure with a ruler. The ferns have established root systems that grip the walls more firmly than the original mortar. This is not ruin — ruin implies loss. This is succession. The fort has become a garden has become a habitat has become a *node*, a dense point of biological activity on an otherwise exposed and wind-scoured ridge.

## The Sound

Stand in the center of the old courtyard and close your eyes.

The wind in the canopy: a broad, rushing hiss that swells and fades irregularly, with creaks from the branches and the occasional sharp snap of wood.

The water: the drip and trickle of the cistern overflow, the tiny patter of condensation falling from leaf to leaf to moss, the almost subliminal gurgle of the spring feeding the cistern from below.

The birds: wrens singing at full territorial volume, blackbird song in long melodic phrases, tit calls in sharp staccato, the wood pigeon's coo, a robin somewhere in the hazel singing a thin silver thread of notes, the alarm chips and flight calls and contact notes of a dozen species going about their urgent business.

The insects: the drone of a bumblebee passing close, the faint whine of a hoverfly.

The hidden sounds: the scraping of the small excavator at the wall base, a rustle in the ferns that could be anything, the wet *plop* of the frog entering water, the tick of a beetle's legs on stone.

It is not cacophony. It is a dense, layered, living broadcast — all of it happening at once, none of it coordinated, all of it *entirely* indifferent to your presence. You are standing in a small stone ruin on a wet ridge in cool air, and the ruin is more alive than you are. It hums. It chatters. It drips and grows and hunts and sings and digs and decays and pushes new leaves into whatever space remains, and there is almost no space remaining, and still it pushes.
