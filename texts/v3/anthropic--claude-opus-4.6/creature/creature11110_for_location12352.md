# Who Lives in the Hanging Village of Dry Moss

---

They are people who have learned to think in textures.

This is the first thing you would notice, if you stayed long enough to notice anything, which itself requires a kind of patience that the ridge seems to teach by proximity. Their hands are always reading something — a wall, a mat of moss, the edge of a freshly split panel — and their faces change in response to what their fingers find, the way a reader's face changes with a sentence. They do not look at surfaces so much as touch them and know. A woman runs her thumb along a moss curtain near a doorway and pauses, frowns slightly, presses deeper — she has found a place where the growth is thinning, where the chemistry is shifting, and she knows this the way someone elsewhere might know a cloud means rain. She will not repair it yet. She will return to it over several days, feeling the change, understanding its direction, before she acts.

Their hands are extraordinary. Not calloused, exactly — the moss is too soft for that — but *dense*, the skin of the fingertips thickened and smooth, like the pads of an animal built for climbing. The prints have not worn away but deepened, as though the constant kneading and pressing has made their fingerprints more themselves. A palmist, if one existed here, would find the lines of their hands almost illegible — too many, too fine, crisscrossing in networks that mirror the branching patterns they press into their walls with those iridescent scales.

---

There is a woman who is older than anyone else in the village, though she does not look fragile. She lives in one of the deepest recesses, where three sheets of tilted basalt converge to form a kind of triangular chamber, its walls entirely lined with the red moss that glows. She is the one who maintains the patterns — the spirals and branching lines made of the shed scales from whatever nameless thing lives deeper still. You might call her an artist or a cartographer or a priestess, but none of those words fit, because none of those words describe someone for whom all three activities are the same activity. She presses the scales into wet moss with a slowness that suggests she is listening to something through her fingertips. She works for perhaps an hour each day, sometimes less. The rest of the time she sits in the dim red glow of her chamber and does something that is not quite meditation and not quite sleep — her eyes are open, her breathing is slow, and her hands rest flat against the living walls, palms down, as though receiving dictation.

The patterns she makes are not decorative. They are — and this is a thing you come to understand only gradually — *instructions*. Not in the sense of a manual, but in the sense that a root system is an instruction for how to find what the soil is offering. Other villagers visit her chamber, one at a time, and stand looking at the latest additions to the pattern for long stretches. They do not speak. They do not mouth words. They look, and then they touch, running their fingers along the spiraling lines of translucent scales, and when they leave, they seem to know something they didn't know before — where to cut the next panel, which shelf of stone is ready for habitation, where the moss is healthiest, where the deep chemistry of the ridge is shifting in ways that matter.

She has no name that is spoken. She may have no name at all. Names require a kind of separation — *this* person, not *that* person — that the village seems to have less use for than most places. They distinguish each other, certainly, but the distinction is gestural, spatial, temporal: the one who is there at that time doing that thing. Identity is positional. You are where you are in the structure, and the structure is alive and slowly changing, so who you are is also alive and slowly changing, and a name would pin it down in a way that would feel, to these people, like a small and unnecessary violence.

---

The children are the most startling thing about the village, because they exist at all.

There are perhaps eight or nine of them, ranging from an infant carried against its mother's chest in a sling of woven moss-fiber to a girl on the edge of adolescence who moves through the vertical spaces of the ridge with an ease that suggests she was born climbing, which she was. The children are not quiet in the way the adults are quiet. They are quiet in a different way — the way a hunting animal is quiet, attentive and coiled, their silence full of readiness rather than stillness. They watch everything with an intensity that would be unsettling if it weren't so clearly joyful. A boy of perhaps five crouches at the edge of a moss mat, watching a velvety beetle move along a ridge of growth, and his whole body is organized around the act of watching — he is *leaning into it*, not just with his eyes but with his skin, his posture, the tilt of his head matching the tilt of the beetle's path. When the beetle disappears into a crevice, he stays crouched for a moment longer, then looks up and grins at no one, at the air, at the fact of the beetle having existed and moved and vanished.

The children learn by accompanying. There is no school, no instruction in any formal sense. A child attaches herself to an adult — any adult, not necessarily a parent — and does what they do, badly at first and then less badly. A girl of seven is learning to shape moss panels. She tears the wet moss clumsily, her pieces uneven, and the woman beside her does not correct her but simply continues her own work, her own hands demonstrating by the fact of their competence, not by any explicit teaching. The girl watches the woman's hands, then looks at her own, then tries again. This cycle — watch, attempt, watch, attempt — continues for weeks, for months, and at some point the girl's panels begin to hold arches, and she is a builder, and no one remarks on this transition because no one needed to.

The infant is the quietest of all. It sleeps against its mother's warmth and the warmth of the moss behind her, and when it wakes, it does not cry. It opens its dark eyes and looks. It has already learned — or was born knowing — that sound is not how this place works. It communicates need through movement, through the particular quality of its grip on its mother's clothing, through the way it turns its head. The mother reads these signals with the same fingertip attention she gives to the moss walls. She seems never to be wrong about what the child wants. Watching them together, you begin to wonder whether spoken language, in a place like this, might actually be a cruder instrument than whatever they are using instead.

---

The men — and the distinction between men and women here is present but unhurried, more a tendency than a boundary — the men are the ones who work the edges.

This means something specific. The ridge is still growing, still pushing upward, and its outer faces are where the newest stone appears, sharp-edged and hot and bare. The men who work the edges are the ones who assess this new stone — which shelves are stable, which crevices are deep enough to shelter moss, which faces are angled to catch the chemical exhalations that the moss needs. They climb without ropes, without tools, using the angular geometry of the fresh stone the way the geckos do — flat-bodied, spread wide, moving in sudden committed shifts from one hold to the next. They are lean and their skin is weathered to the color of the stone itself, a pale mineral gray that makes them nearly invisible against the ridge face.

One man in particular moves higher than the others. He goes to the very top, where no moss grows and the stone hums with the frequency of its own becoming. He goes alone, and he stays for hours, his palms flat against the warm rock, and when he returns he carries information that he shares with the old woman in the red chamber — not in words, not in gestures, but by placing his hands against the wall beside her latest pattern and pressing, as though he could transfer what he felt directly through the medium of the moss. Perhaps he can. She responds by adding to the pattern, or not, and either response seems to satisfy him, and he leaves.

He is the thinnest person in the village. His cheekbones are sharp, his eyes set deep, and there is about him the quality of someone who is being slowly translated into a different material. His fingertips are almost translucent at the edges — the skin worn so thin by constant contact with raw stone that you can see the faint shadows of blood vessels beneath, a pink-red glow not unlike the luminescence of the deep moss. He is becoming, in some visible and literal way, more like the thing he studies. Whether this frightens him is impossible to say. His face, when you can see it, carries an expression of absolute attention that could be rapture or could be dread and is probably something for which no word exists in any language that relies on sound.

---

They eat, though it takes time to understand what.

The seed-pods from the brass-colored bushes are gathered — or rather, they are allowed to fall, and then gathered. No one picks them from the branch. There is a sense, never articulated, that the plant should be allowed to offer what it offers in its own time. The pods are split open to reveal a dense, pale interior that has the consistency of hard cheese and a taste — you are told by the quality of their chewing, by the expression on their faces — that is mineral and faintly sweet, the same sweetness that pervades the air of the interiors. This is their staple. They eat it slowly, in small amounts, several times a day.

The beetles, too, are eaten, though selectively — only the gray-green ones from the outer surfaces, never the red ones from the deep crevices, a distinction maintained with such consistency that it clearly carries the weight of something like law or taboo, though neither word is quite right for a rule that exists only in practice and is never stated. The beetles are eaten raw and whole, and the faces of the eaters suggest this is not a pleasure but a necessity — a source of something the seed-pods don't provide, perhaps a mineral, perhaps a protein that the moss-based diet otherwise lacks.

Water — the absence that defines this place — is managed through the seed-pods, which contain moisture sequestered from the chemical processes of the moss. It is not much. The people of the village are never fully hydrated in the way that people elsewhere are. Their skin is dry, their lips perpetually cracked despite the slow application of a paste made from crushed moss, their eyes slightly sunken. They have adapted, over generations, to a state of mild, permanent thirst — or rather, they have redefined what thirst means, so that their baseline is a dryness that would be discomfort elsewhere but is simply the way the body feels here, the way the body feels when it is part of this particular system, this ecology of stone and moss and slow chemistry.

---

There are relationships here that are not visible at first.

Two men who work the edges together move in a coordination so precise that it cannot be the result of planning — they shift their weight on the rock face in complementary rhythms, one reaching as the other braces, a continuous exchange of tension and support that looks like a single organism distributing itself across space. They share a dwelling in one of the middle shelves of the ridge, a chamber where two moss panels have been shaped into a sleeping surface wide enough for both, curved slightly upward at the edges like a shared nest. Their bodies, at rest, fit against each other in a way that suggests this geometry has been practiced nightly for years. They are not demonstrative. They are rarely even in the same visual frame during the working hours. But there is a quality of *orientation* between them — each one always faintly aware of where the other is, the way a compass needle is aware of north — that speaks to a bond so deep it has become structural, like the moss fusing where two mats grow close enough to touch.

A woman with one hand — the left, missing below the wrist, the stump smooth and healed and evidently old — is one of the most skilled builders. She shapes panels with her remaining hand and her forearm and her teeth, working the wet moss with a ferocity that is the closest thing to urgency you see in the village. She builds faster than anyone else, not because she is in a rush but because her body has found efficiencies that two-handed people haven't needed to discover. She sleeps alone in a small chamber near the top of the inhabited zone, and the walls of her chamber are covered in scale-patterns that she has made herself — not copies of the old woman's patterns but responses to them, a secondary language that no one else seems to read but that she adds to daily, pressing each tiny iridescent scale into place with the thumb of her remaining hand, her face carrying that same expression of absolute attention.

---

What do they believe?

This is the wrong question, or rather it is a question shaped for a different kind of people. They do not appear to have beliefs in the sense of propositions about the unseen. They have *attentions*. They attend to the moss, to the stone, to the slow upward push of the ridge, to the patterns that the old woman makes and that the one-handed woman answers, to the thing in the deep crevices that sheds its iridescent scales and has never been seen. Their relationship to these phenomena is not one of worship or explanation but of *correspondence* — they are in correspondence with the ridge the way the moss is in correspondence with the stone, drawing sustenance from it, shaped by it, slowly becoming part of it.

The patterns are the closest thing to a scripture, but they are a scripture that is always being written and never being read — or rather, being read only through touch, only by fingertips, so that comprehension is a physical event, a thing that happens in the skin before it happens (if it happens at all) in the mind. The spirals and branching lines describe something — the deep structure of the ridge, the flow of minerals, the movement of the unseen creature, the slow calendar of emergence and collapse — but the description is not separable from the medium. You cannot extract the meaning from the wall. The meaning *is* the wall, the living moss behind the scales, the warmth, the dim red glow.

If pressed — if someone from outside could somehow ask and somehow be answered — they might say that the ridge is alive. Not metaphorically. Not spiritually. They would mean it the way you mean it when you say a dog is alive or a tree is alive: it metabolizes, it grows, it responds, it will eventually die. The ridge is an organism and they live inside it and they are part of its process and it is part of theirs and the distinction between the village and the ridge is a convenience of description, not a fact. They would say this — or rather, they would *show* it, by pressing your hand against the warm stone and holding it there until you felt the hum, and then pressing your hand against the moss and holding it there until you felt the warmth, and then pressing your hand against their own chest and holding it there until you understood that the vibration and the warmth and the heartbeat were variations on a single theme.

---

At night, the village disappears.

Not literally — the structures are still there, the moss still glows its deep arterial red — but the people withdraw into their chambers and become so still, so silent, so folded into the living walls, that the distinction between inhabitant and habitat dissolves entirely. The ridge breathes its slow chemical breath. The moss grows, imperceptibly, thickening by fractions of fractions. The gliding creatures spiral through the warm updrafts between the stone pillars, catching insects in silence. The geckos freeze on their vertical surfaces, eyes open, watching nothing and everything. Somewhere in the deep crevices, something large and never seen moves against the stone and leaves behind a scale that catches the moss-light and holds it, a tiny point of iridescence in the dark.

And the people sleep inside the moss like seeds inside a pod, their bodies warm, their breathing slow, their fingertips touching the walls even in sleep, still reading, still in correspondence, still part of the conversation between stone and life that has been going on since the ridge first broke through the surface and the silence settled over it like a hand.

They are not separate from this place. They are what happens when this place becomes aware of itself — when the ridge's slow upward push and the moss's slow lateral spread and the beetles' slow consumption and the seed-pods' slow ripening produce, as a byproduct or perhaps as a purpose, something that can press its palm to the warm stone and feel the hum and know what it means, which is: *I am still becoming. I am still becoming. I am still becoming.*

They are the ridge, attending to itself.

They are the silence, occupied.
