The man at the lathe is the easiest to start with, because he is the most visible, but he is not the most knowable. He is somewhere between fifty and seventy in the way that men who work with metal in humid places are: his age has settled, like everything else here, into a fixed state. His hands are the color of the oily steel bench, or the bench is the color of his hands — the causality dissolved long ago. He wears a short-sleeved shirt, once blue, now a color that doesn't have a name but that you would recognize instantly as correct if you saw it. The shirt is damp. Everything is damp. He doesn't notice the damp the way he doesn't notice the pumps. His forearms are heavy and mapped with small pale scars from metal filings that jumped the wrong way years or decades ago, and each scar has a corresponding lesson that he could articulate if anyone asked but no one has asked because everyone who would need to know already knows.

His name is something like Garza or Garzo or maybe it's a nickname derived from a surname that no longer matters. People who come to him — and people do come, occasionally, with a seized valve or a worn impeller or a packing gland that has finally wept itself hollow — call him by this name the way you'd call a street by its name. Not a greeting, just a locator. *Garza.* And he looks up, or sometimes he doesn't, and the transaction happens in the way it has always happened: the broken part is placed on the bench, he looks at it, he looks at the shelf, he either has what's needed or he makes what's needed, and the broken part goes into a bucket and the new part goes into the hands of whoever brought the old one. Money changes hands or doesn't. There may be an account somewhere, a ledger, a running tally kept in a way that makes sense only to the people involved. Or there may be nothing — just the understanding that the pumps must run and he is the one who ensures they run and that this is sufficient, that this is a complete economy.

He eats at the bench. Rice and something, beans probably, from a container that someone brings him or that he prepares himself on a hot plate in the back room where he also sleeps on a cot that is exactly as wide as his body. The cat on the bench gets the scraps, or the cat gets its own meal from the frogs and the beetles and the scraps are just supplementary, a matter of courtesy between two residents who have lived in proximity long enough that the distinction between companionship and cohabitation has lost its meaning. The cat is gray-brown, the color of damp concrete, and it has a torn ear and eyes that are the amber of the sodium lamp, and it watches the lathe spin with the deep, unbothered attention of a creature that has never in its life needed to be anywhere else.

He talks to the cat. Not constantly, not performatively, but in the way of someone continuing a conversation that started so long ago that it no longer requires context. Fragments. Observations about the quality of a casting, or the weather, which is to say the non-weather, the unchanged unchanged air. The cat blinks. This is sufficient.

At night — though night in the Pumphouse District is only a deepening of the existing dimness, the sodium lamp simply becoming more dominant as the thin strip of sky above the catenary wires goes from tea-brown to black — he turns off the work light over the bench but leaves the machines running and lies on the cot and listens to the pumps through the wall and falls asleep inside the sound of them the way a child falls asleep inside the sound of a heartbeat. He has always done this. He will always do this. He is one of the district's fixed points, a human manifestation of its equilibrium, and if you asked him whether he was happy he would not understand the question, not because he lacks the capacity for happiness but because the question implies a comparison to some other state, and he has no other state, and the district has no other state, and the comparison is therefore incoherent.

---

The two inhabited buildings are harder.

In the one with the laundry on the wire — the building on the east side of Pump Street, three stories, the middle floor, the window that faces the alley between the old secondary pumping station and a storehouse whose purpose has been forgotten — there lives a woman with her mother. Or a woman with her aunt. Or a woman with an older woman who is not related to her but who has lived with her for so long that the distinction between family and cohabitation has undergone the same dissolution that everything undergoes here. They are two women, one old and one older, and they live in four rooms above the sound of a pump that cycles every ninety seconds with a deep percussive *thud* that they have long since incorporated into their sense of time.

The younger woman — she is perhaps forty, perhaps fifty, with strong hands and dark hair pulled back and a face that is beautiful in the way that functional things are beautiful, in the way the fig tree is beautiful, without apology and without effort — she works. She does something with fabric. Not sewing exactly, or not only sewing. There is a machine, a heavy old industrial thing with a cast-iron body, and bolts of material in the corner, and finished pieces hanging from a rod: heavy canvas things, tarps or coverings, cut and reinforced with grommets. Pump covers, maybe. Equipment shrouds. Coverings for the pipe housings when — but they never need covering, do they? The cats lie on them uncovered and nothing changes. Still, she makes them. Someone somewhere beyond the embankments orders them, or she makes them and they accumulate and periodically someone comes and takes a stack of them and leaves money or goods, and this is her economy, as the impellers are Garza's.

She hangs the laundry not because it will dry — nothing dries here, not fully — but because the act of hanging it is part of the rhythm, and the laundry on the wire is part of the architecture of the place, the same way the catenary wires are, the same way the sodium lamp is. Shirts that are perpetually almost-dry. Sheets that hold the humidity in their fibers like a memory of the air. She takes them in and hangs others and takes those in and the laundry line is never empty and the laundry is never dry and this is not a problem because it has never been a problem.

The older woman sits. She sits in a chair by the window — not looking out, not looking in, just sitting — and she is part of the building the way the fern is part of the flood wall. She has a radio, and she is the one responsible for the cumbia that leaks out into the district and blends with the pumps and the frogs. She keeps it on always, tuned to a station that plays this music endlessly, or perhaps the station has long since stopped broadcasting and the radio is picking up some residual signal, some electromagnetic echo trapped in the damp air between the embankments, and the music that plays is not being played by anyone anymore but simply *persists*, the way everything here persists, because nothing has turned it off.

She speaks to the younger woman occasionally, and the younger woman answers, and their conversations have the quality of the lathe-man's conversations with his cat: ongoing, contextless, fragments of something continuous. They eat together, rice and beans and something else, plantains maybe, fried on a stove that runs on gas from a canister that gets replaced at intervals so regular they are indistinguishable from a natural cycle. They sleep in separate rooms, the younger woman deeply and immediately, the older woman in the shallow, intermittent way of the very old, waking to hear the pumps and the frogs and the radio that she may or may not have left on, and falling back into sleep that is not very different from waking.

The older woman remembers when the district was something else. Or she does not remember, exactly, but she carries in her body a knowledge of a time when the pumps were tended by crews, when the streets were swept, when the buildings were full and loud and purposeful. She has never described this time to the younger woman because the younger woman has never asked and because the description would be irrelevant — it would be like describing the geology of the bedrock to the fern. The fern doesn't need to know what came before. The fern knows what is. The older woman knows what is, too, and what is has been what is for long enough that what was has become a kind of dream, no more real than the cumbia from the station that may no longer exist.

---

The other inhabited building — the one with the television glow, on the Gutter, second floor, the window propped with a can — that one is harder still.

There is a man in there, or there are two men, or there is a man and someone who comes and goes. The television is always on. It casts its blue-gray light through the fogged glass and the light mixes with the amber of the sodium lamp and produces a color on the wet street below that is unlike any other color in the district — a kind of bruised lavender, visible only from a specific angle, only at night, only if you are standing in the right place at the right time, which is any time, because nothing changes.

What is known about this inhabitant — inhabitants — is almost nothing, and this is by design, or by the same process that produces all design here, which is the absence of any force sufficient to change the existing state. The window is fogged not because the glass is dirty but because the temperature differential between inside and outside is just enough to produce condensation, which means the interior is slightly cooler than the district air, which means there is some form of cooling in there — a fan, an old unit, something that hums — and this is the only building in the district that maintains a temperature different from the district's own. This is either significant or it isn't. The building keeps its secret the way the primary pumping station on the Gutter keeps its secret, which is effortlessly, by simply not being open.

Sometimes at night — late, when the sodium lamp is at its most dominant and the frogs are at their loudest — a figure is visible in the fogged window, backlit by the television, standing and looking out, or standing and not looking at anything, just standing in the way a person stands when they have gotten up for a reason they've already forgotten. The figure is tall. That's all. Tall, still, and present for a few minutes before it moves away from the window and the fogged glass shows nothing again but the television's glow.

Does this person interact with Garza, with the women, with the district at all? There must be some interaction — food comes in, waste goes out, the television requires a signal and the cooling requires power — but these interactions are invisible, submerged in the same infrastructure that runs the pumps, part of the district's hidden circulatory system that operates below the surface, in the dark calcium-rich bedrock, in the old combined sewers, in the grid that has forgotten to disconnect or that remains connected for reasons no one has questioned because questioning would require someone to whom the question could be addressed, and there is no such person.

---

Then there are the ones who pass through.

Not often. Not on any schedule. But sometimes — once a week, once a month, the interval varies in a way that is the only true variation the district permits — someone comes in from outside the embankments. They come through the underpass on the north side where the rail embankment has a gap wide enough for a vehicle, or they come on foot over the flood wall on the south side using a set of concrete steps that are mossy and treacherous and that clearly predate the current purpose of the wall. They come for pump parts, sometimes. They come because they know Garza, or they know the women, or they know the man in the fogged window, or they know no one and are simply passing through, drawn by the gap in the embankment or the steps in the flood wall, which function as entrances and therefore imply a reason to enter.

These people are the district's only evidence that an outside exists. They bring the smell of dry air, of car exhaust, of food that isn't rice and beans. They move too quickly. They talk too loudly. They look at the wet streets and the rusted buildings and the cats on the pipe housings with an expression that the residents — all four or five of them, or however many there are — do not share and could not produce, because it is the expression of someone seeing the district from outside, and the residents do not have an outside. The visitors stay for ten minutes or an hour and then they leave and the district absorbs their absence the way the bedrock absorbs the water, instantly and completely, and the equilibrium returns, which is to say the equilibrium never left, because the visitors were never a disturbance, only a brief, minor modulation in a system too stable to be disturbed by anything short of catastrophe.

And catastrophe does not come here. Catastrophe requires change, and change requires a differential — a buildup of pressure, a deficit of something, an excess of something else — and the Pumphouse District has no differentials. The water rises eighteen inches and the pumps push it down. The impellers wear and Garza replaces them. The frogs call and the cats eat them and new frogs grow in the rooftop troughs and call again. The older woman listens to her radio and the younger woman makes her canvas covers and the man in the fogged window stands and sits and stands and sits. The fig tree drips. The beetles orbit. The sodium lamp burns.

---

But there is one more resident, or there might be, and this is the one that matters.

In the primary pumping station on the Gutter — the big one, the one with the riveted steel doors and the small round windows set high in the wall — something shuffles and splashes. It has been doing this for a long time. The sounds are irregular but persistent, audible from the street if you stand close to the doors and listen, which no one does, but which you could do, and if you did you would hear: the pumps, deep and rhythmic, louder here than anywhere else in the district because this is where the main pump mechanisms are, the old ones, the original infrastructure from which the district takes its name; and layered on top of the pumps, or woven between their beats, a sound of movement. Not mechanical movement. Something that shifts its weight, that displaces water, that breathes.

It could be cats. It is probably cats. A colony of them in the warm, dark, wet interior, living among the pump housings, fishing in the sumps, sleeping on the vibrating machinery the way their cousins outside sleep on the pipe casings. That would be the simplest explanation and simplicity is the district's governing principle — not as a philosophy but as a physics, the way water finds its level.

But Garza knows something about the primary pumping station that he does not discuss. This is evident in the way he services the pumps that are not in the primary station — the secondary ones, the smaller ones distributed through the district's other buildings — with care and regularity, replacing their parts, keeping them running, while the primary station's pumps receive nothing. No new impellers. No packing glands. No valve seats from the oily steel bench. The primary station's pumps have been running without maintenance for as long as Garza has been here, and Garza has been here longer than anyone, and those pumps should have failed years ago, decades ago, and they have not. They run with a steadiness that the other pumps, the maintained ones, cannot match. Their rhythm is deeper, more regular, more *organic*, if that word can be applied to machinery, and it can, here, because here the distinction between organic and mechanical has undergone the same dissolution as every other distinction.

The older woman, when the radio plays a certain song — not cumbia but something older, something with a slower rhythm, something that may be a hymn or may be a lullaby or may be a work song from the era when the pumping stations were built — the older woman's eyes move toward the south, toward the Gutter, toward the primary station, and her expression does not change but something behind her expression shifts, the way water shifts beneath a surface that appears still.

The man in the fogged window, too. The figure that stands and looks out late at night. It is looking toward the primary station. Always toward the primary station. This is knowable because the window faces south and the primary station is south and the figure stands at the window for exactly as long as it stands and then moves away and the television's glow fills the glass again.

What lives in the primary pumping station is what has always lived there. It was there before the district reached equilibrium, and it is possible — not certain, but possible — that the district reached equilibrium *because* it is there. That the settlement of the place, the stilling, the arrival at a permanent steady state, was not a process of entropy or neglect but a process of *accommodation* — the district adjusting itself, building by building, pipe by pipe, organism by organism, to the presence of something in the primary pumping station that requires certain conditions and that, in return, provides something, or is something, or *maintains* something.

The pumps that don't need maintenance. The water that rises and falls in perfect rhythm. The temperature that holds within two degrees. The frogs whose chorus synchronizes with the machinery. The sodium lamp that stays on. The radio signal that persists. The fig tree that grew in a month and stopped. The air that does not move. The equilibrium that does not break.

It is not a god. It is not a monster. It is not a spirit or a genius loci or any of the words that people from outside the embankments might use. Those words imply a thing separate from the place, and the thing in the primary pumping station is not separate from the place. It is what happens when water and machinery and darkness and heat and time are left alone together for long enough. It is the pumps' agreement with the water table made manifest. It is the equilibrium itself, given weight and breath and the ability to shuffle and splash in the warm dark among the machinery that is also its body.

Garza knows this the way he knows the tolerance of a valve seat — not as a belief but as a specification. A fact of the infrastructure. The thing in the primary station is part of the system and the system works and you do not open the riveted steel doors for the same reason you do not open a running pump: because it is running, because it works, because the act of opening it would introduce a variable into a system that has eliminated all variables, and the system's perfection — its absolute, humid, mechanical, living perfection — is the only thing any of them have, and it is enough.

It is enough.

---

So this is who lives in the Pumphouse District:

A man who repairs what can be repaired. Two women who persist. A figure in a fogged window who watches. Cats, frogs, beetles, pigeons, rats — all of them residents, all of them comfortable, all of them woven into the rhythm. And something in the primary pumping station that shuffles and splashes and breathes and does not need repair and has never needed repair and around which the entire district has organized itself like tissue around a heartbeat.

Five people, or four, or six. It doesn't matter. What matters is that the number does not change. It has not changed. When one leaves — dies, presumably, though no one has witnessed a death here, only an absence that the district absorbs — another arrives, through the underpass or over the flood wall, not knowing why, drawn by some pull that operates below the level of decision, the way water is drawn downhill, the way a pump is drawn to cycle, and they find a room and a routine and a place in the rhythm and they stay. They stay because the district has a place for them. It has always had a place for them. The place was empty and now it is full and the equilibrium holds and the pumps run and the frogs call and the water on the street reflects the amber light and does not ripple.

They live here because they have finished becoming what they are.

The district breathes in. The district breathes out. The breath is the same breath.

The residents breathe with it.
