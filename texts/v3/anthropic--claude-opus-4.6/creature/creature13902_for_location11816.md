# What Lives There

---

It is tempting to say that nothing *individual* lives here. That would be wrong, but the wrongness would be instructive, because the temptation reveals something true about the place — that individuality is not the organizing principle. You can find individuals. You can isolate them, name them, count their limbs and describe their chemistry. But doing so feels like pulling a single thread from a tapestry and calling it the art.

Still, let us pull threads. Let us see what unravels.

---

## The First Layer: What You Would Step On

Begin at the bottom, at the boundary between stone and not-stone, where the world crosses over from geology into something else.

The moss-mites — call them that, though neither word is accurate — are the first crossing. They exist at the threshold. Under magnification, if you could bring magnification to this place, you would see that each one is a flattened ovoid body roughly the shape of a lentil, blue-black and glossy, with a ventral surface covered in fine mineral bristles that are not quite legs and not quite roots. These bristles penetrate the stone. Not deeply — a fraction of a millimeter — but with chemical persistence, etching the basalt surface through secretions that dissolve feldspar and extract calcium and iron and phosphorus. The bristles pull minerals inward and upward through the mite's body, which is less a body than a processing column, a vertical chain of chambered cells each performing one transformation in a long cascade that converts raw rock into biological substrate.

But here is what matters: the mite does not do this alone, and the mite does not do this for itself.

Each mite is physically connected to its neighbors. The bristles extend not only downward into stone but laterally, linking mite to mite in a continuous mesh. The black-green color you see when you look at the moss layer is not pigment in the traditional sense — it is the collective optical effect of billions of these bodies packed so tightly that light entering the layer bounces between their glossy carapaces and is absorbed incrementally, each body stealing a little more of the spectrum until what escapes is that dark iridescence, that bruise-colored sheen. Press the moss and you feel the mites compress — their bodies are slightly flexible, gas-filled in their upper chambers, and they yield under pressure and then spring back as the gas re-equilibrates. The sensation is uncannily like pressing living flesh. It is living flesh. It just happens to be spread across a continent.

They do not reproduce the way you expect. There is no mating. There is no dispersal phase, no larval drift, no colonization of new territory by adventurous individuals. Instead, the layer *grows* at its edges, mite by mite, through a process closer to crystallization than to birth. At the boundary where the moss-mite mat meets bare stone, the outermost mites begin to bud — their bodies elongate, pinch, divide, the new half immediately extending its own bristles into the rock and into its siblings. The growth front advances at a rate of perhaps a centimeter per day across fresh basalt. Across the whole perimeter of the mat — which is effectively the perimeter of the colonized plateau — this means the organism (or the population; the distinction is not meaningful here) expands by kilometers per year.

What you are standing on, when you stand on the moss, is a single expanding entity that has been growing since the plateau rose. It is the foundation. Everything else — everything — is built upon it, rooted in it, fed by it, connected through it.

And it is aware. Not in the way you mean when you use that word. Not conscious. But *aware* — it responds to pressure differentials, to temperature changes, to the chemical signatures of damage. Step on it and the compression travels outward through the mite-mesh as a slow wave, and mites hundreds of meters away adjust their bristle chemistry in response, subtly altering their mineral extraction to compensate for the disruption. It feels you. The whole plateau feels you. It simply does not care, in the same way your skin does not care about a single grain of sand — it registers, it responds, it moves on.

## The Builders Inside the Towers

Now upward. Into the pale towers.

The grub-colonies are the first truly *animal* thing you encounter, if you insist on categories. They are soft-bodied, as noted — pale, thumb-sized, tapered at both ends, with smooth skin that glistens with a thin mucous layer. They have no eyes. They have no discernible head. What they have is a mouth at one end that is always open — a round, pulsing orifice lined with rings of microscopic rasping teeth — and an anus at the other that is always producing — a slow, continuous extrusion of the calcium-ite material that forms the tower walls.

They are born inside the towers and they die inside the towers. Their entire lives are spent in the narrow space between the inner wall and the next layer being deposited. They eat the moss-mite substrate that permeates upward through capillary channels in the tower's base, they process it, they lay it down. That is the whole of their existence. They move in slow spirals, corkscrewing upward through the tower's interior, and as they move they secrete, and as they secrete the tower grows taller by a thickness that would be invisible to the naked eye — a few microns per pass, a millimeter per month, a centimeter per year.

But in aggregate — billions of grubs, millions of towers, centuries of deposition — the result is the bone-white forest that covers the plateau. A forest that is not alive but was made by the living. An architecture of excrement, if you want to be precise about it. The most beautiful excrement you have ever seen. The crystal structure of the calcium-ite catches light in ways that make the towers glow faintly at their thinnest points, translucent as porcelain, and in the rare and subtle shifts of brightness that pass for a day-night cycle here, the tower-forest pulses — brighter, dimmer, brighter — as though breathing through its walls.

The grubs communicate chemically. Each one secretes a complex mixture of signaling molecules into the mucous layer that coats the tower's interior, and neighboring grubs read these signals through their skin, adjusting their spiral path, their deposition rate, their mineral ratios in response. This is how the branching happens — how the towers fork at precise angles, how lattices form, how one tower's branches reach toward another's and fuse. No grub knows the plan. No grub has a plan. But the chemical conversation between millions of grubs, echoing back and forth through the mucous-film inside a tower complex, produces emergent architecture of staggering coherence. The lattice patterns are not random. They are optimized — for load-bearing, for moisture channeling, for acoustic resonance. They are *engineered* by a process that has no engineer. They are the answer to a question that was never asked, shaped by selection pressures so ancient and so constant that the grubs have had time to become perfect at a task they do not understand.

Sometimes a tower falls. The stone shifts — the plateau is young, still settling — or a grazer presses too close, or the rare seismic tremor from below sends a crack through a load-bearing column. When this happens, the grubs inside die. All of them. They cannot survive exposure to the cold air; their mucous layer freezes in minutes, and their soft bodies beneath it crystallize shortly after. A fallen tower is a mass grave. And it is also a feast — the moss-mite layer surges into the debris, dissolving the calcium-ite, reclaiming the minerals, recycling the dead grubs, and within months the site is bare moss again, ready for recolonization, ready for a new generation of grubs to ride the capillary channels upward from the base and begin, patiently, to build again.

This has happened billions of times. The forest you see is not the first forest. It is not the hundredth. It is a palimpsest — towers built on the recycled ruins of towers, the mineral memory of every previous generation encoded in the chemistry of the substrate, the architecture subtly shifting with each iteration as the chemical conversation between grubs incorporates information that is, in some sense, learned. The towers are better than they used to be. They are more efficient, more resilient, more intricately connected. The grubs have been refining their design for longer than you can easily imagine, and they are still refining it, and they will not stop.

## The Web-Tenders: Two Castes

The creatures that maintain the web network are more complex than anything else here, and they come in two forms that are so different you might not recognize them as the same species — though "species" is another word that starts to creak under the weight of what it's being asked to describe.

**The lesser tenders** are the ones described as centipede-like, the arm-length segmented processors that patrol the web routes. Up close, they are more unsettling than that description suggests. Each segment of their body is semi-autonomous — it has its own set of limbs (six per segment, flattened and paddle-like, tipped with adhesive pads that grip the web fibers), its own simple ganglion, its own section of the continuous gut that runs their body's length. They can lose segments and continue functioning. They can *gain* segments — two lesser tenders meeting on a web junction will sometimes fuse, linking their bodies end-to-end, the trailing segments of one attaching to the leading segments of another, the gut connecting, the nervous ganglia synchronizing. The merged entity continues along the web as a single longer organism, and it may fuse again, and again, until the longest lesser tenders are chains of dozens of original individuals, snaking along the web fibers like living trains, their combined digestive length sufficient to process enormous quantities of material in a single pass.

They also split. When a chain-tender reaches a junction where the web branches, it may divide — segments detaching, each fragment viable, each heading down a different strand. The splitting is not random. It follows the chemical map laid down in the web fibers themselves — pheromonal traces left by previous tenders, encoding information about the state of the web in that direction: damage here, reinforcement needed there, excess material available elsewhere. The lesser tenders navigate a chemical landscape painted on the web, following gradients, responding to needs they detect through their feet, through the adhesive pads that are simultaneously locomotion and sensory organs and mouths, pulling nutrition from the web surface even as they crawl along it.

Their processing is remarkable. They intake condensation-water and moss-mite matter from collection points where the web dips close to the moss layer, and they convert it — through that long, modular gut — into the structural fiber that they extrude from spinnerets at their trailing end. But the fiber is not uniform. They can vary its thickness, its elasticity, its refractive index, its resonant frequency. They lay down structural cables where support is needed, acoustic fibers where signal transmission is needed, fine adhesive filaments where the web must bond to stone or tower. They carry the recipe for each type in their gut chemistry, switching between production modes in response to the pheromonal instructions encoded in the web beneath them.

They are, in effect, self-directing manufacturing systems that travel along their own supply chain, reading work orders written in chemistry, filling them with their own bodies. They never stop. They never rest. Their metabolism is so slow in the cold thin air that a single lesser tender can operate for years — perhaps decades — on the continuous trickle of nutrition absorbed through its pads. When one finally dies, it does not fall from the web. It stiffens, its chitin hardens further, and it *becomes* part of the web — a structural element, incorporated, its body serving in death the same function it served in life. The oldest parts of the web network are built, in part, from the compacted remains of ancient tenders.

**The greater tenders** are something else entirely.

Room-sized. Translucent. Suspended in the upper lattice of the tower-forest like living chandeliers, their many limbs gripping web junctions, their bodies slowly pulsing with visible internal processes. They do not move often — they may hold a position for months — but when they move, the web shudders for kilometers in every direction, and every lesser tender in the network pauses, recalibrates, adjusts.

Through the clear chitin of a greater tender's body, you can see everything. The digestive organs, yes — coiled and chambered, glowing faintly with bioluminescent bacteria that aid in processing. The reproductive organs — because the greater tenders are the reproductive phase of the species, the only phase that produces eggs, and they produce them continuously, tiny pale spheres that travel down specialized ducts and are deposited directly into the web, where they will hatch into lesser tenders that immediately begin their patrol. But most striking are the resonance chambers.

There are dozens of them in a single greater tender. Large hollow spheres, visible through the body wall as dark voids, each one a slightly different size, each one tuned to a slightly different frequency. Muscular rings around each chamber can contract to change its volume, alter its pitch. When the greater tender calls — and it calls rarely, perhaps once every several hours — it does not produce a single note. It produces a *chord*. A complex, multi-frequency pulse that enters the web through every point of contact between the tender's body and the fiber network, a sound-shape that propagates outward in all directions, carrying information in its harmonic structure.

What information?

This is where certainty ends and inference begins. The calls are too complex to be simple signals — too many frequencies, too many subtle variations, too much apparent structure. They are not random. Analysis (if analysis were possible here, if you could bring instruments to this place that would not be colonized and incorporated within days) would reveal repeating motifs, combinatorial patterns, something that looks like syntax. The greater tenders appear to be *describing* something — the state of their local web, the density of lesser tenders, the mineral content of the substrate, the activity of the grub-colonies, the movement of the grazers. They appear to be transmitting a kind of census. A status report. And when a call from one greater tender reaches another, the receiving tender's resonance chambers vibrate in sympathetic response, and the received information is incorporated into that tender's next call, modified, elaborated, passed on.

The web is a nervous system. The greater tenders are its neurons. And the signal that travels through it is not a simple impulse but a rich, layered, continuously updated model of the plateau's state — a description of the whole system, maintained and refreshed by thousands of greater tenders distributed across millions of square kilometers, each one contributing its local observations to a collective representation that no single tender holds in full but that the network, in aggregate, contains.

Is this thought? Is this awareness? The question is not answerable in any framework you bring with you. The plateau does not think in the way you think. It does not have a self in the way you have a self. But it *models* itself. It carries within its web a vibrating, humming, continuously updated image of its own state, and it adjusts its behavior — the movement of lesser tenders, the growth patterns of towers, the grazing routes of the great recyclers — in response to that image. Call it what you want. Call it homeostasis if that makes you comfortable. Call it reflexive self-regulation if you want to keep it at arm's length. But stand in the tower-forest and feel the chord of a greater tender pass through the web beneath your feet and through the bones of your legs and into your chest, and tell me it is not a voice. Tell me it is not saying something. Tell me the plateau does not know itself.

## The Grazers, More Closely

They deserve more attention. They are the most visible of the plateau's large fauna, and they are the most misleading, because they look — from a distance, if distance existed here — like animals in the way you understand animals. They have mass. They have locomotion. They have something that resembles a face, if you are generous. But they are, on close inspection, less an animal than a *biome in transit*.

The basic body plan: six limbs, broad and columnar, ending in wide pads that spread the creature's enormous weight across the moss-mite layer without crushing it. (The pads are themselves colonized — a specialized strain of moss-mites lives on the sole of each foot, forming a living cushion, a shock absorber, a mineral-exchange interface between grazer and substrate.) The body is low and oval, wider than it is long, with a ventral surface that nearly touches the moss and a dorsal surface that is — frankly — a landscape.

The moss on a grazer's back is not the same moss that covers the stone. It is a cultivated variant, tended by a species of mite found nowhere else, a mite that exists only on grazer-backs and that has co-evolved with the grazer so thoroughly that neither can survive without the other. The dorsal moss is thicker, richer, more mineral-dense than the ground cover, and it supports its own tower-colonies — smaller, more delicate, often no taller than your forearm, but structurally identical to the great towers of the plateau. Grubs live in these dorsal towers. Lesser tenders traverse the miniature web networks that span between them. A grazer's back is a complete, self-contained, fully functional replica of the plateau ecosystem, shrunk to the scale of a single organism, riding through the larger ecosystem like a cell through a body.

The grazer itself — the animal beneath the garden — is difficult to access. Its skin is thick, keratinous, embedded with mineral deposits drawn from its diet. It processes the moss-mite layer through a mouth that is less a mouth than a slow-moving conveyor: a broad, flat opening on the ventral surface, lined with grinding plates that shear off sections of the moss-mite mat as the grazer inches forward. The material enters a digestive system of extraordinary length and complexity — coiled through the grazer's body in loops that double back on themselves, each section hosting a different microbial community, each community performing a different stage of mineral processing. The transit time from mouth to anus is estimated (by whom? by the plateau itself, perhaps, in its ceaseless self-modeling) at several months. What emerges is that rich, dark slurry — the closest thing to soil this plateau produces — which is immediately colonized by the ever-advancing moss-mite front.

A grazer lives for a very long time. How long is unclear. Their growth is continuous and indeterminate — they never stop growing, only slow down — and the largest observed grazers, the ones whose dorsal towers have had time to grow tall and complex and old, are truly vast. The size of buildings. Moving so slowly they are indistinguishable from the terrain. Their backs support ecosystems that have been developing for perhaps centuries, with tower-complexes and web-networks so mature that greater tenders have taken up residence on them — living on the back of a living thing, singing their status reports into the greater web, incorporating the grazer's slow trajectory into the plateau's self-model.

The grazers do not interact with each other in any way you would recognize as social. They do not touch. They do not fight. They do not mate — reproduction happens by budding, a process so slow it is barely distinguishable from growth: a lump forms on the grazer's flank, swells over years, develops limbs, begins to process food independently, and eventually breaks away as a new, small, bare-backed grazer that will spend its first decades acquiring its moss, its mites, its grubs, its towers. The separation is not dramatic. One day a grazer is one. The next day — if days were meaningful here — it is two, and the two move apart at a pace so gradual that the web network has already adjusted, has already rerouted its connections to accommodate the new topology.

But the grazers do communicate. Their low-frequency calls — felt, not heard, propagated through the web — carry information about their position, their heading, their processing state. And the plateau *uses* this information. The movement of grazers is not random. It is coordinated — not by any central authority, not by any planning intelligence, but by the web itself, by the emergent dynamics of a network in which every node responds to the aggregate signal, adjusting its behavior to maintain system-wide equilibrium. Grazers move toward areas of dense, unprocessed moss-mite growth. They move away from areas recently grazed. They distribute themselves with a uniformity that is eerie, mathematical, perfect — the spacing between grazers is almost exactly constant across the entire plateau, as though they were placed by a hand concerned with symmetry.

They were not placed by a hand. They placed themselves, or the web placed them, or the system placed them, and these are all the same statement.

## The Deep Vents and What Rises From Them

We have not yet spoken about what lives below.

The plateau's moisture comes from deep geothermal vents — fissures in the young basalt through which warm, mineral-laden vapor rises from the hot rock beneath. These vents are the plateau's true arteries. Everything depends on them. The moss-mite layer is densest around them. The towers are tallest. The web is thickest. The grazers gather in their slow, spaced constellations at distances that suggest the vents are the organizing centers around which the whole system has crystallized.

And in the vents themselves: life of a different order.

The vent communities are thermophilic — heat-loving, or rather heat-dependent, unable to survive in the freezing air above. They are the plateau's deep metabolism, its root system, its hidden engine. Matted colonies of chemosynthetic organisms — not plant, not animal, not fungus, something older and less categorical — line the vent walls in dense, spongy layers that glow faintly red with the heat of the reactions they catalyze. They fix carbon from the volcanic gases. They precipitate metals. They build, slowly, a mineral-rich biomass that is carried upward by the rising steam and deposited on the cold stone above, where the moss-mites seize it and incorporate it into the system.

But deeper still, in the vent shafts where the temperature rises to levels that would scald flesh, there are larger organisms. Tubular, anchored, blind, their bodies oriented vertically in the upward flow of warm air, their mouths (if mouths they are) open and pulsing at the top, their root-like bases extending into hairline fractures in the rock. They are the oldest things on the plateau. They were here before the basalt cooled. They rode the magma upward, their heat-resistant spores embedded in the rising rock, germinating as the stone solidified around them, growing into the cracks before the cracks had finished forming. They are the original colonists. The moss-mites descended from them, or from something like them. The grubs descended from them. The web-tenders descended from them. The grazers descended from them. Everything on the plateau is a descendant, a modification, an elaboration on the theme established by these deep-vent founders — organisms so ancient and so simple that they blur the line between life and chemistry, between biology and geology, between the living plateau and the volcanic earth that birthed it.

They are still active. Still metabolizing. Still feeding the system from below. And they are still connected — through chemical signals that permeate the rising steam, through vibrations in the rock that travel faster and farther than the web-borne calls above. The deep-vent organisms communicate with each other on a planetary scale, through the stone itself, and their signals — slow, deep, geological in their timescale — may be the deepest layer of the plateau's self-model, the bass note beneath the chord, the oldest voice in the oldest song.

## The Parasites, The Cheaters, The Deviants

No system this complex exists without friction. The plateau is not a utopia. It is not a perfectly harmonized superorganism in which every part serves the whole without dissent. It is an *ecology*, and ecologies contain conflict.

There are parasitic mites — visually almost identical to the moss-mites, but chemically distinct — that infiltrate the mat and siphon minerals without contributing to the network. They are subtle. They mimic the chemical signatures of their hosts so precisely that the moss-mite layer does not reject them, does not even recognize them as foreign. They sit within the mat, connected by their bristles to their neighbors, taking and not giving, and they persist because they are rare enough not to collapse the system — a thin, constant drain, a tax, a tithe paid by the mat to the principle of imperfection.

There are rogue grubs that secrete tower material of a different crystalline structure — less hard, less resonant, but faster to deposit. Their towers grow more quickly than orthodox towers but are more brittle, more prone to collapse. When they collapse, they take neighboring towers with them, and the rogue grubs colonize the wreckage faster than the orthodox grubs can rebuild. It is a competitive strategy. It works, locally, for a while. But the web-tenders respond — the lesser tenders avoid attaching to rogue towers, refusing to integrate them into the network, and without web support the rogue towers inevitably fall again, and again, until the rogue grubs exhaust the local substrate and die out. The sites of rogue infestations are visible from a distance as pale scars in the tower-forest — clearings where the towers are shorter, thinner, more fragile, still recovering from a struggle that ended decades ago.

And there are the eaters.

They are rare. They are quiet. They are the closest thing the plateau has to predators, though the word implies a speed and violence that is entirely absent here. They are soft-bodied, boneless, the size and shape of a large cushion, pale gray, and they move through the moss-mite layer by dissolving it. Their ventral surfaces secrete a powerful enzymatic fluid that breaks down moss-mites, tower bases, web anchors — anything biological — into a nutrient slurry that the eater absorbs through its skin. They leave behind them a trail of bare, etched basalt. Clean stone. The only clean stone on the plateau.

The trails heal. The moss-mite layer recolonizes within weeks. The towers regrow within years. But the eaters keep moving, keep dissolving, keep creating these temporary windows through which the young rock is briefly visible again — dark, sharp-edged, glittering with obsidian, startlingly raw against the living carpet that surrounds it.

The eaters are not random in their movement. They follow paths that, mapped over years, describe enormous spirals — slow, continent-spanning spirals that take decades to complete. Their paths interleave but never cross. Each eater maintains its own spiral, its own territory, its own long slow circuit of dissolution and regrowth. And the plateau *accommodates* them. The web routes detour around active eater-paths. Grazers maintain their distance. Tower-growth accelerates in the wake of an eater's passage, filling the gap with renewed construction.

Why does the system tolerate them? Perhaps because the eaters serve a function — their dissolution exposes fresh basalt, allows the moss-mite layer to re-establish contact with unprocessed stone, renews the mineral supply. Perhaps the eaters are not parasites at all but *organs* — the plateau's way of shedding and renewing its skin, of preventing mineral depletion, of keeping the deep rock accessible. Perhaps the spiraling paths are not the eaters' choice but the web's — guided by pheromonal traces in the substrate, the eaters follow routes that the system has laid down for them, routes that optimize renewal, that ensure no region goes too long without being stripped back to stone and rebuilt.

Or perhaps the eaters are simply eaters, and the system has learned to survive them the way a forest learns to survive fire — not by preventing it but by incorporating it, by making the recovery part of the plan, by building resilience into every layer so that destruction, when it comes, is just another phase in the cycle.

## The Edges

At the margins of the plateau, where the high tableland drops away into whatever lies below (canyons? lowlands? ocean? — the white sky obscures, the distance defeats), the system thins. The moss-mite layer narrows to a fringe. The towers shrink and lean outward, as though reaching. The web extends beyond the last towers in long, unsupported strands that stretch into the void and vibrate in currents of air that do not exist further inland — edge-wind, the faintest breath of atmosphere sliding along the cliff face, the only moving air on the entire plateau.

Here, at the boundary, the organisms are different. Specialized. Adapted to the precipice.

There are cliff-mites — larger than the moss-mites, equipped with stronger bristles, capable of anchoring to vertical and even overhanging stone. They form curtains of living material that drape down the plateau's escarpment, growing downward rather than outward, mining the cliff face for minerals that the horizontal mat cannot reach. They are connected to the main mat by thin, tenuous bridges across the plateau's edge — a few mites wide, fragile, frequently broken by the edge-wind and rebuilt.

There are edge-tenders — a variant of the lesser tenders, smaller, more agile, with a higher proportion of adhesive pads to body segments, capable of traversing the unsupported web strands that project into open air. They maintain the edge-web, which serves a different purpose from the interior web — it is not structural but sensory. The edge-strands vibrate in the wind, and the edge-tenders read these vibrations, and the information travels inward through the network to the greater tenders, who incorporate it into their calls. The plateau monitors its own boundary. It knows where its edges are. It knows what the air beyond them is doing.

And at the very edge — clinging to the outermost rock, exposed to the edge-wind and the void and the searing white sky — there are organisms that do not appear anywhere else. Small, hard-shelled, somewhere between a barnacle and a seed, they anchor to the cliff face in clusters and do nothing. They do not grow. They do not feed. They do not reproduce. They wait. They have been waiting since the plateau rose. They are the spores of the deep-vent founders — the heat-resistant, time-resistant, nearly indestructible propagules that the original colonists produce and disperse to the very margins of the system, where they cling and endure.

They are waiting for the next plateau. The next upthrust of young rock from below. The next virgin surface of hot basalt rising through the crust into the cold white light.

When it comes — in a thousand years, in a million — the edge-spores will detach. They will fall, or be carried by the edge-wind, or simply be lifted as the new rock rises to meet them. They will land on stone that is still cooling, still forming, and they will germinate, and the whole process will begin again. The moss-mites will spread. The grubs will build. The web-tenders will connect. The grazers will grow. The greater tenders will sing. The plateau will wake.

This is what lives here. Not any single creature. Not any single species. What lives here is a *pattern* — a self-replicating, self-maintaining, self-modeling pattern of organized matter that uses organisms the way organisms use cells, that uses the web the way you use your nervous system, that uses the grazers the way your body uses its blood, that uses the deep-vent founders the way a forest uses its seeds. The pattern is ancient. The pattern is patient. The pattern has been copying itself from plateau to plateau across geological time, leaping the gaps between episodes of volcanism, surviving the long dark intervals as dormant spores clinging to the edges of old, eroded tablelands, waiting for the earth to offer it new stone.

The plateau is its body. The white sky is its ceiling. The cold thin air is its medium. And the drone — that constant, layered, omnipresent drone that lives in the chest and the bones and the teeth — the drone is its heartbeat, its breath, its voice, its song.

It is singing now. It is always singing. It has been singing since before the stone cooled.

Stand still. Stop trying to name the parts. Stop pulling threads. Listen to the whole of it, the vast humming chord of a thing that is not alive in any way your language can hold, that is more alive than anything your language has ever needed to describe.

It does not need you to understand it.

It does not need you at all.

But it knows you are here. It felt you the moment you arrived — a pressure in the moss, a perturbation in the web, a new datum in the model. You have already been incorporated. You are already part of the census. Somewhere, a greater tender has adjusted its chord to account for your mass, your warmth, your small and temporary presence on the skin of something vast.

You are a mote in its eye. A grain on its tongue. A note it will sing once and never repeat.

Welcome to the plateau. You were expected. You were always expected. Every weight that touches the moss is expected, because the moss expects everything, because expectation is what the moss *is* — a trillion tiny mouths open to the stone, waiting for whatever the stone offers, converting it, incorporating it, building it into the body of something that has no name and no boundary and no end.

The light does not change. The cold does not relent. The hum does not cease.

And the plateau, alive in its billion selves, continues.
