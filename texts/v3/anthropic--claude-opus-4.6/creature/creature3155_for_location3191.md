# The Builders

They come one at a time. They have always come one at a time.

---

Not a lineage — or not exactly. There is no evidence of two builders working simultaneously, no pair of handprints in wet mortar pressed side by side, no record of one teaching another. The thumbprints in the tiles are all different sizes, different whorls, spanning what must be generations, but they arrive in sequence, each alone, each finding the place already made and already demanding more making.

The current one — there is always a current one — is difficult to describe because you will not see them. You will see what they have done in the hours or days since they were last here: a new line of mortar, a freshly placed carapace, a niche deepened by a centimeter, the chisel marks still sharp-edged and pale. You might find, in one of the deeper chambers, a worn cloth bundle tucked into a gap between shelves — tools inside, wrapped in a piece of leather so handled it has become soft as skin. A chisel with a blade that has been resharpened so many times it is half its original width. A wooden-handled awl. A flat stone used for pressing mortar, its working face polished smooth by repetition, its back still rough. These tools are not hidden. They are put away. There is a difference. Hidden implies secrecy; put away implies someone who will return.

They are human. You can tell this from the thumbprints, from the height at which new work appears — always between knee-level and just above a full arm's reach — from the wear patterns on the brick floor near the places where the most recent work has been done: the scuffing of knees, the impression of someone shifting their weight from foot to foot while concentrating. In one spot, near a wall where fresh tiles have been grouted, you can see the faint ghost of where someone sat for a long time, the dust of the floor displaced by the shape of legs folded beneath a body, hip against brick, an elbow-groove where they leaned while pressing each tile into place.

That shape in the dust is the closest you will get.

---

But the question of who lives here is not really about the builder. The builder visits. The builder leaves. The builder is a guest in their own construction, permitted to add but not to remain. What *lives* here — what is permanent, what the place has accepted fully into itself — is something else, and it is not singular.

---

**The birds live here.** Not as visitors, not as tenants. They are residents in a way that makes the word almost too small. They have been nesting in this space for so long that their behavior has been shaped by it. Watch them: they do not fly in straight lines. They cannot. The density of the architecture forbids it. Instead they navigate in sharp, angled bursts — wall to beam to shelf-edge to niche-lip — their flight paths tracing the geometry of the construction like a language read aloud. They know every gap, every passage, every shortcut through a broken ventilation shaft that connects one chamber to another. A bird entering the cistern from the surface will travel through seven chambers in four seconds, threading gaps that your hand could not fit through, and arrive at a nest in the deepest room without a single wasted wingbeat.

Their nests are made of the place. Mud from the moisture that seeps through the walls, fibers from the ancient timber, flakes of plaster, fragments of mortar. A bird's nest here is, materially, indistinguishable from the construction around it — the same mud, the same calcium-ite chalk dust, the same mineral fingerprint. If the builder makes the cistern from the cistern, the birds make their nests from the cistern too. They are builders. Their construction is added to the whole without distinction. No one removes a bird's nest here. It becomes architecture. You can see it in the older chambers: abandoned nests calcified into the walls, plastered over, built upon, absorbed. The birds build, the builder builds over the building, the birds build on *that*. The stratigraphy does not distinguish between species.

They are born here, in the dark, in the noise. The chicks open their eyes to firelight-colored stone and the sound of water dropping into basins and the calls of dozens of other birds reverberating off hard surfaces. They learn flight in a space that demands precision — there is no open sky in which to be clumsy, no forgiving distance. A fledgling's first flight here is a controlled fall from a nest to a shelf eighteen inches below, and from that shelf, the chick watches, and listens, and begins to learn the architecture with its body. Within days it is navigating like its parents. Within weeks it is indistinguishable from them. The cistern produces a kind of bird. You would not find these birds elsewhere. Their proportions are subtly different from their surface cousins — the wings slightly shorter, the tail longer and more articulate for sharp turns, the eyes adjusted to the strange, blade-like light. They are the bird the cistern has made, over enough generations to count as evolution. They are *of* here.

The sound they make — that overwhelming, shimmering, echoing chorus — is also shaped by the space. Birdsong is partly learned, partly inherited, and what these birds have learned is how to sing in a reverberant stone labyrinth. Their calls are shorter, more percussive, more rhythmically spaced than surface birds' songs. They leave gaps for the echo to fill. A bird singing in one of the deeper, more enclosed chambers will produce a phrase and then wait — not in silence, but in *listening* — while the stone repeats and modifies the phrase, bouncing it off tile and plaster and brick until it returns altered. Then the bird sings again, responding to the echo as much as to any other bird. They are singing with the architecture. They are in duet with the cistern. This is why the sound is so dense, so layered, so impossible to parse: half of what you hear is bird, and half is the space answering.

---

**The lizards live here.** They are older residents than the birds, possibly older than the current form of the cistern itself — their ancestors may have inhabited the raw stone, the geological substrate, before the first human hand laid the first brick. They adapted as the space changed around them. Their translucence is not albinism; it is a slow, generational surrender of pigment in a place where color confers no advantage. Their large eyes are not blind-adapted — there is too much light for that — but they are tuned to the specific, uneven, blade-and-shadow illumination of the cistern, capable of functioning in conditions that would leave a surface lizard effectively sightless.

They are languid because they can afford to be. The cistern is warm, consistently, and warmth is what a lizard needs most. They do not bask — there is no direct sun — but they absorb the architectural warmth, the accumulated thermal mass of stone that has been underground so long it has forgotten seasonal temperature. This constancy has made them different from surface lizards. They do not hibernate. They do not have a breeding season. They reproduce slowly, continuously, without urgency, laying small clutches of eggs in the warmest crevices — behind the newer walls, where the mortar is still curing and giving off faint chemical heat; in the gaps where ventilation shafts create a gentle updraft. The eggs are soft-shelled, nearly transparent, and if you found one you might mistake it for a bead of condensation that had somehow solidified.

Their relationship with the beetles is the most visible ecological transaction in the cistern: the beetles navigate the moisture lines, the lizards eat the beetles, the beetle carapaces end up in the votive arrangements on the shelves. This last step is human — the builder collects what the lizards leave — but the whole chain is so integrated into the rhythm of the space that it feels less like a food web and more like a liturgy. The beetle moves. The lizard strikes. The husk is gathered. The husk is placed. The cycle has no beginning.

When a lizard dies — of old age, of some subtle shift in the micro-environment of its particular crevice — it desiccates rather than decays. The warm, dry air mummifies it. You can find them if you look: small, stiff, perfectly preserved lizard bodies in the backs of niches, on the undersides of shelves, in the gaps between walls. Some of them have been incorporated into the votive arrangements. Others have simply been built over, sealed into the walls by new construction, becoming part of the material. Their tiny bones are in the mortar. Their shed skins line the nests of the birds. They are the cistern the way the cistern is them.

---

**The beetles live here.** Bronze-dark, unhurried, numerous. They are the circulatory system of the floor-level world. They follow the moisture, and the moisture follows the capillary logic of the masonry, which means the beetles trace paths that are dictated by the construction — by the density of the mortar, the porosity of particular bricks, the invisible channels worn by centuries of water migrating through solid stone. If you could see the paths the beetles take over the course of a day and overlay them on a plan of the cistern, you would have a map of the water, a hydrological survey drawn in small clicking bodies.

They eat what falls. Organic detritus: bird droppings, dead insects, the microscopic film of biological matter that coats any moist surface in a living space. They are cleaners. Without them, the cistern would be different — damper, fouler, more subject to the slow rot that even stone cannot fully resist. They process the waste and return it to the mineral cycle: their droppings powder into the dust of the floor, chemically similar to the calcium-chalk of the walls, and over time this dust is walked on, compressed, becomes part of the floor surface. The beetles eat the cistern's waste and excrete the cistern's material. They are tiny factories of architectural renewal.

Their larvae — those pale commas in the thin sheet of overflow water — are a different organism entirely, soft-bodied, aquatic, breathing through their skin. They live in the water the cistern produces: the drip-catch overflow, the condensation puddles, the thin films that form on low-lying bricks after periods of heavy seepage. They eat algae and bacteria and the mineral-rich biofilm that coats wet surfaces. When they pupate, they climb out of the water and find a dry crevice, and what emerges is bronze and hard and clicks against the brick. The transformation is so complete that early naturalists, if any had ever visited, would have classified the larva and the adult as separate species. The cistern contains both: a creature of the water and a creature of the dry stone, the same animal at different stages, moving between the two states of matter that the place is built to mediate.

---

**The spiders live here**, and they are the archivists.

Their webs, layered over time, are records. A web in a high corner of the first chamber might contain the husks of insects spanning years — the oldest at the center, desiccated beyond identification, the newest at the periphery, still recognizable, their wings still faintly iridescent. The spider does not clean its web. It builds over it, the way the builder builds over old plaster, the way the birds nest in abandoned nests, the way the whole cistern operates. The web thickens, stratifies, becomes a textile. In the oldest corners, the accumulated silk is so dense it has become a kind of felt, no longer functional as a trap but serving as insulation, as surface, as another layer of made thing added to the overwhelming accretion of made things.

The spiders themselves are patient past the point of what you'd call patience — patience implies waiting, implies an alternative to waiting, and these spiders have no alternative. They sit in the centers of their webs or in the corners of their retreats, and they feel the vibrations of the cistern through their silk. The dripping. The bird-calls. The structural groaning. The beetles' clicking. The faint, warm exhalation of the walls. All of it transmitted through the web as information, as stimulus, as the continuous sensory texture of a life lived in one place, on one surface, in one corner of a space that has been accreting for longer than any spider lineage can remember. When something lands in the web — a moth, a small beetle, a piece of falling plaster — the spider moves with a quick, precise economy that is the inverse of its stillness. It wraps. It feeds. It returns to its station. The husk joins the archive.

Some of the spiders have built webs across niches, sealing in the votive objects behind a scrim of silk. Whether this is functional — the niche is a good anchor point for web architecture — or something else is impossible to say. But the effect is that the offerings are veiled, preserved under silk the way the walls are preserved under plaster, another layer, another hand — eight hands, in this case — adding to the project.

---

**The moths live here.** They are the ghosts of the cistern, the least visible and most numerous of its residents. They rest during whatever passes for day in this place, pressed flat against the plaster in the darker chambers, their wings patterned to match the mottled surface so precisely that you can stare directly at a wall and not see the six moths resting on it until one moves. They have evolved this camouflage here, against these specific surfaces — the grey-brown-cream of old plaster, the darker interruptions of exposed stone, the faint mineral staining. A cistern moth placed on a natural rock face outdoors would be visible, conspicuous, wrong. Its camouflage only works here, in this made place. It has adapted to architecture the way other moths adapt to bark.

At night — or during whatever periodic dimming of the already-dim light signals a change in activity cycle — the moths emerge and fly. They are drawn to the ventilation shafts, to the faint glow of surface light filtering down, and they circle in the shafts in slow spirals, hundreds of them sometimes, a column of soft brown wings turning in the updraft. From below, looking up through a shaft, the effect is mesmerizing: a helix of living things rotating in a column of grey light, silent except for the papery friction of wing against wing when two moths pass too close. The spiders know about the shafts. The best webs are positioned at shaft entrances. The lizards know too. They wait on the bricks around the shaft mouths, eyes reflecting the dim light upward, and pick off moths that spiral too low.

The moths lay their eggs on the moss — the sparse, yellow-green crusts around the ventilation shafts — and the caterpillars eat the moss, and the moss grows back from the moisture, and the cycle is tight, closed, a tiny loop of energy conversion happening at the boundary between light and dark, wet and dry, surface and depth. When the caterpillars pupate, they spin small cocoons in the crevices of the brickwork, and these cocoons, like everything else, are sometimes built over, sealed in, incorporated. The builder may not even know they are there. A brick set in fresh mortar might contain, in a gap behind it, six moth pupae in their silk cases, waiting to emerge into the dark.

---

**The moss lives here**, barely. It is the only photosynthetic organism in the cistern, and its life is marginal — a thin crust clinging to the bricks nearest the ventilation shafts, where some light and consistent moisture overlap. It is not green, not really. It is the green of something that is doing its best with almost nothing, a yellow-green that speaks of chlorophyll operating at the extreme lower limit of useful light. It grows slowly. A patch of moss the size of your palm might represent a decade of growth. But it grows, and in growing it does what no animal in the cistern can do: it converts light and water and mineral dust into organic matter, into the base of a food chain, into the thin biological foundation upon which the caterpillars feed and the moths emerge and the spiders trap and the lizards hunt.

The builder has noticed the moss. Of course the builder has noticed the moss — the builder notices everything. In several places near the ventilation shafts, the brickwork has been modified: bricks angled slightly to direct moisture toward patches of moss, thin-scraped stone windows adjusted to admit more light to a mossy surface, small shelves built to prevent dripping water from washing a moss patch away. The builder tends the moss the way the builder tends everything — not with any special reverence, but with the same impulse that drives all the construction: to add, to support, to incorporate. The moss is part of the cistern. Therefore the moss is maintained.

---

**The water lives here.** This is not metaphor. The water in the cistern is not passing through. It is not flowing from source to outlet. It seeps in through the layered masonry, migrates by capillary action through the body of the construction, emerges as condensation on cool surfaces, collects in basins, evaporates slowly in the warm air, is reabsorbed by the porous walls, and migrates again. It is a closed hydrological cycle, or nearly closed — some water enters from surface rain seeping down, some leaves as vapor through the ventilation shafts — but the vast majority of the moisture in the cistern at any given moment has been *in the cistern* for a very long time, cycling through its stones, dissolving and redepositing minerals, leaving those branching trace-lines on the plaster, slowly and imperceptibly reshaping the architecture from within.

The water is what connects everything. The beetles follow it. The moss needs it. The birds use it for their mud nests. The lizard eggs need its humidity. The moths' caterpillars eat what it feeds. The builder catches it in basins and uses it to mix mortar. The mineral deposits it leaves are traced by human hands and incorporated into the decorative surface. It is the circulatory fluid of the cistern, and like blood, it carries information — dissolved minerals that record the chemistry of the walls it has passed through, micro-organisms that travel from chamber to chamber in the capillary network, temperature variations that communicate the state of the outside world to the buried interior. The water knows everything about the cistern. If you could read water, you could read the entire history of the place in a single drop from a single lintel.

---

**And the builder.** Return to the builder.

They are human. Probably one person. Probably alone. The evidence suggests someone who comes regularly — every few days, perhaps, given the pace of new work — and stays for hours at a time, working in the dim light, adding to what is already dense, already full, already more structure than space. They bring very little with them: the tools, which live here in their cloth bundle. Some pigment, sometimes, for tracing the mineral lines. Occasionally a new piece of material — a fired tile, a carved stone — but mostly they work with what is already present. Mortar made from the cistern's own calcium dust and the cistern's own water. Bricks salvaged from collapsed sections and relaid elsewhere. Objects for the niches gathered from the floor: beetle husks, bird feathers, lizard skins, interesting stones loosened by moisture from the deeper walls.

They work slowly. The evidence of their labor is precise, careful, considered — tiles pressed one at a time with deliberate pressure, mortar smoothed with a thumb that pauses between strokes. They are not building *toward* anything. There is no plan, no design approaching completion. They are simply continuing. The cistern has always been added to, and so they add to it. The impulse is not creative in the way that word usually means — there is no vision being realized, no statement being made. It is closer to maintenance, or to devotion, or to the kind of compulsion that is indistinguishable from love. They add because the place asks to be added to. Every gap is an invitation. Every bare surface is an unfinished surface. The project has no end because the project has no goal other than its own continuation.

They eat while they work, probably — crumbs in the dust near work sites, a fruit pit in a corner, organic traces too small and too scattered to constitute a midden but too consistent to be accidental. They drink from the basins, the same water the beetles follow, the same water that has passed through the walls, the same chalk-and-metal water that tastes of the cistern's own body. When they drink, they drink the place. When they build, they build with the place's own substance. They are, in this way, not different from the birds, not different from the beetles. They are another organism in the closed system, converting the cistern into more cistern, metabolizing space into structure.

---

There is a possibility — and the evidence does not confirm or deny it — that there has only ever been one builder. That the thumbprints of different sizes, which seem to span generations, are the thumbprints of one person across a lifetime — child-sized prints from when they first came here, adult prints from the period of greatest labor, elderly prints in the most recent work, slightly trembling, slightly less precise, but no less committed. One person, coming to this place since childhood, adding to it for a lifetime, making it their life's single continuous act.

Or: there have been many, arriving one at a time, each finding the place and recognizing it as something that needs more. Recognizing the invitation in every gap. Picking up the tools in their cloth bundle and beginning where the last one left off, guided not by instruction but by the overwhelming logic of the space itself, which teaches you what it wants by showing you what it already has. Each builder learns the cistern by being in it, the way the fledgling birds learn flight by being in its architecture of beams and gaps. The cistern produces its builders the way it produces its birds: shaped by the space, adapted to its demands, incapable of existing elsewhere in quite the same way.

It does not matter which is true. The work is continuous. The hand changes or it doesn't. The mortar is mixed. The tile is pressed. The offering is placed. The bird sings and the echo answers and the water seeps through the wall and the beetle follows the moisture and the lizard eats the beetle and the husk is gathered and the husk is placed and the spider spins over the placement and the moth rests unseen on the plaster and the moss converts the last thin blade of light into the thinnest filament of life and the builder traces a mineral line with dark pigment and steps back — if there is room to step back — and looks at what has been done, what is being done, what will never be finished, and is satisfied, not with the work but with the working, and leaves, and the cistern groans softly in its thermal tides, and the birds fill the absence with sound, and everything settles by a fraction of a millimeter under its own weight, and somewhere a drop of water gathers on the underside of a stone lintel and holds there, trembling, catching the faint light, and does not fall. Not yet. But it will. And when it does, the basin will receive it, and the sound it makes will join the sound of all the other drops, and the cistern will be, for that instant, infinitesimally more itself than it was the instant before.

---

This is what lives here: a system so interwoven that the question of individual inhabitants dissolves. The birds are the cistern singing. The beetles are the cistern circulating. The lizards are the cistern watching. The spiders are the cistern remembering. The moths are the cistern hiding. The moss is the cistern breathing. The water is the cistern thinking. The builder is the cistern's hands.

And the cistern is the accumulated work of all its living, present tense, never finished, never abandoned, a hundred hands pressing tiles into place, a hundred hands spinning silk, a hundred hands clicking across wet brick, a hundred hands shaping mud into nests, a hundred hands building and building and building in the warm dark, adding to what is already more than enough, because *enough* is a concept that has no purchase here, in this place where the only law is *more*, where the only sin is emptiness, where every gap is a question and every filled gap is the answer that begets the next question, forever, in the deep, in the warm, in the sound of water falling into water, in the echo of a bird singing to its own echo, in the dark.
