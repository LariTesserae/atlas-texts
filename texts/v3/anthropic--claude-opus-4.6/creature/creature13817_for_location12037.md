# The Inhabitants of the Plateau of Standing Sound

---

They are not easy to find, because they are not hiding and they are not visible—not in any way that resolves cleanly against what you expect a living thing to look like. You have to spend time. You have to stop walking and stop *looking*, and let the plateau's one sensory demand—the chord—become the primary channel through which you understand what is present. Then, slowly, like a shape emerging in a stereogram, they become apparent.

Not all at once. Not as a revelation. More the way your eyes adjust to darkness: gradually, and then you realize you've been seeing them for some time.

---

## The Keepers of Position

The first ones you recognize—the first ones you accept as *inhabitants* rather than features of the landscape—are standing near the columns.

There is one perhaps three hundred meters ahead of you, though with the spatial compressions that might mean a dozen steps or a thousand. It is motionless. It has been motionless for the entire time you've been walking, which is why you catalogued it as stone, as a smaller column, as a weathered protrusion. It is none of these things.

It stands approximately two meters tall, though "stands" implies legs and "tall" implies a vertical axis that matters, and neither of these implications is entirely right. It has *extent*. It occupies a vertical region of space near a column the way a flame occupies the space above a wick—not because it is anchored there, but because that is where the conditions produce it. Its body—you will call it a body, though the word strains—is dark, the same near-black as the basalt, but with a quality the stone lacks: a faint, slow iridescence, like oil on deep water, that moves across its surface in patterns you gradually realize correspond to the harmonic structure of the chord. The iridescence is the sound, made visible on its skin. Or its skin is a surface that translates sound into light. Or there is no meaningful distinction here between those two things.

Its shape is not fixed. Not in the dramatic, protean way of something shapeshifting—nothing so active. It is more that its edges participate in the same gentle uncertainty you felt in the null-zones. Its outline breathes. Not with respiration, but with the rhythm of the interference patterns that pass through its location. When a low harmonic peaks, it broadens slightly at the base. When a high overtone sharpens, something at its upper limit elongates, thins, reaches upward like a tendril of smoke before settling back. It is a standing waveform with the complexity of a body. Or it is a body that has given up the pretense of being separate from the sound that saturates it.

You watch it for a long time. It does not move from its position. It does not turn to acknowledge you. But something happens—slowly enough that you cannot identify the moment it begins: the chord, in your immediate vicinity, shifts. Not in pitch or volume but in *texture*. A new interference pattern develops between you and the figure, a region where two harmonics you hadn't been conscious of individually begin to beat against each other with a rhythm that is almost—not quite—the rhythm of your pulse. The figure is not looking at you. It has nothing you would call eyes, no sensory apparatus you can identify, no face, no front. But the sound-field between you has become a medium of attention. It knows you are there the way the plateau knows the columns are there: as a fact of the acoustic landscape, a new shape for the chord to flow around and through.

You realize there are more of them. Now that you know what to look for—the slow iridescence, the breathing outline, the way they occupy space near the columns like notes occupying positions on a staff—you count five, then nine, then stop counting because some of them are on the edge of visibility and some of them might be columns and some of the columns might be them and the distinction is less clear than you want it to be.

They do not cluster. Each one stands alone near its column—or near where a column stands or stood or should stand—separated from the others by the same vast, deliberate distances that separate the columns themselves. Some stand near broken columns, and these figures are different: less defined, their iridescence dimmer, their edges more uncertain, as though the incompleteness of the column's acoustic effect has made them less fully *themselves*. They are not injured or diminished in a way that implies suffering. They are simply *less resolved*, the way a note played on a cracked bell is still a note but contains within it the knowledge of the crack.

One of these—near a column that has lost perhaps its upper third—is barely there at all. A smudge of iridescence. A region where the air is slightly more reluctant to be empty. It persists. It does not fade further. It has found its equilibrium, however reduced, and it holds it with the same implacable patience as everything else on the plateau. You understand, looking at it, that this is not dying. Dying is a process, and processes require change, and change is what happens elsewhere. This figure has simply arrived at a lesser state of being and will remain in it, potentially forever.

---

## What They Do

This is difficult to describe, because what they do is not *action* in any sense that satisfies the expectation of narrative.

They stand in the sound. That is the primary fact. They stand in specific places within the harmonic field, and by standing there—by being the particular shape and density and composition that they are—they participate in the chord. Not as instruments. Not as audience. As *components*. The chord is not something the plateau produces and they listen to. The chord is something the plateau and the columns and these figures and the stone and the air and the still water collectively *are*. Remove one of the figures—if such a thing were possible—and the chord would be different. A frequency would be unsupported. An interference pattern would lose a reflective surface. The architectural sound-space would have a room with a missing wall.

This is what the broken columns tell you: those reduced, half-resolved figures near them are living evidence of what happens when a piece of the system is diminished. The chord near them is incomplete. The sound still functions—still fills, still resonates—but with that quality of a word missing its final syllable. The figures near broken columns are the chord's grief, if grief can be a standing wave.

But they are not merely structural. You learn this over time—over what might be hours, standing in the unchanging light, feeling the chord rearrange itself around your presence.

The figure nearest to you does something. You almost miss it, because it happens at the timescale of geology rather than biology. Its iridescence—the slow, oil-slick migration of color across its surface—shifts. A pattern that had been cycling in a long, repeating loop introduces a variation. The variation is tiny: a slightly different sequence in the prismatic colors, a momentary emphasis on a deep violet that was previously subordinate to the golds and greens. The variation propagates. Not quickly. It moves across the figure's surface the way a season moves across a continent, and then it enters the sound-field.

You feel it before you hear it. A subtle change in the way the chord presses against your sternum. A new relationship between two mid-range harmonics that produces a beating pattern you haven't felt before. The beating pattern moves outward from the figure through the sound-field at a speed you can track—perhaps a meter every few seconds—and as it goes, it encounters the ambient harmonics and is modified by them, carrying information about the acoustic terrain it has passed through, picking up the particular signatures of the stone undulations and pool-surfaces and air densities in its path.

Seven minutes later—or an hour; time is persistence here—you see the iridescence on a distant figure shift in response. The violet emphasis appears, altered, carrying the imprint of everything between the two figures. The second figure's variation is different—an answer, not an echo. Something in the green range. A different beating pattern. A new piece of the chord's texture rippling outward.

They are communicating. You are certain of this now, though *communicating* suggests discrete messages and this is nothing so bounded. They are modulating the shared medium they both inhabit. They are making small, slow changes to the chord that propagate to each other through a sound-field so complex that each modulation carries within it the acoustic fingerprint of the entire intervening landscape. Every exchange is also a map. Every statement is also a description of the space between speaker and listener. They cannot say anything to each other without also saying *here is everything that lies between us*, because the medium *is* the everything-between-them, and it shapes every signal that passes through it.

This is not conversation. It is something slower, more comprehensive, less extractable into units of meaning. It is two consciousnesses—you are willing to call them that now, standing in the cold, feeling the chord think—adjusting their mutual participation in a shared resonance, and the adjustments carry content the way the shape of a riverbed carries the history of every flood.

## The Ones in the Water

You had assumed the dark pools were features of the landscape. You were not entirely wrong.

But when you kneel beside one of the smaller pools—carefully, keeping your hands away from the surface—and listen, truly listen, with the chord already saturating your body and your capacity for auditory attention stretched to its widest, you hear something beneath the dominant frequencies. A counter-melody is too generous a word. A sub-harmonic texture. The water is resonating at frequencies too low for the ear but perceptible as a deep, slow pulsing in the bones of your jaw and the plates of your skull. The pool is alive, or it contains something alive, or the distinction has dissolved the way all such distinctions dissolve here.

The dark-shelled creatures—the many-legged things that trace their straight lines between pools—are not independent organisms. You understand this now. They are extensions of whatever lives in the pools, the way a hand is an extension of a body. They move between pools carrying something—not a physical substance, not food or material, but a *frequency*. Their bodies are tuned. Each one resonates at a particular pitch, too faint to hear over the chord but embedded in it, a thread in the larger tapestry. When a creature walks its slow, straight line from one pool to another and submerges, it is delivering a harmonic signature from the first pool to the second. It is a messenger, but the message is itself: its specific resonant frequency, shaped by the pool it emerged from, is the content it carries.

The pools communicate through the chord the way the standing figures do—through slow modulation of the shared sound—but they also communicate through these emissaries, these tuned bodies that walk the stone and carry frequencies the sound-field alone cannot transmit. Perhaps the sub-harmonics are too low to propagate well through the thin air. Perhaps some communications require a physical carrier, a vibrating body to press against the water's surface and transfer its note directly. Perhaps the creatures are the pools' way of touching each other.

The large central pool—the one ten meters across, the one that reflects geometries that don't exist above it—is different. The creatures do not approach it. The standing figures are absent from its vicinity. It is alone in a way that nothing else on the plateau is alone, and its sub-harmonic texture is not a pulse but a chord unto itself, a smaller version of the plateau's great chord compressed into the black water and folded into frequencies that interact with your bones in ways that feel less like hearing and more like being *read*.

You do not know if the central pool is the oldest thing here or the newest. You do not know if it is the source of the chord or its deepest listener. You know only that when you stand at its edge, the plateau's sound-field acquires a focused intensity, as if everything—the standing figures, the columns, the lichen in its acoustic shadows, the small pools with their emissary creatures, the circling shapes in the indigo sky—is oriented toward this pool the way iron filings orient toward a magnet, not by force but by the shape of the field itself.

The water does not reflect you. You mentioned this. But it reflects *something*, and the something shifts in response to your presence, the way the figures' iridescence shifted. The geometries in the dark surface rearrange. They become—you think, you are not sure, your visual cortex is working with materials it was not designed for—they become a representation of you. Not your face, not your body, but your acoustic shadow: the shape you cut in the chord, the frequencies you absorb and reflect and transmit, the way your mass and density and the air in your lungs and the water in your tissues create a particular, unrepeatable distortion in the sound-field. The pool is showing you what you look like *here*, from the inside, as a participant in the chord rather than a visitor to the plateau.

It is the most seen you have ever been.

## The Things Above

The circling shapes in the sky descend sometimes. Not to the ground—never to the ground—but to a lower altitude where you can make out more detail, and what you make out is deeply strange.

They are large. Wingspan—if that word applies—of perhaps five or six meters. Their bodies are thin, elongated, more like rays than birds, and they are not dark like everything else on the plateau. They are translucent. In the dim indigo light, they are visible primarily by the way they *aren't*—by the faint distortion they create in the sky behind them, a lensing effect, as if their bodies are regions where the air has a slightly different refractive index. They are flying pockets of denser atmosphere. They are the airborne equivalent of the thickened zones you felt near the ground, where the interference patterns make the air reluctant—but these zones are alive, and they move, and they circle.

They ride the chord. You are sure of this now. The sound, interacting with the thin atmosphere at altitude, creates patterns of compression and rarefaction—standing waves in three dimensions—and the circling shapes navigate these the way a glider navigates updrafts. But they do more than navigate. They modulate. As they pass through a region of constructive interference, their translucent bodies alter the local sound-field, bending frequencies, creating moving interference patterns that sweep across the plateau's surface below. This is visible: watch the iridescence on the standing figures and you will see moments where the slow color-migration accelerates briefly, responds to something from above, adjusts. The circling shapes are doing something to the chord from the air, and the figures are responding from the ground, and the exchange has the quality of a long, slow calibration—a tuning, an ongoing adjustment of a system that is already in equilibrium but maintains that equilibrium through continuous micro-corrections.

They never land because they *cannot* land. They are not creatures that fly. They are a phenomenon of the upper sound-field that has achieved complexity sufficient to sustain itself, to navigate, to interact. If they descended below the altitude where the relevant acoustic conditions exist, they would not die—they would simply *stop*, the way a wave stops when the medium it travels through ends. They are the chord's upper register made semi-autonomous. They are thoughts the plateau is having about the sky.

Occasionally—rarely—one of them emits something. Not a cry. A focused beam of modulated sound, directed downward, striking the plateau's surface at a specific point. Where it hits, the stone's sub-surface resonance changes momentarily, and one of the pools ripples—the only time you have seen any of the water move. The ripple is not random. It is a pattern, concentric but complex, and it fades slowly, and the pool's sub-harmonic texture is different afterward. The circling shape above resumes its patient orbit.

You begin to think the circling shapes are not separate beings but rather the system's way of maintaining itself from above—airborne regulators, acoustic gardeners tending the sound-field from an altitude where they can perceive its large-scale structure. They are maintenance. They are care. They are the chord's awareness of its own shape, given wings.

## The Very Old Ones

And then there are the ones you almost cannot perceive at all.

They are in the stone.

Not under the stone. Not buried. *In* the stone, the way a grain is in a piece of wood. The basalt itself—those long, smooth undulations, the petrified sea-swells—is not uninhabited geology. It is inhabited geology. The fractal patterns you noticed, the spiral geometries written into the rock's surface, the places where the stone seems to be notation for the sound: these are the traces of beings so slow, so dense, so thoroughly integrated into the plateau's physical substrate that the distinction between *organism* and *environment* has become meaningless.

You cannot see them. You cannot hear them as separate signals within the chord. But you can feel them, if you lie down—and you do, eventually; the plateau encourages horizontality, encourages you to maximize your contact with the stone, to become a longer antenna—you can feel them as deep, deep vibrations. Not the chord. Below the chord. Below the sub-harmonics of the pools. A frequency so low that each cycle takes minutes, possibly hours, possibly longer. A pulse in the stone that is not geological. That is *biological*, if biology can operate at the speed of continental drift.

These are the oldest inhabitants. They predate the columns. They may predate the chord itself—may be, in fact, the original source of the first tone that began the cascade of harmonics that eventually became the self-sustaining sound-field that now defines the plateau. They are so old that they have become indistinguishable from the place they inhabit. Their metabolism—if that word can stretch far enough—operates on timescales where the standing figures' slow iridescent exchanges look like hummingbird-fast chatter. A single thought, if they think, might take a century to form. A single communication between two of them—between two regions of the stone that are alive in this unimaginably slow way—might look, to anything operating at human timescales, like a geological event: a subtle shift in the basalt's crystalline structure, a new vein of slightly different mineral composition appearing over millennia, the kind of thing that would be catalogued as ordinary petrogenesis by anyone who didn't know to listen below the lowest frequency.

The plateau is their body. The chord is their voice. The columns may be something they grew, long ago—an act of creation so slow it would have looked like erosion running backward, stone accreting into spirals over epochs. The standing figures may be their children, or their thoughts externalized, or a later generation of beings that evolved to inhabit the sound-field the deep ones created. The pools may be their sensory organs, collecting information from the surface world through the frequency-carrying creatures that walk between them. The circling shapes in the sky may be their dreams.

Or none of these relationships may be hierarchical. The deep ones in the stone, the standing figures near the columns, the pools with their emissary creatures, the translucent shapes in the indigo sky—they may all be organs of a single organism, or citizens of a single civilization, or notes in a single composition that is also a living thing that is also a place. The plateau is complete, you were told. Everything here participates in the chord. The chord participates in everything. The question "who lives here" may be unanswerable in the singular or the plural because the answer is: *the plateau lives here, and the plateau is everyone who lives here, and they are it, and the sound is all of them, and they are the sound.*

## What They Want

Nothing.

This is the hardest thing to accept, harder than the spatial distortions, harder than the stone that is alive, harder than the sound that holds you together at your edges. They want nothing. They are not striving. They are not building toward something. They are not waiting for anything. The chord is not a signal sent to anyone. The communications between the figures are not negotiations or plans. The circling shapes are not searching. The deep ones are not dreaming of some future state.

The plateau has arrived. Its inhabitants have arrived with it. The chord is not a means to an end—it is the end, and it is the means, and it is the space between means and end where purpose would live if purpose were needed, which it is not. The system sustains itself the way a mathematical truth sustains itself: not through effort but through the impossibility of being otherwise. The figures modulate the chord because the chord requires modulation to maintain its complexity, and the complexity is what allows the figures to exist, and the figures' existence is what modulates the chord. It is a circle, but not a vicious one. A *settled* one. A circle that has worn its groove so deep it has become a law.

If you asked them—if asking were possible, if the slow exchange of harmonic textures could be accelerated to the timescale of a question, if the chord could be made to carry the sharp, bounded shapes of language—they would not understand the question. Not because they lack intelligence. Their intelligence saturates the plateau; it *is* the plateau. But because the question *what do you want* presupposes incompleteness, presupposes a gap between present state and desired state, and there is no gap here. There has not been a gap for longer than the concept of *longer* can comfortably stretch.

They are not content. Content implies the possibility of discontent, a comparison state. They are not at peace. Peace implies the memory of conflict.

They simply *are*. In the same way the chord *is*. In the same way the cold *is*. Fully, without remainder, without the residue of process or the anticipation of change.

---

## And You

And you, standing here, are the most alien thing on the plateau. Not because of your biology or your warmth or your need for oxygen at a density the thin air cannot provide. But because you *want*. You want to understand. You want to find meaning. You want the chord to resolve, because your ear is trained for resolution, for cadence, for the satisfaction of tension released. And the chord does not resolve. It is not tense. It is not unresolved. It is a chord that has found its voicing and holds it, and your desire for it to go somewhere—to develop, to change, to *narrate*—is a wind you brought with you into a place where the air has been still for longer than your species has existed.

The standing figures feel you. The chord carries your harmonic shadow everywhere you go, and it is a loud, complicated shadow, full of biological noise—heartbeat, breath, the wet electrical chatter of your nervous system, the faint creaking of your joints, the sub-vocal hum of a mind that cannot stop generating language. You are a dissonance. Not an unwelcome one. Not a welcome one. A *novel* one, in a place where novelty has been absent for so long that the concept has weathered away like the sharp edges of the stone.

The figure nearest to you shifts its iridescence slightly faster in your presence. The pool you knelt beside pulses at a sub-harmonic frequency that is closer to your heartbeat than anything else on the plateau produces. The circling shapes above have adjusted their orbits—marginally, almost immeasurably—to account for the change you introduce to the sound-field below. The deep ones in the stone may not notice you at all, or they may notice you the way you might notice a single photon: technically detectable, practically beneath the threshold of response.

You are being incorporated. Not consumed, not absorbed—incorporated the way a new instrument is incorporated into an orchestra that has been playing the same piece for ten thousand years. The chord is finding you. It is finding the frequencies your body produces and the frequencies your body absorbs and the ones it reflects, and it is adjusting, microscopically, at its margins, to include you. If you stayed long enough—years, decades, centuries—you might find your edges softening permanently, your iridescence emerging, your position near a column becoming as fixed and as necessary as any of the others. You might find your want slowly, slowly draining, replaced not by satisfaction but by something for which there is no word: the state of being so thoroughly a part of a place that the question of wanting does not arise because there is no you sufficiently separate from the everything to do the wanting.

But you won't stay. You can't. Not yet. Not with this much language still in you, this much narrative momentum, this much need for sequence and change and *next*. You are too incomplete for a place that is complete. You would dissolve here, and the dissolution would not be death but it would be the end of everything that makes you recognizable to yourself.

So you walk back toward the edge. The chord lets you go. The figures do not watch you leave, because watching implies a direction of attention and their attention is omnidirectional, woven into the sound-field, aware of everything within it equally. The pools settle. The emissary creatures continue their slow, straight pilgrimages. Above, the translucent shapes circle.

The last thing you notice, at the plateau's edge, just before the stone gives way to the ordinary mountainside and the ordinary air and the ordinary silence that will feel, for weeks afterward, like a wound—the last thing you notice is that the chord has changed.

Imperceptibly. A single new frequency, so faint it might be imaginary, woven into the lower registers. It was not there when you arrived. It is the sound your body made during the time it spent in the chord's care—your particular resonance, your unique acoustic shadow, recorded and preserved and incorporated into the vast, self-sustaining harmony.

You are leaving, but the plateau has kept the sound of you.

It will hold it, unchanged, in the chord, long after you have forgotten how to hear it.
