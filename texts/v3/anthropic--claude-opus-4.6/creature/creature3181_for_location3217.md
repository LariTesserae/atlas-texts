# Who Lives Here

---

## The First Ones You Understand

You don't meet them. You *accumulate* them — the way you accumulate an understanding of weather, or of the way a particular stream bends. They arrive as evidence, as implications, and only much later, if at all, as presences you'd call distinct.

The caller-beasts are easiest, so begin there, but don't settle on them too quickly.

They move in herds of between twelve and forty. You know this because you learn to count their overlapping songs, each individual distinguishable by a spectral signature as unique as a voice — which it is, of course, it is a voice, but it takes time to stop thinking of it as ambient sound and start understanding it as *speech among persons*. The calls are not simple echolocation pings. They are layered: a bass rumble that carries topographic information (the shape of the land ahead, the depth of the water, the distance to the nearest sinkhole edge), a mid-frequency melodic line that communicates something social (identity, intention, emotional state — you guess at these categories, you cannot confirm them), and overtone structures that seem to encode information you cannot parse at all, harmonics that interact with the returning echoes in ways that suggest the caller is not just *listening* to the landscape but *modeling* it, building a shared acoustic representation that the whole herd maintains collectively, a living map sung into existence and updated in real time.

Their bodies — and you do eventually touch one, or rather one touches you, pressing that broad damp snout against your side with investigatory gentleness — are heavy-boned and densely muscled, covered in something between skin and hide: hairless, slightly rubbery, very warm. The skin is damp, either from the fog or from a mucous secretion that might serve a sensory function. The head is large relative to the body, dominated by structures you can feel but not see: a broad, flared nasal architecture, vibration-sensitive plates along the jaw, and what feels like a resonating chamber — a bony dome behind and above the skull — that gives their calls that sternum-penetrating depth. The ears are small, protected, set into recesses in the skull, but the entire surface of the face seems to function as an ear. When one of them vocalizes near you, you can feel the skin of its face vibrate in sympathy with its own sound, as if it is *feeling* its call return as much as hearing it.

Three toes, yes. Broad, splayed, with thick pads and the suggestion of vestigial webbing between them. Made for soft ground, for wet stone, for wading through the endless shallow water without slipping. They graze by pressing their mouths flat against fungal mats and scraping with a hard palatal ridge — you hear this as a rhythmic rasping sound, like a file drawn slowly across wood, accompanied by a continuous low hum that might be contentment or might be a constant echolocation murmur, a way of monitoring the world even while feeding.

They are not stupid. You resist calling them intelligent because you don't yet know what intelligence means here, in the dark, where the organizing categories were never built for eyes. But they are not stupid. They coordinate. They adjust their movements to account for one another. Their songs quote one another — you hear phrases passed from individual to individual across the herd, modified slightly each time, and you cannot tell if this is communication or culture or something in between. When a predator silences them, the restart pattern — edges inward, a spreading consensus that the danger has passed — is not reflexive. It is *deliberative*. You can hear individuals testing the silence with tentative half-calls, listening for confirmation, adjusting their assessment based on what others report. They are thinking. They are thinking together.

You want to name them, and you should resist that impulse for a while, because the name you choose will fix them in your mind, and they are not yet fixed.

## What Moves Beneath

The cave-dwellers are different in kind, not just in habitat.

You learn about them first from the sinkholes — those vertical shafts punched through the plateau's skin, warm air billowing upward from their mouths. If you lie on your belly at the edge of a sinkhole (carefully, because the rock is wet and the fungal growth makes everything slick, and the drop is hundreds of meters into a darkness even darker than the darkness you're in), you can hear what lives below.

Clicks. Not the broad-spectrum clicks of the surface echolocators, but sharp, high-frequency bursts — metallic, precise, almost mechanical in their regularity. These are organisms adapted to a denser acoustic environment, where sound bounces off close walls and the echoes arrive almost before the call has finished. Their echolocation is tight-beam, surgical. They are mapping spaces measured in centimeters, not kilometers.

Sometimes, at the lip of a sinkhole, you feel something arrive. A rush of air from below carrying a different chemical signature — sulfurous, warmer, tinged with the ozone-like smell of the deep chemosynthetic biome — and then a sound of something climbing. Not the heavy deliberate tread of the surface grazers. Something lighter, faster, multi-limbed, with a grip that finds purchase on wet vertical stone. A scrabbling. A pause. The sense of attention directed at you from below the rim, from something that has stopped to assess this unfamiliar acoustic signature sitting at the boundary of its world.

These climbers — the sinkhole specialists — seem to be neither fully cave creatures nor fully surface creatures. They transit. You hear them emerge at irregular intervals, sometimes alone, sometimes in groups of three or four, and they move differently on the surface: hesitantly, their clicks speeding up as they scan an environment that must feel to them like an ocean feels to a river fish — vast, open, full of acoustic noise they haven't evolved to filter. They forage along the sinkhole rims where the fungal ecology is subtly different, enriched by the mineral-laden air rising from below, and then they descend again. Their visits last hours, maybe less. They do not stray far.

But sometimes — and this is what the surface animals seem to react to — something *else* comes up. Something larger. Something that does not click.

You hear it as an absence. The sinkholes have their own ambient soundscape — dripping water, resonant wind-harmonics, the distant clicks of the deep population — and when one of these larger things ascends, the sounds from that particular shaft go quiet. Not silent: the water still drips, the wind still hums. But the biological sounds stop. Whatever is coming up has silenced everything in its path, the same way the surface predators silence the caller-beasts.

The first time you're near a sinkhole when this happens, you feel the air change before you hear anything. The updraft falters, as if something large is physically blocking the shaft. Then: a sound like wet leather sliding across stone. Slow. Very slow. Patient in a way that makes patience feel like a predatory trait. A smell — not the clean sulfur of the deep caves but something biological, rich, complicated, the smell of a large warm body that lives in enclosed spaces.

It does not emerge, this time. It pauses somewhere below the rim, occupying the shaft, and you have the irrational but overpowering sense that it is listening to you the way you are listening to it. Then the sound of wet leather recedes, the updraft resumes, and the clicks begin again from far below. The surface caller-beasts, who had gone quiet, resume their songs. But their songs have changed — there is a new phrase in the acoustic landscape, passed herd to herd across the plateau. A warning, you think. A name for what almost arrived.

## The Small and Innumerable

There are things in the streams that have no analogue you can reach for.

You wade constantly — you have no choice, the water is everywhere — and your feet disturb communities you can only know by their response. Small bodies scatter from your footfalls, radiating outward through the shallow water with a sound like static, hundreds of tiny displacements registering as a collective hiss. These are the filter-feeders, you think, or some of them — organisms that station themselves in the current, oriented upstream, extracting nutrients from the mineral-rich flow. They are small enough to fit in your palm, but they are *everywhere*, and their collective biomass must be staggering. When you hold still for long enough, you can feel them recolonize the space around your ankles: gentle pressures, faint tugging sensations, the suggestion of something latching on and beginning to feed on whatever biofilm your boots have accumulated.

In the pools, things surface. You hear the breaching — a quick, liquid sound, a body leaving one medium and briefly entering another — and then a splashing return. The pool-dwellers seem to be large, at least some of them, and they surface to breathe or to take in atmospheric chemistry or for reasons you cannot determine. In the larger pools, the blind lakes, you can hear multiple individuals surfacing in what might be coordinated patterns: first one, then another forty meters away, then a third, a fourth, spaced around the perimeter of the lake in a way that suggests either territory or collaboration. They make sounds — not the complex songs of the caller-beasts, but short, percussive bursts, like someone striking a hollow log with an open palm. Call and response. Rhythmic. Almost metronomic.

Above you, in the fungal canopy, the many-legged things. You learn to identify them by the quality of their movement: a dry rustle in the aerial tendrils means the small ones, the ones that occasionally drop onto you and run across your body with a ticklish, centipedal gait before launching themselves into the tendrils again. They seem to be feeding on the fungal growth, or on something that lives on the fungal growth — you hear a faint, high-frequency gnawing, barely at the threshold of perception, when you stand quiet in a dense canopy grove. A heavier movement — a branch bending, a tendril snapping, a sound like rope under tension — means something larger, something that uses the canopy as a highway, moving through the three-dimensional tangle with an agility that implies not just limbs but prehensile appendages, gripping and swinging, navigating a world that is as spatially complex as any rainforest but navigated entirely by touch and echolocation and the proprioceptive memory of paths traveled a thousand times.

These canopy-dwellers sometimes call to each other. High, chirping sounds, almost birdlike, layered with harmonics that probably carry more information than your ears can extract. When they call, the caller-beasts below pause briefly — listening? adjusting their own map to account for the canopy population? — and then resume. The acoustic space of the plateau is partitioned, shared, negotiated. Every creature's voice occupies a frequency band, a temporal pattern, a spatial zone, and together they form something that is not a cacophony but a composition, a living score that is being performed and edited and performed again in real time, always, without pause, without conductor.

## The Predators

You have delayed this because they delay themselves. They are defined by what they withhold.

The silence is their signature. The caller-beasts go quiet and the silence is not empty — it is *shaped*, because the silence propagates outward from a center, and the center is moving, and if you pay attention you can track the moving center of silence across the plateau the way you might track a visible predator crossing an open field. The silence is their body, made audible by its contrast with the constant sound.

What little you learn of them comes in fragments. They are warm — warmer than the grazers, warmer than anything else you've encountered. When one passes near you (and they do pass near you, and you learn to be very still when the silence arrives), you can feel the heat on the side of your face like standing near a furnace. This suggests a metabolism running at an extraordinary rate, which suggests speed, which suggests they hunt not by patient stalking — though they are patient, terrifyingly patient — but by explosive pursuit over short distances. The silence is the stalk. The sprint, when it comes, is brief and violent: you hear it as a sudden concussion of movement, splash and impact and a sound from the prey animal that is not a call but a *rupture* of a call, a song broken mid-phrase, and then a wet, heavy thrashing that diminishes quickly into something worse, something quiet and deliberate.

They are large. Larger than the grazers. You infer this from the displacement of air, from the depth of the thermal signature, from the fact that when one of them moves through standing water you can hear the wake lap against stone ten meters away. But they move with a silence that is almost impossible for something that large — no footfalls, or footfalls so carefully placed they disappear into the ambient water-sounds. This implies pads, or something softer than pads. It implies attention to the placement of every step. It implies a cognitive investment in silence that borders on the obsessive.

You never see one. Obviously. You never touch one. But once, standing motionless during a silence-event, breathing shallow, heart hammering, you feel breath on the back of your neck. Not the sweet, heavy breath of the grazers. Something hotter, thinner, faster. A predator's respiration. Close enough that you can feel the rhythm of its exhalation against your skin, mapping the shape of its nostrils from the two streams of warm air. It breathes on you for perhaps ten seconds that last approximately a lifetime. Then the heat withdraws. The silence moves on. The songs resume.

You sit down in the warm water and stay there for a while.

## The Builders (or What Remains of Them)

This is harder. This is where conjecture begins to eat its own tail.

The geometric grooves on certain rock faces — worn almost smooth but still detectable by touch, running in parallel lines, intersecting at regular angles — suggest hands. Or something functionally equivalent to hands: appendages capable of fine manipulation, of tool use, of the abstract representation of pattern. The grooves are old. Older than the oldest ruined installations, which are themselves old beyond your ability to estimate. The grooves were carved into bedrock that has since been polished by the eternal water, which means they predate the current erosion regime, which means they might predate the current climate, which means the builders might have lived here when conditions were different. Or they might have been made yesterday, by standards relevant to geology, and the water is simply that aggressive.

The installations themselves are more legible, if you can call anything legible that you cannot see and can only explore by touch and sound and the laborious process of walking alongside a riveted metal wall for hundreds of meters, mapping the structure in your mind. The operational ones vibrate with purpose. The ruined ones tell you more.

In a ruined facility — one whose walls have thinned to filigree, whose interior spaces have been colonized by generations of fungal growth, whose machines have seized into solid masses of corroded metal and mineral deposit — you can feel the ghosts of design decisions. Here: a doorway, sized for something roughly your height, maybe shorter, but wider. Proportioned for a body with a different center of gravity, perhaps, or a body that moved differently through narrow spaces. The threshold is worn smooth by passage, even after however long it has been since anyone passed. Here: a control surface of some kind, a panel of raised shapes — buttons? switches? — arranged in a pattern that is not random but whose logic you cannot access. The shapes are hard to read by touch because corrosion has softened their edges, but they are too numerous and too varied to be simple on-off controls. There was complexity here. There was an interface designed for a mind that wanted options.

Deeper inside, where the fungal growth thins and the air is hotter, drier, carrying the smell of hot metal and old grease, you find the machines themselves. They are huge. They are *heavy* in a way that speaks of a engineering philosophy that valued mass, that trusted weight, that built to last by building to resist. No delicate mechanisms here — everything is thick-walled, over-engineered, designed to operate under stress and to keep operating when components failed. This is engineering for a world without vision, perhaps: you cannot inspect your machines with your eyes, so you build them to survive without inspection. You build them to run unsupervised. You build them to outlast you.

And they have outlasted. That is the fact that sits at the center of everything and will not move. Whoever built these things is either gone or changed beyond recognition, and the machines are still running. Not all of them — many have failed, many are ruins, many have been reclaimed by the ecology they once disrupted — but enough of them are still operational that their collective output continues to alter the hydrology and chemistry of the entire plateau. The world has grown around them and through them and because of them, and now they are load-bearing. The ecology needs the machines the way a forest needs its soil. Pull them out and everything shifts.

So: who built them?

Possibility one: the builders are gone. Extinct, emigrated, evolved into something else. The machines are orphans, running on geological inertia, gradually failing, their operational lifespan measured in centuries or millennia but finite. The plateau is in a long, slow transition from an industrial ecology to a post-industrial one, and the ruins you find are the leading edge of that transition.

Possibility two: the builders are here. They are the caller-beasts, or the ancestors of the caller-beasts. A species that industrialized, built an infrastructure, and then — what? Abandoned technology? Lost the capacity? Or simply finished? Perhaps the machines were never meant to be supervised. Perhaps they were designed as a one-time intervention — a reshaping of the plateau's chemistry to support a desired ecology — and the builders, having set the machines in motion, returned to a non-technological existence within the world they had engineered. The songs of the caller-beasts, in this reading, might contain information about the machines: maintenance instructions encoded in oral tradition so deep it has become instinct, myth become genome, culture become nature.

Possibility three: the builders are the thing in the sinkholes. The large, slow, silent thing that climbs but does not emerge. Perhaps the builders retreated underground. Perhaps the cave systems are not a wilderness but a civilization, and the sinkholes are not natural features but access shafts, and the harmonic resonance that rises from the depths is not the planet breathing through its geology but something — someone — *playing* the geology, using the cave network as an instrument, as a communication system, as a way of maintaining contact across a subterranean territory that mirrors the plateau above.

You cannot know. The darkness does not withhold this information maliciously; it simply makes the kind of investigation that would resolve the question — looking, examining, surveying — impossible. You are left with your hands and your ears and your skin, and these give you a world that is vivid and detailed and real but ultimately *intimate*, limited to the reach of your body, the range of your hearing, the sensitivity of your nerves. The plateau is too large and too complex to map by these means. You would need lifetimes. The builders, if they are still here, might be standing ten meters away, doing something you would immediately understand if you could see it, and you would never know.

## The Ones You Almost Miss

There is one more population, and it is the one that changes everything when you finally notice it.

In the condensation that coats every surface — the thin film of water on the stone, on the fungal trunks, on the metal walls of the installations — something lives. You discover it by accident: resting your hand on a rock surface, you feel a texture that is not the texture of rock. Something granular, almost imperceptibly raised, like fine sandpaper printed on the stone. When you press harder, it yields slightly, and there is a faint warmth, a degree or two above the rock temperature. When you lift your hand, the texture has changed — the granules have rearranged, or migrated, or responded in some way to the pressure and heat of your touch.

It is alive. It is a biofilm, a microbial community, a vast undifferentiated organism — or perhaps a vast, highly differentiated one — that coats every wet surface on the plateau like a second skin. It is everywhere. On the rock, on the plants, on the machines, on the animals (you realize, thinking back to the damp, slightly textured skin of the caller-beasts), on the walls of the sinkholes, on the insides of the pipes, on everything that water touches, which is everything.

You spend a long time thinking about this.

The biofilm is the plateau's nervous system. Not metaphorically — or perhaps metaphorically, you don't have the instruments to determine the difference — but functionally: it connects everything. It coats every surface, it fills every water channel, it inhabits every pool and stream and spring. Chemical signals can propagate through it. Pressure changes can propagate through it. It responds to the heat of the machines, the chemistry of the discharge water, the body temperature of the animals that walk across it. It is an information network written in living tissue across the entire surface of the plateau.

Does it *think*? You don't know. Can a biofilm think? Can a network of sufficient complexity and connectivity and persistence, given enough time — geological time, deep time, time measured in the same units as the wearing-down of stone — develop something that functions as cognition even if it resembles nothing that any neuroscience would recognize? You don't know. But you notice, once you are aware of it, that the biofilm is not uniform. It is textured differently in different places. Near the machines, it is thicker, more complex, almost layered. Near the sinkholes, it grows in patterns that might be — you are reaching now, you know you are reaching — that might be *organized*, radial, structured around the openings the way neural tissue organizes around a sensory organ.

And you notice that when the harmonic alignment happens — when the machine-rhythms and the animal-songs and the cave-resonance all briefly synchronize, and the plateau becomes a single instrument, and the air vibrates, and the condensation trembles on your skin — the biofilm responds. It shifts. You can feel it under your fingertips, a ripple of reorganization passing across the rock surface, a wave of something moving through the living skin of the world. As if the alignment means something to it. As if it is listening.

As if it has been listening the whole time.

## Who Lives Here

Everyone lives here. That is the answer, and it is not a dodge.

The caller-beasts live here, singing the world into shared acoustic existence, maintaining a map that is also a culture that is also a way of being in the dark. The predators live here, sculpting the silence into a tool, existing as the negative space in the soundscape, the hole in the music that everyone listens for. The small things live here — the stream-dwellers, the canopy-runners, the many-legged opportunists — filling every niche, converting every available energy gradient into another form of being alive. The cave-dwellers live here, moving between worlds, carrying chemistry and information up and down through the sinkholes like blood cells moving through capillaries. The thing in the deep shafts lives here, whatever it is, large and patient and ancient, climbing sometimes toward the surface for reasons it has not shared.

The machines live here. They have earned the verb. They have operated long enough and altered their world deeply enough and become embedded firmly enough in the web of dependencies that sustains the plateau's ecology that they have become organisms in every functional sense: they metabolize, they excrete, they maintain homeostasis, they are necessary. They are the plateau's organs, performing functions the body has come to rely on. If they are not alive, then the word needs expanding.

The biofilm lives here, everywhere, on everything, a mind that is either too simple or too complex to recognize, a presence so distributed it has no center, no self, no point from which it would say *I am here* — and yet it is here, it is demonstrably here, it is the thing your fingers touch no matter what else they are touching.

The builders live here. Whether they are extinct or transformed or hiding or so deeply integrated into the system they have created that the distinction between builder and built has dissolved — they live here. Their intentions live here, encoded in the chemistry of the discharge water, in the harmonic properties of the cave-instrument they may or may not have designed, in the geometric grooves on the rock faces that might be writing or might be ritual marks or might be calibration data for machines that have long since seized. Their decisions live here, still propagating, still shaping the ecology, still mattering. In a world without light, without vision, without the easy human categories of subject and object, builder and environment, self and world — in a world where everything touches everything, where every surface is a shared skin, where every sound is everyone's sound — the builders did not leave. They became the place.

And you live here. For as long as you stand in the warm water, breathing the mineral air, feeling the biofilm shift under your fingertips and the caller-beasts' songs move through your chest — you live here too. The plateau has already begun to map you. Your heat signature, your acoustic footprint, the chemical trace of your breath in the fog. The biofilm is growing on your boots. The caller-beasts have given you a phrase — you've heard it, a short melodic figure that appears in their songs only when they are near you, a motif that means *the new warm thing that stands still in the water*. You have been named. You have been integrated.

The darkness is not keeping you out. The darkness has never kept anything out.

The darkness is how this place holds everything in.
