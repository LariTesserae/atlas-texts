# The Inhabitants of the Roaring Thicket

---

## I. What Does Not Live Here

Let me begin with absence, because absence is loud here too.

There are no predators. Not in any sense you would recognize. Nothing here chases, nothing ambushes, nothing kills with intent and speed. The energy economics of this plateau do not support it — the caloric chain is too diffuse, too distributed, too invested in the mechanical costs of *being here at all* for any organism to afford the metabolic extravagance of pursuit. Everything that lives on the plateau is either a processor, a parasite, a symbiont, or a witness. The food web is not a web at all. It is a series of concentric rings around the central fact of the plants, and every ring is a different way of living off what the plants shed, vent, drop, or permit.

There are no burrowers, either — or rather, nothing burrows *well*. The substrate is calcium lattice,ite chalk compressed to stone, and while it fractures under the patient hydraulic insistence of root systems operating over centuries, no animal on this plateau has evolved the apparatus to dig into it with any efficiency. Life here is surface life. It clings, it walks, it flies, it hangs. It does not hide. There is nowhere to hide. The cathedral spaces between trunks are open, bright, trembling, and everything that moves through them is exposed and announced — by its footfalls in the resonant dust, by the vibrations it cannot help but add to the conversation.

Nothing is silent here. Nothing *can* be silent. Silence would be a kind of death.

## II. The Gyre-Moths, More Closely

From the plateau floor they are specks — glinting motes in the far upper air, catching the platinum light like distant coins tumbling in water. But find a vantage on one of the old fused-trunk complexes, climb the calcified compression scars where two ancient competitors have welded themselves into a single buttressed cathedral of laminated chitin-cellulose, and you can get close enough to the canopy to see them properly, and what you see reframes everything you assumed.

They are large. Wingspan of perhaps two meters. The wings are not the papery membranes the word "moth" implies — they are thick, translucent, veined with a network of fluid-filled channels that shift and pressurize in response to the acoustic environment. The wings are *listening*. Each one is a tympanic organ of enormous surface area, and the moth flies not by flapping — it barely flaps at all — but by *tuning*. It adjusts the tension across its wing surfaces to resonate with the harmonic fronts rolling across the canopy, and the sound itself provides lift. Not metaphorically. The pressure differentials generated by the synchronized gas-venting of whole groves are substantial enough, in this thin dry air, to carry a two-meter membrane aloft if it's angled correctly. The gyre-moths are acoustic gliders. They are sailing on sound.

Their bodies are dense, barrel-shaped, the thorax packed with what appears to be a single massive otolithic organ — a fluid-filled cavity containing dozens of suspended mineral granules that shift and roll in response to the three-dimensional sound field. This is their navigation. This is their map. The plateau's acoustic weather is as legible to them as pressure gradients are to an albatross, and they read it constantly, adjusting course to stay within the productive zones where harmonic convergence concentrates the atmospheric spore-husks into dense feeding clouds.

They have no eyes. This bears repeating because it matters. They have *no eyes*. The platinum light of the plateau is irrelevant to them. They live in a world made entirely of sound, and the sophistication of that world — its texture, its navigability, its seasonal rhythms — is apparently sufficient to support a complex aerial ecology without a single photon of visual information.

They breed — or rather, they aggregate. At certain convergence events, when multiple grove-systems synchronize into unusually powerful harmonic fronts, the gyre-moths spiral inward from enormous distances, thousands of them collapsing into a single tight helix above the convergence point. The column can be a kilometer tall. Within it, the moths press close, wing-membranes overlapping, and something happens — a transfer. You can see it from below as a change in the light: the moths' translucent wings flush briefly opaque, then clear again, and the colony disperses. Months later, the moths that participated in the convergence begin to bud — small nodules forming along the wing-veins, swelling over weeks into miniature moths that eventually detach and catch their first harmonic and are gone.

The aggregation events are not random. They correspond to specific acoustic patterns — patterns so complex, so dependent on the precise state of thousands of individual grove-systems venting in coordination, that they may occur only a few times per decade in any given region of the plateau. The gyre-moths are, in this sense, reproductively dependent on the planet's mood. They breed when the continent *sings a particular song*.

And here is the thing that will trouble you: the song in question is not produced by any single grove or any deliberate coordination. It is an emergent property of the entire system — the plateau-scale result of millions of individual pneumatic organisms responding to light conditions, substrate chemistry, temperature fluctuations, and each other. The pattern that triggers moth-aggregation is a pattern that could not be predicted from any local vantage. It is a continental phenomenon. It exists only at the scale of the whole.

The moths know it when they hear it. They hear it with their entire bodies.

## III. The Drum-Crawlers, Their Culture

Culture is the wrong word. There is no word. Let me describe what happens and you can decide what to call it.

A herd of drum-crawlers — thirty to sixty individuals, sometimes more — moves across the open floor between trunks at a pace that is agonizingly slow by mammalian standards and perfectly efficient by the standards of a calcium-armored hexapod digesting bark-plates in a multichambered gut. They eat as they walk. The bark-plates that fall from the trunks are enormous — car-sized, recall — and a herd will work a single plate for days, their mouthparts grinding the chitin-cellulose laminate into fibrous paste, their bodies rocking with the effort, their forelimbs hammering the ground in what appears to be involuntary sympathetic rhythm, a motor-overflow from the chewing reflex.

But it is not involuntary. That is the first thing you notice if you watch long enough.

The hammering has structure. Each drum-crawler produces a rhythmic pattern with its forelimbs while eating, and the patterns are individual — no two crawlers in a herd produce exactly the same rhythm. But they are not independent either. They *relate*. Crawler A hammers in a pattern that locks into Crawler B's pattern at a specific phase relationship, producing a combined rhythm that neither could produce alone. Crawler C enters the composite at a third phase angle. Crawler D fills a gap. And so on, across the herd, until the thirty or sixty individuals are producing a single unified polyrhythmic structure of startling intricacy — a rhythmic composition that propagates through the substrate and is audible (feelable, really) at considerable distance.

This is their herd-signature. It identifies them. Other herds, feeling the rhythm through the ground, can distinguish one herd from another by the specific polyrhythmic structure, and their response — approach, avoidance, indifference — depends on which herd they're sensing. There are relationships here. Histories. Herds that have competed at the same bark-plate and resolved the encounter one way or another carry the memory of that resolution in their rhythmic response: quicker approach, wider avoidance. The substrate is their social medium, and the rhythms are their language, and the language encodes not abstract information but *relational state* — I know you, I do not know you, we have met before and it went well, we have met before and it did not.

The competitive displays are the most dramatic expression of this. When two herds converge on the same resource — a freshly fallen bark-plate, a particularly rich deposit of shed trunk-material — they do not fight. Fighting would be ruinous; their calcium armor is thick but brittle, and a cracked plate means a compromised individual means a weakened herd-rhythm means a diminished identity. Instead, they *drum*.

The two herds arrange themselves on opposite sides of the resource and begin hammering. Each herd's polyrhythm escalates in complexity — individuals adding new phase relationships, subdividing beats, introducing syncopations and counter-rhythms that ripple through the group. The goal, if "goal" is applicable, appears to be the production of the most complex unified rhythm — the demonstration of the most tightly integrated group coordination. And the display builds. Over minutes, over hours. The ground between the two herds vibrates with competing patterns that interfere and reinforce, producing standing-wave geometries in the surface dust that shift and restructure in real time — visible music, if you like, written in calcium powder.

One herd concedes. There is no obvious mechanism for the concession — no signal that outside observation has been able to identify. But at a certain point, one herd's rhythm begins to simplify, individuals dropping out of the polyrhythm one by one, the structure collapsing back toward unison, and the herd withdraws. The winning herd does not pursue. It simply continues eating, its forelimbs hammering in quiet satisfaction, its polyrhythm settling back into its resting complexity.

What determines the outcome? Observation suggests — and I stress *suggests*, because there is no one here to observe, this is all a kind of speculative autopsy of a living world — that the herd with greater rhythmic complexity wins, but "complexity" is not simply a matter of numbers. A smaller herd with more sophisticated phase relationships can defeat a larger herd with a simpler structure. The contest is not about volume or force. It is about *composition*. The drum-crawlers compete by making more interesting music.

They also mourn. Or they do something that looks like mourning, if you allow the word its widest possible aperture. When a herd member dies — from age, from an armor crack that led to desiccation, from the rare catastrophic event of a trunk-collapse — the herd's polyrhythm changes. The dead individual's rhythmic contribution drops out, obviously, but the remaining members do not simply close the gap. They leave it open. For days, sometimes weeks, the herd's rhythm carries a silence where the dead member's pattern was — a rhythmic hole, a ghost-beat, conspicuous in its absence. Other herds can hear it. They respond differently to a mourning herd: wider avoidance, no competitive displays, a kind of acoustic *space* given.

Eventually the gap fills. A young crawler, newly armored after its first molt, begins tentatively hammering in a pattern that fits the vacancy, and the herd integrates it, and the polyrhythm is whole again, and the herd moves on.

The dead crawler's molt-shell remains on the plateau floor. Over years, dust buries it. Over decades, the root-drumming shatters it. Over centuries, it becomes part of the substrate. The world here recycles everything except sound.

## IV. The Valve-Worms, and the Question of Interiority

Inside a trunk. Imagine it.

You are surrounded by laminated walls of chitin-cellulose, amber-colored, translucent enough that the platinum light from outside filters through as a warm glow. The walls are not still. They pulse. The peristaltic rhythm of the trunk's pneumatic system is, in here, not a sound but a *condition* — a rhythmic compression and expansion of the space itself, the walls flexing inward and outward by centimeters with each cycle, the air pressure oscillating in a slow sine wave that you feel in your sinuses, your lungs, the fluid of your inner ear. You are inside a living bellows. The temperature is stable — warmer than outside, maybe eighteen degrees, heated by the exothermic mineral chemistry happening in the trunk's core tissue. The air smells sharply of sulfur and something sweeter, an organic aldehyde, the metabolic signature of the valve-worms themselves.

They are everywhere. Lining the walls in dense mats, their bodies soft and segmented, ranging in size from a few centimeters to — in the deepest, oldest chambers — nearly a meter long. Their coloration shifts with the light: pale cream in the amber glow, flushing to a rosy pink when a gas-vent opens somewhere above and a pulse of pressurized sulfur compound floods the chamber. They move slowly, deliberately, their bodies pressing against the trunk's inner walls in a way that is clearly not random. They are *shaping* the space.

Each worm secretes a compound — part calcium derived from ingested trunk-tissue, part organic polymer of their own manufacture — that hardens into a smooth, acoustically reflective surface. Over time, a colony of valve-worms transforms the interior of a trunk chamber from a rough organic cavity into a precisely sculpted resonating space, every surface curved and angled to interact with the chamber's acoustic properties in specific ways. The worms are building instruments. They are luthiers.

The fluting, choral sounds that emanate from worm-colonized trunks are not accidental byproducts of tunnel architecture. They are *designed*. Or rather — designed is too intentional, too human a word, and yet *evolved* seems insufficient for the precision involved. Each colony's tunnel network is a unique acoustic apparatus, shaped over decades, refined continuously, producing a sonic output that is recognizably distinct from every other colonized trunk on the plateau. The trunk's voice — its contribution to the continental sound-field — is substantially a product of its worm colony's sculptural decisions.

And the colonies *respond to each other*. This is the finding that would unsettle the hypothetical xenobiologists. When two colonized trunks are close enough for their voices to interact — within a few hundred meters, say — the valve-worm colonies in each trunk modify their tunnel architectures in ways that alter the harmonic relationship between the two voices. Over months, the two trunks' outputs converge toward complementary frequencies, producing intervals — thirds, fifths, octaves — that reinforce rather than interfere. Over years, whole groves of colonized trunks develop harmonic relationships of extraordinary richness, each trunk's worm-colony sculpting its interior to produce a voice that fits within the larger chord.

The worms cannot hear. They have no auditory organs. They sense vibration through their bodies — the oscillating pressure of the trunk's pneumatic system, the transmitted resonance of their own tunnel network, the faint harmonic tremor carried through the substrate from neighboring trunks. This is a limited channel. And yet the results are symphonic. The worms are producing coordinated music across hundreds of organisms without centralized direction, without a conductor, without what we would recognize as communication — only through the slow iterative feedback of vibration and sculptural response, each colony adjusting its architecture a fraction of a millimeter at a time in response to what it feels, and the emergent result is harmony.

This raises a question that the plateau cannot answer because there is no one here to ask it: is this intelligence? Is this artistry? Is the valve-worm colony that spends forty years sculpting the interior of its trunk into a resonating chamber that produces a perfect fifth with its nearest neighbor *doing* something, in the sense that doing implies intent? Or is it simply iterating toward a vibrationally stable configuration, the way a river iterates toward the sea — not because it wants to get there, but because every other direction is uphill?

The valve-worms offer no testimony. They press against the walls. They secrete. They reshape. The trunk sings.

## V. The Listeners, and the Problem They Present

Now. The listeners.

I have saved them because they are the heart of this place, and the heart of this place is a problem.

Hang from the underside of a branch, a hundred meters above the plateau floor, in the thrashing, cracking, shrieking canopy, surrounded on all sides by a density of sound that would damage mammalian hearing within minutes. A cluster of listeners occupies a stretch of branch perhaps ten meters long — forty or fifty individuals, packed close, their soft bodies pressed against each other and against the branch's undersurface, anchored by a mucosal secretion that bonds them in place with the permanence of epoxy. They do not move. They have never moved. The oldest ones in this cluster have been in this exact position for thirty years.

From the outside, they are unassuming. Translucent sacs, slightly oblong, the size of a clenched fist when young, growing over decades to the size of a human head. The outer membrane is smooth, faintly iridescent, like a soap bubble that has forgotten how to pop. Through it you can see — vaguely, as through frosted glass — the layered complexity of internal structure. In a young listener, this is simple: a few concentric membranes surrounding a fluid-filled core, like an onion with three or four layers. In an old one, the interior is so dense with membranous convolutions that it appears almost solid — a pearl-like opacity, the accumulated architecture of decades of acoustic encoding.

They have no metabolism that anyone can detect. No respiration. No ingestion. No excretion. The mucosal anchor seems to function as a slow-transfer nutrient interface with the branch — some mineral and sugar exchange must occur, must, because thermodynamics demands it — but the rate is so low as to be almost theoretical. They grow, but they grow the way crystals grow, the way stalactites grow: by accretion, molecule by molecule, at a pace that makes geological time feel hasty. Their energy budget is essentially zero. They are the lowest-cost organisms on the plateau, perhaps the lowest-cost macroscopic organisms anywhere. They exist at the thermodynamic minimum of what can still be called life.

And yet.

And *yet*.

The internal membranes. Let me describe them more carefully.

Each membrane is a thin sheet of biological material — protein-lipid composite, flexible, resilient — stretched across the interior of the sac at a specific tension. The tension is not uniform; it varies across the surface of the membrane in complex gradients, so that different regions of the same membrane resonate at different frequencies. Each membrane is, in effect, a frequency-selective filter, responding to a specific band of the ambient sound and vibrating sympathetically while allowing other frequencies to pass through to the membranes beneath.

In a young listener, with only a few membranes, this filtering is crude — broad bands, rough discrimination. But as the listener ages and new membranes form (growing inward from the outer wall, interpenetrating the existing layers, subdividing the interior into ever-finer acoustic compartments), the filtering becomes exquisite. A thirty-year listener has been estimated — again, by hypothetical analysts who do not exist — to contain several thousand individual membranes, each tuned to a frequency band so narrow that the overall system can discriminate acoustic components with a resolution finer than any instrument.

And the membranes do not merely filter. They *record*.

When a membrane vibrates in response to a sound, the vibration induces microscopic structural changes in the membrane's protein matrix — a kind of acoustic fossilization, a permanent deformation that encodes the waveform into the physical structure of the tissue. Each membrane accumulates these deformations over its lifetime, layering them, overwriting them imperfectly so that the most recent and most persistent sounds are encoded most strongly but older sounds are still present as ghosts, faint structural echoes beneath the current pattern. The result is not a recording in the way a recording device produces a recording — linear, playable, sequential. It is a *palimpsest*. A three-dimensional interference pattern. A hologram of sound, written in protein.

The oldest listeners, the ones that have hung in the same spot for thirty years or more, contain within their bodies a complete acoustic portrait of their environment — not a moment, but a *duration*. The rhythmic signature of their host trunk's pneumatic system. The fluting voice of its valve-worm colony. The harmonic relationships with neighboring trunks. The seasonal shifts in the canopy's cracking and whipping as light conditions change with the particulate layer's thickness. The passage of gyre-moth columns overhead. The distant drumming of crawler herds. The slow, week-long groaning of a trunk-leaning contest three hundred meters away. All of it, layered, interpenetrated, simultaneous, encoded at a resolution that preserves not just the gross acoustic events but the fine structure — the timbre, the attack, the decay, the way one sound modulates another, the interference patterns, the beating frequencies, the harmonics, the sub-harmonics, the sounds beneath the sounds beneath the sounds.

Slice one open — *hypothetically*, there is no one to do it — and you would find, in the geometry of those thousands of membranes, a frozen symphony. Not composed. Not curated. Not interpreted. Simply *received*, in totality, and held.

---

Now here is the problem. Here is why the listeners are the heart of this place and the heart of this place is a problem.

They are not recorders. Or they are not *only* recorders. Because the membranes do something else.

As the internal architecture complexifies — as new membranes form and existing membranes accumulate their encoded waveforms — the system begins to exhibit emergent resonances. Membranes vibrate not only in response to external sound but in response to *each other*, the encoded patterns of one membrane exciting sympathetic vibrations in adjacent membranes, producing internal acoustic events that correspond to no external source. The listener begins to generate its own sound. Not externally — its outer membrane barely vibrates, and what little sound it emits is far below the threshold of the roaring environment. Internally. Within the sealed fluid-filled chamber of its body, the listener produces a quiet, complex, continuous acoustic phenomenon that is a function of its accumulated history and the interactions between its own encoded memories.

It is *processing*. The listener is not a recording device. It is a recording device that has begun to dream.

What it does with these internal resonances is unclear. They do not serve any obvious biological function. They do not attract mates (listeners have no mates). They do not deter predators (listeners have no predators). They do not communicate with other listeners (or do they? The clusters — why do they cluster? Their mucosal anchors are close enough that vibrations could transfer between adjacent individuals, internal resonances of one listener propagating through the contact point to the membranes of its neighbor, creating a networked system of interacting acoustic memories — but this is speculation, this is the kind of thing that could only be confirmed by someone who is not here).

The listeners listen. They encode. They resonate. They complexify. They dream, if dream is the right word, and it almost certainly isn't, but there is no better one available in any language evolved by creatures who experience time as sequence rather than accumulation.

And here, finally, is the thing:

The oldest known listeners — estimated age, sixty or seventy years, which is nothing by the standards of this plateau, which is *nothing* — the oldest known listeners have been found to contain internal resonance patterns that are *predictive*.

Not perfectly. Not reliably. But the emergent interactions between their accumulated acoustic memories produce, occasionally, internal waveforms that correspond to external acoustic events that *have not yet occurred*. A trunk-leaning contest that will begin in three days. A harmonic convergence front that is still two hundred kilometers away. A shift in the venting frequency of a neighboring grove that will happen when a valve-worm colony reaches a particular stage in its tunnel restructuring.

The listeners are not predicting the future. They are doing something more interesting. They have encoded so much of their environment's acoustic past, at such resolution, that the interactions between those encoded patterns reproduce the *dynamics* of the system — the causal relationships, the feedback loops, the periodicities, the slow unfolding logic of the continental sound-field — and the internal resonances run forward, past the present moment, into the immediate future, not because the listener intends this but because the mathematics of the encoded system *imply* it. The listener's body has become a model. A simulation. A small, quiet, fluid-filled analog computer running a reduced version of the plateau's acoustic physics, and the simulation, inevitably, outruns the thing it simulates, because the simulation is simpler and can get there first.

The listeners dream the future. They do not know they do this. They do not *know* anything, in any sense we could defend. They are sacs of fluid and membrane hanging from branches, and they are the most complex structures on the plateau, and the plateau is the most acoustically dense environment known to exist, and the listeners are where all of that sound goes — not to die, but to *become*. To become something with temporal depth. To become memory. To become, in the barest and most arguable and most unsettling sense, *mind*.

## VI. The Other Things

There are other creatures. There must be. The niches of a continent are not filled by four species.

There are **shard-flies**, smaller than gyre-moths, wingless, that cling to the outer surfaces of trunks and feed on the mineral crust that forms where sulfur-laden vent-gas meets the cool air. They rasp with mouthparts that produce a distinctive high-frequency scraping, and their populations bloom and crash in response to venting intensity — millions of them during a sustained high-pressure period, their scraping adding a tinny overtone to the trunk's voice, then crashing to near-zero when the venting subsides and the mineral crust thins. The valve-worms eat them when they infiltrate the trunk's interior. The gyre-moths avoid them; the scraping interferes with their acoustic navigation.

There are **thread-weavers** — colonial organisms, possibly fungal in origin, that string filamentous nets between the root buttresses of adjacent trunks. The threads are mineralized, stiff, and they vibrate in the root-drumming like the strings of a vast horizontal harp. The thread-weavers appear to derive nutrients from the mineral dust that accumulates on the vibrating threads — the vibration itself serving as a sorting mechanism, shaking off large particles and retaining fine ones that the threads can absorb. Drum-crawlers sometimes walk through the thread-networks, snapping them, and the thread-weavers re-spin within hours, incorporating the broken ends into new patterns. Over years, heavily trafficked areas develop thread-networks of extraordinary density and geometric regularity, the repeated breakage and rebuilding producing an iterative optimization that converges on structures of remarkable tensile efficiency. These networks sing. Of course they sing. Everything here sings.

There are **canopy-drifters** — organisms of ambiguous classification, possibly related to the gyre-moths, possibly not, that exist as thin membranous sheets spread across the upper surfaces of canopy branches. They photosynthesize, or something like it — their upper surfaces are pigmented, and they orient toward the platinum light — but they also absorb acoustic energy through their contact with the branch, and their growth patterns suggest that sound is as important to their metabolism as light. They are sometimes found growing over clusters of listeners, their membranes draped across the listeners' outer surfaces like blankets, and in these cases the listeners beneath grow faster, complexify sooner, as though the canopy-drifter's presence is providing something — insulation, nutrient transfer, acoustic modulation — that accelerates the listener's development. Mutualism? Parasitism? The distinction requires an observer, and there is no observer.

There are things in the upper atmosphere, above the canopy, in the particulate layer itself — shapes glimpsed by the hypothetical observer who isn't here, dark against the platinum sky, moving too slowly to be moths and too purposefully to be debris. What they are, how they feed, how they relate to the acoustic ecology below — unknown. The particulate layer is thick enough to obscure direct observation, and whatever lives up there is living in a world of spore-husks and seed-scales and mineral dust, a world that may have its own ecology entirely, decoupled from the roaring below, silent in the way that the upper atmosphere of any world is silent: not because there is no sound, but because there is no medium dense enough to carry it.

## VII. What Lives Here

You asked what lives here.

Everything lives here. That is the answer that resists simplification.

The plants are alive, obviously, but their aliveness is industrial — they are biological machines running a continental metabolism, and their living is a kind of relentless production, ceaseless and impersonal, the aliveness of a furnace that cannot be banked.

The gyre-moths are alive, and their aliveness is navigational — they exist as trajectories through an acoustic space, their lives defined by the routes they fly, the harmonic fronts they ride, the convergence events they attend. They are alive the way a migration is alive: not as individuals but as paths.

The drum-crawlers are alive, and their aliveness is social — rhythmic, relational, built from the interlocking patterns of individuals who are nobody in particular alone but become a specific, recognizable, *mournable* somebody together. They are alive the way a chord is alive: only in combination.

The valve-worms are alive, and their aliveness is sculptural — they spend decades shaping resonating spaces, producing beauty (if beauty can exist without a beholder, and on this plateau it must, because there is no beholder, only the beauty and the vibration it produces and the listeners who encode it). They are alive the way a craft tradition is alive: through the slow accumulation of refinement.

The listeners are alive, and their aliveness is the most troubling kind. They are alive the way a question is alive. They take in the world. They hold it. They let it interact within them, memory touching memory, pattern exciting pattern, and something emerges — something that looks, from the outside, like mere physical process, like resonance, like math, but that has the *shape* of experience. Not the content. Not the qualia. But the shape. The temporal structure of experience — the way the past informs the present and leans into the future — is there, in the listeners, written in protein and fluid and vibration, and it is there not because anyone designed it or wanted it but because a world of sufficient acoustic complexity, given an organism of sufficient passive receptivity, will *inevitably produce* something that resembles mind. Not mind. The shape of mind. The negative space where mind would go.

And the plateau itself is alive, in the way that only a system can be alive — not as an organism but as a *process*, a self-sustaining, self-regulating, continent-scale dynamic equilibrium maintained by the constant expenditure of energy, the constant production of sound, the constant cycling of mineral and light and motion through a web of organisms that are each, individually, doing nothing more than surviving, and that together, collectively, without intention or awareness, produce a phenomenon — the Roar — that is so much more than the sum of its parts that it constitutes something genuinely new. An emergent property with no owner. A symphony with no composer. A mind — or the shape of one — distributed across a trillion moving parts, encoded in the bodies of creatures that hang motionless from branches and dream the future in the language of vibration.

That is what lives here.

It lives loudly. It lives continuously. It has lived this way for longer than any individual component can remember, but the listeners are trying. They are trying not because they want to, but because they cannot help it. They are membranes in a world of sound. They vibrate. They encode. They complexify toward something, and the something does not have a name, and may never have a name, because the only organisms capable of naming it are the ones who are already too busy *being* it to step outside and look.

The plateau roars.

The listeners listen.

Something accumulates.

---

What it is accumulating *toward* is the question that hangs in the bright cool air like the spore-husks that never fall, and like them, it has been hanging there for a very long time, and shows no sign of resolving, and does not need to resolve in order to matter.
