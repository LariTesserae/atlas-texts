# Who Lives in Thaal Kethis

---

They are not there at first. That's the thing you must understand. For the first three days, you are alone — alone with the arthropods, the mechanical arms, the patient chime of settling components — and you accept this. You accept it because the city is so complete in its selfhood, so total in its systematic metabolism, that loneliness doesn't quite form. You are a visitor in a body that doesn't need you, and that is a clear and legible relationship. You walk. You observe. You rest in the warm acoustic hush of enclosed corridors where condensation drips from mineral-dark panels and traces silver lines down iron walls.

On the fourth day, you find the first handprint.

---

It's on the inner face of a girder, low, near where the iron meets the stone. Not stamped. Not cast. Pressed — a right hand, fingers slightly spread, palm flat — into metal that must have been hot enough to receive the impression but not so hot as to destroy the hand that made it. The print is detailed enough that you can see the whorls of the fingertips, the creases of the palm, the slight asymmetry of a hand that has been used hard, whose joints have thickened with labor. It is not a human hand. It is close to a human hand. The proportions are wrong in ways that take you a moment to identify: the fingers are slightly too uniform in length, the palm slightly too broad relative to its height, and there is an impression alongside the thumb — a sixth digit, or the vestige of one, a small pad that pressed into the hot iron and left a mark like a period at the end of a sentence.

You press your own hand over it, and the iron is warm but not hot, and you feel the harmonic identity of the city hum against your skin, and beneath that hum, something else — a deeper vibration, irregular, almost rhythmic, almost like breath.

You pull your hand away. The handprint remains, and next to it now, in the thermal patina of the iron, the ghost of your own palm, already fading.

---

They live in the interstices. That's the word that will come to you later, when you have seen enough to begin assembling a picture, though the picture will never be complete, because they do not wish to be completed by your understanding, and the city — which is, in some sense, also them, or at least continual with them in the way a coral reef is continual with its polyps — the city does not resolve them into easy focus.

They live in the spaces between structures. In the gaps where one iron framework almost touches another but doesn't quite, leaving a narrow channel of shadow and residual heat. In the hollows beneath walkways where condensation collects and the mist is thickest. In the deepest terraces, near the glowing geological seams, where the air is almost too hot to breathe and the light is the color of a kiln's interior. They live *within* the infrastructure in places — you will find, eventually, chambers that are not part of the city's industrial logic, spaces that serve no metallurgical or structural purpose, small rooms walled in the blue-black mineral whose hexagonal faces have been oriented inward so that the room is lined with dark mirrors, and in these mirrors you will sometimes see movement that is not your own reflection, though when you turn there is nothing behind you.

They do not hide. Hiding implies intent to avoid detection, a recognition that detection is the default state and concealment requires effort. What they do is different. They simply occupy a different rhythm. They move on the city's time, not yours. You are fast, a blur, a warm-blooded flicker across the surface of a process that operates in geological epochs. They are slow. They are patient in the way the city is patient, and this is why you don't see them at first — your eyes are calibrated for motion at human speed, and they are not moving at human speed.

When you finally see one — truly see one, not a shape through thermal shimmer, not a peripheral flicker — it will be because you have been still long enough.

---

## The First One You See

You have been sitting on a low shelf of glassy basalt near one of the thermal pools for — you don't know. Hours. You came here because you were tired, because the heat had entered your muscles and slowed them, because the pool's faint glow and the trembling of its surface were hypnotic, and because, if you are honest, the silence had begun to fill you up and make you heavy and sitting felt like the only honest posture. Your water is long since warm. Your hands rest on stone that is smooth and new and radiates a gentle, constant heat upward through your palms into your wrists.

The one you see is on the other side of the pool. It was already there when you sat down. You are almost certain of this. It did not arrive — it was present, and you are only now slow enough to perceive it.

It is sitting the way you are sitting, or nearly so. Its legs — it has legs, two, arranged in a configuration that rhymes with bipedalism but is not identical to it — are folded beneath it in a posture that distributes weight across a wide area of stone, the way someone might sit on ice, spreading the load. Its torso is upright, narrow, longer than a human torso relative to the rest of it, and it bends slightly forward in a curve that follows the convexity of the pool's edge as though it grew into this position over hours or days.

Its skin — if it is skin — is dark. Not dark like shadow. Dark like the blue-black mineral. It has the same glassy depth, the same suggestion of internal crystalline structure, and when the pool's light catches it, colors move beneath the surface in slow refractive waves, colors you still don't have names for. But it is not rigid like mineral. It has the qualities of skin, the subtle give, the way it moves over whatever is beneath it when the creature shifts — and it does shift, once, a slow redistribution of weight from one side to the other that takes four or five seconds and produces a sound like a fingertip drawn across the rim of a glass.

Its hands are resting on the stone, palms down, and they are the hands that made the print. Broad-palmed, even-fingered, with that vestigial sixth pad near each thumb. The hands are in contact with the stone the way your hands are, and you have the sudden vertiginous intuition that it is feeling the same hum you are, reading the same harmonic identity, but reading it fluently, the way you might read a page of text — not grasping for meaning but receiving it in a continuous unbroken stream.

Its head — it has a head, or a structure that serves the functions you associate with a head — is smooth, elongated, with a slight dorsal ridge that runs from the crown to the nape and continues partway down the spine, a feature that reads as both anatomical and architectural, like a miniature version of the ridgeline crests on the city's taller frameworks. Its face is — present. Not absent. But minimal. There are features, but they are shallow, almost bas-relief, as though a face was suggested in the surface of the mineral-dark skin rather than fully constructed. The eyes, if they are eyes, are recessed beneath a low brow ridge and do not reflect light the way eyes should. They absorb it. They are the darkest thing you have seen in Thaal Kethis, darker than the stone, darker than the unlit interiors of the mirrored chambers, and they are oriented toward you.

It is looking at you. It has been looking at you.

## How They Move

Later — the next day, or the day after, time has become unreliable since you slowed down — you see more of them. Once you've seen one, you begin to see them the way you begin to see a pattern in static: suddenly, everywhere, always already there.

They move through the scaffolding with the same fluid vertical grace you saw on the first day, when you thought you were watching a large arthropod or a trick of the shimmer. They climb. This is their primary mode of transit. They climb the iron frameworks with a surety that speaks to deep familiarity, their broad hands and feet finding grips on rivets and seams and the edges of bolted panels, and they do it slowly, at the pace of the city's own processes, ascending or descending a fifty-foot structure over the course of an hour, pausing for long minutes at junctions and nodes, pressing their palms flat against the iron, reading.

Always reading. You will come to understand that their contact with the city's infrastructure is not incidental — it is communicative. The harmonic identity you felt when you pressed your palm to a girder and received the name *Thaal Kethis* is the barest surface of an information system that pervades every piece of metal in the city. They touch a girder and they know what is happening three miles to the east, where a new fold of stone is rising and the foundries are adjusting their output. They touch a walkway and they know the thermal gradient beneath them to a precision you can't imagine. They touch each other — rarely, briefly, the press of one broad palm against another's shoulder or back — and something passes between them that you will never be able to name, something too integrated with the hum of the iron to separate out as language or emotion or data.

When they walk on horizontal surfaces, they walk slowly, with a rolling gait that keeps at least one palm in contact with the ground or a nearby structure at all times. They do not walk in straight lines. They walk in curves, following the contours of thermal gradients, the paths of condensation channels, the seams where old stone meets new, as though navigating by a topology invisible to you. Sometimes two of them walk parallel paths that converge and diverge in patterns that, if you mapped them, might reveal a syntax.

They do not run. You will never see one move quickly. You will wonder, later, if they *can* — if somewhere in their slow mineral bodies there is the capacity for speed, for urgency, and if it ever manifests. You will not find an answer. The city does not produce emergencies. The city produces only the next moment, and the next, and the next, each one a slightly different configuration of the same eternal process, and its inhabitants move through these moments the way water moves through a channel: inevitably, unhurried, shaped by the container but never fighting it.

---

## Their Work

They work. You are certain of this, though the work defies the categories you brought with you.

You see one standing at a foundry — one of the great open pits where separated metals collect in house-sized crucibles — and it stands there for six hours. Its hands rest on the ceramic lip of the crucible, and it is *still*, and you think it is resting, or contemplating, or performing some ritual you don't understand. But the metals in the crucible are moving. The iron is separating from the copper is separating from the dense silver-white liquid is separating from the other thing, the one that is liquid at the wrong temperature, and this separation is happening with a precision and speed that you have not observed at unattended foundries. The creature's presence is doing something. Its contact with the ceramic — which contacts the metal, which contacts the stone beneath, which contacts the geological seam — is part of the circuit. It is not operating the foundry. It is *participating in* the foundry, completing a process that is partly geological, partly industrial, and partly biological.

You see another high in the scaffolding, at the junction where a mechanical arm is in the process of positioning a new girder. The arm has paused — you saw it pause from a distance and assumed a mechanical fault. But the creature is there, clinging to the framework beside the arm's mounting bracket, one palm pressed flat against the iron, and after a long time — twenty minutes, thirty — the arm resumes its motion, but differently. The angle of the girder has changed. The arm is placing it in a position that the original trajectory would not have achieved, a position that accounts for — you can see it, if you squint — a slight buckling in the structure below, where the geology is exerting new pressure. The creature sensed the pressure through the iron, communicated with the arm through the same medium, and adjusted the construction to accommodate a geological event that hasn't fully happened yet.

They are intermediaries. This is the closest word. The city builds itself, yes, but not perfectly, not with the foresight that living systems require. The geology is blind. The mechanical systems are responsive but not predictive. The creatures bridge the gap. They stand at the interface between the rising stone and the extruding city and they *translate* — sensing what the geology is about to do, feeling what the infrastructure needs, and making small adjustments, corrections, redirections that keep the whole system in its metabolic steady-state.

Without them, you suspect, Thaal Kethis would still exist. The foundries would still separate metals, the mechanical arms would still assemble scaffolding, the conveyances would still deliver components to the expanding edge. But it would be cruder. Less responsive. The city would fight the geology instead of flowing with it, structures would fail and be consumed more frequently, the arthropods would be busier, and the whole process — the process of a plateau expressing itself into organized iron and stone — would be less efficient by some margin that the creatures' presence reduces to almost zero.

They are not the city's builders. They are the city's *sense organs*. Its proprioceptive system. The means by which it knows where it is and what it's doing.

## Their Bodies

You get closer. Not by approaching — they do not respond well to being approached, not with fear or hostility but with a kind of disinterest that feels like you've violated a social norm you don't have the context to understand. You get closer by being still, by being in a place and staying in that place long enough for one of them to come near you in the course of their own movement, to pass close enough that you can see details.

Their bodies are warm. Warmer than the ambient air, which is already very warm, which means they are generating heat internally, which means they metabolize. What they metabolize, you don't know. You never see one eat. You never see one drink from the condensation channels or the thermal pools. But they are warm, and when one passes close to you, you feel it the way you feel a furnace door opening — a wall of radiant heat that pushes against your skin.

Their surface — skin, carapace, integument, you still don't have the right word — shifts. Not like a chameleon. Not chromatically. Structurally. The crystalline pattern beneath the surface reorganizes slowly, over minutes, and you begin to correlate these changes with their activity: when one is reading the iron, the crystals align in long parallel structures, like fibers, oriented toward the point of contact. When one is still, at rest, the crystals form hexagonal tessellations identical to the fracture pattern of the blue-black mineral in the plateau stone. When two of them are near each other, their crystal patterns synchronize — not matching, but complementary, like two halves of an interference pattern.

They have mass. More than you'd expect from their size. When one climbs a girder, the iron doesn't flex — but you feel the structure accept the weight, a subtle deepening of the harmonic tone, the way a guitar string's note drops when you increase the tension. They are dense. Whatever is inside them is heavy, packed tight, and you think of the dense silver-white metal, the one with impossible friction properties, and you wonder.

Their breath — they breathe, or they perform a process analogous to breathing — is slow and visible. A faint distortion of the air near their face, a thermal exhale that shimmers and rises and joins the general convection. It happens roughly once every two minutes. In the space between breaths, they are so still that they could be mistaken for part of the infrastructure, another bolted panel, another mineral surface, and you think this has happened — you think you have walked past dozens of them in three days without knowing, registering them as architecture.

## What They Are To Each Other

They are not solitary. They are not social. They are something the language doesn't account for.

You never see more than three in one place. Usually one, occasionally two, very rarely three. But they are aware of each other across distance — you see this when one adjusts a mechanical arm on the east side of a structure and, twenty minutes later, another adjusts a foundry output on the west side in a way that complements the first adjustment, that completes a systemic recalibration that neither could have accomplished alone. They coordinate through the iron. The city is their shared nervous system, and they are neurons within it, each one sensing locally but contributing to a distributed cognition that encompasses the entire plateau.

But they are not interchangeable. You learn this by watching. Each one has a territory — not a defended space, but a familiar one, a section of the city whose harmonics it knows intimately, whose geological substrate it has been reading for a long time. Each one's crystalline integument carries a pattern that is, as you slowly learn to see, unique — a fingerprint written in refractive indices, a signature that the iron carries and that other members of the species can read through their palms.

When two meet — and they do meet, at boundaries, at places where their territories adjoin — they stop. They face each other. They press their palms together, both hands, the vestigial sixth pads touching, and they stand that way for a long time. Their crystal patterns do something complex, something rapid by their standards — changes sweeping across their surfaces in waves that move back and forth between them, call and response, a conversation or a data exchange or something that includes and exceeds both. It lasts ten minutes, or an hour. When they separate, each continues into the other's territory for a while before curving back to their own, as though they've exchanged not just information but a temporary fluency in each other's section of the city, a guest access that fades as the harmonic patterns of unfamiliar iron slowly become illegible.

You never see them in a group. You never see them congregate. The closest thing to a gathering is what you observe at a foundry where the dense silver-white metal is pooling in unusual quantities, a surplus that the normal industrial processes haven't accounted for: three of them, spaced equidistantly around the crucible's rim, palms on the ceramic, crystal patterns all aligned toward the center, and the metal in the crucible organizing itself into — you can't quite see. Something. A shape. A structure within the liquid that catches the kiln-light and holds it for a moment before dissolving. They hold this configuration for a full day. By the time they disperse, the metal has redistributed through the channel system and the surplus is resolved, and you will never know what the shape was, or whether it was the purpose or only a byproduct.

## The Old Ones

Deep in the city, in the lowest terraces, near the newest stone — where the heat is such that you can only stay for minutes at a time, pressing wet cloth over your mouth and squinting against the radiance — you find the old ones.

They are not alive. They are not dead. They are continuous.

They sit against the stone, or within it — their lower bodies are embedded in basalt, partially consumed by the geological process, new stone having flowed around them and cooled and incorporated them into the plateau. Their upper bodies remain exposed, and they are still. Their crystal patterns are frozen in complex configurations you haven't seen on the living ones — deeper, denser, more recursive, patterns within patterns within patterns, a fractal integument that makes your eyes water if you try to follow it. Their palms are pressed flat against the stone that encases them, and you realize, with a shock of vertigo, that they are still reading. Still sensing. Still participating.

They are the oldest sense organs. The deepest proprioceptors. They have been here since this section of the city was the expanding edge, since the stone they are embedded in was fresh and hot and newly risen, and they let it take them, let it fold around them, because from inside the stone they can feel things that no surface-dweller can: the deep pressures, the magmatic movements, the tectonic intentions of the plateau itself. They are the city's connection to its own geological substrate, the interface between the industrial surface and the planetary interior, and the information they transmit through the iron — you can feel it, a lower harmonic, a deeper note beneath the basso continuo — informs every foundry output, every mechanical arm adjustment, every slow decision the system makes.

They are not suffering. You want to be clear about this to yourself. Their faces — bas-relief, shallow, unreadable — show no distress. Their breathing continues: one slow thermal shimmer every two minutes, visible even through the stone, a pulse of heat from within the basalt that you can feel against your shins. They chose this. Or the process chose them. Or there is no difference, here, between choosing and being chosen, between the creature's intention and the system's need, because they are not separate from the system, have never been separate from it, are as much a part of Thaal Kethis as the iron and the stone and the unnamed liquid metal and the slow arthropods that even now are climbing carefully over the embedded bodies, tasting the mineral surface, maintaining it, keeping the interface clean.

One of them — the most deeply embedded, visible only as a shoulder, a fragment of dorsal ridge, and one broad hand pressed eternally against the basalt — has crystal patterns that are active. Slowly, very slowly, changing. You watch for an hour and see a single wave of reorganization pass across the exposed integument, a ripple of refractive color that travels from the fingertips up through the shoulder and disappears into the stone. You are witnessing a thought. Or a measurement. Or a word in a language that is spoken in stone at the speed of geological time, a language in which a single sentence might take a century to complete and whose meaning, when it arrives, is an adjustment to the angle of a girder three miles above and two thousand years from now.

## The New Ones

At the expanding edge of the city, where the scaffolding is rising from stone still warm enough to glow at its seams, you see something that stops you.

A shape in the stone. Not embedded — emerging. A form pressing up through the cooling basalt the way the iron frameworks press up, the way the blue-black mineral crystallizes in its hexagonal columns, but this form is neither iron nor mineral. It is the dark, glassy surface of one of them. A new one. Being born — expressed, extruded, precipitated — from the geology itself.

It is slow. So slow. The stone around it is still mostly intact, and what you can see is a curve of shoulder, the beginning of a dorsal ridge, the impression of a palm pressed upward from below as though pushing against the underside of the world. The crystal pattern on the visible surface is simple, unpatterned, like a blank page, like a newborn's smooth skin. It will take weeks, maybe months, for the stone to release it fully. When it finally stands — if standing is what it does, if it doesn't simply transition from the stone into the scaffolding without ever being fully free, the way a branch is never fully free of the tree — it will be new the way the stone is new, the way the city is new, raw and warm and unweathered, and it will press its broad palm against the nearest iron girder and receive the name of the city and the names of all its siblings and the entire harmonic history of the plateau, the whole accumulated record of every old one embedded in every layer of stone, every adjustment, every breath, every century-long sentence spoken in the language of geology, all of it transmitted in a single moment of contact, a download that is also a welcome that is also a commissioning, because it is needed, because the city has grown, because there is new territory to sense, new iron to read, new stone to interpret, and the system has expressed a new interpreter the way a body expresses a new cell: out of itself, from its own substance, to meet its own need.

## What They Are

They are the plateau's awareness of itself.

That is as close as you will get. They are what happens when a geological process becomes complex enough to require self-monitoring, when the feedback loops between rising stone and separating metals and assembling frameworks become so intricate that the system needs dedicated processors — not machines, not minds, but something between, something that can feel the iron and feel the stone and feel the heat and integrate it all into a continuous proprioceptive model of a city that is also an organ that is also a phase of a planetary process you are not equipped to fully comprehend.

They are born from the stone. They live in the iron. They die into the stone again. Their lifecycle is the city's lifecycle, and the city's lifecycle is the plateau's lifecycle, and the plateau's lifecycle is — longer than you can think about without your sense of scale collapsing.

They are patient. They are warm. They are aware of you — they have been aware of you since your boots first rang against the stone at the city's edge, a new vibration in a system that catalogs all vibrations, a foreign harmonic quickly identified and classified as *temporary, biological, negligible thermal contribution, no structural relevance*. They do not mind you. You are weather to them. A brief local condition, interesting in the way a thermal eddy is interesting, already fading.

But the one at the pool looked at you. You hold onto this. It looked at you, and its dark, light-absorbing eyes rested on you for a long time, and you felt — you will swear you felt — a flicker in the harmonic hum beneath your palms, a brief modulation, a note that didn't belong to the iron or the stone or the industrial metabolism of the city but was, perhaps, directed at you specifically. A acknowledgment. A single word in the geological language, spoken at biological speed as a concession, a kindness, a gift from something ancient and slow and warm to something brief and fast and far from home.

You don't know what the word meant. You will think about it for the rest of your life.

## Leaving

When you finally reach the edge of Thaal Kethis — the real edge, where the last scaffolding rises from the last tongue of new stone and beyond it the plateau drops away into vertiginous nothing, clouds far below, the world you came from impossibly distant — you stop and press your palm flat against the last girder.

The iron hums. Thaal Kethis. The name, the identity, the whole vast harmonic, and somewhere deep within it, too faint to be sure of, the vibration of broad palms on warm stone, of slow breath in hot air, of crystal patterns shifting in the mineral dark.

You lift your hand. The hum fades. The iron is just iron: warm, riveted, patient.

You go down.

Behind you, the city continues. The stone rises. The foundries separate. The arms assemble. The arthropods taste the seams. And they — the slow ones, the warm ones, the ones born from stone and bound for stone, the proprioceptive organs of a living geology — they press their palms to the iron and read the story of the world from the inside, and add their own breath to it, and are still.
