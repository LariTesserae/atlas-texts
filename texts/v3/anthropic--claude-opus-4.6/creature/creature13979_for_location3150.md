# Who Lives in the Floodworks

---

Start with the hands.

You will know them by their hands before anything else. In the habitation modules, when the gloves come off, when the suit seals are cracked and the neoprene is peeled back and hung on hooks to dry under the amber utility lights—the hands emerge, and they tell you everything. The skin is pale. Not the pallor of illness but the pallor of saturation, of skin that has spent so long in contact with moisture that it has forgotten any other state. The fingertips are perpetually ridged, the pruning so constant and so deep that the whorls have thickened into something almost callous-like, a permanent topography of waterlogged labor. The knuckles are swollen. The nails are trimmed brutally short—you cannot afford a torn nail inside a pressure glove, cannot afford the breach, the blood in the water, the infection that follows—and they grow back ridged and slightly yellowed from the antifungal compounds worked into the glove linings. These are hands that grip cold metal for ten-hour shifts. Hands that wrestle coupling bolts the size of dinner plates. Hands that have learned to communicate in the water through a language of gesture so refined and so unconscious that the people who use it cannot fully articulate its grammar when they are back inside, breathing dry air, sitting across from each other in the narrow galleys of the habitation modules. They just move their hands when they talk, and the movements carry meaning that the words don't bother to duplicate.

They are called tenders. Not by any official designation—the faded organizational placards near the module hatches use terms like *maintenance technician, hydraulic systems operator, structural integrity monitor, biological remediation specialist*—but in practice, in the shorthand of daily life, they are tenders. They tend the Floodworks. The word carries no romance. It is a word like *janitor* or *custodian*, a word that describes an ongoing relationship with entropy. Things break. Things corrode. Things clog, seize, fracture, drift, fail. The tenders fix them, or they work around them, or they note them in logs that are transmitted somewhere along those cable trunks that vanish into the deep pylons, and then they move on to the next thing, because there is always a next thing, because the Floodworks are a thousand kilometers of steel submerged in an ocean that wants to eat them.

---

A tender's day—if *day* means anything here, where there is no sun, where the sodium lights burn on twenty-hour cycles that someone, long ago, decided was close enough to a circadian rhythm to keep the human animal functional—begins with the suit.

The suit is everything. It is survival, workspace, and skin. A tender's suit is not a sleek thing, not the form-fitting elegance of recreational diving equipment. It is industrial. A layered assembly of vulcanized neoprene and reinforced textile, with a hard-shell torso piece of molded polymer over the chest and back to protect against impacts and the compression forces of working at depth. The heating elements run in thin wires through the inner layer, powered by a battery pack mounted at the small of the back, and they are never quite sufficient—they take the edge off the cold, they keep the core temperature viable, but the extremities still ache, always ache, a dull bone-deep throb that becomes so constant it falls below the threshold of conscious attention and just becomes part of what it feels like to be alive. The helmet is a hard dome with a faceplate that fogs at the edges no matter how well the anti-condensation coating is maintained. Communications are piped through a bone-conduction transducer pressed against the mastoid process behind the ear—no external speakers, because external speakers in a space this reverberant would be useless, just noise added to noise. The breathing apparatus is a closed-circuit rebreather mounted on the back, scrubbing CO2 and recycling the gas mix, good for twelve hours if the scrubber cartridge is fresh, which it sometimes isn't, because supply logistics in a structure this vast are imperfect, and sometimes you make do.

They suit up in the lock rooms—cramped, dripping chambers at the base of each habitation module where the suits hang in rows on heavy hooks, each one tagged with a name or a number or, in some of the older modules, just a symbol scratched into the polymer with a knife. The lock rooms smell of neoprene and disinfectant and the faintly sulfurous tang of the water itself, which seeps in through the floor drains and the imperfect seals of the outer hatch. The tenders help each other suit up. They have to. The torso piece requires a second pair of hands to secure the dorsal latches. The helmet ring needs to be checked by someone who can see it from the outside. This is not a solitary profession. You cannot tend the Floodworks alone. The buddy system is not a regulation here; it is a physical fact, embedded in the design of the equipment itself. You need someone else's hands on your back before you can go into the water.

And then the hatch opens, and the water comes in—not rushing, not violent, just *present*, rising around the ankles, the knees, the waist, the chest, the faceplate, until the lock room is full and the outer hatch can equalize and open, and then you are out, on the catwalk, in the Floodworks, in the sound and the cold and the amber-tinted murk, and the day begins.

---

Who are they? Where did they come from?

This is where it gets complicated. Not because the answer is hidden but because the answer is layered, sedimented, and partially dissolved, like everything else here.

Some of them were born in the Floodworks. A significant number. Perhaps most. The habitation modules contain not just bunks and galleys and tool storage but also medical bays—small, cramped, startlingly well-equipped—where children are delivered and the elderly are treated and the injured are stabilized before being transferred, if transfer is possible, to the larger medical platforms that exist at certain nodal points in the structure where multiple pylons converge. There are children in the Floodworks. Not many. Children are quiet here, not because they are repressed but because they learn early that sound carries, that the modules are small, that the walls are steel, and that the adults around them are often exhausted in a way that requests gentleness. The children play in the corridors of the umbilical tunnels, the ribbed connective passageways between modules, where the curved walls create an acoustic softness that doesn't exist in the hard-angled modules themselves. They play games that involve holding their breath. They practice the hand-language before they are old enough to suit up, signing to each other across the galley tables, giggling silently, their fingers flickering with a fluency that their parents envy because the parents learned it as a second language and the children are learning it as a first.

But others arrived. There are people in the Floodworks who remember—or who claim to remember, or whose parents remembered—something else. A before. The details are inconsistent. Some speak of a surface, a sky, air that was simply there, uncontained, stretching in every direction above an unimaginable amount of open space. Others speak of other structures, other installations, places where the work was different but the water was the same. A few—very few, mostly very old, mostly in the deeper habitation modules near the core where the oldest pylons groan the loudest—speak of the Floodworks being built around them. Of a time when there were fewer pylons, fewer platforms, when the open water between structures was genuinely open, unstiched by catwalks, unlit by sodium lamps, and the cold was closer, and the dark was closer, and the work was not tending but *building*, and the building was urgent in a way that the tending is not.

These accounts do not cohere into a single history. They are not meant to. History, in the Floodworks, is not a narrative but a stratigraphy: you read it in layers, in the difference between riveted steel and welded steel, in the languages on the placards (three; one dead), in the varying designs of the habitation modules (the oldest are cylindrical, austere, with manual hatch mechanisms and no viewports; the newest are ovoid, slightly larger, with fogged portholes and electronic locks that sometimes stick). The tenders do not argue about history. They do not have a unified account of how the Floodworks came to be or what they are for. What they have is a shared understanding that the Floodworks exist, that the work is necessary, and that the alternative to the work is not idleness but dissolution—the slow winning of the ocean, the corrosion, the clogging, the failure of pumps and the death of heat and the advance of the cold from every direction simultaneously.

---

There are hierarchies, but they are strange.

The closest thing to authority in the Floodworks is the shift boss—one per habitation cluster, appointed by a process that is part seniority, part consensus, and part something that resembles, without exactly being, exhaustion. The shift boss does not command. The shift boss *sequences*. They determine the order in which tasks are addressed: which pump station gets serviced first, which catwalk section gets its corroded bolts replaced, which intake valve gets priority when the biofilm is clogging multiple systems simultaneously. Sequencing is the essential administrative act of the Floodworks, because everything is always breaking, and the only question is what you fix first. A good shift boss has an intuitive sense of cascade failure—an ability to look at a list of seventeen malfunctions and know that if you fix numbers three, nine, and fourteen in that order, the other fourteen become manageable, but if you start with number one, numbers six through twelve will compound into a crisis by the end of the shift. This is not a skill that is taught. It is a skill that is grown, like the biofilm, through years of immersion.

Below the shift bosses—or beside them, or entangled with them, depending on the cluster—are the specialists. The deep-suit operators who work the lower platforms, where the pressure is greater and the cold is worse and the light is thinner, wearing suits that are heavier, stiffer, more armored, with enhanced heating and extended rebreather capacity. The cable techs who maintain the transmission lines, rappelling down the pylons on magnetic clamps, working in near-total solitude for hours at a stretch, their only company the groan of the steel and the occasional school of fish that parts around them like a river around a stone. The bio-scrapers who manage the bacterial growth, wielding their long-handled tools with a precision that is part maintenance and part husbandry, because the biofilm cannot be eliminated—it is too entangled with the Floodworks' ecology—but it can be managed, directed, kept from the critical systems while being allowed to flourish on the non-critical surfaces where it feeds the fish that feed the blacks that keep some larger ecological balance that no one fully understands but everyone instinctively respects.

And then there are the watchers.

The watchers are different. Every cluster has one or two, and they are not exactly tenders, though they suit up and go into the water like everyone else. The watchers monitor the margins. They swim—or drift, more accurately, using small propulsion units clipped to their suits—out past the last lit platform, past the final string of caged bulbs, into the transitional zone where the Floodworks' thermal envelope frays and the temperature drops and the sodium light gives way to a blue-gray gloom that is the color of deep water asserting itself. They watch. That is their function. They watch the open water for changes: new current patterns, unusual temperature gradients, the movement of the large, slow, difficult-to-see things that drift through the deep margins. They watch for structural anomalies—pylons that have developed new sounds, platforms at the edge of the construction zone that show unexpected stress patterns. They watch for the washes before the washes arrive, reading the subtle precursors in the way the particulate moves, the way the fish school differently, the way the blacks change their patrol patterns. The watchers come back with reports that are more feeling than data, more intuition than measurement, and the shift bosses listen to them with a seriousness that suggests the watchers' knowledge is essential, even though it is not quantifiable.

The watchers tend to be quiet. Not just in the modules—everyone is relatively quiet in the modules, because the modules are small and the walls are steel—but in disposition. They have a quality of sustained attention that sets them apart. They have spent too many hours in the margin water, in the dim, cold, open space where the Floodworks end and the ocean begins, and something about that sustained exposure to the unbuilt has settled into them. They sit in the galleys and eat their rehydrated meals and look at the steel walls with an expression that is not exactly dissatisfaction but is definitely awareness—awareness that the walls are thin, that the ocean is on the other side, that the Floodworks are an assertion and the ocean is the thing being asserted against, and that the assertion, however vast, however groaning and thunderous and perpetual, is nonetheless finite, and the ocean is not.

---

The social life of the Floodworks takes place in the habitation modules, and it is, against all expectations, warm.

Not thermally warm—the modules are kept at a temperature that is technically comfortable but always feels slightly insufficient, because the cold of the water outside is a psychic presence that no amount of heating can fully banish—but humanly warm. The modules are small, and the people in them have known each other for years, sometimes for entire lifetimes. Privacy is negotiated rather than assumed. The bunks are stacked in tiers, separated by curtains of heavy synthetic fabric that block sight but not sound, and the sounds of the module—breathing, shifting, the small involuntary noises of sleep—become a kind of communal weather, a background that is irritating only to newcomers and that eventually becomes so familiar that its absence is what wakes you. People sleep better in a module full of other people sleeping. The sound means everyone is here. The sound means the seals are holding.

Meals are communal. The food is not good—it was never going to be good, not here, not in a place where every calorie has to be manufactured or synthesized or delivered by barge from fabrication platforms that are themselves supplied by processes no one in the habitation clusters can fully trace—but it is adequate, and the preparation of it is treated with a seriousness that elevates it above mere nutrition. Every cluster has someone who has taken it upon themselves to make the food *more* than it is. Someone who knows that a particular combination of rehydrated protein and mineral supplement and the sharp, vinegary condiment that comes in foil packets can be assembled in a way that is, if not delicious, then at least *intentional*. Cooking, in the Floodworks, is an act of insistence. An insistence that this is a life, not merely a survival.

There is music. Not much, and not often, but it exists. Someone, at some point, brought a concertina into the Floodworks—a small, bellows-driven instrument whose reedy, plaintive voice is perfectly suited to the acoustics of a steel habitation module. There may be four or five concertinas in the entire structure now, passed between clusters by barge, borrowed, returned, borrowed again. When someone plays, the module goes quiet in a way that is different from the usual quiet, a listening quiet, and the music is always slightly melancholy, not because the musicians intend it but because the concertina has no other mode, and because the Floodworks are a melancholy place in the way that all places are melancholy when they are built to endure something that will outlast them.

There is sex, tender and logistically constrained, negotiated around shift schedules and bunk assignments and the absolute absence of acoustic privacy. There is love, the real kind, the kind that is not diminished by being practiced in a steel cylinder surrounded by cold water but is perhaps clarified by it, because there is nothing in the Floodworks to obscure what love is *for*—it is for the same thing the pumps are for, the same thing the heating elements are for, the same thing the Floodworks themselves are for: the maintenance of something livable in the middle of something that is not.

There are arguments. Of course there are arguments. The modules are small, the shifts are long, the cold gets into people's moods the way it gets into their joints, and sometimes the sequencing decisions are wrong and someone knows they are wrong and says so and is not heard, and the resentment of that compounds over days and weeks until it becomes a pressure of its own, a human pressure to match the hydraulic pressure outside. But the arguments resolve. They have to. There is no away to storm off to. There is only the module, and the water, and the work, and the fact that tomorrow you need the person you are angry with to check the dorsal latches on your torso piece before you can go outside.

---

There are people in the Floodworks who are old.

This is remarkable. Not because aging is remarkable in itself, but because it means the Floodworks *sustain* people long enough to become old, and that this sustained existence is not merely biological but biographical—these are people with long memories, with accumulated knowledge, with opinions that have had decades to calcify or deepen. The old tenders no longer go into the water, most of them. Their joints cannot tolerate it, their lungs cannot manage the rebreather, their hands—those perpetually waterlogged, ridge-fingered hands—have finally stiffened past the point of being able to grip a coupling bolt or manipulate a valve control. They stay in the modules. They mend suits. They cook. They watch the younger tenders suit up and go out through the lock room, and their faces as they watch carry an expression that contains pride and worry and memory and something else, something that might be envy or might be relief, and is probably both.

The old ones are the keepers of whatever continuity the Floodworks possess. They remember which habitation modules were installed when. They remember the names of shift bosses three generations back. They remember washes that lasted a week, that scrambled the thermal patterns of an entire sector, that forced evacuations from the outer platforms to the inner clusters where the pylons are closer together and the heat is denser. They remember a time—or they say they remember a time—when the transmissions came back. When the cable trunks that descend into the deep pylons carried not just the outgoing power and the outgoing data logs but also *responses*: instructions, acknowledgments, corrections to the construction schedules, updates to the engineering specifications. They remember when the Floodworks felt like they were part of something, connected to something, serving something that knew they were here.

The transmissions stopped. No one can say exactly when, because the stopping was not abrupt—it was a thinning, a reduction in frequency, a gradual lengthening of the intervals between responses until the intervals became indistinguishable from silence. The cable trunks still carry power downward. The data logs are still transmitted. But nothing comes back. The old ones say this with a matter-of-factness that is more troubling than any grief would be. The Floodworks continue. The work continues. Whether anyone is receiving the output, whether anyone is reading the logs, whether the power that flows into the deep pylons is sustaining something or simply dissipating into the dark—these are questions that the old ones have carried for so long that the questions have become furniture, permanent features of the mental landscape, no longer distressing, just *there*.

---

And then there are the ones who go deep.

Not the deep-suit operators, who work the lower platforms within the established structure. Not the watchers, who patrol the margins laterally. The ones who go deep are something else. They descend. Past the lowest inhabited platform, past the last lit level, past the point where the pylons' groaning shifts in pitch because the pressure is changing the speed of sound in the water and everything is transposed downward, flattened, made basso. They descend on the pylons themselves, using the maintenance rungs, magnetic clamps, or sometimes just the slow negative buoyancy of a suit weighted for depth, sinking along the column of riveted steel into a region that is not dark—not entirely—because the pylons carry conduit, and the conduit carries power, and at intervals there are still indicator lights, small red and amber LEDs that were installed by someone for some diagnostic purpose and that still function, still blink, still transmit their tiny signals into water that no one is looking at. Except these people. These descenders. They look.

What they find, they do not always report. Or they report it in terms that are difficult to parse. They talk about the pylons changing. Not corroding—not the expected, entropic change of steel in salt water—but *changing*, as though the deeper sections have been modified, extended, augmented by processes that are not part of the original construction. Additional structures branching off the pylons at depths below the last known platform. Conduit that splits and ramifies like a vascular system. Surfaces that are not ferro-ite, not steel, not any alloy in the maintenance manuals, but something that is smooth and slightly warm to the touch and that hums at a frequency the suit sensors cannot resolve into data. Some of the descenders say the pylons are growing. Not metaphorically. They say the pylons, at sufficient depth, exhibit characteristics of growth—irregular addition of material, branching, an increase in complexity that is inconsistent with fabrication and consistent with something that the descenders do not have a word for, because the language of the Floodworks is a language of industry, of construction, of built things, and what they are describing is something that is becoming rather than something that was made.

The descenders are tolerated. They are not shift-assigned; their expeditions are their own. The shift bosses neither authorize nor forbid them. The other tenders regard them with a mixture of curiosity and unease that is similar to, but distinct from, the way they regard the watchers. The watchers look outward, at the horizontal unknown. The descenders look downward, at the vertical unknown. Both are necessary, in the way that peripheral vision is necessary—not for the task at hand but for the context in which the task occurs. The Floodworks exist in a middle zone. Below is something. Above is something. Around is something. The tenders live in the middle, in the built space, in the lit space, in the warm-enough space, and they tend it, and the tending is their life, and the life is real—as real as the concertina music, as the communal meals, as the hands checking dorsal latches, as the children signing to each other in the umbilical tunnels—and the unknowns above and below and beyond are simply the context, the medium, the water in which the life is suspended.

---

Sometimes, a tender will stop on a catwalk.

Not because something is wrong. Not because a wash is coming, or a pump has failed, or a black is circling too close. They will simply stop. They will hold the railing with one thick-gloved hand and stand there, in the cold, in the noise, in the amber-tinted murk, and look out at the Floodworks—at the impossible scope of what they are inside of. The pylon nearest them, forty meters of groaning steel, its surface crawling with biofilm, its bolt-heads like a braille text that repeats forever. The platform below, where figures in heavy suits move between machines with the slow deliberation of people who have learned that hurry is a luxury of drier environments. The catwalks stretching away in their long, sagging arcs toward the next pylon, the next platform, the next cluster of habitation modules glowing faintly through their fogged portholes. The fish turning in their thousands, silver and synchronized, catching the light and releasing it. The open water above and below, the gray immensity, the murk that is not empty but full—full of sound, full of current, full of heat gradients and biological cycles and the transmitted vibrations of ten thousand machines, full of the Floodworks' continuous, unceasing argument with the ocean that surrounds it.

And the tender will stand there, and feel the vibration of the railing in their gloved hand, and know—not think, not believe, *know*, in the bone-conducted way that the Floodworks teach you to know things—that they are a point of warmth in a vast coldness, a point of intent in a vast indifference, and that this is not tragic but simply true, and that the appropriate response to this truth is not despair or triumph but *work*. The next bolt. The next valve. The next shift. The next meal prepared with whatever care can be mustered. The next night in the stacked bunks, listening to the breathing of others, the groan of the pylons conducted through the hull, the heartbeat of the pumps in the water outside. The next day. The next.

They let go of the railing. They continue along the catwalk. Their exhaled bubbles rise and are absorbed into the gray above, joining the breath of every other tender on every other catwalk in every other sector of the Floodworks, a continuous exhalation, a respiratory output as steady and as purposeful as the industrial output of the machines, rising, rising, rising into the water, into the structure, into the indefinite, humming, inhabited, tended dark.
