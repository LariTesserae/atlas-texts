# Who Lives on the Canopy Plateau

---

She does. Though "lives" is a word she would pause over, turning it in her mouth like a pebble, weighing whether it applies.

She has been here for what she would estimate, if pressed, as four years. She does not track time with any precision. The plateau has no seasons — or rather, it has one continuous season, a perpetual saturated spring, the moss always growing, the condensation never stopping, the light always that same ferocious white blaze whenever the sun is up and then a brief, grey-violet twilight that lasts an hour before full dark. She knows roughly how many darks she has experienced. She stopped counting them individually around the time the number became a kind of weight she didn't want to carry. Now she counts in clusters, in phases: the time before the second generator failed, the time after she relocated her shelter, the period when the coring grid was still legible, the period after the moss erased it.

Her name — her given name — she has not spoken aloud in a long time, and the last time she tried, as a kind of test, the sound of it was swallowed so completely by the water-roar that she wasn't sure she'd actually produced it. She may have just mouthed it. She sometimes thinks of herself in the third person, as *the keeper*, or *the technician*, which was her title when there were titles, when there was a team, when the camp had a manifest and a radio and a purpose that could be articulated in language someone at a funding body would understand.

---

She is tall and has become thin in a way that is not unhealthy but specific — the thinness of someone whose body has been pared by altitude and constant dampness and a diet that is adequate but never quite generous. Her hands are extraordinary. They are the hands of someone who has spent years working in water — not swimming in it, but immersed in it atmospherically, perpetually wet, never fully dry. The skin of her fingers is thick and pale, almost white, with deep creases that hold moisture and fine green lines where moss spores have established themselves in the folds of her knuckles. She does not try to remove them. She tried, in the first year, scrubbing her hands with a wire brush she found in the pump house, and the spores returned within a week, and she understood then something about the plateau that she had previously only understood intellectually: it is not a place you keep separate from. The boundary between her body and the environment is negotiable, and the negotiation is not going in her favor, and this does not frighten her the way it once did.

Her boots rotted in the second year. She made replacements by wrapping her feet in layers of the dense basal moss — the dark, compressed stuff from the lower strata — and binding it with cable stripped from one of the defunct installations. The result looks primitive but functions surprisingly well. The moss is naturally insulating, and it grips the wet surfaces better than any rubber sole. She has essentially built shoes out of the ground, and the ground doesn't mind. Sometimes she examines the soles and finds that the moss she harvested weeks ago is still growing, still photosynthesizing, still alive against the warmth of her feet. She is wearing a piece of the plateau that has not realized it has been separated from the rest.

---

Her shelter — her current one, the third — is built against and partly inside one of the canopy-tree columns, the largest she could find, a braided trunk nearly three meters in diameter at the base. She cut into it, or rather she separated its braids, working with a pry bar and patience over the course of several weeks, opening a cavity in the interstitial space between the major root-stems. The interior was already partially hollow — the growth pattern of the organism leaves gaps, voids where the braiding is loose — and she enlarged this into a space roughly two meters deep, a meter and a half wide, and tall enough to sit upright in but not stand. The walls are living. They grow inward at a rate she can perceive over weeks, and she trims them back with a knife when they begin to encroach on her sleeping space. This is her primary ongoing negotiation with the plateau: she makes room, and the plateau fills it, and she makes room again.

Inside the cavity, she has arranged what remains of the expedition's equipment with a neatness that borders on the ritual. There is a sleeping pad — synthetic, one of the few things the moss cannot easily digest, though it is trying, a faint green bloom colonizing one corner. A stove that runs on diesel siphoned from the operational generator, connected by a length of rubber tubing that she maintains carefully, replacing sections as the root system attempts to incorporate them. A steel box containing notebooks — dozens of them, filled with small, precise handwriting in pencil, because ink runs in this environment. A rain jacket that she wears constantly and that has become, through accumulated spore-fall and condensation minerals, almost rigid, a kind of carapace that is half garment and half geological deposit. A radio that has not received a signal in over two years, which she keeps because it is the last object she owns that was manufactured in a place with dry air.

---

She eats what the plateau offers, which is limited but sufficient. The canopy-trees produce, at their bases, a cluster of pale tuber-like structures — technically rhizomes, part of the organism's vegetative reproduction system — that are starchy and bland and contain enough calories to sustain her if she eats four or five per day. They taste like wet bread. She boils them on the diesel stove, not because they require cooking but because the act of cooking is one of the few rituals she maintains that distinguishes her from the other organisms on the plateau. She could eat them raw. She chooses not to. The boiling produces steam, and the steam rises from her shelter-cavity and joins the general moisture of the air, and she finds this satisfying in a way she cannot fully articulate — her metabolism participating in the water cycle, her breath and her cooking becoming part of the system's hydrology.

She also harvests condensation, though this is almost redundant — the air itself is practically drinkable. She has a set of collection surfaces, sheets of corrugated steel leaned against the pump house, where water runs down in steady trickles and she catches it in a container. The water is very pure. It tastes of nothing except a faint mineral sweetness. She drinks enormous quantities of it, far more than she would at lower altitude, because the dry wind and the exertion of moving through the moss-floor dehydrate her even as every surface around her is saturated.

---

Her days have a structure, though structure may be too rigid a word. She wakes inside the tree-column, in her sleeping cavity, and the first thing she perceives is the sound — the water-roar is so constant that it has become the texture of silence for her, the baseline against which she detects other sounds: the chug of the pump, the hum of the generator, the occasional sharp cry of the raptor that lives at the plateau's edge. She emerges from the cavity into the blazing light. She eats. She walks.

The walking is the core of her practice. She has a route — not a path, because paths are impossible here; the moss fills any impression within days — but a sequence of landmarks she follows: this canopy-tree, then that pump housing, then the coring grid (what remains of it), then the lattice tower, then the edge. The route takes most of the day. She walks it slowly, because the ground demands slowness, and because she is observing.

She is still, in her mind, a scientist. The expedition — whatever it was, whoever funded it, however many people once comprised it — was here to study the plateau's growth dynamics, and she is still studying them, even though there is no longer anyone to report her findings to. The notebooks in the steel box contain meticulous records: measurements of the moss-floor depth at fixed points, estimates of the canopy-trees' vertical growth rate, observations of the water-lenses (she has named several of them, not sentimentally but indexically: Lens 1, Lens 4, the Long Lens, the Lens Near the Generator), records of the root-mass's advance over the machine installations. She measures the rate at which the lattice tower is being pulled into the ground. She measures it every time she visits. She has charts. The rate is accelerating. The tower has sunk eleven centimeters in the time she has been observing it alone, and the roots around its base have thickened visibly, wrapping the angle-iron in a grip that is gentle and absolute.

---

She does not know, with certainty, what happened to the others. There were six at the start — she remembers this clearly, can list their names, does list them sometimes in the privacy of her shelter, a whispered roll call that the water-roar immediately buries. They left in stages. The first two were evacuated by helicopter when the radio still worked and the funding body still acknowledged the project's existence. The next two left on foot, descending through the cloud layer on the eastern face, where a route had been flagged with orange tape. The tape is gone now, taken by the moss or the wind, and she does not know if they reached the bottom. The fifth — and this is the part she turns over most often, the part that lives in the notebooks as a gap, a series of entries that circle around something without quite stating it — the fifth did not leave. The fifth went to take measurements at the northern edge and did not return, and she searched for three days, and she found one of his boots near the overhang, the moss lip that extends out over the void, and the overhang had a fresh fracture, a place where a section of root-mass had broken away and fallen, and below there was only cloud.

She recorded this in the notebook. She wrote it in the same small, precise hand she uses for growth-rate data. She noted the date (approximately), the location, the condition of the boot, the dimensions of the fracture. She did not write what she felt because the notebook is for data, and also because what she felt was complicated and large and did not have edges, and the plateau's sound was enormous, and her hands were wet, and the boot was already beginning to be colonized by spores.

---

She talks to the machines. Not in the way of someone who has lost their grip on the distinction between the animate and inanimate — she is precise about what the machines are and what they are not — but in the way of someone who recognizes them as the only other persistent non-biological presences on the plateau. They are her colleagues now. The pump gets a greeting every time she passes. *Still going*, she says, or mouths, and presses her hand to its housing to feel the vibration, the heartbeat. The generator gets more conversation. She checks its fuel level — there is a reserve tank, large, buried somewhere under the moss, connected by a pipe she has traced for about twenty meters before it disappears into the root-mass — and she estimates it has perhaps another eighteen months of fuel, maybe two years if she reduces her own consumption. She does not know what the generator powers. The cables run into the moss and she has not been able to follow them to their terminus. She suspects — it is a suspicion that has grown in her over years, nourished by observation and by the particular quality of attention that prolonged solitude produces — that the generator powers something deep in the root-mass, something the original expedition installed and buried and that the plateau has since grown over and integrated, and that this something is part of why the plateau grows the way it does, and that turning off the generator might change the system in ways she cannot predict.

So she doesn't turn it off. She maintains it. She is the keeper.

---

There is something else about her that is harder to describe — a quality that has developed gradually, the way the moss develops on a surface, not as an event but as an accumulation.

She has begun to feel the plateau as a single thing.

Not metaphorically. Not in the poetic-spiritual sense of oneness with nature. Literally. She has spent so long observing its growth patterns that she has developed an intuitive sense of it as one organism — not just the canopy-trees, which are clearly all the same species and probably all connected through the root-mass, but the entire system: moss, trees, water, root-mass, the chemistry cycling through all of it, the machines being slowly absorbed into it, the mineral being drawn up from the stone, the condensation being pulled from the air. It is one living process. She is embedded in it. She is fed by it. She is, in her small warm way, being processed by it — her breath becoming its humidity, her waste becoming its nutrient, the moss on her knuckles connecting her, cell by cell, to the organism that constitutes the ground.

She does not find this threatening. She found it threatening in the first year, and then alarming in the second, and then interesting in the third, and now, in what she believes is the fourth, she finds it accurate. She is a component. Not a captive — she could leave, probably, if she were willing to descend through the cloud layer on the eastern face without route markers, without knowing if there is still a trail, without knowing if anyone is expecting her at the bottom — but a component. She serves a function. She maintains the machines that the plateau is digesting. She is the plateau's enzyme, breaking down the last residues of human intention into forms the system can absorb.

She finds this funny, sometimes. Alone in her shelter, boiling a tuber, the water-roar filling the world, she will smile — a real smile, not grim, not ironic — at the idea that she has become a digestive organ for a plant. It is so far from what her life was supposed to be. It is so far from the grant proposal. It is so far from the helicopter that dropped her here with five other scientists and six months of supplies and a satellite phone and a clear mandate and a return date.

---

But here is what she knows, what keeps her: the plateau is building something. Not building in the way humans build, with intention and blueprints, but building in the way a coral reef builds, or a delta builds — accumulating, accreting, becoming more itself with each year. It is constructing a new landform. It is making soil where there was no soil. It is making hydrology where there was no water. It is making, slowly, the conditions for a world that does not yet exist — a world in which the cloud-valleys below are filled not with vapor but with forest, in which the raw granite is sheathed in living substrate from summit to base, in which the entire mountain range is one continuous organism, breathing and dripping and thundering with the sound of water falling through ten million leaves.

She is watching the beginning of this. She may be the only one watching. And so she stays, and she walks her route, and she takes her measurements, and she talks to the pump, and she trims the walls of her shelter, and she eats her bland tubers, and she writes in her notebooks in small precise handwriting that no one may ever read, and the moss grows over her bootprints before she has gone twenty paces, and the sound is enormous, and the light is blinding, and she is cold, and she is wet, and she is not unhappy.

---

At night — the brief night, the grey-violet compression of hours between the long bright days — she lies in her cavity inside the living tree and listens to the sound change. The water-roar doesn't stop, but it deepens, the temperature drop increasing condensation so that the drops are heavier, fatter, striking the leaves with more weight. The wind drops. The canopy-trees make a different music at night: slower, more resonant, almost tidal. She can feel the tree around her doing something — not growing, exactly, but adjusting, shifting its internal pressures, its root-braids tightening incrementally. She is inside a living thing, and the living thing knows she is there, in whatever way a plant can know — it grows around her, it accommodates her, it does not crush her or expel her, and she has chosen to interpret this as acceptance rather than indifference, because at this altitude, in this solitude, the distinction has ceased to matter.

She sleeps. The plateau grows. The pump runs. The moss advances. Somewhere, beneath a meter of living ground, a cable carries current from the generator to a destination that was installed by people who are gone, and the current does whatever it does, and the system continues, and the water falls, and the water falls, and the water falls.
