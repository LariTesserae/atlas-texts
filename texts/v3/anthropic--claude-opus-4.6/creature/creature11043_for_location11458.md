They are so small that the word "creature" overstates them. And yet.

---

The one nearest to you — if you are crouched in the shallows near the center channel, if you have been still long enough for the water to forget you — is moving along the seam where a rimstone dam meets the floor. It is not walking so much as *advancing*, each leg placement a decision made by something that does not decide. Its body is a translucent comma, a wisp of calcium-colored tissue stretched over a scaffold so fine that the internal structures should be visible but aren't, because there is nothing inside dark enough to see. No pigment. No eyes. The antennae extend forward into water that is, from its perspective, the entire universe — warm, mineral-rich, unchanging, and infinite in the way that forty steps of ankle-deep water is infinite when you are the size of a grain of rice and have never encountered an edge you couldn't eventually walk around.

It does not know about the ceiling. It has no concept of "above" that extends past the surface tension it will never break. The dripping — that soft, arrhythmic patter that you hear as the room's only voice — registers to it as something else entirely. Pressure variations, maybe. Faint concussive events in the medium it breathes and walks through and is. Each drop that lands in the channel sends a ripple so slight that you cannot see it, but the isopod pauses when one passes. Not startled. Nothing here is startled. It pauses the way a person pauses mid-step when they think they've heard their name in a crowd, and then it resumes, because the ripple was not food and was not threat and was not mate, and those are the only categories that matter, and even "matter" is too strong a word.

It grazes.

The biofilm it eats is a kind of ghost. You cannot see it, cannot feel it, would not know it was there even if you pressed your thumb to the submerged stone and held it up to the grey light from the crack. But the isopod knows. Its mouthparts — and here the scale defeats description, because its mouthparts are smaller than the smallest thing you have ever deliberately looked at — work against the stone surface with a motion that is less "eating" and more "attending." It moves its body forward a distance that is meaningless in any unit of measurement you use daily, and the patch of stone behind it is imperceptibly cleaner, and the patch of stone ahead of it is imperceptibly richer, and this is its life. This is the whole of its life. It will do this until it stops doing this, and then the calcium in its translucent body will return to the water, and the bacteria will repopulate the clean patch, and another isopod — or the same one, in a sense, because they are all so identical that individuality is a courtesy extended by the observer — will graze it again.

---

There are more. You begin to see them the way you begin to see stars after your eyes adjust — not appearing, but *registering*, one by one, in places you were already looking. Here: one on the slope of a flowstone terrace, motionless or moving so slowly that the distinction requires more patience than you have. There: two, close together, near one of the cave pearls, their paths converging or diverging, impossible to say which. Another in the deeper part of the channel, slightly larger than the others — or slightly closer, which amounts to the same thing in this light.

They do not school. They do not flock. They do not herd, swarm, cluster, aggregate, or gather. Their proximity to each other is incidental in the way that the proximity of two raindrops on a window is incidental. They are each alone in the way that a thing can only be alone when it has never developed the machinery for loneliness. They are aware of each other — an antenna-touch when two paths cross produces a slight lateral adjustment, a micro-correction, two curves diverging — but the awareness is chemical, mechanical, a lock-and-key interaction between surface molecules that says *this is another one of me, adjust heading by some small angle* and nothing more.

And yet they have partitioned the floor. Not deliberately. Not with intention or negotiation or conflict. But over time — deep time, isopod time, which is somehow both faster and slower than the geological time that built this room — their collective grazing has produced a pattern. The biofilm is not uniform. It is thicker near the drip-points, where the mineral rain feeds the bacteria more generously, and thinner on the open flats between dams. The isopods gravitate toward thickness without knowing they are gravitating, and their paths, over generations, have worn certain routes more than others, and those routes are now the paths that new isopods follow because the biofilm is thickest at the edges of where it has been grazed, in the regrowth zone, and so the paths are self-reinforcing, and what has emerged is something that looks — if you could map it, if you could see at this scale with this patience — like a network. Like a city seen from very far above. Like a map of nowhere with its own roads.

The rimstone dams that someone broke are part of this network. Not because the isopods understand what the breaks mean. But the altered water flow changed the mineral distribution, which changed the bacterial density, which changed the grazing patterns, which means that whoever chipped those precise notches in the calcite lips — centuries ago, millennia ago — is still, in a sense, directing traffic in a city of creatures who do not know they are in a city and would not understand the concept of direction.

---

Now attend to one.

This one. The one on the shelf — the augmented shelf, the one with the chisel marks being slowly swallowed by deposition. It is not in the water. It is on the wet stone surface above the water line, in the thin film of condensation that coats everything here, and it has been making its way toward the shallow limestone bowl. It moves differently out of the water — more cautiously, if caution can exist without a mind to feel it. Its legs find less purchase. Its body is more visible against the darker stone, a fleck of pale translucence that catches the grey light in a way the submerged ones do not. It looks, in this light, like a piece of the ceiling that fell and survived.

The bowl. The worn-smooth bowl that someone carved and used. Inside its concavity, the condensation gathers slightly deeper than on the flat stone around it. A tiny pool within a pool within a room that is itself a pool. And the biofilm inside the bowl is different — thicker, maybe, or differently composed, fed by whatever trace minerals leached from the stone when it was carved, when fresh rock was exposed to water for the first time. The isopod does not know this. It simply reaches the rim of the bowl and continues, tipping over the edge and descending into the shallow interior, and begins to graze.

It is eating from a bowl. A bowl that was made for — what? We don't know. Someone carved it and used it and left it here and the water has been filling it ever since and the bacteria have been colonizing it ever since and now this creature, this grain-of-rice-sized comma of translucent nothing, is eating from it as though it were made for this purpose, which it was not, but purposes have a way of being replaced by other purposes when enough time passes, and eventually the last purpose is the one that looks like it was always the point.

The bone awl beside the bowl is furred with mineral. The isopod will not visit it. There is no biofilm on its surface — something about the material, the calcium phosphate of the bone, inhibits bacterial colonization, or the geometry is wrong, or the chemistry is subtly hostile. The awl persists in its own small zone of sterility, untouched by the only living things that might touch it. It is the loneliest object in the room, in a room where loneliness is not a concept that applies.

---

The mites on the ceiling. You cannot see them. I said this already. But they are there.

They are smaller than the isopods, which makes them nearly impossible to think about. They live in the crevices between calcite formations — in the spaces where one blunted nub presses against another, where the mineral curtain-folds have grown together and left, between their fused surfaces, gaps no wider than a hair. The mites navigate these gaps. Their world is the ceiling the way the isopods' world is the floor, and the two worlds do not intersect, separated by a distance of four or five feet that might as well be the diameter of the Earth.

They eat — what? Fungal hyphae, perhaps. Bacterial films of a different composition than the ones below. Organic residues so scant that calling them "food" elevates their status considerably. They reproduce. They die. Their bodies are absorbed into the mineral crust. They have been here for a span of time that is, like the water level, meaningless to name. They are not adapted to this environment so much as they are *of* this environment — reduced, simplified, stripped of every feature that does not serve the single purpose of persisting in a space where there is nothing to do but persist.

They are colorless. They are eyeless. They have no predators, no competitors, no parasites. They are the fauna of a world where fauna is an afterthought, a minor chemical footnote in a story that is primarily about water and stone.

---

And here is what lives here, really.

Not the isopods. Not the mites. Not the bacteria, which are alive in the way that a process is alive, without interiority, without experience, running their metabolic reactions in the dark like tiny factories that no one built and no one oversees.

What lives here is the *system*. The dripping and the grazing and the deposition and the dissolution. The water arriving and leaving at exactly the same rate. The minerals descending from the ceiling and accreting on the floor. The bacteria converting those minerals into a film that the isopods convert into more isopods that die and become minerals again. The bowl filling with condensation. The chisel marks being erased. The cave pearls growing by a thickness that would not register on any instrument you could carry down here. The broken rimstone dams guiding water along paths chosen by someone who is not here, feeding bacteria that feed crustaceans that do not know they are being fed by a human decision made in a time so distant that the very concept of "human decision" has been composted into geology.

It is all one thing. One slow, warm, dripping, grazing, accreting, dissolving thing. And it lives the way a heartbeat lives — not because any single component is alive in a way that would satisfy you, but because the whole pattern persists, and repeats, and maintains itself, and has done so for so long that it has outlasted everything that could have named it.

The isopod in the bowl finishes grazing a patch no larger than the period at the end of this sentence. It turns — slowly, slowly — and begins on the next patch. The water in the bowl is warm. The air above the bowl is warm. The stone of the bowl is warm. The isopod is warm. Everything is the same temperature, and the dripping continues, and the grey light fails to cast shadows, and you are still the least stable thing here, with your quick blood and your cooling skin and your lungs full of someone else's exhalation, crouching in water that has forgotten the difference between itself and the floor.

You could leave. You probably should.

Nothing here would notice. Nothing here would fail to notice. Noticing is not a thing that happens in this room. There is only the grazing, and the dripping, and the slow mineral patience of a place that completed itself before you were a species and will complete itself again after the last isopod dissolves back into the warm, still water and the bacteria close over the gap like a wound healing in a body that does not feel pain, because it does not feel anything, because feeling is not what it does.

What it does is *continue*.

And it will.
