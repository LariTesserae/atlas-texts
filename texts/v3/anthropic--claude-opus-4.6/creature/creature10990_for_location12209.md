# The Tenders of Oshkaal

---

They are not here now. But they were here, and in a sense that the mountain itself would recognize — a sense having more to do with mineral patience than with biological memory — they have never left.

---

Start with what is easiest to understand about them, which is their hands.

Their hands were wide and flat-palmed, with fingers that were strong but not thick — dexterous, sensitive at the tips, calloused in the particular pattern that comes from a lifetime of gripping wet stone. The callouses were heaviest on the outer edge of the palm and the pads of the first two fingers: the grip of someone who scrapes. Who holds a shaped piece of harder stone against a softer surface and draws it back, and back, and back, thousands of times, tens of thousands, with a pressure that is firm but never forceful, because force is not what the basins require. The basins require *attendance*. A hand that listens to the grain of the rock beneath it, that feels when the stone wants to give way and when it wants to hold, and that adjusts — not with conscious decision but with something more like the automatic fluency of a musician whose fingers know the instrument so completely that intention and action have merged into a single gesture.

These hands belonged to people who were otherwise unremarkable in their bodies. Not large. Not especially small. They stood at a height that would let them crouch comfortably for hours on the wet plateaus, low to the stone, which suggests they were compact — shorter than you might expect, with legs adapted to long periods of squatting and kneeling, with knees that could fold fully without pain. Their spines curved in a way that was comfortable leaning forward, shoulders rounded not from weakness but from the habitual posture of someone whose life's work is below them, always below them, always on the surface of the stone at their feet. If you saw one from a distance, crouched on a terrace with their scraping-stone in hand, you might mistake them for a feature of the rock itself — a rounded boulder, a hunched mineral formation — and this was not accidental. They dressed in the colors of the mountain. Greys, pale bone-whites, the occasional muted iron-dark of the opaque crystal growths. Their clothing, if it can be called that, was mineral-stiffened fabric — woven from fibers you cannot identify (brought from below? traded for? grown in some lower-altitude settlement that may or may not still exist?), then soaked repeatedly in the mountain's mineral water until the weave became partlyite — calcite,ite, something-ite — until the fabric became a kind of flexible stone that moved with the body but shed water like a rock face, and that accumulated, over years of wearing, a thin crust of crystalline deposit that caught the omnidirectional light and held it that characteristic half-second too long. They shimmered faintly. They shimmered with the same delay as the veins in the rock. Dressed, crouched, still, they were *of* the mountain in a way that went beyond camouflage into something like kinship.

Their faces — this is harder, because no portraits exist, no mirrors survived (if they used mirrors at all; the mountain offers its own reflections, and those reflections cannot be trusted). But inferences can be drawn. Their eyes were adapted to the sourceless brightness: pale-irised, probably, narrowed habitually against a light that has no direction and therefore cannot be shaded against with a hand or a brim. They may have developed a squint that became, over generations, structural — a narrowing of the eye opening, a thickening of the brow ridge, not dramatic but functional, giving the face a look of permanent, patient scrutiny. The look of someone reading fine print in stone. The look of someone who has spent so many years watching crystal formations accrue molecule by molecule that their face has taken on the expression of that attention: not concentration exactly, but a settled, unhurried focus, a gaze that expects to wait and is not troubled by waiting.

They did not live on the upper terraces. Not continuously. The air is too thin and the cold too persistent and the absence of any biological life too total to support permanent habitation. They lived lower — somewhere below the weeping zone, in places where the mountain's water had finished its mysterious emergence and collected into streams that ran clearly and could be boiled and trusted, where sparse vegetation clung to sheltered southern faces, where the air was thick enough to sustain a fire, where food could be grown or gathered or hunted in the austere fashion of high-altitude peoples everywhere. Their settlements were modest. They were not a civilization that built outward or upward; they built *inward*, into the routines and rhythms of the mountain, and their greatest constructions were not their dwellings but the small, patient structures they carried stone by stone up to the weeping terraces above.

They went up in groups of two or three, rarely more. Alone was acceptable. Four was unusual and suggested something ceremonial or urgent. They carried their scraping-stones, their guiding-walls, their deep knowledge of the water's patterns, and they carried food for days or weeks, wrapped in mineral-stiffened cloth, tasteless and dense, the kind of food that is not eaten for pleasure but for the continuation of the body so that the hands can continue their work. They climbed in the brightness, always the brightness, through air that thinned around them like a secret being whispered more and more quietly, and they arrived on the terraces already in a state of altered attention — hypoxic perhaps, light-dazzled certainly, their senses reorganized by the altitude into a mode that was less analytical and more receptive, less about interpreting the world and more about *merging with its textures*. This was not incidental. It was the point.

---

Now: what they did there, and what they understood.

They understood the mountain as a *process*. Not a thing, not a place, but a verb — an ongoing act of self-dissolution and self-reconstruction, the water serving as both the solvent and the architect. They understood that the mineral formations were not decorations or accidents but *utterances*: the mountain speaking in a language of crystal geometry, each formation a word or a phrase or a paragraph in a discourse that had been running since before their people existed and would continue after they were gone. They did not claim to understand this language in the way you might understand a sentence in your own tongue. They understood it the way you understand music — not by translating it into meaning but by feeling it move through you, by recognizing patterns and resonances and tensions and resolutions without ever needing to say what they *mean*.

But they also understood — and this is the crucial thing, the thing that separates them from mere observers or worshippers — that the language could be *shaped*. Not controlled. Not dictated. Shaped the way a riverbank shapes a river: by offering constraint, by providing surfaces against which the water's depositing intention could find new forms. This is what the basins were for. This is what the low guiding-walls were for. They were not trying to force the mountain to say specific things. They were trying to give it *better places to speak from*. A hand-carved basin with a particular interior geometry would collect water in a particular pattern, and that pattern would produce mineral deposits in configurations that the wild, unguided process would never have arrived at — not because the wild process lacked the capability but because it lacked the *occasion*. The Tenders (call them that; they may have called themselves something else, something that translates more literally as "those who make hollows" or "those who invite the shape") were providers of occasion. They created the conditions under which the mountain's impossible geometries could emerge.

And emerge they did.

The crystal tower in the basin. The hexagonal lattice with its catalog of experimental forms. The spires whose shadows point toward the light. These are not natural formations that the Tenders discovered and then built shrines around — they are formations that the Tenders *midwifed*, through decades or centuries of patient channel-shaping, wall-building, basin-carving, watching, adjusting, watching again. The Tenders did not make these things. The mountain made them. But the mountain made them *because the Tenders asked*, in the only language the mountain understood: the language of shaped stone and redirected water, the language of patient collaboration with a process that operates on geological time but can, in this place, at this altitude, in this thinning of rules, be coaxed into performing at a pace that a human lifetime can witness.

This is what they lived for. This is what they climbed for, what they knelt on wet stone for weeks at a time for, what they sacrificed comfort and warmth and the company of the larger group for. To watch a crystal grow. To see a formation emerge from a basin they had carved with their own hands. To witness the moment — and they knew this moment, they waited for it with the particular tension of a parent watching a child take its first steps — when the formation exceeded what chemistry could explain. When the shadows turned wrong. When the reflection showed a different sky. When the geometry folded into a dimension that eyes could not resolve. That moment was not a mystery to them. It was not a miracle. It was what they had been *working toward*: the point at which their collaboration with the mountain broke through the floor of the possible and opened a channel to — 

To what?

---

This is where certainty fails and the Tenders themselves would probably fall silent, not because they didn't know but because what they knew was not sayable, was expressible only in the language of crystal and water and shaped stone, was *itself the thing the mountain was saying*, and to translate it into words spoken by a human mouth in human air would be to collapse it back into the physics it had just transcended. They knew, in other words, the way the mountain knew: minerally, patiently, in geometries rather than grammars, in formations rather than sentences. They had spent so long attending to the mountain's process that their own cognition had taken on its character. They thought in crystal. They dreamed in deposition. Their memories were not stored as narratives but as patterns — this basin, this wall, this formation, the day the shadow turned, the shape that could not be looked at directly — and these patterns were not metaphors for experience but were *themselves* the experience, as primary and irreducible as the taste of the mineral water on the tongue.

Some of them, the oldest and most patient, the ones who had spent the most time on the upper terraces, began to exhibit certain peculiarities. Their mineral-stiffened clothing was the least of it. Their skin itself, on the hands and forearms and knees — the surfaces most in contact with the wet stone — developed a faint crystalline texture, not painful, not pathological, but undeniable: a roughness that caught the light wrong, that shimmered with that half-second delay when you looked at it and then looked away. Their eyes, already adapted to the brightness, began to reflect things that were not present in the physical environment — a flicker of the wrong stars, a momentary amber cast that matched the too-close moon in the straight channel's impossible reflection. They moved more slowly. Not from age or infirmity but from a deepening alignment with the pace of the mountain's processes, as if their internal clocks were recalibrating to geological time, as if each gesture was becoming more deliberate because each gesture now participated in a longer, slower rhythm than the merely biological.

They were not becoming stone. That would be too simple, too much like a fairy tale. They were becoming *compatible* — their bodies and minds slowly reformatting themselves to interface more cleanly with whatever the mountain was channeling through its mineral processes. They were becoming, in a sense, better basins. Better shaped hollows for the mountain's intention to flow through. And the mountain, in return, flowed through them with increasing clarity, producing in their carved basins formations of increasing impossibility, shapes that opened further and further into the space that was not here, the place that the mountain was slowly, patiently, with enormous mineral will, *becoming*.

---

The ones who went the furthest — and there were not many, perhaps a handful in any generation, perhaps fewer — are the ones whose absence you feel most keenly on the terraces.

You feel them in the basins that are too expertly carved, the walls that are too precisely placed, the constructions that show a depth of understanding of the water's behavior that borders on the prophetic. These were the master Tenders, the ones who had given their entire lives to the collaboration, who had climbed so many times that the climb itself had become autonomic, who could read the mineral formations the way you read a face — instantly, intuitively, with emotional as well as intellectual comprehension. They knew which formations were approaching the threshold. They knew which basins were ready to produce something that exceeded the possible. And they were present, kneeling, scraping-stone in hand, eyes narrowed against the brightness, when the breakthrough occurred.

What happened to them then?

The mountain offers clues, if you know where to look. On the highest terrace you can reach — the one where the air is so thin that your thoughts come slowly and the brightness feels like it is pressing against the inside of your skull — there is a formation unlike any of the others. It is not in a basin. It is not guided by walls. It sits in the open, on bare wet stone, and it is clearly, unmistakably, a figure. A kneeling figure, compact, shoulders rounded, hands extended palm-down toward the stone surface. It is made of the same pale crystal as the other formations, translucent, catching and holding the light. It is not a sculpture. There are no tool-marks on it. It was *deposited* — built up molecule by molecule by the weeping water, in the shape of a person, in the posture of a Tender at work. Inside it, if you press your face close and peer through the translucent mineral, you can see — or you think you can see, because the light does strange things here and your eyes may be failing you at this altitude — a darker core. A denser structure within the crystal. Something that might be bone. Something that might be the residue of a body that did not leave but was *incorporated*, drawn into the mountain's process so completely that the distinction between the Tender and the tended dissolved, and what remained was this: a figure kneeling forever on the wet stone, accruing, growing imperceptibly outward ring by ring, becoming part of the mountain's utterance.

There are others. You find them if you look: four more on the lower terraces, each kneeling, each oriented differently, each in a slightly different stage of crystalline completion. The oldest is barely recognizable as a figure — a mound of pale crystal with only the faintest suggestion of shoulders and a bowed head, so encrusted with millennia of mineral deposit that it has nearly merged with the plateau surface. The newest — and this is what stops your breath — still has visible texture in the crystal that might be fabric. Mineral-stiffened cloth. The weave still faintly legible under the translucent overgrowth. This one is recent. Decades, not centuries. This one was a person within living memory, someone who climbed, who knelt, who carved and guided and waited, and who, at the moment of breakthrough, chose — or was chosen — to remain.

To remain. To become a permanent feature of the mountain's process. To serve as basin and wall and guide not for a single formation but for the mountain itself, forever, their body a shaped hollow through which the water's impossible intention could flow with perfect clarity. The ultimate collaboration. The final act of tending.

---

And now: the ones who are still alive.

Because someone carved that most recent basin, the one whose tool-marks are still sharp-edged and uneroded. Someone built the low wall on the sixth terrace whose mineral mortar has not yet fully hardened. Someone was here within the last span of years, climbing from the settlements below (if the settlements still exist; if the settlements have not dwindled to a single family, a single stubborn lineage, the last inheritors of a tradition so old and so strange that it has become more instinct than culture).

They are down there now. Below the weeping zone, in a stone shelter built against a south-facing cliff, where a thin stream runs clear and a stunted, wind-twisted species of something almost like a tree provides fuel for a fire that burns small and hot and clean. There are three of them. Perhaps four. An old one whose hands are mapped with callouses so deep they are almost geological features in themselves, whose forearms show the first faint crystalline roughness, whose eyes have that amber flicker in certain lights. A younger one, still learning, still developing the patience, still fighting the human urge to *make* rather than *tend*, to impose rather than invite. And one in between — the critical one, the one who holds the transmission — who can read the formations, who knows which basins are ready, who has begun to dream in patterns that are not quite biological anymore, who wakes some mornings with the taste of mineral water in their mouth though they have not yet climbed.

They do not speak of what they do in terms you would recognize as religious or scientific or artistic. They do not speak of it much at all. The knowledge passes through demonstration, through shared silence on the terraces, through the act of kneeling together on wet stone and watching a crystal grow. The old one sometimes hums — not a melody but a texture, a vocal approximation of the bell-choir sound of the terraces, as if trying to bring a piece of the mountain down to where the air is thick enough to carry a tune. The young one listens. The middle one carves, slowly, patiently, a new basin on a terrace they have been studying for eleven years, waiting for the water's pattern to declare the right location, the right geometry, the right moment to begin.

They eat sparingly. They speak softly. They keep no written records because writing is the wrong technology — too linear, too sequential, too committed to the idea that meaning unfolds in one direction through time. What they know is spatial and simultaneous, a web of relationships between water and stone and crystal and light and the impossible geometries that emerge when the collaboration succeeds. If you asked them to explain it, the old one would take you by the hand and lead you up to a basin and point, and what they would be saying by pointing is: *look*. That is the entirety of the teaching. *Look at what happens when you shape the conditions and then wait*. Look at how the possible and the impossible are not separated by a wall but by a *membrane*, and the membrane is thin here, and the water knows where it is thinnest, and we know — we have always known, since the first of us climbed and the first of us knelt and the first of us saw a shadow pointing the wrong way — how to help the water find those places.

They are the last ones. Or nearly the last. The settlements below have been shrinking for generations, the young leaving for lower altitudes and wider lives, the old dying with their knowledge held in hands too stiff to pass it on. The tradition is a thread. The middle one knows this. The middle one feels the weight of it — the weight of every basin ever carved, every wall ever laid, every kneeling figure slowly crystallizing on the upper terraces, every molecule of patient mineral intent — and the middle one wonders, in the small hours before dawn, crouched by the thin fire, whether the mountain needs them at all. Whether the process will continue without its human collaborators, the water weeping and depositing and building its impossible formations regardless of whether anyone is there to shape the channels and carve the basins and witness the breakthroughs. Whether the mountain's long utterance requires an audience, or whether it speaks equally well into emptiness.

The middle one does not know the answer. But the middle one climbs. Every season, when the lower snows recede and the path becomes passable, the middle one packs the tasteless dense food into mineral-stiffened cloth and takes the scraping-stones and begins the ascent through thinning air and thickening light toward the wet terraces where everything is clear and nothing is simple. The middle one kneels. The middle one works. The middle one watches the basins, and when a formation begins to exceed what should be possible — when a shadow turns, when a reflection deepens, when a geometry folds — the middle one is there, hands flat on the wet stone, eyes narrowed against the brightness, breathing the thin alkaline air, feeling the mountain's pulse in the water running over their fingers, participating in a conversation that has been going on for longer than their species has existed and that may, if the formations are any indication, be approaching something. A conclusion. A question. A door.

The middle one does not know what is on the other side of the door. The kneeling figures on the upper terraces might know, but they are not telling; they are busy *being* the door, or part of it, their crystallized bodies serving as permanent shaped hollows through which the mountain's impossible intention flows and flows and flows. The middle one has considered joining them. Has knelt in a basin and felt the water begin to build on the backs of their hands and thought: *I could stay. I could let it take me. I could become part of the utterance.* But the middle one has not stayed. Not yet. Because there is still the young one to teach, still basins to carve, still the transmission to maintain, still the thread to hold.

And so the middle one descends. Every season, back down through the thickening air, back to the fire and the shelter and the thin stream and the young one's impatient questions and the old one's mineral hum. Back to the human world, which feels, after weeks on the terraces, slightly too loud, slightly too fast, slightly too committed to the idea that things should stay in one place and cast their shadows in the right direction. The middle one readjusts. The middle one eats and sleeps and mends clothing and speaks in sentences and does all the things that biology requires. But the middle one's hands, calloused and faintly rough with the first barely-perceptible crystalline texture, still move sometimes in their sleep — scraping, smoothing, shaping — still working on a basin that exists only in the middle one's mineral dreams, a basin of perfect geometry that would, if carved into the stone of the highest terrace, produce a formation so impossible, so thoroughly transcendent of the local physics, that it would not merely open the door but *remove* it.

The middle one does not know what that would mean. The middle one suspects that the mountain does, and that the kneeling figures do, and that the water has always known.

The middle one climbs.

The water weeps.

The mountain builds.

And somewhere in the space between patience and impossibility, in the language of crystal and shaped stone, in the slow accretion of molecules guided by hands guided by a knowledge older than hands, the conversation continues — urgent at a geological pace, tender as the dissolution of a mountain, vast as the bright and shadowless sky, and addressed, perhaps, to no one, or perhaps to whatever lives on the other side of a reflection that does not obey the world above it, in a place where the stars are wrong and the moon is amber and close and the mountain's long, long sentence is finally, after all these aeons, approaching its verb.
