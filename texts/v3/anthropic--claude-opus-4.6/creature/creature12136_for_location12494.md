# Who Lives Here

---

The city itself lives here. That is the answer you resist but cannot escape.

Not metaphorically. Not in the way people say a house "has character" or a garden "wants" to climb the wall. The city lives here the way your liver lives in your abdomen — as an organ, purposeful, embedded, unaware of itself in any way you could interrogate but *functioning* with such specificity that calling it alive is simply a statement of fact.

But you asked who. You wanted inhabitants. So.

---

**Begin with the roots.**

Beneath the moss-carpeted plazas, beneath the packed root-streets, in the dark compressed layers where organic matter and young basalt meet, there is a network so vast and so densely interconnected that it constitutes, by any reasonable measure, the largest single living structure in this place. It is mycorrhizal — fungal — but it has grown beyond any mycorrhizal network you have reference for. It does not merely connect trees. It *is* the connecting. Every tower, every bridge, every vine-cable and root-wall and floating photosynthetic mat on the reservoir lake is threaded through with it, and through it they share water, sugars, phosphorus, nitrogen, electrical signals so faint they can only be detected by the responses they produce.

The fungal network does not think. But it *decides*, in the way that a river decides to flow downhill — by the accumulated weight of a trillion small chemical gradients, each one meaningless, the sum of them producing behavior so coherent it looks like intention. When a section of root-street begins to weaken, the network redirects nutrients there. When a tower's heartwood spirals need reinforcement, new growth appears precisely where it is needed. When the wind shifts — though the wind rarely shifts — the flower-pipes on the towers adjust their apertures over the course of days, minutely, a fraction of a millimeter, to maintain the chord.

The chord matters. You don't know why it matters. The fungal network doesn't know why it matters. But the chord is maintained with the same non-negotiable precision as the water distribution, the structural integrity, the photosynthetic output of the reservoir mat. It is part of the homeostasis. The drone is not decoration. It is a vital sign.

So the first inhabitant is this: the network. The underneath. The thing that has no body of its own but inhabits every body here. It is the oldest living component of the city, older than any individual tower or root-bridge, older perhaps than the plateau's surface. It may have been here when the basalt was still warm. It may have been what *called* the plants — not consciously, not with intent, but chemically, the way mycorrhizal fungi in young volcanic soils send out tendrils and exudates that say, to whatever seeds arrive on the wind: *here, here, anchor here, I will feed you, grow.*

And they grew. And it fed them. And they grew *more*. And at some point the feeding became building and the building became architecture and the architecture became a city and no one — no single organism, no committee, no mind — ever decided any of it. It simply emerged, the way a crystal emerges from a supersaturated solution: inevitably, following rules that were always latent in the chemistry, producing something that looks designed because the rules themselves are elegant.

---

**Now the wood.**

The towers are not single trees. You suspected this. Each tower is a collective — dozens, perhaps hundreds of individual trunks that germinated close together and were guided by the fungal network into helical growth patterns, their cambium layers pressing against each other over decades until they fused. The heartwood spiral-stairways inside are not carved; they are the natural result of multiple trunks growing in a slow vortex around a shared center of gravity, each trunk adding its own ring of growth, the combined effect producing a rising helical ramp of dense wood bounded by softer, more porous tissue that eventually decays and leaves the hollow interior.

Each tower, then, is a community that has become a structure. The individual trunks are still technically alive — still drawing water, still photosynthesizing through their crown-flowers, still adding fractional millimeters of new growth each year. But they have been so thoroughly incorporated into the collective architecture that they no longer function as separate organisms in any meaningful sense. They are like the cells of your body: alive, technically autonomous at the biochemical level, but utterly committed to the project of being part of something larger.

What species are they? You would not recognize them. They are not species that exist outside this plateau. They may have been, once — wind-borne seeds from lowland forests carried up to the young rock, ordinary pioneer species that colonize fresh volcanic surfaces everywhere. But the fungal network changed them. Not genetically, or not only genetically. Epigenetically. Developmentally. It fed them specific compounds at specific times in their growth and those compounds activated latent possibilities in their genomes — the capacity for helical growth, for bark-fusion, for the production of tubular flowers calibrated to resonate at particular frequencies. These were not mutations. These were options that were always there, in the deep code of what a plant can be, waiting for the right chemical context. The fungal network provided that context. The trees became what they were always capable of becoming, which happened to be: towers.

So the second inhabitant is: the wood. The vertical. The community-become-architecture, a thousand merged trunks standing in the permanent afternoon, transpiring gently, growing imperceptibly, maintaining their structural integrity not through any conscious effort but through the sheer momentum of being alive and being held in place by the network below and the flowers above and the water running through them like blood.

---

**The vines.**

You noticed the braided cables in the infrastructure district — the great horizontal runs of woven vine that vibrate with the passage of fluids and signals. These are a different kind of inhabitant. Where the tower-trees are sessile, monumental, committed to their locations for the span of their existence, the vines are — not mobile, exactly, but *exploratory*. They grow. Constantly. Their tips extend at rates that are unusual for plants, centimeters per day, probing the spaces between towers, finding anchor-points, wrapping and braiding and thickening until a new cable is established. Then the growing tip moves on, leaving the cable behind as a permanent piece of infrastructure.

The vines are the city's capacity for change. Not rapid change — nothing here is rapid — but *adaptive* change, the slow rerouting of connections in response to shifts in the city's needs. When the reservoir lake's water level fluctuates (it does fluctuate, slightly, on cycles you cannot perceive because they are longer than the span of your attention), the vine-cables respond by growing new channels or allowing old ones to lignify and harden into permanent aqueducts or, sometimes, to be slowly strangled and reabsorbed as the network withdraws its support and the vine's own tissues are broken down by fungi and returned to the system.

The vines do not know they are doing this. They are responding to chemical gradients in the substrate, to the availability of light at their growing tips, to the mechanical stresses of wind and weight. But the fungal network *mediates* those gradients. It creates conditions that encourage growth in one direction and discourage it in another. It is gardening the vines, except the gardener is also a garden, and the garden is also a city, and the city is also just a very large chemistry experiment running on geological time.

The vines are the third inhabitant: the connective. The reaching. The part of the city that is always, in its slow way, under construction.

---

**The mosses.**

You barely noticed them, because they are everywhere, the way you barely notice the color of air. The moss that carpets every horizontal surface — every plaza, every root-street, every window-sill where the ferns grow in their precise rows — is not one species but many, and they are perhaps the most sophisticated component of the city's living surface.

The mosses regulate humidity. They regulate temperature. They regulate the albedo of every surface they cover, absorbing or reflecting light in proportions that maintain the thermal equilibrium of the spaces they inhabit. They are, in aggregate, the city's skin — its interface with the atmosphere, its first line of defense against desiccation or waterlogging, its thermostat.

In the plazas, where the moss grows thickest, you can see — if you kneel and look closely, which you should — that the surface is not uniform. There are patterns. Not obvious patterns, not geometric shapes or symbols or anything that suggests communication or art. But *functional* patterns: regions of slightly different moss species, or different densities of the same species, arranged in ways that channel water droplets toward the nearest root-channel, or that create micro-climates of slightly warmer or cooler air, or that — and this is the part that will keep you kneeling on the moss-floor for a long time, staring — that produce subtly different colors of green in arrangements that, from above, from a height you cannot reach, might resolve into something.

You don't know what they would resolve into. You can't get high enough. The towers don't have observation decks. The dome is closed.

The mosses are the fourth inhabitant: the surface. The interface. The part of the city that touches the air and the light and the water and mediates between them and everything beneath.

---

**The ferns.**

The small ferns growing in precise rows on the tower window-sills deserve their own consideration, because they are the most *decorative*-looking element of the city, and that word — decorative — opens a door you may not want to walk through.

They are not decorative. They are functional. Each row of ferns on each window-sill is oriented to capture light at a specific angle and to transpire water at a specific rate, creating a microclimate inside the tower-room behind the window that is subtly different from the climate of the room above or below. The ferns differentiate the tower's interior into zones. Temperature zones, humidity zones, zones of slightly different atmospheric chemistry. Why? For what purpose? The rooms are empty. No one lives in them. No one has ever lived in them. The zones exist for the benefit of — themselves? The tower? The network?

Or perhaps the question of purpose is the wrong question. Perhaps the ferns grow in rows on the window-sills because the conditions there — the light, the moisture, the wind, the chemical signals from the tower's living walls — produce rows. Perhaps the microclimate differentiation is not a purpose but a *consequence*, and the consequence has been incorporated into the system's homeostasis because the system incorporates everything, wastes nothing, optimizes relentlessly not because it is trying to optimize but because optimization is what happens when a billion chemical feedback loops run for long enough.

The ferns are the fifth inhabitant: the detail. The granularity. The part of the city that reminds you, when you look at it closely enough, that there is no lower limit to the complexity here, that every surface and every space, no matter how small, is occupied and integrated and alive.

---

**The flowers.**

The great inflorescences that crown the towers. The organ-pipes. The voices of the city.

They are the most visible inhabitants but perhaps the least understood, because their function — the drone — is the function you can least explain in purely biological terms. Everything else the city does makes ecological sense. Water management: essential. Structural integrity: essential. Thermoregulation, nutrient cycling, gas exchange: essential, essential, essential. But the drone? The constant harmonic chord that fills the valley and reverberates off the root-walls and can be heard, you suspect, for kilometers beyond the city's edge?

Here is what you can say: the flowers are tubular. They are adapted to channel wind. They produce sound. The sound is not random — it is structured, harmonic, consistent. The flowers are maintained by the network with the same priority as the water system and the structural wood. When a flower-pipe cracks or wilts (rarely), it is replaced by new growth with extraordinary speed, and the replacement flower is tuned — by the shape of its tube, by the thickness of its walls, by the angle of its opening — to produce the same pitch as its predecessor.

The chord is maintained.

Why? Here is one hypothesis: the sound is a byproduct of wind management, the flowers serving primarily as baffles or funnels that direct airflow across the tower-crowns in ways that optimize gas exchange and cooling, and the sound is simply what happens when you push air through tubes. Here is another hypothesis: the sound serves a communicative function, carrying information across the city's surface that the underground fungal network cannot transmit, a kind of aerial nervous system complementing the subterranean one. Here is a third hypothesis: the sound is structural, the vibrations reinforcing the tower-wood's integrity in ways that are too subtle to observe but real, a constant massage of the cellular architecture that prevents fatigue and cracking.

Here is the hypothesis you don't want to articulate: the city makes the sound because the city makes the sound. Because over the eons of its self-construction, it arrived at this configuration, this chord, this equilibrium, and the chord is part of the equilibrium, and the equilibrium includes the chord, and to ask *why* is to ask why a crystal has six sides. It has six sides because that is what it is. The city drones because the city is a city that drones. The sound is not for anything. The sound is the city being complete.

The flowers are the sixth inhabitant: the voice. The outermost expression. The part of the city that reaches beyond its own borders and fills the air and says, to anything that can hear: *I am here. I am here. I am here.* Not as a message. Not as a warning. Just as a fact, stated continuously, to no one.

---

**The water.**

Seventh inhabitant, though calling it an inhabitant feels wrong — it is more like a medium, a substance the city is partly *made of* and partly *organized around*. The water rises from the young basalt through fractures and porous layers, driven by geothermal heat and capillary action and the tremendous osmotic pull of the root-network above. It enters the city from below, is channeled through the root-streets and vine-cables and tower-trunks, emerges in the dark upwelling pools, feeds the reservoir lake, evaporates from the moss-surfaces and the transpiring walls, rises into the permanent afternoon sky, condenses on the upper canopy-spires when the clouds drift through, and falls again. A closed loop. A circulatory system with no heart — or with a million hearts, every cell of every root drawing water upward, every stoma on every leaf releasing it, the entire city functioning as a single vast pump that moves water from the deep rock to the atmosphere and back again.

The water is not alive. But it is not inert, either. It carries dissolved minerals, organic acids, signaling compounds, nutrients, waste products. It is the blood of the city, and like blood, it is both a substance and a system, both a material and a process. Without it, everything stops. With it, everything continues. The water does not live here. The water *is* here, the same way space is here, the same way time is here: a condition of the city's existence, not a resident of it, but so thoroughly entangled with every living process that separating the water from the life would be like separating the ocean from its waves.

---

**The salamander.**

You saw it. Dark-backed, glistening, the size of a cat. It crossed the plaza in an unhurried diagonal and vanished.

It is the closest thing to a resident you will find here that has eyes, legs, a brain, volition. And it is — you must understand this — *not part of the city*. It is a visitor. A squatter. A small animal that has found the moss-floors damp and warm and the water-channels full of the tiny invertebrates that inevitably colonize any wet environment, and that has decided, in its simple amphibian way, to stay.

There are perhaps a few dozen of them. Perhaps more. You rarely see them because the city is vast and they are small and secretive and largely nocturnal — or they would be nocturnal if there were night here, but there isn't, so they have settled into rhythms dictated by their own physiology rather than by the light, sleeping in root-crevices for long hours, emerging to feed, retreating again. They eat what they find. They leave their waste on the moss-floor, where it is almost immediately broken down by the fungal network and recycled. They breed, presumably, in the warm pools. Their tadpoles, if they have tadpoles, swim in city water and breathe city oxygen and consume city algae and are, for the span of their larval lives, almost indistinguishable from any other small aquatic organism in the system.

The city does not notice them. Or — it notices them the way your body notices a mote of dust on your skin: registers, does not react, continues. The salamanders are below the threshold of relevance. They consume nothing the city needs. They damage nothing. They contribute nothing of significance. They are simply *there*, the way wind-blown seeds land on rooftops and germinate in gutters — opportunists occupying a niche that exists only because the city's own completeness leaves margins, cracks, tiny unutilized spaces where something small and unambitious can persist.

And yet. Watch the salamander when it crosses the plaza. Watch its unhurried gait, its pauses, the way it lifts its blunt head and seems to *listen* to the drone. It does not listen, of course. It does not have the neural architecture for musical appreciation. It is sensing vibrations through its jaw, through the bones of its skull, interpreting them as environmental data — the drone tells it nothing has changed, the city is stable, there is no predator, no storm, no disruption. The drone is, for the salamander, a kind of all-clear signal, and in its presence the animal is calm, unhurried, unafraid.

The salamander is the eighth inhabitant, and the most like you: a creature with a body and a direction and a small warm life, moving through a place that was not built for it, drawing comfort from its constancy, unable to comprehend it, not required to.

---

**What might be wings.**

You heard them. A papery flutter in the upper canopy, brief, then gone. Something is up there — something that flies, or glides, or at least moves through the air in the high spaces between tower-crowns. You cannot see it. You will not see it. The canopy is dense up there, the flower-structures enormous, and whatever moves among them is either very small or very good at concealment or both.

Here is what you can infer: it is not a bird. There are no birds here. The city's sound-environment — that constant, all-encompassing drone — would make birdsong pointless, and birds without song are birds without territory, without mating displays, without most of the behavioral architecture that makes a bird a bird. Whatever flies up there has no need for sound. It navigates by other means. Scent, perhaps, or magnetism, or the detection of the faint electrical fields that the flower-pipes generate as wind moves through them.

It might be an insect, if insects exist here at the macro scale. It might be something else — a gliding amphibian, a winged seed-structure that has achieved something approaching locomotion, a fragment of the city itself that has broken free and is drifting through its own airspace like a shed cell circulating in the bloodstream.

You don't know. You will not know. The city has its upper stories, its inaccessible canopy-world, and whatever lives there lives beyond your reach and your comprehension, and the city does not care that you are curious, and the flutter does not come again.

The ninth inhabitant: the unseen. The overhead. The part of the city's ecology that exists in the vertical space you cannot access and that may or may not be alive and may or may not be part of the city and may or may not be watching you right now from behind a curtain of white silent flowers on the dome's curved surface.

---

**The dome.**

At last. The center. The closed structure with no openings, no windows, no doors, where the white flowers grow silently and the sound you hear — or imagine — through the shell is the sound of growth that has reached its destination.

What lives inside the dome?

You pressed your ear to the shell. You heard the deep sound. And now you must confront the possibility — not a certainty, never a certainty, the city does not deal in certainties, only in states of equilibrium — that the dome contains the densest, most concentrated expression of whatever the city *is*.

Here is what might be inside:

The root-network, at its most compressed and interconnected. A knot of mycorrhizal tissue so dense it has become something else — not brain, not nervous system, not processor, but *node*. A place where the chemical signals from every part of the city converge and interact and produce emergent patterns that are — not thoughts. Not decisions. But *states*. Configurations of molecular activity that correspond to the city's current condition the way a face corresponds to an emotion: not the thing itself, but a readable expression of it.

Or: nothing. An empty space, a hollow at the heart, a void around which the city organized itself the way a pearl organizes itself around a grain of sand. The dome is not a container. It is a *boundary*. What it encloses is absence, and the absence is structurally necessary, a still point in the turning world of growth and maintenance, a place where the system does not reach because the system needs a place it does not reach in order to have shape, in order to have an inside and an outside, in order to be a *thing* rather than an undifferentiated mass of living tissue spreading without limit.

Or: a seed. Something at the center of the dome that is not yet what it will be. A structure in arrested development, held in stasis by the city's homeostasis, waiting — not consciously, not with anticipation, but in the way that a seed in dry soil waits — for conditions that have not yet arrived. The city is complete, yes. But complete the way an egg is complete: finished in its current form, containing within it the potential for a radically different form, a form that will emerge only when some threshold is crossed, some signal received, some condition in the deep substrate or the permanent sky or the unwavering light finally *changes* for the first time in however long this afternoon has lasted.

Or: something you cannot conceive of, because you are an animal with eyes and hands and a brain that models the world in terms of objects and agents and purposes, and the thing inside the dome is none of those things, belongs to none of your categories, and the reason the dome has no openings is not to keep you out but because *openness* is an animal concept, a concept that implies that seeing is understanding, that access is knowledge, that if you could just get inside you would *know* — and the city, in its vast non-thinking wisdom, in its billion-year-old chemical patience, knows (without knowing) that you would not. You would see tissue. You would see moisture. You would see darkness and density and the slow pulse of hydraulic movement. And you would not understand, because understanding is not what the dome is for. The dome is not for anything. The dome is.

The tenth inhabitant: the interior. The unknown. The living heart of a living city that has no heart, that has no center, that has a center anyway, that is closed, that breathes, that sounds the deep sound of maintenance, that will not open, that does not need to open, that is complete.

---

**And you.**

Standing in the warm unmoving light. Hearing the drone. Feeling the dampness on your skin. You are the eleventh inhabitant, the temporary one, the one with questions, the one who walked the root-streets and touched the bark-walls and circled the dome and heard the sound and wanted — needed — to know who lives here.

Everyone you've met so far. The network, the wood, the vines, the mosses, the ferns, the flowers, the water, the salamander, the unseen flutter, the closed interior. None of them will greet you. None of them will acknowledge you. You can stay here for the rest of your life and the city will grow around you the way it grows around a stone — slowly, incorporating, not hostile, not welcoming, simply *continuing* — and eventually your bones will become part of the root-matrix and your calcium will feed the mosses and your water will rejoin the circulation and the flowers will drone over the place where you stood and there will be no monument, no marker, no memory, because the city does not form memories. The city forms *structures*. And you were never quite structural enough to matter.

But you were here. You heard it. That counts for something, even if only to you. Even if the city cannot count, has never counted, will never count. You stood inside a living thing that built itself into a civilization without ever having a thought, and you thought about it, and that — the thinking — was yours alone, the one thing in the entire Grown City that belonged to only you.

Take it with you when you leave. Nothing else here is yours to take.
