# The Inhabitant

---

She is already here.

She has been here the entire time you've been standing in the hollow, reading the walls with your hands, letting the silk-tunnel creatures map your knuckles. She came in through the root-door passage while you were listening to the basin, and she has been sitting in the second alcove—not the largest, not the smallest, the one whose opening faces the densest cluster of hanging colony—with her knees drawn up and her back pressed into the living root-curve, and she has been watching you the way the salamander watches: without eyes, exactly. Without eyes as you would use them.

She sees. But not with what you'd call sight.

---

Her eyes are open. That is the first thing you would notice if the light held long enough—they are open, and they are not vestigial, not sealed, not clouded over. They are dark, fully dark, the iris and pupil merged into a single absorptive surface that reflects nothing back. They function, but they function at the absolute basement of the visible spectrum, pulling signal from gradients of warmth and bioluminescence that your own retinas would discard as noise. She does not experience darkness the way you do. For her, this hollow is not dark. It is *low.* A low room. A low hum of thermal and luminous information that she reads the way you read a face in candlelight—enough to work with, enough to know.

When the basin creatures flared, she saw you entire. Your shape, your heat, the fog of your exhaled breath, the silk creatures pausing on your skin. She saw you and she did not move, and that not-moving was not fear. It was assessment. She has been assessing since the flare, and she is still assessing, her breathing so controlled it produces no sound that rises above the tick of the water, no fog that reaches beyond the alcove's edge.

She is deciding what you are. Not *who*. What.

---

Her body is small. Not stunted—proportioned, complete, dense with the particular compactness of someone who has never had the luxury of unnecessary mass. She would stand to your mid-chest if she stood, but she would not stand. Not yet. Not while you're here. She moves through this space on her hands and knees and belly, and her body is shaped by that movement the way a swimmer's body is shaped by water. Broad through the shoulders and upper back. Arms longer than you'd expect relative to her torso, heavily muscled through the forearms, the hands wide and flat with fingers that spread and grip with a prehensile certainty that makes your own hands feel like mittens. Her hips are narrow. Her legs are strong but short, bent at rest in a deep squat that she can hold for hours because her joints have been holding it for years—since before memory, since whatever childhood exists in this world of root-walls and permanent dusk.

Her skin is cool. Not cold—there is blood beneath it, warm blood, a mammalian furnace running at the lowest sustainable output—but cool, regulated, heat held inward and parceled out with a miser's precision. The surface of her skin is not smooth. It has a quality—a fine, almost imperceptible texture, like suede, like the nap of moss, that serves multiple purposes you would need weeks to catalogue. It holds a thin film of moisture from the saturated air, and that film is not passive; it is a sensory organ, a whole-body humidity detector that tells her the state of the hollow's transpiration cycle the way your inner ear tells you which way is down. The silk-tunnel creatures move across her skin freely, and they leave something behind—a faint secretion, a residue that her skin absorbs, and which you suspect is medicinal, or nutritive, or both.

She is not naked. But what she wears is difficult to separate from what she is.

Around her hips and over one shoulder, a wrapping made from something fibrous—not woven, *felted*, pressed and matted into a thick, flexible layer that sits against her skin like a second dermis. It is made from the molt-fibers of the hanging colony creatures, she has gathered them over seasons, pressing them wet into sheets that she works with her hands until they become cloth. The fabric is alive with mites. This is intentional. The mites keep it clean, keep it from harboring the fungi that would otherwise colonize any organic surface in this humidity. She smells like the hollow—resinous, damp, faintly sour in the way of healthy fermentation—and the mites in her clothing are part of that smell, part of the ecosystem she carries on her body wherever she moves.

Her hair is thick, dark, and has not been cut so much as managed—twisted into ropy cords that she keeps pulled back and pinned against her skull with a clip made from something that might be bone, might be the hardened casing of one of the silk-tunnel creatures grown to unusual size. The cords serve a purpose beyond aesthetics: in the perpetual damp, loose hair would mat, would mold, would become a liability. The twisted structure lets air reach her scalp. She figured this out, or someone taught her. Someone before her, in this hollow or another one.

---

Her hands.

You need to understand her hands.

They are the most important thing about her, more important than her eyes, more important than the deep acoustic sensitivity of her inner ear that lets her map the hollow by the echo of a single water drop. Her hands are her primary interface with everything. They are calloused, yes—the description above got that right—but the callouses are not uniform. They are *patterned*. Thick pads on the heels of both palms, where she grips and presses and fits. Smoother skin on the fingertips, deliberately maintained, kept sensitive by some practice or substance, because the fingertips are where the fine work happens. She reads the root walls with her fingertips. She reads the silk structures. She reads the health of the colony creatures by running her fingers along the canopy underside and feeling the vibration of their clustered bodies. She reads the water in the basin by dipping two fingers and holding them still, feeling the coiling movement of the luminous filter-creatures, gauging their activity, their number, their mood if mood is the right word.

She shapes stone with these hands—not by knapping, which requires a percussive control that would echo through the hollow and disturb the colony—but by abrasion. Rubbing. She has stones cached in the root wall near the largest alcove, pairs of them, a harder against a softer, and she works them against each other in slow, patient, circular motions that produce a sound so quiet it blends with the water-tick and disappears. It takes her days to produce an edge. Weeks to produce a tool. She has four tools that she considers finished. They rest in a silk-lined cavity in the wall, and the silk-tunnel creatures tend them, keeping the cavity dry, keeping fungal growth from colonizing the handles she has bound on with sinew from the salamander-things she traps and eats.

She eats the salamanders raw. She eats them fresh. She kills them quickly, a precise strike to the base of the broad skull, and she eats every part—the meat, the organs, the skin with its camouflage tubercles. She eats the fruiting bodies of the wall fungi, but selectively, and only at certain stages of emergence. She knows which ones are safe by texture, by the way they feel between her fingertips, by a faint difference in the thermal signature that her palm detects when held close. She eats some of the silk-tunnel creatures, the larger ones, biting them cleanly and chewing the soft bodies with a matter-of-fact efficiency. She drinks from the basin, but carefully, cupping water with one hand and bringing it to her lips without disturbing the filter-creatures, without reaching in farther than the second knuckle.

She does not eat the colony creatures. She does not harm them. When one dies—and they die often, their individual lifespans are short, weeks perhaps—it falls from the ceiling cluster, and she finds it by the change in the heat pattern above, by the faint *tick* of its small body hitting the sphagnum. She picks it up. She holds it. And then she takes it to the basin and slides it into the water, where the luminous filter-creatures receive it and begin their slow disassembly. This is a ritual, or it is waste management, or it is both, and she does not distinguish between those categories because the distinction does not exist in the logic of this place.

---

She is not alone. But she is alone right now.

There are others. The passage behind the root-door leads to a network—not extensive, not a city, but a linked series of hollows, five or six of them, spread through the lowland over a distance you could walk in an hour if you knew the way. Each hollow is shaped. Each has its own basin, its own colony, its own population of silk-tunnel creatures and salamander-things. The hollows are connected by root-passages that she and others have grown over what must be years—decades—by the same patient method of cutting and wedging and waiting for the living architecture to fill in around the intention.

The others are like her. Small, dense, dark-eyed, quiet. They move between hollows on schedules governed by the colony creatures' cycles, by the fruiting patterns of the wall fungi, by the availability of the salamander-things, by signals she reads in the bioluminescent flicker of the basin fauna that you could not detect if you spent a year learning. They communicate with each other in sounds—but not speech as you would recognize it. Low clicks, tonal hums, breath-shapes that carry information the way the water-tick carries the state of the hollow's hydration. Spoken in the dark, these sounds map to the acoustic geometry of the root-walled spaces; they carry farther and more clearly than any whispered word because they are shaped to the architecture, fitted to it the way the stones in the basin are fitted to the earth.

She learned this language from someone. There was an older one, once, in this hollow, who shaped the largest alcove and began the root-door passage. The older one is dead now. The bone clip in her hair might have belonged to the older one, or it might not; the attachment to objects in this world is practical, not sentimental, and when someone dies their tools are redistributed by need, their body given to the basin. But the older one's work persists in the walls. The choices they made—which root to cut, which direction to nudge the growth, where to place the stone basin relative to the canopy punctures so the rain would fill it without flooding it—these are the older one's thoughts, still thinking, still shaping the hollow years after the thinker entered the water.

She knows this. She feels it in the walls.

---

She is not hiding from you. She is doing something more precise than hiding. She is *withholding herself from the space you occupy*. She has compressed her thermal signature by pressing her back into the root wall, which wicks her body heat into its damp lattice and disperses it. She has slowed her breathing to a rate that produces no audible turbulence. She has tucked her hands against her stomach, where the mite-colony in her felted wrap will keep them warm enough to remain functional when she needs them. She is a hollow within the hollow, a negative space, and if you did not know she was here—and you don't—you could search this place with your hands and miss her, because she would shift as you approached, sliding along the wall with a fluidity that the silk-tunnel creatures would not even register as movement, and when your hand arrived at the alcove she had just been sitting in, it would be empty, and still warm in a way you would attribute to the root wall's own faint biological heat.

She is not afraid of you. She is not unafraid either. She is in a state that does not map to those words—a state of total sensory engagement, every surface of her body reading the space, reading you, reading the responses of the colony above and the filter-creatures below and the silk-tunnel traffic in the walls, integrating all of it into a continuously updated model of the situation. She knows your mass. She knows your temperature. She knows that you breathe too fast and too loud and that your body is radiating heat at a rate that means you are losing it, which means you are not adapted, which means you will become distressed before long. She knows this and she is factoring it in.

She is waiting for you to leave.

Not because she wants you gone, necessarily. But because you are *loud*. Not in the acoustic sense—you are standing still, you are trying to be quiet—but in every other register. Your heat is shouting. Your smell is shouting. The silk-tunnel creatures are detouring around you in patterns that disrupt their wall-maintenance cycles, and the colony above has shifted its clustering in response to your thermal column, pulling slightly away from the area directly above you, and the filter-creatures in the basin have dimmed. You are an event. You are a disturbance propagating through a finely tuned system, and every minute you stand here the system is adjusting, spending energy it would rather not spend, and she can feel every adjustment the way you can feel someone bumping your chair.

She will wait. She is extraordinary at waiting. She can hold this compressed stillness for hours—she has done it before, when a burrowing predator from the surface found one of the canopy punctures and pushed its blunt, sensing snout into the hollow, filling the air with the wrong kind of heat, the wrong kind of smell. She waited then. The predator left. The system recovered. She emerged and checked the colony and the basin and the walls, running her hands over everything, re-reading the state of her world.

She will do the same when you leave. She will emerge from the alcove slowly, unfolding her body joint by joint, pressing her palms flat to the sphagnum floor and feeling for the disturbances you've left in its surface. She will check the basin first. Then the colony. Then the wall-silk. She will run her hands over the pegs and the provisions, verifying that nothing has been taken, noting the way the luminous thing on the bladder has shifted its position—it's moved toward the side nearest where you were standing, attracted by your heat or your novelty. She will touch it. It will pulse. The colony will rustle. And the hollow will re-establish its equilibrium around her, the way it always does, because she is not a disturbance in this system.

She is the system.

---

There is one more thing.

In the deepest part of the largest alcove, behind the root-door, beyond the passage that leads toward the neighboring hollows, there is a chamber she has been growing for three seasons. It is barely large enough to hold her in a crouch. The walls are denser here—she has redirected more roots, packed the lattice tighter, created a space that is structurally distinct from the rest of the hollow. It is warmer. The root density holds heat. The sphagnum lining is thicker, replaced more often, the freshest and driest she can find.

She is building a nursery.

She has not told anyone, in clicks or hums or breath-shapes. The others have not been to this part of her hollow. But the colony creatures know—a small splinter group has already begun to cluster on the ceiling of the new chamber, drawn by the elevated warmth, and their presence draws the silk-tunnel creatures, and the silk-tunnel creatures draw the mites, and the mites condition the sphagnum, and the system is already bootstrapping itself into the new space, preparing, the way a body prepares before the mind has decided anything.

She presses her hands against the nursery walls at least once a day. She feels the roots growing. She feels the density increasing, the structure solidifying. She has planted a wedge of shaped wood that will, in a season, redirect a thick rhizome into an arch over the entrance—a load-bearing arch, the kind the older one taught her to grow, the kind that will hold even when the root system above shifts in a storm, even when the earth moves, even when the weight of the canopy presses down with the full burden of rainfall.

She does not yet know if there will be someone to put in the nursery. That is not the point. The point is that the space exists, that it is being grown, that the system is expanding to include something that has not yet arrived. The point is the wedge of shaped wood and the patient, daily reading of the wall and the small colony of warm-blooded creatures already gathering on the ceiling of a room that is still becoming a room.

The point is: even here, in the dark, in the cold, in a wound in the ground that never healed, at the bottom of a world that may not have a sun—

Even here, someone is building forward.

---

She hears you shift your weight. She tracks the sound. She waits.

The water ticks. The colony breathes. The silk flows through the walls.

She is home.
