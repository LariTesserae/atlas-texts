# The Ones Who Count

They are not far. They are never far. But they are *low* to the ground and the color of the ground, and in the strobing, shadowless, pewter-colored non-light of this place, your eyes slide over them the way water slides over the stone—without catching.

---

There are perhaps two dozen of them across the span of flats you can see, though "see" is generous. You become aware of the first one only because it moves against the rhythm of everything else. The crawlers move with geological patience. The ribbon-swimmers move with fluid, schooling urgency. The drifting sacs move with the wind. But this—this thing on the next stone swell—moves with *hesitation*. It stops. It starts. It adjusts. It reconsiders. It is the only thing on the flats that appears to be making decisions it is not fully committed to, and that quality of *indecision*, of visible deliberation, is what makes it finally resolve out of the background and into your recognition as a living, thinking creature.

It is smaller than you expected. Smaller than you, certainly—perhaps the size of a large dog if a large dog had been pressed flat and widened, as though the low sky had been leaning on its ancestors for so long that they eventually accommodated it. Its body plan is built around a broad, low center of gravity. Four limbs, thick and short, ending in wide pads that splay against the wet stone with obvious grip—you can see the tendons work as each pad flexes and conforms to the surface, finding purchase on stone so smooth that you yourself would struggle to walk on it without slipping. The skin is dark, that same iron-grey as the bedrock, but alive with a subtle mottling that shifts as the lightning shifts, lighter patches blooming and fading in what might be camouflage or might be something else—some involuntary response to the electromagnetic saturation of the air, the skin itself reacting to the static charge the way your arm hair rises before a close strike. The texture is dense and finely ridged, like wet leather that has been worked and worked until it is half-mineral itself, and it gleams the same way the stone gleams: with a thin film of iron-tinged water that it does not bother to shed.

The head is broad and flat, with a heavy shelf of brow—or what functions as brow, a ridge of thickened tissue and maybe cartilage that extends forward over the sensory organs like an awning. The eyes beneath it are the first surprise. They are large, larger than seems proportionate, and they are *pale*—a washed-out amber, almost golden, with wide pupils that are not round but horizontally elongated, like a goat's, evolved to take in as much of the dim, flat light as possible. In the lightning-flash they contract sharply and then dilate again, and there's a nictitating membrane that sweeps across them with each major pulse, a reflex so fast and so constant it looks like a tic. These eyes are built for a world of relentless visual noise, and they have learned to filter it, to see *through* the strobing rather than be blinded by it. When the creature on the stone swell turns its head in your direction, you understand immediately that it has seen you, has been seeing you, has been watching you with the same patient, evaluative attention with which you've been watching it. The gaze is not hostile. It is not friendly. It is *assessing*, with the calm, unhurried quality of something that has a great deal of time and very few emergencies.

There are no external ears. Instead, along each side of the head and trailing down the thick neck, there are rows of small, slightly recessed openings—tympanic pits, maybe, or something analogous—dozens of them, each one a slightly different size. You realize with a slow chill that these are *tuned*. Each pit resonates at a different frequency. The creature does not hear the thunder as you hear it, as a wall of undifferentiated roar. It hears it the way a musician hears a chord: broken into components, each frequency arriving at its own dedicated receptor, the whole cacophony parsed and separated and *read*. The thunder is not noise to this creature. The thunder is information. It tells you where the storm cells are, how far, how fast they're moving, whether the rain will come here or there, whether the pools are filling or draining three swells over. It is a sonar built not from signals the creature emits but from signals the world provides—endlessly, generously, never stopping.

The mouth is a wide, lipless line, set low on the flat face. It opens, occasionally, and you see that it is full of short, dense, peg-like structures—not exactly teeth, not exactly baleen, something in between—designed for crushing and grinding. The crawlers. It eats the crawlers. You watch, later, as one of them finds a large crawler on the stone, picks it up with broad, dexterous forepads—the digits are short and thick but surprisingly nimble, four of them, with blunt claws that can grip and pry and, crucially, *knap flint*—and turns it over with a practiced motion, exposing the soft underside. It does not eat it immediately. It examines it. Runs a pad across the growth rings on the shell. Then it eats it, crushing through the ceramic-hard shell with a series of deliberate, grinding bites, the sound of which you cannot hear at all beneath the thunder.

But the crawlers are not the only food, or even the primary food. You begin to understand this as you watch more of them—as your eyes learn the trick of finding them against the stone, and you realize there are more than two dozen, there may be forty, fifty, spread across the visible flats in a distribution that mirrors the crawlers' distribution, that mirrors the water-films' distribution, that mirrors the rain. They spend most of their time at the edges of the shallow pools, and the tool you found—the knapped quartz blade—makes sense now, because they use them to scrape the rock at the waterline where the microbial film is thickest, where the chemolithotrophic crust concentrates into a dense, paste-like layer of biological mineral wealth. They scrape it, collect it on the blade's flat, and eat it directly, or—and this is the thing that stops you—they sometimes carry the scrapings to another individual. A smaller one. One that is not scraping for itself.

The young are nearly invisible. They are half the size of the adults and flatter still, pressed so close to the stone they look like shadows, and they stay near the pools' centers where the water is deepest—where the ribbon-swimmers school, where the drifters' fallen membranes collect, where there is the most to scavenge with the least effort. They do not yet have the heavy brow ridge. Their eyes are proportionally even larger, enormous pale coins in flat dark faces, and they blink constantly—the nictitating membrane working overtime as though they haven't yet calibrated it to the lightning's rhythm. An adult brings a blade loaded with mineral paste, and the young one takes it—not from the blade, but blade and all, gripping it in its own small forepads and scraping the paste off with its mouth and then *holding the blade*, turning it, examining it with those huge blinking eyes the way the adult examined the crawler's growth rings. Learning the tool. Learning what it is for.

---

They do not speak. Not in any way you can identify as language—no vocalizations, no calls, no cries. The thunder would make that pointless. Any sound they produced would be swallowed instantly by the roar. But they are not silent communicators either. Watch their skin. The mottling—the shifting patterns of lighter and darker grey that you thought might be camouflage or static response—is *both* of those things and also a third thing. When two of them are near each other, the patterns synchronize, then diverge, then synchronize again in a different configuration. It is not fast. It is not the rapid chromatic signaling of a cuttlefish. It is slow, deliberate, shifting over the course of seconds—a vocabulary of gradients, of spreading and contracting pale regions against the dark, readable even in the dim, flickering light because the eye that evolved here is built to detect exactly this kind of subtle, slow change against a background of violent, rapid change. The lightning is fast and bright. The skin-speech is slow and dim. They carved out a communicative niche in the one channel the thunder left open.

You watch two adults meet near a cairn. Their skin ripples through a sequence: a bloom of paleness spreading from the center of the back outward, then contracting to the limbs, then darkening entirely, then blooming again from the head backward. The other responds with a variant—the bloom starts at one side and sweeps across. They do this for several minutes, standing a body-length apart on the wet stone, their wide pale eyes fixed on each other's flanks. Then one of them turns and moves to the cairn and adds a stone. The other watches. The first one scratches something on the new stone—you are too far away to see what. Then they separate, moving in different directions across the flats, and their skin fades back to the neutral, shifting camouflage of solitude.

---

The cairns are not graves. They are not territorial markers. You become increasingly certain, as you watch, that they are *records*.

The circle with the central dot: you see one of them scratch it, and then stand near it, and its skin-pattern does something you haven't seen before—a rapid, almost convulsive flickering, a whole-body pulse that starts and stops and starts, like someone trying to say something in a language they are still inventing. It is facing the sky when it does this. Facing the cloud ceiling, the place where the sun would be if you could see it, and the flickering has a quality of *reaching*, of *trying*—

And you think: the circle with the dot is the sun.

They have never seen it. Perhaps their ancestors did, long ago, before the storms became permanent, before the clouds closed over the world like a fist. But these ones have not. The sun is a legend, an inherited memory encoded in the slow language of shifting skin, a shape scratched on stone because it is too important to trust to any one body's lifespan. The dot in the center is not a pupil, not a marker of center—it is an assertion of *presence*. Something is up there. Something warm and bright that none of them have directly experienced. Something that would explain the brighter region that moves across the cloud ceiling, that provides the only rhythm in this rhythmless world, that tracks from one unseen horizon to the other and then vanishes, leaving the flats to the deeper dark where the lightning is everything.

And the tally marks beneath it—the counting—

They are not counting days since the thunder stopped. The thunder has never stopped. The tally marks are something else.

You watch one of them, late in the cycle when the brighter region has moved to the edge of the sky and the light is draining and the lightning is becoming the world's only illumination. It is crouched near a cairn, its forepads working a quartz blade against the flat stone surface, scratching a new mark. One more line in a row that already has hundreds, some of them weathered nearly to nothing, some of them fresh. It finishes the mark and sits back—if "sits" is the right word for how these low, broad bodies settle their weight—and places one pad flat on the stone surface, on the marks, and goes still.

Its skin does the reaching-flicker again, the convulsive whole-body pulse aimed at the sky.

And you understand, finally, that it is not counting toward something or away from something. It is counting *itself*. Counting the days it has been here, alive, on this stone, in this thunder, under this ceiling that hides the thing it draws but has never seen. The marks are a declaration, scratched into rock that will outlast the scratcher by eons: *I was here on this day. I was here on this day too. And this one. And this one.* A ledger of existence kept against the indifference of a world so old that its mountains have been worn to nothing, a world that would erase every trace of these creatures in a geological heartbeat if they stopped refreshing their marks, stopped building their cairns, stopped teaching their young how to hold the blade.

The tool by the pool was not forgotten. It was *left*. The way you might leave a note on a door for someone you expect to come by later. A blade by the water where the microbial crust is rich, placed on a flat stone so it won't be carried away by the shifting water-film. A gift, or an invitation, or simply a practical kindness: *I was done here and you might not be. Here. Use this.*

---

There are old ones.

You find them near the largest cairns—the ones with the most marks, the ones whose base stones are so weathered they are almost unreadable. The old ones are bigger than the others, but not by much; mostly they are *thicker*, their skin darker and more ridged, encrusted with a fine mineral deposit that they no longer bother to scrape away, so that they are becoming, slowly and visibly, part of the landscape they live on. Their eyes are cloudier but no less attentive. Their forepads are calloused into something nearly indistinguishable from the stone they've spent a lifetime gripping. They move less. They scrape less. They eat less, though the younger ones bring them food—crawlers, mineral paste on blades, even the occasional ribbon-swimmer caught in a draining pool and carried, still writhing, across the stone.

The old ones spend most of their time at the cairns, adding stones, adding marks. Their skin-speech is different from the younger ones'—slower, more complex, with layered patterns that move at different speeds simultaneously, as though they are saying several things at once or saying one thing with more nuance than a younger skin can manage. When a younger one approaches an old one at a cairn, the younger one's skin goes muted, almost blank—a listening posture, a readiness to receive. And the old one's skin blooms into something extraordinary: a full-body narrative of shifting light and dark that goes on for minutes, telling—*teaching*—something that the younger one watches with its entire body pressed to the stone and its huge pale eyes unblinking.

You cannot read it. You will never be able to read it. But you can see the shape of what it is: a history, a record, an oral tradition that is neither oral nor written but *dermal*, passed from skin to skin in a language of light and pattern that requires no sound because sound was never available, that requires no permanence because the cairns provide the permanence, the stones and their scratched symbols serving as mnemonic anchors for a living knowledge that rides on the surface of bodies that are themselves slowly turning to stone.

---

Sometimes one of them dies.

It happens quietly—or rather, it happens at the same volume as everything else, which is the volume of unceasing thunder, so "quietly" means only that the creature itself makes no fuss. It settles near a cairn, usually its own cairn, the one with its marks. It places its pads flat on the stone. Its skin goes through one last sequence—not the reaching-flicker, not the conversational blooms, but something private and slow, a gradual dimming, a withdrawal of pattern, a return to the uniform dark grey of unmarked stone. And then it is still, and the mineral rain begins its work immediately, coating the body in the same iron-ochre film that coats everything here, and within days it is a shape on the stone that could be mistaken for a natural formation, and the crawlers are already climbing it, grazing its surface with their ceramic patience.

The others do not mourn in any way you can recognize. But they visit the cairn. They add to it—not a stone from elsewhere, but a piece of the dead one's own encrusted skin, pried carefully from the body before the crawlers take it all, placed at the top of the cairn like a capstone. Mineral skin on mineral stone. And beneath it, scratched fresh: the circle with the dot.

*You were here,* it says. *Under the thing we have never seen. You were here and you counted your days and now someone else will count theirs.*

---

One of them is watching you now. It has been watching you for some time. It is crouched on a low swell of stone perhaps fifty meters away, its body pressed flat and motionless, its wide amber eyes catching the lightning in pale flashes. Its skin is doing something you haven't seen directed at you before—a slow, cautious pattern, a tentative extension of paleness toward you and then a retraction, like someone reaching out a hand and then pulling it back, uncertain. It does this three times. Four times.

It is trying to talk to you.

You do not have the skin for it. You cannot answer in its language, cannot bloom or dim or ripple your surface in the slow, meaningful gradients it is offering. You are a creature of sound and air, standing dumbly in a world where sound is owned by the sky and air is thick with metal and water. You are a smooth, patternless enigma to it—a thing that does not speak, does not scrape, does not count, does not build. A drifter without filaments. A crawler without rings.

But it does not stop trying. The pattern repeats—reach, retract, reach, retract—and then, slowly, it changes. The paleness extends further and stays longer before withdrawing. The rhythm slows. It is simplifying its language for you, the way you might speak slowly and clearly to someone you suspect does not share your tongue. It is offering the most basic version of whatever it has to say, stripped of nuance, reduced to a single, repeating pulse:

*Here. Here. Here.*

A statement of presence. An acknowledgment that you exist on this stone, in this thunder, under this hidden sun. The most fundamental unit of its language, the thing that underlies every tally mark and every cairn and every circle scratched on ancient rock. Not *hello*, not *who are you*, not *what do you want*. Just:

*Here.*

And you stand there, rain-slicked, deafened, tasting iron on your tongue, and you realize that this is enough. This is the entire vocabulary that matters in a world this old, this scoured, this loud. The thunder says nothing. The stone says nothing. The rain says nothing. But the creatures on the stone, the ones who scrape and count and teach and build and die and leave their mineral skin on cairns—they say the only thing worth saying, the only thing that can be said against the immensity of a world that does not care whether anyone is listening:

*Here. I am here. I am still here.*

The lightning pulses. The thunder rolls. The crawler at your feet rasps its slow way across the stone. And the creature on the swell watches you with its pale, ancient, patient eyes, its skin still reaching toward you in that slow, hopeful rhythm, waiting to see if you will find some way—any way—to say it back.
