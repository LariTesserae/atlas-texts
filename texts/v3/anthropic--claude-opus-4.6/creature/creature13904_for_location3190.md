# The People of the Hanging Gardens

---

They are smaller than you expect. Not dramatically so — not a miniature people, nothing from a fable — but compact, dense-boned, with a center of gravity that sits low in the hips. Their feet are wide. Remarkably wide, with toes that spread and grip independently, almost prehensile, the result of lifetimes — of generations of lifetimes — spent on surfaces that are never flat, never dry, never quite stable. They walk on root-bridges and flooded stairways and moss-slick platforms with a rolling, sure-footed gait that looks casual until you try to follow them and find yourself clutching at handholds while they move on unbothered, mid-conversation, carrying a basket of crabs in one hand and a sleeping infant in the other.

Their hands are extraordinary. Not large, but *articulate* — the fingers move with a specificity that borders on uncanny, each digit seeming to operate with its own intelligence. This is what a life of knotwork does. A life of coaxing young roots into position, of feeling the grain of living wood in the dark, of tying cordage underwater by touch alone. Their fingertips are permanently ridged and thickened, not calloused exactly but densified, the skin having developed a texture almost like fine bark. When they touch a root-wall, they read it the way a physician reads a pulse — they can feel growth direction, moisture content, whether the wood is healthy or stressed, whether the calcium has begun to accumulate beneath the bark.

Their skin holds moisture the way the settlement does. It is never dry. In the mist-saturated air, their skin has a permanent slight sheen, and yes — for the paler among them, there is a greenish cast, subtle but real, as if the chlorophyll that saturates their world has begun to claim them by reflection or perhaps by something deeper. There are those who say that certain families, the ones who have been here longest, whose lineage roots as deep as the oldest calcified structures, carry a faint photosynthetic symbiont in their skin — an algae that lives in the upper dermis and contributes some small, negligible amount of energy in exchange for warmth and moisture. Whether this is true or merely a story they tell about themselves is unclear. What is clear is that they do not sunburn, ever, despite the altitude, and their skin when cut heals with a faintly greenish scar.

## The Breathers

They breathe differently.

This is the first thing a visitor would notice, before the feet, before the hands, before the green-tinged skin. The air here is so saturated with moisture that breathing it is halfway to drinking it. A lowlander's lungs rebel — the heaviness, the thickness, the feeling that each breath must be wrung out before the next one can be taken. But the people of the caldera breathe this air the way a fish breathes water: effortlessly, automatically, with a rhythm that is slower and deeper than any lowlander's.

They take perhaps eight breaths per minute at rest. Each breath is full, unhurried, drawn from the belly rather than the chest, held for a moment at its peak as if being tasted, then released in a long steady exhale that sometimes, in the quiet, is the loudest sound a person makes. Children breathe faster, of course, but by adolescence the rhythm has settled into that slow, deliberate cadence, and it gives them an air of preternatural calm that visitors sometimes mistake for passivity or dullness. It is neither. It is the patience of someone whose entire world operates on the timescale of root-growth. Urgency is not unknown to them — they can move fast when a platform fails or a child slips into deep water — but the default state is this measured, rooted stillness.

They can hold their breath for a very long time. Three minutes is unremarkable. Five is common among the adults who regularly navigate the flooded lower passages. The divers — those who tend the deep granaries, who visit the places where the dead are laid, who occasionally descend to the deepest levels where the calcified roots become indistinguishable from the caldera's own stone — can manage seven or eight minutes, moving slowly, conserving, their heart rate dropping to something that would alarm a lowland physician. They do not gasp when they surface. They simply resume breathing, that same slow eight-breaths-per-minute rhythm, as if the interruption were no more significant than a pause between sentences.

## The Families

Family here is not nuclear. It is architectural.

A family is defined by the structure it inhabits, and a structure is defined by the family that grew it. The two are inseparable. When a child is born, a root is begun — coaxed from the parent tree of the family's dwelling, guided outward or upward or down into a new direction, and this root *is* the child's, in a legal and spiritual and practical sense. As the child grows, the root grows. As the root is trained and shaped and woven into the existing structure, the child is trained and shaped and woven into the existing family. If the root takes well — if it lignifies into a strong, useful member of the architecture — the child's place in the family is secure. If the root fails, dies back, refuses to grow in the intended direction, this is not blamed on the child, exactly, but it is *noted*, and it carries a weight that outsiders might find difficult to understand. A person whose birth-root failed is not outcast, but they carry a kind of structural ambiguity. They may graft into another family's architecture, may begin a new root in a new dwelling, but the original failure is recorded in the knotwork histories, and it means something — something about fit, about direction, about the relationship between intention and growth.

Marriage — or what functions as marriage — is the interweaving of two roots from two different structures. Literally. Two families negotiate the joining, and then the actual woody roots of two actual trees are coaxed toward each other, encouraged to graft, to fuse, to become a single continuous piece of living wood. This takes years. The engagement period is the growth period: the time it takes for two roots to touch, to press against each other, to begin the slow biological process of grafting bark to bark, cambium to cambium. If the graft takes, the marriage is real, is structural, is load-bearing. If the graft fails — if the roots refuse to fuse, if one rejects the other, if disease enters the joint — the union is not forbidden but it is understood to be unsupported, architecturally and perhaps otherwise.

Divorce, consequently, is an act of surgery. A calcified root-graft cannot be separated without killing part of the structure. An early-stage graft can be cut, but the scar remains in the wood forever, visible, readable, part of the building's permanent record.

Children belong to the structure they were born in, not to either parent individually. If both parents die, the child is not orphaned — the *house* still stands, the birth-root still grows, and the other inhabitants of that structure (aunts, cousins, elders, the complicated web of people whose roots share the same tree) continue the raising. A child is only truly orphaned if their birth-root dies *and* their dwelling-structure is abandoned, which happens so rarely it is the subject of the oldest and most sorrowful songs.

## The Root-Shapers

Not everyone grows buildings. Most people participate in the maintenance of existing structures — trimming new growth that pushes in unwanted directions, packing clay into gaps, clearing the water-channels, harvesting calcified root-stone from the deep levels. But the *shapers* — the ones who begin new structures, who plan the growth of a room or a hall decades before it will be inhabitable — these are specialists, and they occupy a social position somewhere between architect and priest.

A shaper must understand wood the way a surgeon understands flesh. They must know the species — there are at least four distinct varieties of the mangrove-banyan used for construction, each with different growth rates, different root-thicknesses, different responses to water and mineral content. They must understand the hydrology of the caldera — where water pressure will support or undermine a foundation, where calcification will strengthen a joint, where it will make a living root too rigid to absorb the stresses of wind and weight. They must think in decades. A shaper planning a new hall is composing a symphony that will not be fully audible for fifty years, and they must hear it now, in the green shoot, in the bending of a thumb-thick root toward a frame of old deadwood.

Shapers train for most of their lives. Apprenticeship begins in childhood and continues until the master declares the student capable of *hearing the wood* — a phrase that is not metaphorical. The trees here make sound. The creaking under load, the subsonic groaning, the specific pitch of a healthy root versus a stressed one — shapers learn this language the way musicians learn pitch. They press their ears to root-walls. They press their hands flat against growing trunks and feel the vibration of water moving through xylem. They sleep in the crooks of trees and claim to dream in the patterns of growth-rings.

The oldest shaper currently alive is a woman whose name, translated roughly, means *the-weight-she-distributed*. She is perhaps eighty. She has not left the caldera in sixty years — has not, by her own account, wanted to. She moves through the settlement slowly, barefoot, one hand always trailing along a root-wall, and she stops sometimes in the middle of a passage and cocks her head and listens, and then she ties a knot in a length of cord she carries and moves on. These knots are instructions. Someone will read them, later, and understand that a particular root needs to be redirected three inches to the left, or that a pool-terrace is losing water through a crack that hasn't become visible yet, or that a calcification front has reached a joint and the joint needs to be relieved of load before it becomes too rigid to flex in the next storm.

She is growing something. She has been growing it for forty years. It is a single structure — a hall, perhaps, or something more complex — that extends from the mid-level of the settlement down into the flooded deep, and its purpose is not entirely clear to anyone but her. The roots she has guided are thicker than any others in the caldera. They are already beginning to calcify. She speaks of it sometimes, briefly, without explanation: *it will hold the center when the center needs holding.* People trust her. They do not understand her, but they trust her, the way you trust the sound of a building that has stood for a hundred years.

## The Knotters

If the shapers are architects, the knotters are scribes.

But that comparison, too, is inadequate. A knotter does not simply record; they *encode*, in a tactile language of twists and tensions that can be read in the dark, underwater, by feel alone. The knot-language is not alphabetic. It does not map onto speech the way writing maps onto speech. It is its own mode of thought — spatial, textural, sequential — and fluency in it requires a way of thinking that literate visitors find almost impossible to acquire.

A knotted cord records debts and obligations, birth-roots and graft-marriages, the direction and growth-rate of specific architectural roots, the health of crab-pools, the depth of the caldera's water at different seasonal marks. But it also records *relationships between these things* — a knot-sequence might encode the fact that a particular family's crab-pool is underproducing *because* a root-growth in the adjacent structure has shifted water flow *because* a graft-marriage two generations ago connected two root-systems that now compete for the same mineral upwelling. The cord doesn't just say what happened; it says *how things are connected*, and reading it requires the ability to feel those connections as a physical topology under the fingers.

Children learn basic knots from the age of four or five — simple identity markers, numbers, the standard sequences for common goods and obligations. But true knotting, the kind that encodes complex historical and architectural relationships, is a specialization as demanding as shaping. Master knotters are few — perhaps a dozen in the settlement at any time — and they are consulted the way one might consult a legal archive and a structural engineering firm simultaneously.

There is one knotter — young, perhaps thirty, intense in a way that makes others slightly uneasy — who is attempting something that has not been done before, or at least not in living memory. He is encoding the sound of the settlement. The creaks, the water-rhythms, the frog-choruses, the specific pitch of each major structural root under its current load — he is translating all of this into knot-sequences, creating a tactile record of what the caldera *sounds like* at this moment in its long becoming. He works at night, in the flooded lower passages, where the sounds travel differently through water and stone, and he ties knots that other knotters cannot read, or can read only partially, the way a musician might half-follow a score written in an unfamiliar system. He says he is doing this because the sound will change — because the calcification will alter the resonance of every root it reaches, because the settlement a century from now will creak in a different key — and someone should remember how it sounded before.

No one has told him to do this. No one has told him to stop.

## The Deep-Tenders

There are those who go down.

Not everyone does. Most people's lives are lived in the mid-levels — the inhabited platforms and rooms and walkways that occupy the zone between the upper canopy and the waterline. The upper canopy is for fire-keeps and drying and the occasional lookout who scans the slopes below for weather or strangers. The flooded lower levels are for transit, for storage, for the practical business of managing the submerged infrastructure. But the *deep* — the lowest reaches of the caldera's flooded core, where the calcified roots become indistinguishable from the basalt they grip, where the water is cold enough to ache and dark enough to blind — this is a separate domain, and the people who tend it are a separate kind of people.

The deep-tenders are not a caste, exactly. Anyone can train for the work. But in practice, they come from certain families — the ones whose dwellings sit lowest, whose birth-roots extend down into the flooded zones, whose children grew up navigating submerged corridors and holding their breath in the dark. They are the ones who maintain the deep granaries, who check the sealed vessels where grain and dried legumes are stored in cold anoxic water that preserves them for years. They are the ones who lay the dead in their final places — the submerged alcoves deep in the root-architecture where bodies are given to the cold, mineral-rich water and begin the slow process of calcification that will, in time, make them part of the structure itself.

This is how the dead are kept here. Not buried, not burned — *incorporated*. A body laid in the deep water, in the right place, near the right mineral upwelling, will accumulate calcium deposits over years, decades, centuries. The soft tissues are taken by the cold and the eels and the slow bacterial processes of anoxic decay, but the bones remain, and the calcium coats them, and eventually they become root-stone — indistinguishable from the calcified wood around them, part of the foundation, part of the load-bearing structure of the settlement. The oldest deep-tenders can navigate to specific ancestor-remains by feel, can touch a calcified mass in the dark water and say: *this was my grandmother's grandmother, and this root grew through her ribcage and is now part of the floor you sleep on.*

The dead hold up the living. This is not a metaphor here. It is engineering.

The deep-tenders are the ones who have the closest relationship with the creature in the deep water — the large thing, the old thing, the one that breathes like a bellows and silences the frogs. They do not worship it. They do not feed it or propitiate it or claim to understand it. But they encounter it, regularly, in the dark — a vast smooth flank brushing past in the flooded corridor, a displacement of water that tells them something very large has just moved through the space they are about to enter. They wait. They let it pass. Some of them, the ones who have been diving longest, say they can feel its heartbeat through the water — slow, impossibly slow, perhaps four beats per minute, a rhythm even more patient than their own. They say it knows the deep architecture better than anyone. They say it navigates the calcified passages — corridors that were once living root-structures, now fully mineralized, essentially stone tubes full of dark water — with a familiarity that suggests it has been using them since before they were stone. Since they were still wood. Since the trees were young.

One deep-tender, a woman in her fifties with the flat affect of someone who has spent too much time in cold dark water, told a visitor once: *It is the first resident. We are its houseguests. It does not mind us. We try not to give it reason to.*

## The Children

The children are amphibious in a way that the adults only approximate.

Below the age of about ten, before the social structures of family obligation and specialization begin to shape their days, the children of the caldera exist in a state of near-feral fluidity. They move through the settlement vertically rather than horizontally — up the root-trunks to the canopy, down through the flooded passages to the mid-deep, through gaps in the architecture that are too small for adults, along root-bridges that are too young and flexible to bear grown weight. They are wet constantly. They are naked much of the time, or wearing the minimum moss-cotton wraps that propriety requires. Their hair — whatever its original color — tends toward a brownish-green from the algae-rich water they spend half their time submerged in.

They form loose, shifting groups that are not quite play-groups and not quite work-teams but something between. A cluster of six children aged five through twelve might spend a morning exploring a newly grown root-passage, an afternoon harvesting snails from the mid-level pools, and an evening diving the flooded corridors in an elaborate game whose rules seem to involve holding your breath longer than anyone else while navigating a specific route in complete darkness. The game is training. They know this. They don't care. It is also a game.

The smallest children — three, four years old — are kept from the deep water by older children more than by adults. There is a collective understanding among the young that the deep is earned, that you go deeper as you get braver and more capable, and that taking a too-young child into the flooded passages is not forbidden but *wrong* in a way that requires no rule to enforce. The older children enforce this boundary with a seriousness that surprises visitors. A twelve-year-old who can hold her breath for four minutes and navigate the mid-deep by feel will physically block a five-year-old from following her below a certain waterline, not with anger but with the calm authority of someone who understands the stakes.

Babies are carried in slings of woven moss-cotton that are damp by design — the moisture keeps the infant's skin from drying and cracking in the rare moments when it is exposed to direct air. Newborns are dipped in the caldera's water within hours of birth, a brief immersion in a shallow pool that is the closest thing the settlement has to a universal ritual. The water is cold. The baby screams. This is expected and welcomed — the scream is the first negotiation with the world, the first protest against a reality that is colder and wetter than the one just left, and the fact that the baby protests means the baby is strong enough to protest.

## The Elders

Old age here is a process of slowing to match the settlement's pace.

The elderly do not withdraw from community life — there is nowhere to withdraw *to*, in a place this small and this densely interwoven. But they slow. Their breathing, already slow, becomes slower still. Their movements become more deliberate, more rooted, more like the movements of the trees themselves. An elder sitting on a root-platform, legs folded, hands resting on the bark, eyes half-closed in the green-silver light — it becomes genuinely difficult to distinguish them from part of the architecture. They are still. They are damp. They are patient. They are listening to the building.

Elders are the primary storytellers, but "story" is a misleading word for what they do. They narrate the settlement. They speak the knotwork histories aloud, not as recitation but as *interpretation* — reading the cord, feeling the knots, and translating the tactile record into spoken words that fill in the gaps the knots can't capture. Tone of voice. Motivation. The specific quality of a storm that shifted a root-line forty years ago. The sound a building made the night before a platform failed. These details exist in no knot-sequence; they exist in the elders' memories, and they are spoken into the mist, and the children absorb them, and some of the children will become knotters who will find ways to encode some fraction of what they heard, and the rest will be lost, and this is understood and accepted, because not everything can be preserved, and the caldera has been teaching this lesson for longer than anyone has been listening.

The oldest elder currently alive — not the shaper, but a man even older, whose age is uncertain because he has stopped counting and because the knot-records from his birth-year were lost in a flood — spends most of his time in the lower levels, not quite submerged but close to the waterline, in a room where the floor is wet and the walls are calcified and the sound of the deep water is louder than the sound of the air. He does not speak much. He breathes. He listens. Occasionally he reaches out and touches the wall and nods, as if confirming something. People visit him not to hear him talk but to sit near him and share the quality of his attention, which is so focused and so quiet that it makes the settlement's sounds louder by contrast — the frogs, the water, the creaking, the deep slow breathing of everything that lives here, including the thing in the deep water whose heartbeat, some say, this old man has finally matched.

Four beats per minute. In, held, held, out. In, held, held, out. Becoming stone. Becoming the mountain. Still alive.

## The Visitors

They do come, rarely. Traders from the lower slopes, carrying the iron blades that the settlement cannot make for itself, carrying salt, carrying news of a world that operates at a pace the caldera-dwellers find disorienting and faintly absurd. The traders climb for two days through cloud forest and scree and arrive at the caldera's rim gasping in the thin wet air, and they look down into the green bowl of mist and vegetation and they see nothing that looks like a settlement, nothing that looks like a place where people live — just trees, water, fog, the sound of frogs.

Then someone emerges from the green. A wide-footed, green-tinged figure in damp moss-cotton, moving along a root-bridge that the trader didn't realize was there. The figure is unhurried. They are always unhurried. They carry a knotted cord that specifies the terms of trade — what the settlement needs, what it offers, how much is fair. The trader reads the cord (lowland traders learn the basic trade-knots if they want to do business here) and the negotiation begins, and it is conducted in a mixture of speech and knotwork, each medium filling in what the other lacks.

The traders do not stay long. A day, sometimes two. The mist gets to them. The sound gets to them. The constant moisture, the damp clothes, the feeling of breathing water. And something else — a quality of time, a sense that each hour here lasts longer than it should, that the settlement is operating on a clock that runs at a fraction of normal speed. The traders leave eager to return to dry air and fast time and the reassuring hardness of stone walls. They talk about the caldera-people with a mixture of respect and unease, the way you might talk about someone who has found an answer to a question you're not sure you want to ask.

Occasionally — very occasionally — a visitor stays. A lowlander who climbs up with the traders and descends into the caldera and breathes the wet air and feels the root-floor flex beneath their feet and hears the deep layered chorus of water and frogs and wood, and something in them recognizes this place the way a root recognizes the direction of water. They stay. They are given a place in an existing structure, or they begin a root of their own — the settlement does not refuse newcomers, but it does not hurry them either. A newcomer's first years are difficult. The breathing, the cold, the slowness, the disorienting democracy of moisture. Their feet widen, eventually. Their breathing slows. Their hands become more articulate, more sensitive, more capable of the fine tactile work that this place demands. They learn knots. They learn to navigate the flooded passages. They learn to sleep in the creaking, the dripping, the frog-song, the deep slow pulse of a living building that is becoming stone.

They do not think of themselves as having joined a community. They think of themselves as having been *grafted* — slowly, painfully, with the possibility of rejection — onto a structure that was growing before they arrived and will continue growing after they are calcified bone in the deep water, holding up a floor that someone else's children will sleep on.

## What They Know

They know something that is difficult to articulate and that they do not try to articulate, because articulation is a fast-time activity and what they know operates at the speed of calcium deposition.

It is something about the relationship between the living and the structural. Between the growing and the bearing. Between the organism and the architecture. They live inside living things that are becoming stone, and they are living things that will become stone, and the water that flows through them flows through the buildings flows through the mountain, and the mountain is not a metaphor for permanence because the mountain itself is changing, is being infiltrated by roots that are becoming calcium that is becoming rock, and the rock was once a volcano that was once a liquid, and nothing is permanent but everything is *structural*, everything bears weight, everything is load, and to live well is to distribute your weight correctly across the structure that holds you so that the structure can go on holding after you have become part of it.

They know this. They know it in their wide feet on the springy root-floor, in their slow breath, in their hands reading the grain of living wood, in their children diving the dark water, in their dead becoming the foundation, in the great patient creature breathing in the deep.

They are not in a hurry. The mountain is not in a hurry. The water carries its minerals upward, and everything it touches slowly becomes itself, and this is enough, and it takes exactly as long as it takes, and they are grateful — not in the way of people who have been given a gift, but in the way of a root that has found water and knows, without thinking, that this is what it was reaching for.
