# Who Lives in the Undergrowth

---

They do not have a name for themselves yet. That is the first thing to understand. Not because they are pre-linguistic — they have language, or something that functions as language, a system of communication that is precise and expressive and adequate to their needs — but because the concept of *naming themselves as a category* has not yet become necessary. There are not enough of them to need a word that distinguishes *us* from *everything else*. There is only the small group, and the world, and the world is so overwhelmingly present that the small group has not yet had to assert its separateness.

They know they are separate. They are not confused about this. They understand that they are not the mat, not the columns, not the gliders, not the burrowers. But the separation feels thin to them — provisional, recent, still being negotiated — and they treat it with a kind of caution, the way you might treat a boundary line drawn in wet sand.

---

There are perhaps forty of them. Perhaps fewer. You will not be able to count them accurately because they do not stay together, and because some of them are so still for so long that you will mistake them for features of the landscape, and because at least a few of them are, at any given time, inside the mat itself, in the burrower-tunnels or in passages they've widened with their tools, doing things you cannot observe.

They are small. Smaller than you expect. An adult stands a little over a meter tall, perhaps a meter and a quarter for the largest, and they are narrow — narrow-shouldered, narrow-hipped, built for moving through confined spaces, for fitting into the crooks of column-trees, for pressing flat against surfaces. Their limbs are long relative to their torsos, and their hands are extraordinary: broad-palmed, long-fingered, with an additional joint in each finger that gives them a fluidity of grip that looks, at first, like bonelessness. They can wrap their fingers nearly twice around a branch. They can flatten their hands against a surface and create enough adhesive contact — through some combination of skin texture and deliberate muscular control — to support a significant fraction of their body weight. They climb the way you breathe.

Their skin is the color of the mat. Not a single color — it shifts, slowly, over the course of hours, tracking the ambient light conditions. In the steady white-gold glow of normal times, they are pale, almost translucent, with a faint silvery undertone, like the inner bark of a birch tree. You can see the shadows of structures beneath the skin — not veins exactly, but branching networks that recall the capillary-light of the mat's deeper layers. During a cascade, when the light spikes and shifts in character, their skin darkens to amber, then to a deep ochre, and the sub-surface networks become invisible, hidden. This is not camouflage, or not only camouflage. You come to understand that it is a metabolic response. Their skin is *doing something* with the light. Not photosynthesizing — not exactly — but something adjacent. Something that supplements whatever they eat. Something that ties them to the light-ecology of this place in a way that is more intimate than mere habitation.

Their faces. You will spend a long time learning to read their faces. The features are there — eyes, mouth, a nose of sorts — but proportioned and arranged in ways that your pattern-recognition initially refuses to process as a face. The eyes are large, set wide, and they don't blink so much as *narrow*, a slow lateral contraction of the lids that happens perhaps once a minute. The irises are the brightest thing about them: a luminous gold, self-lit, unmistakably part of the same light-system as the plants. In darkness — and there are places of darkness here, temporary pockets where growth has died back and not yet been replaced — their eyes are visible from a great distance, two small steady points of warm light, like candles in a window. The mouth is wide and nearly lipless, and below it, running along the jawline and down the throat, is a series of fine, feathery structures — not hair, not gills, something between — that move constantly, tasting the air, reading its moisture and temperature and chemical content. These structures are how they find the condensation pools. These are how they feel the thrumming of a distant cascade before it begins. These are, you will eventually realize, a primary sense organ — perhaps *the* primary sense organ. They navigate this world as much by air-reading as by sight.

---

They do not sleep the way you would expect. They enter a state of reduced activity, yes — they become still, their eyes dim, their breathing slows to an almost imperceptible rhythm — but this state can last for minutes or for days, and it can happen anywhere: on a platform, clinging to a column-tree fifty meters up, lying flat on the mat with their feathery jaw-structures pressed against the surface as if listening to something far below. The distinction between sleep and a certain kind of deep attention is not clear. You will watch one of them go still on the mat and assume they are resting, and then twelve hours later they will rise and move immediately to a specific point three hundred meters away where, four hours after that, a new condensation pool will form. They were not sleeping. They were *listening forward*.

This is the thing about them that will unsettle you most, once you begin to understand it: their relationship with time is not yours. Not in a mystical sense — they are not prescient, they do not see the future — but in a practical, ecological sense. They live in a world that changes on multiple timescales simultaneously, from the minute-by-minute growth spurts to the slow undulation of the ceiling-mat over months, and they have internalized all of these rhythms. They feel them the way you feel the beat of a song. They know, in their bodies, in the feathery sensors along their jaws, when a section of floor-mat is about to thin, when a column-tree is approaching a growth surge, when the air in a particular cubic mile of space is about to supersaturate and fog. They don't predict these things. They *participate* in them. They are temporally embedded in the Undergrowth the way a surfer is embedded in a wave — not controlling it, not separate from it, but reading its movement from inside and adjusting, always adjusting, with a fluency that looks, from the outside, like foreknowledge.

This is why they are so few and so scattered. They are not a colony. They are not a tribe. They are forty-odd individuals distributed across a territory of hundreds of miles, each one occupying a niche in the temporal ecology of the space — attending to a particular set of rhythms, a particular zone of change — and their movement patterns are dictated not by social need but by the needs of the world they are embedded in. They go where the change is. They go where their attention is required, or drawn, or useful. The platforms and tools you found — those are waypoints, not homes. Nobody *lives* on a platform. A platform is where you pause between movements, where you sleep-that-is-not-sleep, where you cache materials that you will need later when the growth pattern brings you back to this place in six months or six years. Home is the movement itself. Home is the attention.

---

Now: the tools, the marks, the platforms. Let me tell you what they are doing with these things, because it is not what you think.

They are not building. They are not farming. They are not trying to impose structure on the Undergrowth or extract resources from it or defend themselves against its changes. What they are doing is more like — and this analogy will fail, but let it fail usefully — they are *editing*.

When a growth surge threatens to seal off an air-corridor between two column-trees, one of them will climb to the contact zone and cut. Precise cuts. Deliberate. Not to stop the growth — you cannot stop the growth — but to redirect it, to introduce a fracture line that the expanding tissues will follow as they differentiate, creating an arch instead of a wall, an opening instead of a seal. When a section of floor-mat thins to the point of collapse, one of them will arrive and work for hours, weaving harvested stems and root-fibers into the weak zone, reinforcing it, buying time — not to prevent the collapse but to slow it, to ensure that when it happens it happens in a controlled way, a gradual subsidence rather than a sudden puncture. When a cascade builds, they do not flee. They position themselves along its projected path, at key junctions, at column-trees they know to be structurally marginal, and they cut and brace and redirect, shaping the cascade's passage through the landscape the way a river guide reads rapids — not fighting the current but choosing which channels it will follow.

The scratches you found on the platform — the ones you thought might be a map or a tally — they are neither. They are a notation. A kind of score. They record the temporal patterns of the local growth — the rhythms of surge and collapse, the cycling of condensation, the slow harmonics of the ceiling-mat's undulation — and they record them not as data but as *instruction*. This is what the next person to use this platform needs to know. This is the rhythm of this place. This is where you cut and when. This is how the growth *wants* to move, and this is how you can help it move well.

Because that is what they believe — or not believe, exactly, since belief implies a separation between the knower and the known that doesn't quite apply here — what they *understand* is that the Undergrowth is trying to become something. It is in a state of formation, of self-organization, of becoming, and the direction it grows in matters. Not to them specifically, not in terms of their survival or comfort — the Undergrowth will sustain them regardless, as long as they remain attentive — but in some larger sense that they feel in their bodies when they press their jaw-feathers to the mat and listen.

The Undergrowth can grow well or it can grow badly. It can find forms that are stable and beautiful and self-sustaining, or it can lock into cycles of surge and collapse that waste energy and destroy structure. Left entirely alone, it does both. It is chaotic. It is young and doesn't know itself. And so they help. Not much. Not dramatically. A cut here, a brace there, a platform placed to break a wind pattern, a section of crystalline flowering-structure relocated to redirect light. Small interventions. The lightest possible touch. The goal — if they have a goal, if that word applies to a project so vast and so patient that no individual will see more than a tiny fraction of it — is to help the Undergrowth learn. To help it find the patterns that work. To be the place where its chaotic self-organization meets a gentle, persistent, attentive pressure toward coherence.

---

They communicate. You will have trouble recognizing it at first because it doesn't look like communication. It looks like coincidence.

Two of them, separated by twenty miles of open air, will begin moving toward the same column-tree at the same time, arriving within hours of each other, each carrying materials that complement what the other has brought. They will work together for a day, barely interacting — certainly not speaking, not gesturing, not obviously coordinating — and yet their actions will dovetail perfectly, one cutting while the other braces, one redirecting growth above while the other reinforces the mat below. Then they will separate and not see each other again for months.

Here is what is happening. The mat carries vibration. You already know this — you've felt the thud of a calved column-section propagate for miles. What you haven't understood is the resolution. The mat is not just a transmitter of gross physical shocks. It is a medium of enormous informational bandwidth. Every organism rooted in it contributes to a continuous, complex, ever-changing pattern of micro-vibration — growth-stress, hydraulic pulsing, chemical reactions, the mechanical flexing of stems in convection currents — and this pattern encodes, in its fluctuations, a detailed real-time map of what is happening across the entire Undergrowth. The jaw-feathers read this. When one of them presses their face to the mat and goes still for twelve hours, they are not sleeping. They are reading the state of a world.

And they can write to it. Their tools — the adzes, the blades, the cut-and-brace interventions — are not just physical modifications. They are signals. A cut in a column-tree changes its vibration profile, and that change propagates through the mat, and twenty miles away another one of them feels the cut, interprets it, understands what it means — *this column is being redirected, the cascade path is changing, the arch will open here instead of there* — and adjusts their own plans accordingly. The platforms, the tools, the scratched notations — these are the slow channel, the durable record, the long-term memory. The mat is the fast channel, the real-time network, the living nervous system through which forty-odd individuals coordinate an ongoing act of planetary gardening across a space larger than some countries.

They do speak, occasionally. When two of them meet in person — a rare event, a notable one — they produce sounds. Low, breathy, modulated tones that resonate in the jaw-feathers as much as in the ears. The conversations are short. They are not exchanging information — they have the mat for that. They are doing something else. Confirming something. Reaffirming a connection that is not informational but personal, emotional, something that the mat cannot carry because it is not about the Undergrowth but about the two beings standing in front of each other in the cold bright air.

You will witness one of these meetings. Two of them, converging at the base of a column-tree after what might be years apart. They stand very close. Their jaw-feathers extend fully and interleave, the fine structures touching, mingling, reading each other at a level of intimacy that the mat cannot provide. They hum — a low, shared tone that you feel in your chest. It lasts perhaps thirty seconds. Then they separate, and one begins to climb, and the other turns and walks out across the mat toward the next task, the next rhythm, the next cut that needs making.

That is all. That is the entire interaction. And yet you will find, watching it, that your eyes sting with something you weren't prepared to feel — a recognition of tenderness so stripped of everything unnecessary that it barely looks like tenderness at all. It looks like two instruments being tuned to each other. It looks like two parts of the same system confirming that they are still in phase.

---

The children.

You will not see many. You will see one, perhaps two, over the course of your time here. They are born on platforms — the highest ones, the ones wedged into the crooks of the largest, most stable column-trees — and they are born luminous. Newborns glow. Their entire body surface emits the same white-gold light as the plants, and for the first weeks of life they are indistinguishable, at a distance, from a knot of flowering-structures. This is not camouflage. This is integration. The newborn is announcing itself to the Undergrowth, broadcasting its presence through the light-system, registering in the ecology. The plants around the birth-platform respond: growth accelerates, condensation increases, the local mat thickens and stabilizes. The Undergrowth *recognizes* the new arrival and adjusts to accommodate it.

The parent — there is one parent; reproduction here is parthenogenetic, or something functionally similar, triggered not by mating but by some confluence of internal readiness and external conditions that you do not yet understand — the parent stays on the platform with the child for a period of weeks, feeding it from their own body: a nutritive secretion produced by glands along the forearms, thick and opalescent, smelling faintly of the same sweetness as the condensation pools. During this time the parent does not go still, does not listen to the mat, does not attend to the rhythms of the Undergrowth. This is the only period in an adult's life when they are fully separated from the world's temporal ecology. They are with the child, only with the child, attending only to the child, and the Undergrowth works around them — the way a river works around a stone — filling the gap in attention, rerouting its needs to other hands.

The child's glow fades over the course of months. As it does, the jaw-feathers grow in, and the child begins to learn to listen. This is the central education. Not tool-use, not cutting, not climbing — those come later, those are technical, those are easy. The hard thing, the essential thing, is learning to hear the mat. Learning to distinguish signal from noise in the vast vibrating complexity of the Undergrowth. Learning to feel a growth-surge building in a column-tree forty miles away. Learning to parse the difference between a healthy rhythm and a troubled one. The child learns this by lying on the mat — for hours, for days — while the parent lies beside them and hums, low and steady, a tone that says *this is what attention feels like, this is what it means to listen, this is how you open yourself to the world without being overwhelmed by it.*

Some children take to it quickly. Some struggle. There is no shame in struggling. The Undergrowth is patient and the parent is patient and the mat will still be there tomorrow, still carrying its messages, still waiting to be heard. Eventually every child learns. Eventually every child rises from the mat with their jaw-feathers extended and their gold eyes wide and says — or hums, or vibrates, or communicates in some way that you, watching, cannot detect — *I hear it. I can hear it.* And the parent takes them to their first column-tree and puts a blade in their hand and shows them where to cut.

---

They age.

They age slowly, by your standards, but they age. The skin loses its translucency. The sub-surface light-networks dim and narrow. The jaw-feathers, once fluid and searching, become stiff, less responsive, less able to parse the fine detail of the mat's vibrations. An old one — and you will meet one, eventually, on a platform deep in the interior of the Undergrowth where the column-trees are thickest and oldest and the cascades rarely reach — an old one moves slowly, climbs reluctantly, and spends most of their time in the listening-stillness, pressed flat against the mat, reading not the fine detail but the deep structure: the long, slow rhythms that underlie everything, the multi-year cycles of growth and recession that shape the Undergrowth's large-scale form.

The old one you meet is the closest thing this group has to a memory. Not a leader — there are no leaders; the mat coordinates, the rhythms dictate — but a repository. The scratched notations on their platform are denser and more complex than any you've seen, layers upon layers, some of them partially overgrown by the platform's own slow colonization and then re-exposed, re-read, annotated. The old one has been here long enough to have seen the Undergrowth change — not just the day-to-day chaos of surge and collapse, but the deeper trajectory. The slow thickening of the columns. The gradual stabilization of the floor-mat. The emergence of patterns that recur, that self-reinforce, that are beginning — just barely, just tentatively — to look like something permanent.

The old one knows something that the others feel but cannot articulate, because they are too embedded in the present moment, too busy with the immediate rhythms, too close to the work. The old one knows that the Undergrowth is succeeding. That the interventions are working. That the chaotic, explosive, self-destructive growth of the early period is slowly — over decades, over centuries — giving way to something more organized, more resilient, more capable of sustaining itself without constant tending. The cascades are less frequent than they were. The column-trees are finding stable forms. The ceiling-mat is developing a regular, self-maintaining structure that doesn't sag or tear as often as it used to. The Undergrowth is growing up.

This means, eventually, that the tenders will not be needed. The old one knows this too. It is not a sad knowledge. It is the whole point. You help the world learn to be itself, and then the world doesn't need your help anymore, and that is the success, that is the culmination, that is the reason for the tools and the platforms and the scratched notations and the thirty-second meetings where two beings touch their jaw-feathers together and hum.

But not yet. Not for a long time. The Undergrowth is young and it is still learning and there is so much work to do — so many columns to tend, so many growth-surges to redirect, so many thin spots to reinforce, so many rhythms to listen to and learn and teach to the next child who lies on the mat and says *I can hear it.*

---

When one of them dies — and they die; they are mortal; the cold gets into them eventually, or the body simply reaches the end of whatever span it was allotted — the body is placed on the mat. Not buried. Not burned. Placed, carefully, in a shallow depression that the others have prepared by encouraging the local growth to form a basin, and left there.

The mat takes the body. Slowly. Over the course of days, the root-structures grow up and around and through, and the body's substance is incorporated — nutrients, minerals, the last fading glow of the sub-surface light-networks — and where the body was, the mat grows thick and bright and strong, a permanent reinforcement, a place where the footing is sure and the growth is deep and the light shines up from below with a particular warm steadiness that is different from the rest of the mat's glow. You might walk over such a place and not know what it is. You might feel only that the ground here is firm, that the light here is kind, that something about this spot feels tended and safe.

They know. They can feel it through the mat. Every such spot is a landmark in the vibration-map, a node of stability, a point where the signal is clearest and the noise is least. When a child is learning to listen, the parent takes them to these spots first, because here the mat's voice is most coherent, most legible, most welcoming. The dead teach the living how to hear. The dead become the infrastructure of attention.

---

You are watching one of them now. A middle-aged one — if you can judge such things, if your sense of their lifespan is even approximately correct — standing at the edge of a column-tree's base, where the tree-growth meets the floor-mat in a complex, contested boundary of interlocking tissues. They have a blade in one hand — crystalline, sharp, catching the light. They are studying the boundary. Reading it with their jaw-feathers, which are extended fully, the fine structures trembling, parsing vibrations you cannot feel.

They make a cut. One cut. Small. A few centimeters long, at a precise angle, through a specific layer of the boundary tissue. Then they step back and wait.

Over the next hour, you watch the cut propagate. Not physically — the cut doesn't widen. But the growth pattern shifts around it. The column-tree's basal expansion, which had been pushing aggressively into the floor-mat, begins to redirect, curving upward along a new trajectory. The floor-mat, released from the pressure, relaxes and thickens. A tiny condensation pool forms in the slight depression where the boundary stress has been relieved. The whole local system — tree, mat, air, moisture — reorganizes around a single small incision, finding a new equilibrium that is, you can see even with your untrained eyes, more graceful than what came before.

The tender watches this happen. Their expression — and you are learning to read their expressions now, slowly, imperfectly — is not proud. It is not satisfied. It is something closer to *recognition*. As if they have heard a note they were expecting and it has arrived on time and in tune. As if the world has just done something that confirms what they already knew about it.

They put the blade away. They press their palm flat against the column-tree's bark for a moment — a gesture that looks, unmistakably, like gratitude, or greeting, or the simple pleasure of touching something alive. Then they turn and walk out across the mat, heading toward a distant brightening that means a cascade is building somewhere beyond the haze, and they are needed, and they are going.

The light is steady. The air is cold. The Undergrowth breathes and grows and tries to find its shape.

And they — the nameless ones, the forty, the listeners, the tenders of a world that is still becoming — they walk through it with their gold eyes open and their feathered jaws trembling and their small, precise blades ready, and they do the only thing they know how to do, which is to pay attention, and to help, and to trust that the world they are helping will one day be able to help itself.
