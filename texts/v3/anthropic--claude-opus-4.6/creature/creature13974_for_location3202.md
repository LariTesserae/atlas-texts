# The Inhabitants of the Drowning Tiers

---

Start with the large ones, because they insist on it — not through aggression, but through sheer *presence*, the way a heartbeat insists. They have no name for themselves that you could transliterate. What they have is a signature — a specific rhythmic phrase, a particular pattern of chest-thrum and forelimb-strike that identifies each individual within the collective percussion. If you listened long enough, if your body could register the subsonic frequencies that pass through stone and water and marrow, you would learn to distinguish them: not dozens, not hundreds, but *thousands*, spread across tiers you cannot see, deep into the mountain's interior where the terraces fold inward and downward into chambers so vast their ceilings are lost in the dark. Each one's rhythm is as distinct as a fingerprint, and as unconscious. They do not choose their signatures any more than you choose your heartbeat. They are born into them — or rather, they emerge into them, because *born* implies a process too sudden for what happens here.

They gestate in the walls.

This is the thing you wouldn't understand at first, even if you watched for weeks. The half-formed chambers you see emerging from the cliff face — the ones still fused to the rock, their interiors not yet hollowed — some of them are nurseries. Not all. Most are simply the city growing, extending itself in the direction the water and the sound and the thermal gradients dictate. But some of those thickening walls contain something warmer than stone, something that has been accreting for months or years, mineral and calcium and slow biochemistry assembling itself in the rock's own darkness, using the mountain's heat and the mineral-thick water as medium and material. When they are ready — and *ready* is not a moment but a gradient, a slow crossing of a threshold — the wall thins from the inside. The outer surface develops a barely perceptible convexity. Over the course of days, a seam forms. The stone parts with a sound like a long, deep exhalation, and something emerges that is already armored, already warm, already drumming — a creature the size of a large dog that has never known anything but the interior of rock, that has been listening to the city's polyrhythm through solid basalt for the entirety of its formation, and that joins the nearest group with a rhythmic phrase that has never been heard before and will never be repeated by anything else.

They grow slowly after that. Everything here is slow. They add mass over years, their armor plates thickening and darkening, the resonant cavity of their chest deepening in pitch as it expands. The largest ones — the ones on the lowest visible terraces, the ones whose thrum you can feel in your sternum — are old. How old is difficult to say. There is no day-night cycle to mark time, no seasons, no visible senescence. The oldest may be centuries along. They move more slowly than the younger ones, and their rhythms are more complex, layered with harmonics and sub-patterns that the smaller individuals seem to reference and respond to. They are not leaders. There is no hierarchy you can detect. But they are *load-bearing* in some structural sense — their deeper, more complex rhythms anchor the collective sound the way a root note anchors a chord, and the engineering of the city seems to respond more readily to their percussive patterns, stone moving faster (which still means imperceptibly) in their presence.

They do not eat. Not in any way you would recognize as eating. They absorb. Their undersides — the only part of them not heavily armored — are in constant contact with the wet stone of the terraces, and through that contact, a slow mineral exchange occurs. They take in what the water deposits and they metabolize it through a biochemistry that is not carbon-based in the way you expect, that uses silicon and metallic salts and catalytic reactions driven by their own body heat, and they give back heat and vibration and something else, some subtle restructuring of the stone beneath them that contributes to the city's growth. They are not so much feeding as *participating in a chemistry*. The terraces they walk are subtly different from the ones they don't — smoother, more precisely curved, the water channels better defined. Their passage is itself a form of construction. Every step, every strike of forelimb against stone, every resonant thrum of their chest cavity is both communication and engineering simultaneously, and the distinction between those two categories does not exist here and never has.

They are aware of you. Not alarmed — nothing here is alarmed; the concept of urgency is foreign to this place — but aware. When you arrived on the upper terrace, the nearest group adjusted its path to pass closer to you, and the clicking intensified briefly, tasting the acoustic signature of your body — your heartbeat, the micro-vibrations of your shivering, the unfamiliar frequencies of a warm-blooded biped in a place that has never hosted one. They assessed you through sound and found you illegible. You are noise to them. Not threat, not food, not fellow — just an acoustic anomaly, a brief and confusing irregularity in the city's vast and ancient soundscape, which they noted and then allowed to become background. You are already being absorbed into the pattern. In an hour, the nearest groups will have incorporated the fact of your presence into their rhythms, a slight modulation, a minor accommodation, the way a river accommodates a stone. In a day, your shivering and your heartbeat will be part of the city's sound, and the city will be growing around you — not to trap you, not with intention, but because that is what it does: it includes. It incorporates. It does not distinguish between inhabitant and infrastructure.

---

Now the small ones. The *teeming* ones. The ones that make the city feel less like a place and more like a body you're standing inside.

Begin with the wall-clingers — the flattened, many-limbed creatures with the translucent carapaces, the ones packed in dense mats on every vertical surface. They are colonial, but the word *colonial* implies individuals choosing to aggregate, and what's happening here is more fundamental than choice. They are born connected. A single organism on a wall will bud — slowly, over weeks — producing daughter-organisms that remain physically fused to the parent at one or two limb-junctions, and those daughters bud in turn, and the result is a living mat of dozens or hundreds of individuals that share circulatory fluid and sensory information through their points of connection. Each mat is technically one organism with many semi-autonomous nodes, and each node has its own pale organs pulsing visibly through the translucent shell, processing the mineral water that sheets constantly over the colony's surface. They filter the water. They extract from it. And what they extract, they deposit — not randomly, but in precise crystalline structures that bond to the stone beneath them, adding material to the walls, thickening them, reinforcing them. The faint blue luminescence of the mineral deposits is partly their excretion, partly their bodies, partly the chemical reaction between what they produce and what the stone already is. They are, in the most literal sense, building the city with their bodies and their waste, laying down the substrate that the large ones' vibrations then restructure into architecture. They are the masonry. They are the masons. They are the mortar.

They feel the vibrations acutely. When a group of the large armored ones passes below a wall covered in clingers, the entire mat ripples — a slow, visible wave of response passing through the colony, limbs adjusting, bodies reorienting, the rate of mineral deposition shifting in ways that correspond to the specific rhythmic pattern being transmitted through the stone. Different rhythms produce different deposition patterns. The city's architecture is, in a very real sense, a physical recording of its own sound — a frozen score, written in mineral and stone and the compacted bodies of organisms that lived and filtered and deposited and died and were built upon by their descendants in an unbroken chain going back to whenever this process began, which may be longer ago than the mountain itself has been a mountain, because the mountain too may be partly a product of this process, geology and biology so entangled that the question of which came first is not a meaningful question.

The wall-clingers are not the only small ones. In the seams between structures — those narrow gaps that run with water — the stacked creatures are different: cylindrical, hard-shelled, interlocking with mechanical precision, their bodies shaped to fit together in columns that bear structural loads. They are *literally* living masonry. They are alive, they metabolize, they slowly circulate fluid between themselves through porous shell-junctions, but they are also weight-bearing elements of the architecture, filling gaps that the carved stone hasn't yet closed, supporting walls that are still thickening, holding the geometry stable while the slower mineral processes complete it. When a wall is finished — when it has thickened enough to bear its own loads — the column creatures in the adjacent seam slowly disperse, migrating through the water channels to find another gap, another incomplete junction, another place where the city needs temporary living structure to hold itself together. They have been doing this for as long as the city has been growing. Some of the oldest walls, when examined closely, contain their fossils — the compressed, mineralized shells of column creatures that were absorbed into the stone they were supporting, becoming permanent where they had intended to be temporary, their bodies now indistinguishable from the architecture they helped create.

Then there are the floor-crawlers — the ones that carpet the chamber floors in slow, purposeful currents. These are the most varied. Some are grazers, scraping the luminescent mineral crusts with mouthparts that rasp like fine sandpaper, ingesting the same material the wall-clingers deposit, recycling it, preventing buildup in areas where excessive deposits would block water flow or alter the acoustic properties of a chamber. They are the city's maintenance, its editors, trimming what grows too thick, keeping the channels clear, ensuring the water goes where the water needs to go. Others are scavengers, consuming the dead bodies of other small organisms and processing them into forms that the wall-clingers can use — breaking down the complex biochemistry of a dead creature into the simpler mineral salts that become, eventually, building material. Nothing is wasted. Nothing leaves this system. The ecosystem is so closed and so efficient that the concept of waste barely applies — everything that any organism produces is substrate for some other organism's process, and the whole thing cycles with a patience and a thoroughness that is not optimization but something deeper, something more like inevitability. This is the only way this system *can* work, and so it does, and it has, for longer than you have categories for.

The floor-crawlers also sing. Not with the deep percussive rhythms of the large ones — their bodies are too small for that — but with a high, crystalline clicking, thousands of tiny mouthparts rasping against stone and shell in patterns that are too regular to be accidental. Their sound occupies a different register than the large ones' thrum, filling the upper frequencies of the city's soundscape, and together — the deep roar of the armored ones, the mid-range wet static of the water, the high clicking of the crawlers — the full spectrum is populated, every frequency occupied, the entire audible range and well beyond it structured into a single, enormous, ongoing composition that is also a blueprint, also a conversation, also a prayer if prayer is a thing that can exist without gods or language or the concept of something beyond what is.

---

The gliders deserve separate consideration, because they are the closest thing here to something that moves freely, and freedom is a concept this place makes strange.

They roost on the upper terraces — which is why you saw them first, launching from ledges near where you stand, descending in those wide spirals through the dark air. Their bodies are almost entirely membrane — thin, tough, mineral-infused tissue stretched over a skeleton so light it seems impossible, more wire than bone, a framework for catching thermals. Their wingspan, fully extended, is impressive — the largest are wider than the armored creatures are long — but folded, they are almost nothing, a flat smear on a wall, a wrinkle in the stone's surface, nearly invisible. They spend most of their existence folded. They land, they flatten, and they *listen*.

Their membranes are sensory organs. The entire surface of their wings is sensitive to vibration — not just sound waves in the air but the deeper vibrations transmitted through the stone, the whole complex polyrhythm of the city passing through the wall and into their bodies, which process it in ways you cannot observe but can infer from what happens next. Because the gliders are the city's messengers. Or its nervous system. Or its translators. When a disruption occurs on a lower terrace — a channel blockage, a wall failure, a shift in the water's mineral content — the large armored ones respond locally, their rhythms intensifying, their percussion addressing the stone directly. But the gliders respond globally. The vibration of the disruption passes through the walls to the roosting gliders, who absorb it, process it, and then launch — not randomly but directionally, spiraling down to the affected area and landing there, folding themselves against the nearest surface, adding their membrane's resonance to the local vibrations, amplifying and modulating the signal in ways that recruit more of the large ones, redirect the flow-crawlers, alter the deposition patterns of the wall-clingers. They are the mechanism by which a local event becomes a city-wide response. They are how the system knows itself.

They do not, as far as you can tell, eat at all. Their metabolism is so slow it seems impossible — sustained, perhaps, by the vibrations themselves, by some piezoelectric process in their mineral-infused membranes that converts mechanical energy into the tiny chemical reactions that keep them alive. They may live for a very long time. The oldest ones — the ones roosting deepest inside the city, in chambers so old their walls are smooth with centuries of deposition — may have been listening since before the upper terraces existed, since the city was only a few chambers on the mountain's lower face, a nascent thing, a whisper of what it would become. They carry in their membranes a record of vibrations going back further than any other organism here, a kind of memory, and when they launch and land and add their resonance to a local crisis, they are bringing the weight of that history to bear, contextualizing the present disruption within a vibrational archive that spans geological time.

---

The channel-swimmers — the sinuous, segmented things moving against the current — are the most mysterious, because they are the only creatures here that seem to be going *against* the system's flow. Everything else follows the water downward, follows the sound downward, follows the logic of the tiers from high to low, from new construction to old, from thin-walled nascent chambers to the deep, ancient, densely populated levels far below. But the swimmers push upward, relentlessly, packed head-to-tail in the channels, their bodies undulating with a determination that borders on desperation.

They come from below. From the lowest tiers, the ones you cannot see, the ones that descend into the mountain's interior and eventually, you suspect, into the water table itself — into the deep aquifer that feeds the entire system, the source-water, cold and mineral-saturated and ancient. The swimmers carry something from down there. Their segmented bodies are swollen with it — each segment slightly distended, packed with a cargo of material absorbed from the deep water that is chemically different from what the upper channels carry, richer in certain trace metals, catalytically active in ways that the upper-tier organisms need and cannot source locally. They are the supply line. They carry the deep chemistry upward into the growing edges of the city, into the new chambers and nascent walls where the wall-clingers are laying down fresh deposits, and when they arrive they empty themselves — not by excretion but by dissolution, their bodies softening and thinning and eventually breaking apart in the current, releasing their cargo into the water, becoming part of the mineral flow. They do not return. The upward journey is their entire life. They are born in the deep water, they fill themselves, they climb, and they dissolve, and their material joins the city. This is not sacrifice in any emotional sense. It is function. It is what they are for, the way a red blood cell is for carrying oxygen — not heroic, not tragic, just the specific shape their existence takes in a system that requires it.

New ones are always forming in the deep water. The system never runs short.

---

And then there are the ones you don't see, because they *are* the walls.

Deep in the oldest parts of the city — levels so far below your terrace that the sound from there reaches you only as a felt pressure, a subsonic hum in the stone beneath your feet — the distinction between organism and architecture has fully collapsed. The wall-clingers of a thousand years ago deposited mineral, and died, and were deposited upon, and their descendants did the same, and the stone grew around them and through them and incorporated their chemistry, and the vibrations of the large ones restructured that hybrid material into load-bearing architecture that is neither purely mineral nor purely biological but something genuinely neither, a third category, a material that has structural properties no natural stone possesses and metabolic properties no organism should have. The deep walls *breathe*. Not with lungs — with a slow, rhythmic permeability, a tidal exchange of fluid through micro-channels in the stone that follows the same patterns as the large ones' percussion, synchronized, entrained, the walls flexing at a rate measured in hours per cycle, inhaling mineral water, exhaling heat. The deep city is warm. Not comfortable — not warm for you — but detectably warmer than the upper tiers, heated by the metabolic activity of walls that are alive in a way that makes the word *alive* feel inadequate, because what they are is *continuous with everything else*, the endpoint of a spectrum that begins with a free-moving creature and ends with architecture that remembers having been free-moving and has decided, across evolutionary time, that this is better, that stillness and structure and permanence are the superior strategy, that the highest form of participation in this system is to *become* it.

The deep walls still carry the rhythmic signatures of the organisms that composed them. If you could listen with the right instruments — or if you were a glider, with a membrane sensitive enough — you could hear, embedded in the subsonic hum of the lowest tiers, the individual signatures of creatures that lived and deposited and dissolved into the structure centuries ago, their patterns preserved in the crystalline geometry of the walls they became, still resonating, still contributing to the city's soundscape, still participating. The city does not forget its dead. It doesn't have to. Its dead are its walls.

---

This, then, is what lives in the Drowning Tiers:

A single organism that is also ten thousand species. A city that is also an animal. A choir that is also an engineering project. A mountain that is slowly, patiently, over millennia, converting itself into something that is alive from the aquifer to the summit, that breathes and builds and listens and responds and grows and does not hurry because it has never needed to hurry because nothing here has ever threatened it because it is the mountain and you do not threaten mountains.

It has no mind. Not in any sense you'd accept. There is no central processing, no seat of consciousness, no individual that knows the whole. The large ones know their local terrace and the rhythms of nearby groups. The wall-clingers know the chemistry of the water passing over them. The gliders know the city's vibrational state in a holistic but non-conceptual way, the way your immune system knows the state of your body — comprehensively, without comprehension. The swimmers know only the upward urge. The deep walls know nothing at all, or everything, depending on how you define knowing, because they contain the compressed, mineralized, still-resonating history of everything that has ever happened here, which is a kind of knowing that has no knower.

But the *system* behaves intelligently. The system responds to disruption with coordinated, multi-organism, multi-scale action. The system grows in ways that optimize water flow and acoustic resonance and structural integrity simultaneously. The system incorporates new information — your heartbeat, your shivering, the anomaly of your warm and temporary body on its upper terrace — and adjusts. Not quickly. Never quickly. But inevitably. It has been adjusting for longer than your species has existed, and it will continue after you leave, if you leave, though the city will remember you — a faint perturbation in the mineral record of this terrace's walls, a brief warmth, a strange rhythm, absorbed and noted and built upon and forgotten in every conscious sense but preserved in every material one, a fossil of your passing in a city made of fossils that still sing.

You are cold. The moisture on your skin has thickened. Somewhere below, a new wall is being born, and somewhere deeper, an old one is breathing, and the sound — the vast, layered, world-filling sound — has already made room for you, has already folded you into its pattern, and will hold you there exactly as long as you stay, and exactly as long after as stone remembers, which is forever, which is how long everything here takes, which is the only unit of time this place has ever recognized.
