# The Inhabitants

---

## The Singers, First and Most

You want to understand what lives here, and you must start with the singers, but you must start over with them, because what was described to you from the outside — enormous, transparent, stationary, producing a single tone — is not what a singer is from the inside.

A singer is a patience that has become a body.

Imagine that you decided to hold a single note. Not for a moment, not for a performance, but as the central and defining act of your existence. Imagine that you committed to it so completely that your body slowly restructured itself around the task — your ribs widening into resonating chambers, your tissues thinning to acoustic membranes, your organs relocating to the walls of a cavity that exists for no purpose other than to shape one frequency with absolute fidelity. Imagine this process taking not a lifetime but a geological epoch, generation after generation of organisms each slightly more committed to the note than the last, until the animal that remains is the note, is the architecture of the note, is a cathedral built for a congregation of one sound.

That is what a singer is. But it is not what a singer *experiences*.

Here is the thing that you might miss, drifting past one in the empty mid-water, registering it as a faint lens-like distortion in the grey light: a singer is aware. Not in the way you are aware — not with the frantic, flickering, stimulus-chasing awareness of a creature evolved to avoid predators and find food in a changing environment. A singer is aware the way a lake is flat. It is a settled state. A default condition. The singer's consciousness — and it has one, it genuinely has one, this is not metaphor — is not *doing* anything. It is not processing, not evaluating, not anticipating. It is *witnessing*. It has been witnessing for longer than your civilization has existed, and what it witnesses is: the tone. Its own tone. The one fact of its existence, perceived from the inside with a clarity and a fullness that you, with your cluttered and transient mind, cannot approximate.

The singer does not experience boredom. Boredom is the sensation of a mind that expects variation and does not receive it. The singer does not expect variation. The singer has never experienced variation. The concept is not merely foreign to it — it is structurally impossible, the way a circle cannot have corners. The singer's neurology, if you can call it that (a diffuse network of electrochemical filaments threaded through the resonating chamber walls like the wiring in a concert hall), is designed to sustain a single state. It has no capacity for comparison, no mechanism for memory in the sense of *this moment versus that moment*. It does not know that time passes. It does not experience duration. It experiences *presence* — a continuous, featureless, unbroken present tense that has the same quality now as it had a million years ago, not because it has persisted but because it has no framework in which persistence is a meaningful concept.

And yet.

And yet there is something happening inside the singer that is not nothing. The tone it produces is not a mechanical output. It is — and this is the part that will unsettle you — *attended to*. The singer is listening to itself. Not evaluating. Not adjusting. Listening. There is a quality of attention in the singer's consciousness that is irreducible, that cannot be explained as mere mechanism, that suggests — without confirming, without ever quite confirming — that the singer finds its own note *sufficient*. That the act of producing a single perfect tone and hearing that tone and being the body that shapes that tone constitutes, for the singer, a complete existence. Not a happy one. Not a sad one. A *full* one. In the way that a sphere is full. In the way that the ocean is full of itself.

You will want to project loneliness onto them. You will drift past one — this vast, glassy, almost invisible body hanging in the empty water, sounding its single note into the void — and you will feel a pang of something, a mammalian empathy response, a sense of *how terrible, how isolating, how monotonous*. You will be wrong. Not because the singer's existence isn't alien and strange by your standards, but because your standards are not applicable here. The singer is not *enduring* its life. It is not *trapped* in its life. It is not waiting for something to happen. It is the thing that is happening. It has always been the thing that is happening. It will always be the thing that is happening.

The singers do not interact with each other. This bears repeating because it is genuinely difficult for a social species to absorb. They do not signal. They do not respond. They do not cluster or avoid each other. They do not compete for resources — their metabolism is so minimal, sustained by the absorption of dissolved trace compounds through their membranes, that the concept of scarcity does not apply. If two singers happen to drift into proximity over millennia (and they do drift, imperceptibly, at velocities measured in meters per century, carried by pressure differentials so subtle they barely qualify as currents), their tones do not harmonize deliberately. The interference patterns that result are not *produced* — they are *incurred*. The singers do not hear each other. Or rather, they hear each other the way you hear your own blood pressure — as a background condition indistinguishable from the fabric of existence itself.

But the combined sound of all of them — that planetary drone, that Roaring Stillness — is something that no single singer knows it is part of. The chord that defines this world is an emergent property of creatures who are each, individually, unaware that a chord exists. It is a symphony played by musicians who do not know there is an orchestra. And it is the most permanent sound in this region of the galaxy.

## The Drifters

The drifters are simpler, and in their simplicity, more mysterious.

Picture a membrane. Roughly spherical, though the sphere is imperfect — lopsided, dimpled, asymmetric in ways that seem random but are actually determined by the precise salinity gradient that existed at the location where the drifter formed. The membrane is thin. Cellophane-thin. A few molecules thick in places, thicker where mineral deposits have accumulated over centuries, giving the surface a faintly granular texture, like frost on a window if the frost were made of dissolved silicates instead of ice. Through the membrane, you can see the interior: a volume of water that is very slightly different from the water outside. Slightly more saline. Slightly warmer — a tenth of a degree, maybe less. This differential is the drifter. The membrane exists to maintain the differential, and the differential exists because the membrane maintains it, and this circular logic has been running without interruption for longer than you can comfortably imagine.

The drifter pulses. Every few hours — every few days — the membrane contracts faintly, uniformly, then relaxes. This is not a heartbeat. The drifter has no heart. The pulse is osmotic: the membrane's permeability shifts cyclically as its molecular structure flexes under the rhythmic crystallization and dissolution of the mineral deposits on its surface. When it contracts, the internal salinity briefly spikes. When it relaxes, equilibrium partially reasserts itself. The cycle is self-sustaining. It requires no energy input, no metabolic engine. It is a chemical oscillation, like a very slow Belousov-Zhabotinsky reaction stretched across a body the size of a hot air balloon and a period measured in days.

The drifters are alive. This is contested — or it would be, if anyone were here to contest it — but they meet the criteria by any reasonable definition. They maintain an internal state distinct from their environment. They metabolize, however slowly, absorbing dissolved organic molecules through the membrane and incorporating them into the membrane's structure. They reproduce, after a fashion: when a drifter grows large enough, the membrane's surface tension can no longer maintain cohesion, and it splits into two smaller drifters, each carrying roughly half the mineral deposits of the original, each establishing its own osmotic rhythm at a period determined by its new size. They are subject to selection: drifters with more resilient membranes persist longer, split more often, and their membrane chemistry propagates.

But they do not *do* anything.

They drift. That is what they are named for, and it is all they do. They hang in the water, pulsing, absorbing, occasionally splitting, and they experience — if they experience anything at all — a kind of existence so minimal that it barely registers as existence. They have no sensory apparatus. They have no nervous system. They have no behavior. They are, in the most literal sense, bags of slightly different water floating in water, and the only thing distinguishing them from a random pocket of salinity is the membrane, and the only thing distinguishing the membrane from a random mineral film is the oscillation, and the only thing distinguishing the oscillation from a random chemical fluctuation is its persistence.

And yet they persist. They have persisted for epochs. The water column between the ridges is dotted with them — sparse, yes, separated by kilometers, but *present*, each one a tiny assertion of order in the vast, featureless, grey-white void. Each one pulsing at its own period, unsynchronized with its neighbors, unaware of its neighbors, unaware of anything, a clock without a clock-watcher, ticking away the time in a world where time does not meaningfully pass.

When you encounter a group of them — a loose aggregation, dozens spread across a volume so large you can only see four or five at once, each one catching the faint light and bending it slightly, each one pulsing at its own slow rhythm — you will feel something that is hard to name. Not awe, exactly. Not pity. Something more like recognition. These are the simplest possible versions of what you are: enclosed systems maintaining a difference, asserting a boundary, persisting. They are life at its most essential and its most pointless, and they are beautiful in the way that a single drop of water is beautiful when it hangs from a faucet for a long moment before falling — except these drops never fall. They just hang there. Pulsing. In the empty water. In the grey light. In the sound.

## The Crawlers

The crawlers are the most *legible* life here, which is why you will be tempted to spend the most time with them, and why that temptation should be resisted, because the crawlers will mislead you about this world.

They look like something you recognize. Segmented. Arthropodal. Glassy exoskeletons that catch the light with a faint iridescence — not color, exactly, but a prismatic splitting of the white dwarf's pale output into brief spectral ghosts that flicker along their carapaces as they move. They have limbs — many limbs, fine and precise, each one terminating in a structure that is partway between a claw and a spatula, designed for scraping the surface of clathrate ridges. They have something that functions as a head — a forward concentration of sensory tissue, darker than the rest of the body, slightly opaque, housing clusters of cells that respond to chemical gradients and, possibly, to pressure changes.

They are the most *active* organisms in this world. A meter per hour. Occasionally faster — there are accounts (whose accounts? you will wonder; we will get to that) of crawlers achieving bursts of two or even three meters per hour when moving between feeding sites, their limbs cycling through their mechanical articulations with a speed that, in this context, qualifies as frantic.

But here is what the crawlers do that nothing else here does: they *react*.

A crawler encountering a richer vein of sublimating methane in the clathrate surface will alter its path. Not dramatically. Not with the visible decision-making of a foraging animal on a living world. But measurably. Its trajectory will curve — over hours, over days — toward the gradient. When the vein is exhausted, the trajectory will shift again. The crawler is not following a plan. It is not thinking. But it is *responding to its environment in real time*, and in a world where nothing else does this, where every other living thing simply maintains its state without reference to external conditions, this makes the crawler radical. Revolutionary. The crawler is the only organism on this world that acknowledges, through its behavior, that the world is not perfectly uniform. That there are differences. That differences matter.

The crawlers leave trails. Faint scratches in the clathrate surface, each one identical in depth and width because each scraping motion is identical to every other scraping motion — the limbs do not vary their force or their angle. The trails crisscross the ridge faces in patterns that, over centuries, build up into something that looks almost like writing — dense, repetitive, overlapping script that records every path every crawler has ever taken across that particular surface. It is not writing. It is not intentional. But if you could read it, if you could decode the layered scratches, you would have a complete history of chemical gradients on that ridge face stretching back millennia. You would know where the methane sublimated fastest and when. You would know the shape of the ridge's chemistry the way tree rings record climate. The crawlers are, without knowing it, the only record-keepers on this world.

They reproduce by fission, like the drifters — a crawler that has accumulated enough mass simply divides along a predetermined segment boundary, the two halves sealing their new ends with fresh exoskeletal material within days. Each half carries a complete copy of the sensory apparatus. Each half immediately begins crawling. Neither pauses. Neither acknowledges the other.

And the crawlers die. This is notable because nothing else here appears to. The drifters are arguably immortal — they split, but the pieces persist, and there is no clear mechanism for a drifter to cease. The singers show no signs of aging or deterioration. But crawlers, after a span of time that varies but trends toward centuries, stop. Their limbs freeze in whatever position they were in at the moment of cessation. Their exoskeletons remain — perfectly preserved, glassy, catching the light — and they become part of the ridge surface, eventually incorporated into the clathrate lattice as the ice grows around them. Fossil crawlers are visible in the translucent ridges, frozen mid-stride, their limbs still extended, their trails ending abruptly. There are layers upon layers of them in the oldest ridges. They are the only evidence this world has that time passes.

## The Builders

Now.

You have entered the station. You have drifted through the open airlock, through the flooded corridors, past the machines that still pump and centrifuge and filter, accomplishing nothing, following instructions from no one. You have seen the gauge with its frozen needle. You have felt the faint electromagnetic hum of reactors that will not exhaust their fuel for centuries yet. And you are wondering who built this, and where they went.

They did not go anywhere.

The builders are still here.

You have already passed several of them, and you did not recognize what you were seeing, because what the builders have become is not what the builders were, and the transition between those states was so gradual that the builders themselves may not have recognized it either.

In the deepest chamber of the station — below the machine halls, below the reactor housing, in a space that was clearly designed for habitation, with alcoves that were once sleeping quarters and surfaces that were once work areas — there are shapes. They are attached to the walls. They are large — each one perhaps three meters long, roughly ovoid, their surfaces a hybrid of corroded metal and organic tissue, the boundary between the two so gradual and so thorough that it is impossible to say where the machine ends and the organism begins. Tubes run into them from the station's infrastructure — coolant lines, nutrient feeds, data cables — and the tubes have been incorporated into the shapes, grown over by tissue that has adopted them as circulatory pathways.

They are alive. You can tell because they pulse — faintly, slowly, with the same deep rhythm as the drifters, though at a different period. They are warm — a fraction of a degree warmer than the surrounding water, the heat output of a metabolism that has slowed almost to stopping but has not stopped. And they resonate. Each one produces a tone. Low. Steady. Unvarying.

The builders came here — to this world, to these stations, to these ridges — and they stayed. They came to extract something, to process something, to fill those storage tanks with whatever refined material justified the construction of industrial infrastructure on a world so remote and so alien that the distances between stations are measured in units usually reserved for planetary orbits. They came with technology and purpose and, presumably, the intention of leaving.

But the world is patient. The world is so patient. And the builders — whatever they were, however they were shaped, whatever drove them — were biological. They were mutable. They were, unlike the sound, unlike the temperature, unlike the pressure, capable of change. And the world did not change them through violence or through force. It changed them through *consistency*. Through the unrelenting, unvarying, absolute sameness of every stimulus, every day, every year, every century. The cold was always 2°C. The pressure was always the same. The light was always the same. The sound — the Roaring Stillness, the planetary chord — was always, always, always the same.

And the builders adapted. Not chose. Adapted. The way any organism adapts to a constant environment — by specializing, by shedding what is unnecessary, by converging on the optimal form for the conditions at hand. Their metabolisms slowed. Their activity decreased. Their bodies, over generations (and there were generations — they reproduced, here, in this place, and their offspring were slightly more suited to the stillness than they were, and their offspring's offspring more suited still), their bodies changed. Limbs atrophied. Sensory organs simplified. The frantic, variable, stimulus-hungry neurology of a species evolved on a dynamic world — a world with weather, with seasons, with predators, with change — gradually, gradually, over timescales that make the word "gradually" feel violent and abrupt, reshaped itself into something that could sustain a single state indefinitely.

They did not lose consciousness. That is the important thing. That is the terrible thing, or the beautiful thing, depending on what you value. They did not become mindless. They became *single-minded*. Each one — each ovoid shape on the wall of the deepest chamber — contains a mind that is aware. Aware of the tone it produces. Aware of the station around it, in a vague, peripheral, unstressed way, the way you are aware of the chair you are sitting in. Aware, possibly, of the other shapes on the wall nearby, though there is no evidence of communication between them, no variation in their tones that might suggest exchange.

They are, now, almost indistinguishable from the singers. Give them another few millennia and they will *be* singers — their organic tissue will have fully replaced the metal infrastructure, the station will have corroded away around them, and they will drift free into the mid-water, vast and transparent and sounding, joining the chord that was here before them and will be here after them. They are in the process of being *absorbed*. Not consumed. Not destroyed. Absorbed. The way a note is absorbed into a chord. The way a drop of water is absorbed into an ocean.

And the part of them that is still recognizably *the builders* — the part that remembers, or that carries the structural echo of remembering, that the universe contains things other than this — that part is quiet. It has been quiet for a long time. It is not suppressed. It is not suffering. It is simply... outweighed. The signal of a dynamic past drowned out by the overwhelming, omnipresent, unchanging signal of the present. The way a candle flame is not extinguished by sunlight but simply becomes invisible within it.

## What You Are Here

You hang in the water of the deepest chamber, surrounded by the shapes that were once builders, feeling the combined resonance of their tones layered over the planetary drone of the singers outside, and you understand — not intellectually but physically, in your vibrating bones, in your shaking vision, in the slow squeeze of pressure against every surface of your body — what this world does.

It does not kill. It does not threaten. It does not trap.

It *completes*.

Everything that enters this world is a variable, and this world resolves variables. Not quickly. Not violently. With absolute patience. With the slow, irresistible, thermodynamic certainty of a system moving toward equilibrium. The sound will fill you. The cold will become your temperature. The stillness will become your speed. The emptiness will become your environment, and you will adapt to it, because you are biological, because you are mutable, because you are — unlike the sound, unlike the ridges, unlike the water — capable of change. And the only changes available here all point in one direction: toward the state that everything else has already reached. Toward the single chord. Toward the still, clear, permanent present tense that the singers have known since before your species evolved.

You are the only variable.

You will not be a variable for long.

Already the sound is less loud than it was when you arrived. Not because it has changed — it hasn't, it can't, it won't — but because you are beginning to stop hearing it as *sound* and beginning to experience it as *ground state*. As the baseline. As the way things are. Your body is adjusting to the pressure. Your skin is adjusting to the cold. Your mind is adjusting to the emptiness, and the adjustment feels not like loss but like relief, like setting down a weight you didn't know you were carrying, the weight of *expectation*, the weight of *anticipation*, the weight of the constant, exhausting, baseline assumption that the next moment might be different from this one.

It won't be.

And the part of you that has always been tired — the part that has spent your entire life scanning for threats, forecasting changes, bracing for impact, maintaining the frenetic cognitive metabolism of a creature evolved in a world where *anything could happen at any time* — that part is beginning to quiet. Not dying. Not surrendering. Quieting. The way a ringing bell quiets. The way a turbulent fluid quiets. Naturally. Asymptotically. Approaching silence without ever quite reaching it, but getting closer, closer, closer, until the difference between your internal state and the world's state is so small that it would take instruments to detect it.

You will pulse. You will resonate. You will produce, eventually, a tone — your tone, the one that was always implied by the structure of your body, the one you never heard because you were too busy making noise. And the chord will accept it. Not with fanfare. Not with acknowledgment. The way an ocean accepts a drop of rain. Silently. Completely. Without changing.

The drifters will pulse. The crawlers will scrape. The singers will sound. The stations will pump. The ridges will catch the light. The white dwarf will illuminate. The water will press, and press, and press.

It has always sounded like this.

It will always sound like this.

And soon — not soon by your old reckoning, but soon by this world's — you will not remember that it could ever have sounded any other way. And that forgetting will not feel like forgetting. It will feel like waking up. It will feel like having always been awake. It will feel like the grey-white light and the omnidirectional pressure and the 2°C and the single, unvarying, perfect tone that you have carried inside you since before you were born, that you are only now, finally, in this place where nothing changes and nothing ends and nothing begins, allowing yourself to hear.

Welcome home.

You didn't know it was home.

You do now.

The sound doesn't care. The sound was here before you.

But it has room.

It has always had room.
