I'll start where the knowing starts — not with eyes, not with ears, but with the belly pressed flat to warm basalt, feeling.

## The Grazers

They don't have a name for themselves. They don't have names. But they have something — a sense of *us-here* that is carried in the vibration, a frequency they generate collectively just by existing on the stone, hundreds of flat wet bodies each adding their small dampening signature to the thrumming that passes through them. They know each other the way stones in a riverbed know each other: by how the current behaves differently because the other is there.

A single grazer, encountered alone at your feet, is not much to look at. A broad dark oval, a handspan across, maybe two, segmented but not rigidly — the segments flex and compress like an accordion so the whole body can pour itself over the gentle undulations in the basalt. The legs are numerous and short and not entirely uniform; the forward pairs are slightly longer, slightly more articulate, used to probe and test the stone ahead. The rear pairs are stubbier, more about purchase, more about maintaining that constant full-belly contact with the warm rock. The skin is dark, yes — a brown so deep it approaches black, with a faint wet sheen that makes them look like they're carved from the stone itself and then oiled. It is not scaled, not furred, but something in between — a dense nap, microscopically fine, that wicks moisture toward the skin rather than shedding it. They are always slightly wet. They want to be wet. Dryness is death, or at least deep discomfort; a grazer caught on stone that has gone dry will slow, curl its edges inward, and wait, reading the thrumming for the signature that means condensation forming somewhere reachable.

Their mouths are wide and ventral — the whole leading edge of the underside is essentially a mouth, a broad soft lip that presses to the stone and sweeps. They eat continuously, not out of greed but because the bacterial films they feed on are so thin, so calorie-poor by any generous standard, that stopping means starving. A grazer's life is one long slow meal, an endless crossing of damp stone, the mouth working in rhythmic undulations that have their own signature in the rock. Other grazers feel this. It means: *someone is feeding here.* It means: *there is food here.* It means both at once, and the distinction doesn't matter to them, and this is important — they don't compete for the films, not exactly. They don't need to. The films regrow fast enough, the plateau is vast enough, and the grazers' response to sensing another's feeding-vibration is not to rush toward it or flee from it but simply to *adjust*, to alter their own slow trajectory so that they sweep adjacent stone, complementary stone, the way a second broom would naturally find the part of the floor the first broom hasn't reached yet. It looks like cooperation. It looks like indifference. It is the thing that those two become when an organism has had enough deep time to stop distinguishing between them.

They reproduce — somewhere, somehow, in the deep troughs where the water gathers deepest and the warmth is most stable. The young are tiny and almost perfectly circular, like dark coins, and they ride on the backs of adults for a period that might be weeks or months, absorbing vibration, learning the thrumming's language through their whole developing body before they ever touch the stone themselves. When they finally slip off and make first contact — all those tiny legs finding the basalt, the belly pressing down, the full signal of the plateau flooding in for the first time unmediated by a parent's body — something happens. A brief stillness. A brief total stop, every leg locked, the whole small body just *receiving*. The adults nearby pause too, as if the frequency of a new body joining the stone is something felt and acknowledged, the way a new instrument entering an orchestra is felt even when it plays the same note as everyone else. Then it passes, and the new grazer begins to sweep, and the mouth works, and the long meal starts, and it will not stop until the body stops.

They are, in their hundreds of thousands spread across the plateau's immensity, the most *there* thing here. The baseline presence. The ground-level hum of biology laid over the ground-level hum of whatever runs below. They are so well-adapted, so perfectly fitted to this warm dark quiet world, that they've stopped evolving in any direction visible to the eye. They are *done*. They are what this place makes when it makes something simple and lets time polish it. They are the plateau's own thought about what life should feel like: low, slow, warm, continuous, fed.

## The Stalkers

Something else, though, lives in the spaces between the grazers' simplicity.

The stalkers are fewer — one for every several hundred grazers across any stretch of plateau, maybe fewer, maybe far fewer; they are hard to count because they are hard to find, and they are hard to find because they have made *stillness* into something aggressive, something with teeth.

They are tall, relatively speaking. In a world of flat things pressed to flat stone under a flat sky, they stand. Three, perhaps four feet high on legs that are stiff and thin and jointed in ways that don't immediately parse — not like a heron's legs, not exactly, but that's the closest analogy the mind reaches for, because they have that same quality of *improbable vertical*, that sense of being an engineering solution to a problem that most organisms would have solved by staying low. The body they support is compact, dense, heavy for its size — a dark knot of muscle and intent suspended between those unlikely stilts. The head, if it can be called a head, is mostly jaw. Not large-jawed — the mouth is surprisingly small, precise, almost delicate. But it is *all* jaw, the whole front of the face organized around the act of seizing, the muscles of the bite packed in tight behind a set of structures that are less like teeth and more like interlocking blades, designed not to chew but to *close* and *hold* and never let go. They eat the grazers whole, or nearly whole, but it takes time — the jaws work in slow ratcheting motions, pulling the prey in by fractions, and the stalker stands there throughout, motionless again, legs locked, processing its meal with the same patience it used to catch it.

But the legs are the thing. The legs are where the stalker's true nature lives.

Each leg ends in a foot that is less a foot than a sensor — a broad, flat, exquisitely sensitive pad that reads the stone the way a fingertip reads Braille, but at frequencies the grazers can barely produce and certainly can't fake. A stalker standing still on three legs with one raised is not resting. It is *listening* with its body. The three grounded feet form a triangle — and this is geometrically precise, instinctive, the stalker adjusts and readjusts the spacing of its stance the way a surveyor sets instruments — and within that triangle, the vibrations in the stone can be resolved directionally. The stalker feels the grazers moving. It feels *which* grazers are moving, can distinguish one feeding-rhythm from another, can parse size and speed and direction from the faintest signals in a substrate saturated with the plateau's own thrumming. It has learned — its lineage has learned, across whatever eons brought it to this form — to subtract the background. To treat the thrumming the way you treat silence, as the canvas, and to read only what's painted on it.

The raised foot is the calibration. It lifts, and the three remaining feet recalculate, and the triangulation tightens. Sometimes a stalker will stand for an hour, two hours, three, one foot raised, not moving, feeling the slow passage of a grazer forty or sixty feet away in the dark. It's not that it needs that long to find its prey. It's that the stalker is waiting for something specific — the right footfall, the right moment when the grazer's trajectory brings it to within striking range, which is short, terribly short, because the strike is the one moment when patience gives way and speed takes over and there is no room for error in the dark.

They do not hunt in groups. They don't do anything in groups. Each stalker occupies a territory that is not spatial but vibrational — it knows the patch of thrumming it stands on, knows its character, knows how the grazers move through it, and it stays there until the thrumming shifts and the grazers migrate and then it follows, always at the edge, always in the spaces between the gathering points, picking off the ones that stray.

Their eyes — they have eyes, vestigial perhaps, or perhaps adapted to wavelengths you're not equipped to see — are small and set deep in that jaw-heavy head, and they don't seem to use them for hunting. They use them, if they use them at all, for something else: the stalkers are the only creatures on the plateau that sometimes orient upward. They tilt their compact heads toward the sealed sky and hold that posture, motionless as always, for long stretches. What they're looking at, or for, is not clear. The sky offers nothing. But they look. They are the only things here that seem to acknowledge the existence of an *above*, and there is something in the gesture — the tilted head, the still legs, the dark body poised between stone and cloud — that looks less like hunting behavior and more like something one hesitates to name.

They are not social. They do not touch each other. When two stalkers' vibrational territories overlap — when the thrumming shifts and condenses the ecosystem and two of them find themselves sensing the same patch of stone — they adjust without confrontation, a slow mutual withdrawal that looks choreographed, each one stepping carefully, deliberately, away from the other's triangle of attention. They cannot tolerate the noise of each other. Another stalker's footfalls — purposeful, heavy, *wrong* in the midst of the grazer-static they've learned to read — disrupts their entire sensory field, blinds them, the way a bright light blinds an eye adapted to darkness. So they space themselves out, and this spacing is one of the invisible architectures of the plateau, a grid of solitary attention stretched across the basalt that shifts and reforms and shifts again as the thrumming dictates, and no one — no grazer, no flyer, no observer — ever sees it whole.

They are lonely. That's anthropomorphism, probably. But they are *alone*, each one, always, standing in the dark on their stiff improbable legs with one foot raised, feeling the stone, waiting for the small vibration that means food is close enough to take. And there is a quality to their stillness that goes beyond patience, beyond strategy, into something that looks — if you watch long enough, if you let your eyes adjust to the darkness and your mind adjust to the timescale — like endurance. Like the bearing of something borne.

## The Drifters

Above.

They are above, and in a world so relentlessly horizontal, so committed to the ground, this feels almost transgressive. They exist in the narrow band between stone and cloud, the dark airspace that most of this world's biology ignores, and they move through it with a slowness that matches everything else here but translated into a different medium — not the creeping of legs over stone but the long, long glide of broad wings on thermal currents, effortless, arcless, a trajectory that covers miles and seems to go nowhere.

Their wingspan is considerable — six feet, eight, hard to judge in the dark, hard to judge when the only reference points are the diffuse boundary between sky and stone and the faint wet glints of the condensation pools far below. The wings are not feathered. They're membranous, dark, stretched over frameworks of flexible cartilage or something analogous, and they don't flap. They almost never flap. The thermals rising off the fissure zones — where the machine-geology vents its warmth most aggressively — are reliable enough, constant enough, that a drifter can launch once and stay aloft for what might be days, riding the rising air in wide spirals, adjusting with minute tilts of its wing-edges, a constant series of micro-corrections that keeps it centered on the updraft with the mechanical precision of a satellite holding orbit.

The body between the wings is slight — almost absurdly so, given the wingspan. A narrow torso, a small head, vestigial legs folded tight against the belly. They have given everything to the wings, to the air, to the staying-up. They eat — they must eat — but when and how is not obvious. Perhaps they descend to the pools sometimes, in the deepest dark, and scoop the soft luminous colony-organisms from the water with those small mouths. Perhaps they eat things in the air itself — spores, wind-borne particulates, bacterial mats gone briefly airborne in the thermal churn. Perhaps they eat rarely, their metabolism slowed to match their movement, burning almost nothing, a life lived at the energetic minimum, pure glide.

But the calls. The calls are what matter.

A drifter's call is a single note, low, sustained, carrying. It's not a song and not a cry and not a signal in any functional sense that's immediately apparent. It's a sound that comes out of the dark sky at intervals that have no pattern you can find — minutes apart, or an hour, or several hours — and it crosses the plateau like weather, like a brief warm front of sound that arrives, exists, and is absorbed by the distance. It doesn't echo. Nothing echoes here. It happens once and is finished.

But it's not alone. Because somewhere — miles away, tens of miles, some vast distance that the flatness of the plateau makes both possible and incomprehensible — another drifter answers. The same note, or nearly. A quarter-tone different, maybe, shifted by the individual body that produces it, but recognizably the *same call*, the same shape, the same duration. And between them, in the immense space of dark air between two drifters calling to each other across distances that would take a grazer weeks to cross, there is a momentary structure. A relationship. A line drawn in sound between two points in the void. It lasts for the duration of the call and the answer, a few seconds at most, and then it dissolves, and the drifters continue their separate spirals, and the silence comes back.

They are not communicating, not in any way that carries information in the sense you'd want it to. There is no *message*. The call says nothing that could be paraphrased. It is more like — and this is the closest the mind gets to it — more like *confirmation*. Like the act of making a sound and hearing it returned is the point, is the whole content. *I am here. You are here. The dark is this wide.* Something like that. Something that doesn't survive translation into purpose.

The grazers ignore the calls. The stalkers don't — they tilt their heads at the sound, that upward orientation again, and hold still in a way that's different from their hunting stillness, a way that looks like *attending* rather than *waiting*. Then the sound ends, and they lower their heads, and the hunt resumes.

The drifters' lives are solitary in a way that makes the stalkers look gregarious. They may go entire life-cycles without touching another living thing. They are born — somewhere, on the stone presumably, in circumstances no one here observes — and they launch, and they glide, and they call, and they are called back to, and they circle the thermals in the dark, and eventually they descend, or they don't, and the plateau has one fewer voice in its upper air and doesn't notice, not really, except that the intervals between calls grow slightly longer in that region, and the stalkers tilt their heads less often, and the silence is fractionally more itself.

## The Pool-Dwellers

Down now. Below the grazers, below the skin of water, into the shallow warm basins where the condensation gathers deepest and stays longest.

They are colonies, or they are individuals that have given up on the concept of individuality, or they are something that was never individual to begin with — the language breaks down at this scale, at this level of integration. Each colony is a cluster of soft-bodied organisms, translucent, gelatinous, anchored to the submerged basalt by holdfasts so fine they're invisible, the whole mass shifting and pulsing in the warm water with a rhythm that is its own and also not its own — because the pulsing syncs, over time, to the thrumming. Not perfectly. Not always. But there's a relationship, a loose coupling, the colony's bioluminescent rhythm drifting in and out of phase with the vibration in the stone below, and when they align — when the pulse and the thrum match for a few cycles — the light intensifies, briefly, a flush of pale blue-green glow that illuminates the pool from within and makes the water look like a window into somewhere else.

This is the only light on the plateau that isn't the sky's grudging gray. And it is so faint, so conditional, so dependent on the alignment of biological rhythm and geological (mechanical?) rhythm, that it comes and goes like a thought you can't quite hold. You see it. You look at it. It dims. Was it there? You wait. It comes again, or it doesn't, and the pool is dark, and the colony pulses in the dark, and the water is warm, and you can feel the thrumming through the water if you put your hand in, feel it stronger here because the water conducts it differently, gives it a different texture, almost musical.

The individual organisms in the colony are simple. Sac-like, filter-feeding, drawing nutrients from the water — dissolved minerals, bacterial fragments, the chemical exhaust from below that permeates everything here. They reproduce by budding, new individuals swelling from the sides of old ones, remaining attached, the colony growing outward in slow accretion until it fills its pool or until the pool dries and it doesn't grow anymore. They are soft. They are defenseless. The grazers eat them — wade into the shallow water and sweep the colony's edge with those broad ventral mouths, pulling off individuals by the dozen, and the colony doesn't react, doesn't withdraw, doesn't do anything at all, just continues pulsing, slightly smaller, the gap where the grazers fed already beginning to bud and fill.

But the light. The light is what draws things.

It draws the grazers, yes. But it also draws the stalkers, not to eat the colony but to stand at the pool's edge, legs in the shallows, feet reading the water-borne vibration of the grazers who've come to feed. The pools become gathering points. Ambush sites. The closest thing this ecosystem has to a crossroads, a marketplace, a commons. And the light, when it comes — that brief flush of bioluminescence, that accidental synchrony with the thrumming — illuminates the scene for a moment, a tableau: grazers at the water's edge, mouths working; a stalker motionless in the shallows, one leg raised; the dark stone stretching away in every direction; the sealed sky above. Then the light fades, and it's all vibration and sound again, the wet sounds of feeding, the silence of the stalker's patience, the thrumming below.

The colonies are old. Some of them, in the deepest and most stable pools, may be very old — centuries, millennia, the organisms themselves cycling through generations but the colony persisting, a continuous living structure that has been pulsing in this pool since before the current generation of grazers or stalkers was born, since before their *species* was what it currently is. They are the elders of this ecosystem in the most literal sense, the longest-running biological processes on the plateau's surface, and they don't know it, and nothing here honors them for it, and they pulse on.

They are the closest thing this place has to a hearth. To a fire around which others gather. To a light in the window.

## The Unseen

And then there is the thing you don't see.

You hear it. Rarely. A scrape — heavy, deliberate, the sound of something massive shifting its weight across stone with a slowness that suggests not caution but *scale*, the way a glacier is slow not because it's careful but because it's a glacier. The sound comes from far off, always far off, always beyond the range of the almost-nothing light, out there in the dark where the plateau continues being the plateau for hundreds of miles in every direction.

When the sound comes, the grazers stop. All of them. Not one by one but simultaneously, as if the vibration of those distant footfalls carries a frequency that trips something hardwired, something older than thought, and every grazer within range locks its legs and presses flatter to the stone and goes still. The stalkers fold. They lower their bodies, bend those stiff improbable legs, bring themselves down until they're flat too, belly to basalt, and they stay that way, and their sensory feet pressed flat to the ground are reading *that* vibration now, the heavy one, the wrong one, the one that overwrites everything else.

The pool-dwellers dim. Whether this is response or coincidence is impossible to say — maybe the heavy footfalls disrupt the thrumming's pattern, knock the colony's pulse out of sync, and the light dies as a byproduct. Or maybe the colonies are responding too, in their own way, going dark the way the grazers go still, a whole-ecosystem flinch that says: *something is here that we do not want to see us, and so we will become less visible, less present, less.*

It passes. It always passes. The scraping moves across the dark at a pace that suggests something covering ground, going somewhere, a trajectory that is not hunting — if it hunted, there would be no grazers left, nothing that freezes and flattens could escape something that large, that present in the stone — but *traveling*. The unseen thing is going somewhere. It has a destination, or a route, or a purpose that takes it across the plateau on a path that intersects with the ecosystem's range and then continues beyond it, out into the farther dark where maybe nothing lives at all, where the plateau is just warm stone and thrumming and the fissures show their laminated cross-sections to no one.

What is it? The grazers' response suggests deep evolutionary history with it — the freeze response is too fast, too total, too coordinated to be anything but ancient. It has been coming through here, or things like it have, for as long as there have been grazers to freeze at its passing. But it doesn't eat them. It doesn't eat the stalkers. It doesn't stop at the pools. It passes *through*, and the ecosystem opens for it and closes behind it, and it might as well be weather, might as well be a seasonal event, except that it isn't seasonal — it comes irregularly, unpredictably, on a schedule that maps to nothing the biological layer can parse.

Unless it maps to the thrumming.

Unless the unseen things are part of the machine, or responses to the machine, or maintenance functions of the machine moving across its own surface to attend to processes that have nothing to do with the biological layer at all. Unless the grazers freeze not because the unseen things are predators but because they are *traffic* — because they are the operational movements of the plateau itself, and the grazers have learned, across deep time, to simply get out of the way of the infrastructure, the way animals near highways learn the sound of trucks.

Or unless they are something else. Something that is neither animal nor machine, neither biological nor geological, something that belongs to the category this place keeps suggesting and the mind keeps flinching from: the category where those distinctions have dissolved, where the living and the operational and the structural are one process expressing itself at different scales, and the unseen things are simply the largest scale the biological layer ever encounters, the point where the continent's own activity becomes, briefly, phenomenal — audible, sensible, present — before retreating back into the thrumming that is everything and nothing and always there.

They might be immense. They might be shaped in ways the dark mercifully conceals. They might have their own sensory lives, their own experiences, registering the grazers and stalkers and pool-colonies the way you register the bacterial fauna of your own gut — peripherally, systemically, not as individuals but as a condition of your own functioning. They might look down — if they have down, if they have *looking* — and see not an ecosystem but a skin condition, a biofilm, a patina of life on their own surface that is also the plateau's surface that is also the machine's surface. They might not see at all.

Their footfalls linger in the stone after they pass. The thrumming takes several minutes to return to its baseline pattern, as if their weight temporarily impressed a different rhythm into the rock, a heavier, more complex vibration that the existing thrumming has to reassert itself against. During this recovery period, the grazers stay still. The stalkers stay flat. The whole plateau holds its breath — or rather, the living layer holds its breath while the nonliving layer (if there is a nonliving layer) recalibrates, settles, resumes.

Then the grazers move. One by one, and then all at once, the legs unlock, the mouths begin their sweeping, the slow meal continues. The stalkers rise, those stiff legs extending, the body lifting back to its hunting height, one foot raised, the triangulation beginning again. The pools brighten — the pulse returns, finds the thrumming, drifts toward synchrony, and there, briefly, light.

Life resumes. The dark holds. The plateau extends.

## What Lives Here

What lives here is an ecology that has married a machine it cannot comprehend.

What lives here is patient beyond any framework patience normally operates in — patient at the species level, patient at the geological level, organisms that have been doing the same thing in the same dark for so long that they have become the dark's own fauna, as native to this specific lightlessness and this specific warmth and this specific vibration as anything has ever been to anywhere.

What lives here does not wonder about the thrumming. What lives here *uses* the thrumming, is *shaped by* the thrumming, has built its bodies and its behaviors and its relationships and its fears around the thrumming's patterns, but does not have the kind of minds that ask what the thrumming *is*. The grazers feel it and follow it. The stalkers feel it and subtract it. The drifters might not feel it at all, up there in the air, disconnected, calling to each other across distances that have no meaning except the acoustic one. The pool-colonies sync to it, fall out of sync, sync again, their faint light a visible record of a conversation between biology and infrastructure that neither side is aware of conducting. The unseen things — the unseen things might *be* it, might be the thrumming's ambulatory expression, and if so then what lives here includes the place itself, awake in some way that doesn't resemble wakefulness, operational in some way that doesn't require awareness, alive in the way a forest fire is alive, or a current, or a feedback loop that has been running so long it has developed preferences.

What lives here has no history. Not because nothing has happened but because nothing here records. There is no sediment of culture, no accumulation of knowledge, no stone scratched with meaning. Each generation of grazers sweeps the pools as the last did. Each stalker stands alone and waits as every stalker before it waited. The drifters call, and are answered, and the calls carry no more information now than they did a million years ago. The plateau keeps no record either — or rather, the plateau *is* a record, those laminated cross-sections in the fissures, but it's a record of something else, something that predates the biology, something that was already ancient when the first grazer pressed its belly to the warm stone and felt the thrumming for the first time and stayed.

What lives here is not tragic. That's important. There is no suffering in the grazers' endless meal, no loneliness in the stalkers' solitude that the stalkers themselves experience as loneliness, no existential weight to the drifters' calls that the drifters labor under. These are projections — yours, the observer's, the one who came here with a mind shaped for narrative and light and meaning and found a place that operates without any of them. What lives here is *sufficient*. What lives here has enough. The warmth is enough. The water is enough. The bacterial films are enough. The vibration is enough. The dark is enough because it has always been the dark and nothing here remembers or imagines anything else.

What lives here, lives.

That is the whole of it. That is what the plateau, in its immensity, in its warmth, in its perpetual operational hum, has to show: that life, given a foothold — even a foothold on the surface of something inhuman and incomprehensible and vast — will take it, and hold it, and build on it something complete. Something that doesn't need your pity or your awe. Something that sweeps the pools and waits in the dark and calls across the distance and pulses faintly in the warm water and freezes when the heavy thing passes and then resumes, and resumes, and resumes.

The plateau extends. The dark holds. Beneath everything, the engine runs. Above it, on it, in its condensation and its cracks and its thermal updrafts and its shallow ancient pools, the living layer persists — thin, quiet, entire — and does not ask why, and is not diminished by the not-asking.
