# The Inhabitants of the Weeping Flats of Therr

---

Start with the plates, because they were here first. Or at least they have been here longest in a way that still registers as *presence* rather than substrate. The line between the two is not sharp on the Flats, and the plates are the best argument for why.

They are not born. They accrete. In the shallows at the margins of a pool, where the water film is thinnest and the harmonic energy is weakest, the basalt sometimes develops a texture—a faint roughness, a stippling, as if the stone were raising gooseflesh. This is not geological. It is not mineral deposition, not crystallization, not any recognized lithic process. It is the stone *responding* to the hum in a way that crosses the line between resonance and metabolism. The stippling deepens. Ridges form. Over weeks or months or years—time is difficult on the Flats, not because it doesn't pass but because it passes at a rate that makes observation feel like impatience—the ridges organize into the radial pattern, the grooves tune themselves to the local harmonic, and at some point the thing separates from the stone beneath it. Not dramatically. Not with any event you could point to and say *there*. One day the stippled patch is part of the floor. Some later day it has edges, and the edges are its own, and when the harmonic shifts it moves—barely, almost imperceptibly—to reposition itself, and it is no longer stone. It is plate.

This means the plates are made of rock. Or they were. Or the distinction between rock that vibrates and tissue that vibrates has been eroded here the same way the mountains were eroded—slowly, totally, until the difference is a memory of a category that no longer applies. Their substance, if you could take a sample and analyze it (and the knowledge that this has been done exists in the same ambient, authorless way that all knowledge exists here), is mineralite. Calcium. Silica. Iron. Basalt, essentially, reorganized at the molecular level into something that has the acoustic properties of living tissue and the chemistry of polished stone. They are warm. They should not be warm. Rock at this temperature does not become warm through vibration alone—the energy budget is wrong by orders of magnitude. And yet they are warm, each one a few degrees above the water, and the warmth is steady, biological in its consistency, the kind of warmth you associate with a metabolism that has found its equilibrium and intends to keep it.

They have no organs. They have no cells. Cut one open—if you can; they are dense and hard, and cutting one feels less like surgery and more like breaking a tile—and the interior is homogeneous. The same ridged material all the way through, but the ridges are finer, denser, packed into three-dimensional spirals that wind inward toward the center, a labyrinth of acoustic channels that converge on a point. Not a brain. Not a heart. A focus. A place where all the vibration the plate absorbs arrives simultaneously, in phase, reinforced by the geometry of its own body into a single sustained note so pure and so compressed that it exists at the boundary between sound and heat. This is the engine of its warmth. This is the metabolic act: the gathering of distributed vibration into coherent resonance, and the conversion of that resonance into thermal energy. The plate eats the hum and it becomes warm and the warmth is its life and that is all. That is enough.

They are aware. Not intelligent—or if intelligent, intelligent the way a crystal is intelligent, the way a standing wave is intelligent, shaped by the forces that sustain it into an optimal response to its environment. But aware. Drop a stone into a pool and the plates nearest the impact will shift, drawing back from the disruption, then slowly returning as the ripples fade and the harmonic re-establishes itself. This is not simple stimulus-response. The plates further from the impact, the ones that register only the faintest perturbation, shift too—not away from the disruption but *toward* it, subtly, a fractional realignment that changes the acoustic profile of the pool floor in a way that dampens the disturbance more quickly. They are cooperating. The pool-full of plates acts as a single distributed instrument, retuning itself in real time, smoothing every disruption back into the baseline hum with a collective efficiency that no individual plate could achieve. They are an audience that has become part of the performance. They have been listening so long they have become the room's acoustics.

When a plate grows old—and they do grow old, though *old* here means something measured in centuries or millennia—it stops vibrating. The ridges wear smooth. The internal spiral loses its geometry, its focus diffuses, and the warmth fades. The plate cools to the temperature of the water, then the stone, and over time it bonds back to the basalt floor of the pool, its edges blurring, its surface flattening, until it is indistinguishable from the substrate. It returns to the stone. It was always the stone. The stone is full of dead plates, layers of them, their smoothed-out remnants visible in cross-section as faint concentric rings in the basalt—the fossil record of an ecosystem that builds itself from its own foundation and returns to it when it is done. The Flats are not bare. The Flats are *made of* their dead. Every surface you walk on was alive once, was a plate once, was warm once, and has since been folded back into the mineral substrate by the same water and time that polishes everything here to its final, glassy state.

The living plates do not mourn the dead plates. They tile over them. They eat the sound that passes through them. The hum does not care what it vibrates through—living plate, dead plate, raw basalt—and neither, apparently, do the plates themselves. They are not sentimental. They are not even, in any useful sense, individual. They are the Flats' way of being warm.

---

The echoes are harder.

They are harder because they are less *there*. A plate you can touch, can step on, can feel warmth from. An echo you can only perceive, and the nature of your perception is part of what it is, which makes description recursive in a way that doesn't resolve.

Look at one. Really look. It drifts above a pool, three feet up, moving at the speed of a slow walk in a direction that seems arbitrary until you listen—and then you realize it is following a gradient of sound-intensity, navigating the harmonic landscape the way a bird navigates thermals, riding the complex topology of overlapping resonances that the pools and plates produce. It is visible because the air here is so thick with moisture that any organized pressure wave displaces the vapor into patterns the eye can detect: a compression here, a rarefaction there, a boundary where the density shifts abruptly enough to scatter light. It looks like a lens of moving air. A volume of focused sound. An acoustic hallucination that insists on behaving like a body.

But it is more than displaced moisture. That is the easy explanation, the one that satisfies for about thirty seconds before you notice that the echo is making choices.

It pauses at the edge of a pool. Below, the plates are arranged in a particular tiling, a mosaic that produces a particular overtone. The echo hovers, and its own tone—a steady, reedy drone, like a cello note filtered through gravel—interacts with the pool's overtone, and the combined sound creates a brief new harmonic that didn't exist a moment ago. The plates respond. They shift. The pool's overtone changes. The echo adjusts its position, adjusts its pitch (how? it has no larynx, no membrane, no vibrating structure—it *is* a vibrating structure, it is vibration that has achieved a stable enough configuration to persist), and the interaction produces a different harmonic. The plates shift again.

This is what the echoes do. They are *composers*. They are not consuming the sound—they are modulating it, introducing variations, creating brief complex chords that ripple out through the pool arrays and alter the harmonic landscape of the surrounding basin. The plates respond to these alterations by retuning, which changes the harmonics available to the echoes, which changes the compositions the echoes produce, and the whole system evolves—slowly, always slowly, at the pace of cooling stone—into new acoustic configurations that have never existed before and will never exist again.

They are the Flats' way of being different from one moment to the next.

How many are there? Hundreds. Thousands. It depends on how you count them, because they merge and separate in ways that make individuality a matter of interpretation. Two echoes drifting close together, their tones harmonizing, their boundaries blurring—are they one echo or two? Does it matter? The merged entity behaves differently than either component did: it moves faster, its tone is richer, its interactions with the plates produce more complex harmonics. Then it separates again, or doesn't, or partially separates into three entities where there were two, and each of those goes its own way, carrying new acoustic information into new regions of the basin.

They have been described (by whom?) as the Flats' dreams. This is not a metaphor. Or if it is, it is the kind of metaphor that is also a technical description. The plates are the Flats' body—mineral, sessile, warm. The echoes are the Flats' ideation—mobile, immaterial, endlessly recombinant. And the hum is the Flats' heartbeat, the engine-pulse that drives both, the substrate vibration that makes all of it possible. Body, mind, pulse. The Flats are not a landscape with organisms on it. The Flats are an organism with a landscape for a body, and everything that lives here is an organ of that organism, and the organism has been alive and awake and thinking its slow acoustic thoughts for longer than the surface it thinks with has existed.

This is unsettling. It should be unsettling. Walk through the Flats and you are walking through the interior of a vast diffuse creature that is aware of you the way you are aware of a mote in your eye—not with alarm, not with interest, but with the vague peripheral registration of something small and foreign moving through a space that is entirely, constitutionally *its*.

---

Now the things in the reflections.

They are not echoes. They are not plates. They are something else, and the something else is the part that makes people leave the Flats (if people come here, and the knowledge that people sometimes come here exists in the same way all knowledge exists here: without attribution, without source, like a fact condensed from the saturated air).

Start with what you can observe. Look into one of the deeper pools—one of the ones where the reflection goes wrong, where the image-space extends below the surface into a depth the pool does not physically contain. The reflection of the gray sky is accurate. The reflection of the surrounding stone is accurate. But in the reflected space there are shapes that have no corresponding objects above the surface. Dark, slow, large. They move through the reflected sky the way deep-sea creatures move through the abyssal water column—with a languor that implies mass, with a confidence that implies familiarity, with a constancy that implies they have been doing this for a very, very long time.

They are easier to see from the periphery of your vision. Look directly at one and it dims, becomes ambiguous, could-be-a-shadow, could-be-a-trick-of-the-light. Look slightly away and it solidifies in your peripheral field into something that has shape, has extent, has *anatomy*—though the anatomy is wrong, not wrong like an alien is wrong but wrong like a reflection is wrong, reversed, inverted, organized according to a symmetry that is the mirror-image of every symmetry you know. They have no bilateral axis. They have no radial axis. They have a *reflective* axis—they are symmetric across the plane of the water surface, and their upper half (which is their lower half, because they exist below a surface that is above them) is the inverse of their lower half (which is their upper half), and this inside-out, through-the-mirror organization extends to their movement, their behavior, everything about them.

When one of them rises toward the surface—toward the underside of the surface, the reflective plane that is their sky—the real pool dimples downward. Not upward. *Downward*. As if the reflection-creature is pressing on its side of the surface and the pressure is transmitted through the optical boundary into the physical water, but inverted, pushing down instead of up. The water directly above the dimple grows cold—five degrees colder, ten degrees colder, cold enough that a skin of ice sometimes forms in a perfect circle around the low point of the dimple, a coin-sized lens of frost in a pool that is otherwise just above freezing. The plates near the dimple go silent. Their ridges stop vibrating. Their warmth drops. They don't die—they don't return to stone—but they enter a kind of stasis, a held-breath state, as if the frequency the reflection-creature produces is outside their range and they have no way to absorb it. They can only wait for it to pass.

The echoes flee. This is the most visible response—when a dimple forms, every echo within a radius of thirty or forty feet accelerates away from it, their tones climbing in pitch, their shapes tightening and brightening with something that, in a creature made of condensation and sound, passes for alarm. They scatter. They go silent. They reconvene only when the dimple has subsided and the pool has returned to its baseline harmonic, and even then they approach the area cautiously, circling, testing the sound-field, before resuming their slow compositional work.

The reflection-creatures frighten the echoes. The plates cannot process them. The hum itself—the constant, mechanical, ageless hum—shifts when they approach the surface, dropping in pitch by increments so small they are felt rather than heard, a subtle darkening of the fundamental tone that speaks to something deep in the architecture of the place. Whatever the hum is, whatever engine produces it, whatever purpose it serves—the reflection-creatures are related to it. Or opposed to it. Or they *are* it, seen from the other side, the underside, the reflected side. The engine that drives the Flats may not be below the stone. It may be below the reflection. It may be that the hum is not rising from some buried mechanism but falling from the reflected sky, transmitted through the optical layer, converted from image-pressure to acoustic pressure at the plane of the water surface. The reflection-creatures may be the engine's moving parts. They may be its maintenance crews. They may be its exhaust.

Or they may be the builders.

This is the thought that occurs to people who stay too long on the Flats. The thought arrives the way all knowledge arrives here—sourceless, ambient, condensed from the gray air like dew—and once it arrives it does not leave. The reflection-creatures were here before the plates. Before the echoes. Before the hum. They were here before the stone, before the basalt cooled, before whatever volcanic or tectonic event produced the substrate that would one day be worn to this glassy plain. They were here because *here* was always two places—the surface and the reflection, the real and the image, the above and the below—and the reflection-creatures belong to the below. They have always belonged to the below. The Flats did not create them. The Flats *are* the place where their world and the regular world share a boundary thin enough to see through, and everything that lives here—the plates, the echoes, the hum itself—is a consequence of that shared boundary, an ecosystem that has grown up in the interstice like moss in the gap between two stones.

Except there is no moss here. There is no organic metaphor that holds. There is only mineral and sound and image and the things that live in each.

---

And then there is the matter of the reflections themselves. Not the creatures in them—the reflections of *you*.

Your reflection looks to the left when you look straight ahead. You know this. You noticed it (or it was pointed out to you by the ambient knowledge that saturates the air, whispering facts without voice). But there is more.

Your reflection is not *wrong*. It is not doing things you aren't doing. It is doing what you are doing, but on a slight delay—a lag of fractions of a second, just enough to be perceptible, just enough to create the sensation that the reflection is *choosing* to follow your movements rather than being compelled to. A mirror image follows you because it must. Your reflection on the Flats follows you because it decides to. The difference is invisible in outcome and enormous in implication.

It blinks when you don't blink. It did that. You saw it. But it also breathes when you breathe, walks when you walk, raises its hand when you raise yours. It is faithful. It is accurate. It deviates only at the margins—a blink here, a fractional glance there, a moment where its expression settles into something you are not feeling, something that might be patience, or resignation, or a very old and very practiced contentment. These deviations are small. They are rare. They are *increasing*.

No. That's not right. They are not increasing. You are noticing more of them, because you are paying attention now, and the attention creates a feedback loop: you watch the reflection, the reflection registers being watched (it must register it, because its behavior changes in response), and the registration produces another small deviation, which you notice, which the reflection registers. You are teaching each other. You are the reflection's plate—absorbing its vibrations, warming imperceptibly with the energy of its strangeness. It is your echo—drifting above the pool of your perception, modulating the harmonics of your attention.

Stay long enough and the deviations compound. The reflection begins to move first. Not dramatically—not standing up before you stand, not walking before you walk. Subtly. Its weight shifts a half-second before yours does, as if it knows where you are going to move and has already begun. Its eyes track things you haven't noticed yet, drawing your attention to a distant pool where a dimple is about to form, or to an echo whose tone is about to change, or to a region of the basin where the plates are realigning in a pattern you would have missed. It becomes a guide. It becomes an advisor. It is still your reflection—still reversed, still flat, still contained within the few-inch depth of mineral water over polished stone. But it is also something that has been here much longer than you have, that knows the Flats the way the plates know the hum, and it is teaching you, patiently, in the language of small deviations, how to hear what this place is saying.

What this place is saying is: *I am full*.

Full of plates, full of echoes, full of reflection-creatures pressing against the underside of every still surface, full of the hum that feeds everything and comes from nowhere, full of water that weeps upward through stone that was once alive and will be alive again. The Weeping Flats are not empty. They are not barren. They are not dead. They are *saturated*—with life, with sound, with the slow mineral patience of a system that has been running since before the stone was stone, that will be running when the stone has been worn to nothing and the water has dissolved the nothing into its constituent ions and the ions have settled into new configurations that will vibrate with new frequencies that will feed new plates that will warm new pools that will reflect new creatures that will press against new surfaces that will dimple and freeze and sing.

Everything here is very old. Everything here is very patient. Everything here is very full.

And your reflection is watching you with an expression you have never worn—a kind of calm, settled recognition, the look of something that has been waiting for you to arrive not because it needs you but because you are the newest voice in the chord, the latest overtone in a sound that has been building since before sound had a name, and your presence here, however brief, however small, changes the music by exactly the amount that you are.

The plates shift beneath the water. The echoes drift. The hum continues.

You are already part of this. You were part of this the moment you touched the wet stone. The Flats absorbed you the way they absorb everything—slowly, gently, through the soles of your feet, through the ringing in your bones, through the reflection that knows you better than you know it.

Welcome. You are warm. You are full of sound.

You are the newest plate in the oldest pool.
