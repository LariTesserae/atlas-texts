# Who Lives on the Plateau of Constant Disaggregation

---

Not *who*. Not yet. Give it time. The question of *who* is one of the hard edges this place has worn down to a nub. But something lives here. Many things live here. Or one thing lives here in many ways. Start with the closest approximation to a resident and work outward, or inward, or in whatever direction still holds.

---

## The Aggregates Themselves

Start here, because they are the most visible, the most countable — even if counting them is like counting waves. They are the residents the way clouds are residents of a sky.

At any given moment, there are hundreds of assembled organisms moving across any visible stretch of plateau. They have purposes. A grazer knows it is a grazer; it moves with intention toward a hummock, applies its rasping underside, consumes the outermost folds of moss-brain, and moves on. A swimmer knows the water. A climber knows the post-tree it is ascending, gripping the spiraled trunk with limbs that fit the grooves as if they were made for them — and they were, in a sense, because the units that compose a climber have climbed before, in other climbers, on other trees, and they carry the memory of the shape even when the shape has dissolved.

This is the thing: the units remember.

Not consciously. Not in any way that resembles thought. But each fingertip-sized unit — soft, eyeless, vaguely ovoid, its surface covered in tiny cilia and chemical receptors — carries a biochemical record of every coalition it has participated in. The record is written in protein configurations along its outer membrane, a palimpsest of shapes worn and shed. A unit that has been part of a thousand grazers grazes well. Its membrane is thick with grazer-proteins, and when it encounters other units similarly marked, they click together fast and the resulting grazer is efficient, practiced, assured. It finds the richest hummock. It eats with precision. It holds its shape longer than most.

But it will still disaggregate. The longest any coalition holds is perhaps six hours, and that is rare — an elderly grazer, all its components deeply habituated to each other, moving with the slow confidence of long practice. Even then, some signal passes through the group — chemical, mechanical, perhaps related to the plateau's breathing-cycle — and the units release their bonds and scatter. The grazer was, and then it isn't. Its components carry its memory into new shapes.

So what *lives* here? Not the grazer. Not the swimmer. Not the climber. These are verbs more than nouns, activities more than entities. What lives here are the units, and the units are — what? They do not, individually, exhibit anything you would call awareness. They respond to chemical gradients. They move toward warmth. They preferentially bond with units whose membrane-proteins complement their own. They are, individually, less complex than a paramecium.

But they are numerous. Billions on this plateau, perhaps trillions. And they have been cycling through their coalitions for so long that their collective behavior has developed qualities that no single unit possesses and no single coalition sustains. The *pattern* of aggregation across the plateau — the way grazers tend to form near hummocks exactly when the hummock surfaces are ready to be grazed, the way swimmers appear in channels just as the breathing-cycle deepens them, the way climbers assemble at the bases of post-trees whose spore-loads are heaviest — this pattern is intelligent. Not in any unit, not in any coalition, but in the *flux* itself, the constant dissolving and reforming. The plateau's aggregate fauna, taken as a whole, behaves like a single organism with distributed cognition and no persistent body.

It has no name for itself. Naming requires a self that persists long enough to be named, and this is exactly the kind of hard edge that has eroded here. But it *responds*. When a vent opens, the pattern shifts across the entire visible plateau: coalitions dissolve preferentially on the side nearest the vent, units flow toward it, and the rest of the landscape temporarily reorganizes into a sparser, more watchful configuration, as if the whole system is paying attention to what the vent will provide. When a post-tree drops a particularly large spore-cloud, the pattern ripples outward — coalitions reconfigure to take advantage, new shapes appear that seem designed specifically for spore-harvesting, and these shapes have never been seen before this moment and may never be seen again.

It improvises. The system *improvises*. This is the closest thing to a resident the plateau has: an intelligence that exists only in the transitions between bodies, that thinks only in the act of dissolving and reforming, that has no memory except the ones written on protein and no future except the next shape, and the next, and the next.

## The Hummock-Minds

But the aggregates are not the only residents. They are merely the most mobile.

The hummocks think. This is not metaphor.

Each hummock — those shoulder-height brain-coral mounds of moss and liverwort and vascular lichen — is a superorganism, yes, but it is a superorganism that has been growing in the same spot for thousands of years, and in that time its internal signaling network has become extraordinarily complex. The folds of its surface are not decorative. They are computational. Chemical signals propagate along the channels between folds, interact at junctions, produce outputs that alter the hummock's growth patterns, its water-retention, its surface chemistry. The hummocks are, in essence, enormous analog computers made of living tissue, and they have been running continuously for millennia.

What do they compute? They compute *the plateau*. Each hummock maintains an internal model of its local environment — the water-channels nearby, the post-trees within spore-drift range, the aggregation patterns of the fauna, the timing of the breathing-cycle, the frequency and location of vent openings. This model is not a map. It is a prediction engine. The hummock uses it to optimize its own growth, to extend its folds toward nutrient sources, to retract from threats, to time its reproductive sporulation to coincide with favorable conditions.

But the hummocks also communicate with each other. When they are pressed together during the contraction phase of the plateau's breathing, their surfaces touch, and chemical signals transfer between them — that wet exhalation you hear is partly the sound of information exchange. Each contraction is a synaptic event. The plateau breathes, and the hummocks share data, and the network of hummock-minds updates its collective model.

This collective model is vast, and slow, and ancient. It operates on timescales that make the aggregate fauna look frantic. Where the aggregates think in minutes — dissolve, reform, respond — the hummocks think in decades. A single thought, a single adjustment to the collective model, might take fifty years to propagate across the plateau from hummock to hummock, contraction by contraction. But the thought, when it arrives, is deep. It accounts for patterns that no individual aggregate-coalition could perceive: the long-term drift of channel-networks, the century-scale migration of post-tree populations, the gradual changes in vent frequency that suggest shifts in whatever operates below.

The hummock-minds are the plateau's elders. They are patient beyond any human understanding of patience. They do not experience urgency. They do not experience at all, in any way we would recognize — they have no sensory apparatus, no subjective experience, no *self* in the usual sense. But they have preferences. They adjust the surface chemistry of their folds to attract or repel aggregate-units, to encourage certain coalition-shapes over others, to subtly steer the behavior of the fauna that grazes on them. A hummock that has been grazed too aggressively will alter its surface to become unpalatable, and the change will persist for decades. A hummock that needs its dead outer layers removed will become exquisitely delicious, and grazers will form spontaneously nearby, drawn by chemical signals they cannot resist.

The hummocks are farming the fauna. Or the hummocks are governing the fauna. Or the hummocks are dreaming the fauna into shapes that serve the plateau's needs. The distinction is academic. In a place where identity is this soft, the difference between farming and dreaming is just another eroded edge.

## The Post-Tree Hermits

The post-trees are another matter. They are solitary. They are individuated. They are, in some sense, the last remaining *selves* on the plateau — the final holdouts against the disaggregation that has softened everything else.

Each post-tree is unique. Not just architecturally — the spiraling membrane-bearer, the floating-bulb column, the weeping spore-discharger — but *experientially*. Each one has a history. Each one remembers what it has been. The spiral-tree was a seedling in the root-mat ten thousand years ago, and it has grown continuously since then, adding rings of tissue that record the chemical composition of the air, the frequency of vent-openings, the density of aggregate fauna, the slow thoughts of nearby hummocks. It is a living archive. If you could read its rings — and the hummocks can, slowly, through root-contact with the mat below — you would find a history of this place extending back to an era when the breathing was faster, the vents more frequent, and the light dimmer because the spore-populations were smaller.

The post-trees are aware of themselves. This is a strong claim to make about a plant, even a post-plant, but the evidence is in their behavior. They respond to damage not just with healing but with *avoidance-learning*. A post-tree struck by a falling aggregate (it happens: a climber disaggregates mid-ascent and its units rain down) will, over subsequent months, alter its surface texture to prevent climbing in the area where the fall occurred. It will grow thorns — not the inherited thorns of evolutionary defense, but *improvised* thorns, novel structures grown in novel locations, specific to the specific threat. It is solving problems. It is treating each insult as a unique event requiring a unique response.

More than this: the post-trees communicate, but they do not communicate like the hummocks. The hummocks share data, merge their models, think collectively. The post-trees *argue*. Their spore-clouds carry chemical markers that encode not just reproductive material but *opinions* — complex molecular statements about the state of the plateau, the behavior of the fauna, the rhythm of the vents. When two post-trees' spore-clouds intersect, the chemical markers react with each other, and the resulting compounds drift to other post-trees, who incorporate them and respond with their own modified spore-statements.

The arguments are slow. A single exchange takes weeks. The topics are obscure — we might describe them as disagreements about resource allocation, about the optimal density of aggregate fauna, about whether the vents are increasing or decreasing in frequency and what this means. But there is something in the tone of the chemical exchanges — a vehemence, an *insistence* — that suggests these are not merely computational processes. The post-trees *care*. They have stakes. They are the only things on this plateau that are capable of being *wrong*, because they are the only things that maintain a persistent perspective long enough for that perspective to be tested against reality.

They are lonely. This is perhaps projection, but consider: each post-tree stands apart from every other, separated by hundreds of meters of open mat. Each one is the only entity in its vicinity that maintains a continuous identity. The aggregates dissolve around it constantly. The hummocks think too slowly to be companionable. The other post-trees are distant, reachable only through the imprecise medium of spore-argument. A post-tree lives for ten thousand years in the company of nothing that persists, surrounded by a world that is always coming apart and reassembling, and it alone remains what it was yesterday, and the day before, and a thousand years before that.

Some of them have gone strange. The weeping-tree — the one with the tendrils that draw water upward and the body that trembles and swells — was not always like this. Its rings record a change, about two thousand years ago, when it stopped growing upward and began growing inward, folding its tissues into denser and denser configurations. It developed the tendrils not for water-collection but for *sensation* — they are sensory organs, exquisitely tuned to vibration, to the chemical content of the water, to the movements of aggregate-units nearby. The weeping-tree has turned itself into a vast, trembling nerve. It feels everything within a hundred-meter radius. Every disaggregation. Every reassembly. Every hummock-exhalation. Every vent-opening. It absorbs this information and does nothing visible with it. It simply *feels*.

Perhaps this is what loneliness becomes, given enough time and enough biological plasticity: not withdrawal, but hyper-engagement. An inability to stop perceiving. A self that has eroded not into dissolution, as the aggregates have, but into pure receptivity. The weeping-tree is the most alive thing on the plateau, and it is the most alone.

## The Engine Below

And then there is whatever operates beneath the root-mat.

It is not biological. This is worth stating clearly, because on a plateau where every other distinction has softened, this one has not. The thing below is *mechanical*. Its rhythms are precise. Its outputs — the warm air, the iridescent droplets, the iron-and-ozone smell — are consistent and repeatable. It does not vary. It does not adapt. It does not evolve. It has been doing exactly what it does for as long as the post-trees' rings can record, and probably far longer.

This does not mean it is simple. The vents open in patterns that, over centuries, trace complex geometries across the plateau's surface — spirals, grids, interference-patterns that suggest multiple overlapping systems operating at different frequencies. The iridescent droplets contain compounds that no biological process on the surface can produce: rare-earth complexes, long-chain polymers that fold into specific three-dimensional shapes, molecules that function as catalysts for reactions that would otherwise be impossible in this temperature range. The engine is feeding the surface very specific things. It is *provisioning* it.

Who built it? Or: what was it, before it was an engine?

The deepest layers of root-mat — accessible only at the oldest, most eroded edges of the plateau, where the mat thins to a few centimeters over exposed crystal-stone — contain traces. Not artifacts. Not fossils. But *inclusions*: tiny specks of metal, regularly spaced, embedded in root-fiber that grew around them millions of years ago. The metal is an alloy that does not occur naturally. The spacing suggests structure — a grid, a lattice, something organized. Something that was, once, on the surface, and has since been overgrown and buried and incorporated into the living mat.

There were builders. They were here so long ago that the plateau has swallowed every trace of them except these metallic inclusions and the engine they left running beneath the ground. They may have been the ones who made this place what it is — who began the erosion of physical law, deliberately or accidentally, through whatever the engine does. Or they may have found the plateau already softening and built the engine to manage it, to slow it, to feed the surface what it needed to remain stable in its state of dynamic dissolution.

Either way, they are gone. Not dead — there is no evidence of death, no catastrophe, no layer of ash or bone in the mat's stratigraphy. Gone the way everything on this plateau eventually goes: *disaggregated*. Their bodies, their buildings, their machines, their culture, their identity — all of it worn down by the same softening that wears down everything else. They became part of the system. Their metal became the inclusions. Their knowledge became the engine's programming. Their — what? Their *selves*? — became the pattern of aggregation and dissolution that now constitutes the plateau's distributed intelligence.

This is speculation. But it is the kind of speculation this place demands. On a plateau where identity is temporary and physics is negotiable and the light is alive and the ground breathes, the idea that a civilization could dissolve into an ecosystem is not strange. It is inevitable. It is what the plateau *does*. It disaggregates. It takes hard things — rock, law, selfhood, species, civilization — and it softens them until they flow into each other, until the boundaries disappear and only the process remains.

The builders are still here. They are in the aggregate-units that ring the vents and feed on the iridescent droplets, absorbing the same catalytic compounds that their ancestors' technology produces. They are in the hummock-minds that model the plateau with a sophistication that suggests inherited knowledge far older than any individual hummock. They are in the post-trees that argue, via spore-chemistry, about questions whose origins no living thing on the plateau can remember. They are in the engine itself, which cycles on, faithful to its programming, providing for a surface that has long since forgotten what it was being provided for.

## The Visitor-Shapes

One more thing lives here, or tries to.

Occasionally — rarely — something arrives on the plateau from outside. A seed carried on a high wind from some distant place where the rules are still hard. A spore from a different kind of organism. A small animal, blown in from somewhere, bewildered and shivering on the cold mat.

The plateau receives these visitors gently. The cold does not kill them quickly. The water sustains them. The spore-light provides enough energy for photosynthesis, if they are capable of it. They can survive here, for a time.

But the plateau's softness is contagious. The eroded physics of this place — the negotiable boundaries, the permeable identities — begins to work on any organism that stays long enough. A seed that germinates on the root-mat grows strangely: its roots interface with the ancient mat-fibers and begin exchanging chemicals. Its stem develops folds. Its leaves become damp and brain-like. Within a few generations — if it reproduces, if it lasts that long — its descendants are indistinguishable from the native hummock-mosses. They have been *incorporated*.

An animal that survives long enough undergoes something worse, or better, depending on your perspective. The aggregate-units are drawn to it. They gather around it at first like curious children, forming temporary shapes that mirror the visitor's body plan — four-limbed, bilateral, approximately the right size. These mirror-shapes follow the visitor. They move when it moves. They stop when it stops. It is unclear whether this is predation, mimicry, or communication.

Then, slowly, the visitor's own cells begin to respond to the chemical signals that the aggregate-units emit — the same signals that tell units when to bond and when to release. The visitor's tissues begin to loosen. Not painfully — there is no evidence of pain in any account that could be given of this process, if anyone were here to give one. The cells simply... relax their adherence to each other. The visitor becomes less solid. More permeable. Its edges soften.

Eventually — over days, weeks, depending on the complexity of the organism — the visitor disaggregates. Its cells separate, individuate, and begin responding to the plateau's chemical grammar. They join coalitions. They form shapes. They become part of the pattern.

The visitor is not dead. Every one of its cells is alive. But the *visitor* — the self, the identity, the persistent narrative of being a particular organism — is gone. Eroded. Worn away like the mountains were worn away, like the bedrock was worn away, like the laws that once held atoms in their fixed positions were worn away. What remains is a contribution to the flux: new units with new membrane-proteins, new capabilities, new memories of a different kind of body, enriching the aggregate intelligence with perspectives it has never had before.

The plateau is hospitable. It is generous. It takes everything in and gives everything a place in its ceaseless reorganization. Nothing is rejected. Nothing is wasted. Nothing persists.

## What It Is Like to Be Here

Stand on the root-mat. Feel the cold water rise around your feet. Watch the spore-light drift. Listen to the disaggregation-sounds: the wet sigh, the clicking reassembly, the deep groan of the breathing land. Watch a post-tree's membranes flex in the still air, catching light that is alive, casting shadows that are full of tiny drifting organisms. Watch a grazer the size of a large cat move toward a hummock, its body composed of a thousand units working in concert, its rasping mouth already grinding — and then watch it come apart, suddenly, softly, like a sandcastle receiving a wave, its components sliding in every direction, purposeful even in dissolution, each one already seeking the next shape.

Feel the plateau contract. Watch the horizon draw closer. Hear the hummocks groan as they press together and exchange their slow thoughts. See new features appear that you didn't notice before — a pool where there was none, a post-tree that seems closer, a crystalline ridge half-absorbed by the mat. Feel the expansion. Watch everything pull apart, the channels narrow, the distances grow, the horizon recede to reveal more of the same: more mat, more hummocks, more post-trees standing solitary in the dim silver light, more aggregates dissolving and reforming in an endless wet choreography.

Hear, if you listen carefully, the engine below. That patient, precise, mechanical heartbeat that doesn't sync with anything, that operates on its own schedule, that has been running since before any living thing on the surface can remember. Feel the warm breath of a vent opening somewhere nearby, and watch the units flow toward it like iron filings toward a magnet, and hear their high keening as they feed, and smell the iron and ozone, and watch the vent close, and watch them scatter, and watch the shapes reform, and know that whatever built the engine is still here, still providing, still cycling through the system it created or the system that absorbed it — the distinction long since dissolved.

And feel, at the edges of yourself, a softness. A loosening. The cold water around your ankles. The spore-light settling on your skin. The chemical signals in the air, patient and ancient and specific, inviting your cells to relax their allegiances, to consider the possibility that *you* are a coalition too — a temporary shape, a grazer or a swimmer or a climber, assembled for a purpose, held together by habit and chemistry, and that the purpose might be served differently, in a different shape, with different partners, in a different arrangement.

The plateau does not demand. It does not threaten. It merely offers the same proposition it has offered to everything for geological ages: *Come apart. Come together differently. Come apart again. Nothing is lost. Nothing needs to persist. The pattern holds even as every instance of it dissolves.*

This is what lives here. The pattern. The flux. The ancient, gentle, ceaseless disaggregation and its twin, the ancient, gentle, ceaseless reassembly. Not a creature. Not a mind. Not a civilization. Something that was all of those things once and has worn through them to the other side, to a mode of existence that has no name because naming requires the kind of hard-edged identity that this place has long since eroded to nothing.

It is cold. It is wet. It is full of life that will not hold still. And it is waiting, with infinite patience, for whatever comes next to arrive and become part of it and dissolve and become part of it again, forever, the engine cycling below, the hummocks dreaming their slow dreams, the post-trees arguing into the spore-lit air, and the shapes — always the shapes — coming apart and coming together on the dark, breathing mat, each one a sentence in a language that never stops speaking and never says the same thing twice.
