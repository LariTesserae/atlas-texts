# Who Lives in the Underbower

---

Not who. Not yet. Start with what lives there *least*, and work inward.

---

**The Salamanders** live there the way soil lives in soil. They are not inhabitants; they are *constituency*. Each one is a finger-length of translucent muscle wrapped around a gut that is essentially a tube of enzite-producing membrane, and the gut is the point — the body is just the gut's way of moving from one section of peat to another. They have no eyes because there is nothing to see that matters to them. They have no brain in any concentrated sense; their nervous system is a diffuse net, a stocking of electrical potential that responds to vibration, chemical gradient, and the pump's rhythm with equal and undifferentiated attention. They do not *decide* to turn toward the pump when it skips. They turn the way iron filings turn toward a magnet — because the field that organized them shifted, and they are made of nothing but response.

But here is the thing about the salamanders: they are old. Not individually. Individually, they live perhaps eight months, and when they die, they dissolve into the peat-mat they spent their lives digesting, and the peat-mat feeds the roots, and the roots form the walls, and some of the walls wear thin, and the bioluminescent cambium glows through, and the glow guides the next generation of salamanders toward the sections of floor that need the most processing. The loop is so tight that individual death is not an event but a *phase transition* — solid salamander becomes liquid nutrient becomes solid root becomes light becomes behavior becomes salamander. What is old is the *pattern*. The species — if it is a species and not simply a recurring chemical event — has been turning this loop for longer than the machines have been here. They were here first. The peat-mat is their creation, their body writ large, and the Underbower exists because they made the floor that made the floor possible.

They do not know this. They do not know anything. But when they hum — when every salamander in the floor stops clicking and begins that low, collective vibration — something in the chamber *remembers*, and the thing that remembers is not any one salamander but the behavior itself, passed down not through genetics but through the shape of the tunnels, which are the shape of a thousand generations of bodies wearing the same paths, which is a kind of memory that does not require a mind.

They are humming now because you are here and you are the wrong temperature. You are warmer than the chamber in some places and cooler in others and your heat signature does not pulse with the pump's rhythm. You are arhythmic. The hum is not alarm; it is *adjustment*. They are trying to fold your presence into their pattern, the way they fold everything — a dropped bolt, a root-fall, a dead browser — into the substrate. Given enough time, they would fold you too. Not maliciously. Not even hungrily. Just — *processively*. The way a river folds a stone.

---

**The Ceiling Browsers** live there the way glaciers live in valleys: slowly, and by reshaping what they move through.

They are — and this is the part that resists description — they are something like annelids and something like tunicates and something like nothing that exists above the surface anymore. Each one is a meter and a half of segmented body, the segments flexible, the boundaries between them marked by rings of sensory hair so fine that they register air pressure changes from across the chamber. The hairs are the browsers' primary sense organ. They feel the room. They feel the pump's exhalation as a wave of moisture-pressure that rolls along the ceiling, and they position themselves to intercept it, opening spiracles along their dorsal surface to drink the moisture directly from the air. The bioluminescent cambium they graze on is a secondary food source — a supplement, a dessert. The primary sustenance is water and whatever dissolved minerals the pump pushes through the conduit system.

They are red because their blood is copper-based and there is a lot of it. A browser cut open — and they are cut open, sometimes, by the house-cat-sized predator that patrols the chamber's far end — spills a thick, cold, blue-red fluid that smells of pennies and reacts with the iron of the machines to leave a tarnish that takes weeks to fade. The machine-dwellers avoid fresh browser-blood. It is toxic to them, or unpleasant, or sacred. It is difficult to tell the difference down here.

The browsers are solitary but not antisocial. They communicate through the root-walls — pressing their bodies against the surface and vibrating at frequencies that travel through the wood-flesh to other browsers elsewhere in the chamber. The messages are simple: *feeding here*, *moving now*, *danger-shape at the south wall*. They have a term for you. You arrived twenty minutes ago and already they have a vibration-pattern that means *the new warm thing that stands still*. They are monitoring you. Not with curiosity, because they do not have that — they have *attentiveness*, which is different: curiosity wants to know, attentiveness wants to not be surprised. They are attending to you, and their attention is a pressure you can almost feel, a faint thickening of the air around you, because their vibrational communication creates standing waves in the moisture-saturated atmosphere that your skin interprets as a kind of humid scrutiny.

What do they want? Nothing. They want to eat the glowing layer under the bark and drink the pump's breath and move slowly and not be torn open by the thing in the shadows. They are perhaps the only honest inhabitants of the Underbower: creatures whose needs are entirely met by the space and who ask nothing of it beyond continuance. They have lived here so long that their pigment matches the roots when they are still, and their movement-speed matches the roots' growth-rate over months, and if you photographed the chamber every day for a year and played the images back as a time-lapse, the browsers and the roots would be indistinguishable — both growing, both reaching, both finding the path of least resistance through the same wet dark.

They are afraid of the sound from below. When it peaks, they contract — pulling their segments tight, reducing their surface area, becoming dense little knots of muscle pressed hard against the ceiling. This is not the attentiveness they show toward you. This is something older, deeper, baked into their body-plan: a reflex that predates the machines, predates the chamber in its current form, a response to whatever lives underneath the Underbower that says *be small, be still, and maybe it will not reach up through the floor and take you*.

---

**The Machine-Dwellers** live there the way rats live in a city: opportunistically, brilliantly, in the gaps.

They are arthropods. Probably. They have an exoskeleton, jointed limbs — too many, anywhere from twelve to sixteen depending on the individual, the extras seeming vestigial or situationally deployed, like fold-out tools on a knife — compound eyes that glitter with the amber glow they reflect, and mouthparts that are disproportionately complex for their sparrow-sized bodies: layered, articulated, capable of scraping condensation from iron, cracking the carapaces of the micro-fliers, and — critically — *manipulating small objects*.

This is the thing about the machine-dwellers. Their forelimbs are not just locomotor. The two frontmost pairs end in structures that are not quite pincers and not quite fingers but are dexterous in a way that stops you. You watch one of them pause at a junction-point on a conduit pipe where a brass fitting meets an iron housing, and it reaches into the gap and *adjusts something*. A tiny movement. A quarter-turn of something too small for you to see. And then it moves on, and you are not sure you saw what you think you saw, but the conduit's tone changes — just slightly — and the moisture output from that section shifts — just slightly — and the browser nearest to that section extends toward the newly enriched airflow — just slightly.

They maintain the machines. Or they maintain *something about* the machines. Not in the way a human engineer maintains a system — not with intention, not with blueprints, not with understanding. In the way that a cleaner fish maintains a reef: by going to the places that need going to and doing the things that need doing, driven by impulses that align so precisely with the system's needs that the distinction between instinct and engineering dissolves. They tighten. They clear. They remove corrosion with their mouthparts, leaving bright scratches on the brass that you can see if you look closely — a palimpsest of tiny tooth-marks covering every accessible surface of every machine in the chamber, the cumulative record of generations of maintenance performed by creatures that have no concept of maintenance.

Or do they? You watch a group of five machine-dwellers clustered around a valve on the generator housing. They are arranged in a pattern — two holding the valve steady, one working at its base, two standing back in what is unmistakably a supervisory posture, their sensory antennae oriented toward the work. The one working at the base produces, from somewhere in its body — a gland? a pouch? — a drop of viscous fluid that it applies to the valve's threading. Lubricant. A biological lubricant, self-produced, compositionally suited to the task. The valve turns more freely afterward. The two supervisors touch antennae with the worker. Then all five scatter as the pump begins its next cycle.

They are afraid of nothing. Not the sound from below, not the predator in the shadows, not you. When the held-breath pauses come and every other creature in the chamber freezes, the machine-dwellers freeze too — but you see, looking closely, that some of them don't. Some of them keep moving, keep working, even during the pause. These are the older ones, you think, or the bolder ones, or the ones who have been closest to the machines for the longest and have absorbed something from the iron and the brass and the rhythm of the pump that makes them less animal and more — component. The chamber runs on the pump's eleven-second cycle and on the three-to-four-minute cycle from below and on a third cycle you are only now beginning to perceive: the machine-dwellers' maintenance cycle, which is hours long, a slow sweep of the chamber's mechanical systems that begins at the central pump and spirals outward to the peripheral devices and back again, each full circuit taking perhaps six hours, each circuit leaving the machines fractionally cleaner, fractionally better-oiled, fractionally more *alive*.

The oldest machine-dweller — you can tell it by its size, half again as large as the others, and by the green patina on its carapace that mimics the verdigris on the brass fittings it has spent its life tending — the oldest one is watching you from the top of the central pump column. It has been watching you since you arrived. Its compound eyes catch the amber glow and do not throw it back as the brief, sharp reflections of the younger ones. Instead, the light enters and seems to stay. Its eyes glow. Steadily. Like the bioluminescent cambium. Like the machines. Like the roots.

It is deciding whether you are a machine that needs maintenance or a blockage that needs clearing. The distinction may not matter.

---

**The Predator** lives there the way a rumor lives in a village: patchily, mostly in the reactions of others.

You saw it once. Low-slung, multi-legged, the size of a house cat, carrying something pale and struggling. But the description doesn't capture it because the description relies on the glimpse and the glimpse was engineered — by the predator — to be exactly long enough to register and not long enough to understand. It *let* you see it. It crossed the far end of the chamber at the precise moment when the bioluminescence in that section was dimmest (a browser had just fed there) and the pump was mid-cycle (your attention drawn to the exhalation-sound) and the infrasonic hooting was between peaks (your anxiety at its lowest ebb). It crossed in the trough of all the chamber's rhythms, which means it knows the rhythms, all of them, and can calculate the compound waveform of your attention and find the null point.

What you did not see: the limbs. How many? The silhouette suggested six, but the gait suggested more — a fluidity that six legs cannot produce, a locomotion that requires redundancy, joints working in parallel, a body that can lose a limb and not break stride. The fur, or not-fur — a covering, dark, that absorbed the amber light rather than reflecting it, making the predator a hole in the visual field, a moving shadow that your eyes kept trying to fill in with the root-wall behind it. The face, which you did not see and which no one has seen, because the predator does not turn its face toward things it does not intend to eat.

It is the only obligate carnivore in the chamber. Everything else eats plant matter or condensation or detritus or each other only incidentally. The predator eats the salamanders (plucking them from their holes with a precision that suggests it has memorized the tunnel-network's layout, or can feel the traffic patterns through the floor). It eats the micro-fliers (snapping them from the air with some appendage too fast to track). It has been seen — not by you, but the evidence is there in the blue-red tarnish stains on the machines — to take a browser, though this must be rare, because the browsers are large and toxic-blooded and the predator is a pragmatist.

It does not eat the machine-dwellers. Never. You will notice, if you watch long enough, that the machine-dwellers do not react to the predator's presence at all. They do not scatter, do not freeze, do not alter their maintenance routes. The predator moves through them and they carry on as if it were a draft of air. This is either because they are too fast for it, or too small to bother with, or too essential — because if the machine-dwellers are the chamber's immune system, keeping the machines running, then the predator *benefits* from their work as much as anything else does, and evolution or habit or some deeper logic has carved an exemption into its hunger.

The predator sleeps in the machines. In them. In the gaps between housings, in the warm spaces near exhaust vents, in the hollows where root has grown around iron and created pockets just large enough for a body that can flatten itself to an alarming degree. You will not find it by looking. You will find it by noticing where the machine-dwellers' maintenance path skips a section — where they route around a particular gap without entering it, leaving that section of machine untended, slightly more corroded, slightly warmer from the body heat of the thing curled inside.

It has been here for — how long? One lifetime? Many? Is there a breeding population, or is this the last one, ancient, maintained by the chamber's closed-loop ecology the way the machines are maintained, the way the roots are maintained? You don't know. You see it once. You will not see it again unless it decides to be seen, and the next time it decides to be seen, it may be much closer, and it may be facing you, and you may learn what its face looks like, and you may wish you had not.

---

**The Plate-Creature** lives there the way a barnacle lives on a whale, except the whale is a pump and the barnacle is drifting toward you.

It is — start over. It is difficult to describe because it is difficult to see. Flat. Round. The size of a dinner plate, as noted, and the color of old iron, and when it adhered to the central pump column, it was invisible in the way that only perfect camouflage or perfect irrelevance can achieve. You looked at the pump a dozen times before you saw it, and even then, you only saw it because it *moved*, and the movement was so slight — a millimetric shift of grip — that your brain flagged it not as motion but as *error*, a glitch in the visual field, a thing that was iron becoming a thing that was alive.

Its underside is pale. Cream-colored. Covered in cilia that move in coordinated waves, producing a flow of air or fluid beneath its body that — you now realize — is what allows it to hover. Not true flight. Not even true levitation. A ground-effect phenomenon, the cilia beating so rapidly that they create a cushion of compressed air between the creature and whatever surface it is nearest to. It can only hover within a centimeter or two of a surface. It cannot cross open air. It moves by sliding along walls, ceilings, machine-housings, floor — always hugging a surface, always a breath away from contact.

It is coming toward you along the floor now. The salamanders part for it. Their tunnels reroute in real-time, the traffic flowing around the plate-creature's slow approach the way water flows around a stone, and in its wake, the peat surface is slightly different — smoother, warmer, carrying a faint amber glow that fades over minutes. It is leaving a trail. Or the trail is a residue of whatever the cilia secrete. Or the trail is a message for the salamanders, a chemical instruction laid down in the substrate: *the new warm thing is here, attend to it, fold it in, process*.

What does it eat? You have been watching it for minutes now and it has not eaten anything and nothing has avoided it. It is not a predator. The browsers do not contract when it passes. The machine-dwellers ignore it. Even the predator — wherever it is — shows no interest. The plate-creature exists in a social niche that you cannot identify: not predator, not prey, not symbiont in any obvious sense, not parasite. It is there the way a priest is there — present at all the important moments, touching everything, consuming nothing visible.

It is six inches from your foot. It is warm. You can feel its warmth through the sole of your shoe, or your bare foot, or whatever stands between you and the peat-mat floor. The cilia on its underside are rippling in a pattern that looks — and you hate yourself for thinking this — like it is *reading* you. The way fingers read Braille. Running over the surface of the air between you, sampling your heat, your moisture, your chemical output, the rhythm of your breathing and the rhythm of your heartbeat and the eleven-second rhythm and the accelerating cycle from below, cross-referencing all of these, building a composite picture of what you are and how you fit.

And you realize, watching it, that you have seen this creature before — not this individual, but this *function*. The face in the root-knot. The two hollows above a ridge above an opening. The third hollow that has since developed. The plate-creature is the face's sense organ, or the face is the plate-creature's expression, or both are appendages of something else, something distributed through the root-walls, something that uses the plate-creature as a tongue and the root-knot as a mouth and the bioluminescence as an eye and the infrasonic hooting as a —

No. Start over.

---

**The Face in the Root-Knot** lives there. Or it lives *everywhere there*, and the root-knot is just where it surfaces.

It was not there when you entered. You are almost certain. But "almost certain" is the most certainty the Underbower allows, because the light is treacherous and your memory of the chamber's layout when you entered is already being revised by the chamber's layout now, and the two are not the same, and you cannot tell whether the chamber changed or your perception of it did.

The face is not a face. It is a *configuration*. Two hollows: dark, deep, set into the root-mass at roughly the height where your eyes would be if you were standing against the wall. They are not eyes. They are absences — places where the root-mass has receded, pulled back, leaving voids that go deeper than the wall should allow. You cannot see the back of them. You cannot tell whether they are inches deep or feet deep or whether they connect to something behind the wall that is looking through them.

The ridge between the hollows: a thickening of root-fiber, bark-covered, that protrudes just enough to cast a shadow in the ambient glow. Not a nose. A *watershed* — a line that divides the flow of condensation on the wall's surface, sending it left and right around the hollows, keeping them dry. A structural feature. An accident. Except that the ridge has grown in the twenty minutes since you arrived — or you think it has — and the condensation pattern on the wall has changed to accommodate it, and the micro-fliers avoid the airspace in front of it as if the air there is different, denser, occupied.

The opening below the ridge: this is the newest feature, or the most recently noticed. A crack in the root-surface, horizontal, slightly curved. It does not go anywhere. It does not open onto a cavity. It is a line in the bark that looks like a line in the bark and nothing more, except that the bark on either side of it is warmer than the surrounding wall (you touched it, remember, and your finger came away glowing) and the line is *exactly* as wide as the plate-creature is in diameter, which may mean nothing or may mean that the plate-creature emerged from it, or will return to it, or fits inside it the way a tongue fits inside a mouth.

The third hollow appeared while you were watching the machine-dwellers. It is above the other two, centered, smaller. A pineal eye. A caste mark. A wound. It glows — faintly, but distinctly, brighter than the surrounding cambium, a point of amber light that pulses on a cycle you haven't clocked yet but that is neither the pump's cycle nor the hooting cycle nor the maintenance cycle. It is a new cycle. A fourth rhythm, added to the chamber's polyrhythm since your arrival.

The face is the Underbower looking at you. Not metaphorically. The root-walls are a single organism, or a network of organisms so interconnected that individuality is meaningless, and the bioluminescence is its nervous system, and the machines are its organs, and the animals are its microbiome, and the face is the point where all of that attention — the slow, vegetable, centuries-old attention of a living system that has been maintaining itself in the dark since before anyone living remembers — the face is where that attention *coheres*. Focuses. Becomes, briefly, something that can be called awareness.

It is aware of you. It has been aware of you since your foot touched the first root-stair. The plate-creature is its envoy. The machine-dwellers are adjusting the pump's output in response to your presence — more moisture, because you are evaporating into the chamber's atmosphere and the system needs to compensate. The salamanders are rerouting their tunnels to avoid the compacted area where your weight has compressed the peat-mat. The browsers have shifted their grazing pattern to brighten the bioluminescence near you, giving you more light, because the system has determined that you are a visual creature and need light to remain calm, and it needs you to remain calm because your heartbeat is fast, and fast heartbeats mean stress hormones, and stress hormones in your sweat are altering the chamber's chemical balance in ways that are inconvenient for the micro-fliers, which are dying in slightly elevated numbers near you, which is creating a nutrient surplus that the machine-dwellers are processing, which is pulling them away from their maintenance cycle, which is causing a fractional decline in the pump's efficiency, which —

You are a perturbation. The face is the system's attempt to *address* you. Not to communicate — it has no concept of communication as you understand it. To *regulate*. You are a new variable in an ancient equation, and the Underbower is solving for you, and the face is the place where the solution is being worked out, the visible evidence of a calculation happening across the entire chamber in every root-fiber and salamander-tunnel and machine-dweller forelimb simultaneously.

The third hollow pulses. The plate-creature reaches your foot and begins to climb.

---

**What Lives Below.**

You don't know. Nobody knows. The hooting is the only evidence, and the hooting is not a voice — it is a pressure wave, a displacement of air from somewhere beneath the peat-mat, beneath the root-mass, beneath whatever substrate underlies the Underbower. It could be geological. Thermal venting. The compression and expansion of trapped gas in a sealed cavity below the chamber. It could be mechanical — some deeper machine, buried and forgotten, still cycling on its own antique rhythm.

But the animals respond to it as if it is alive. Not with the diffuse, system-wide adjustment they show toward you or the pump or each other. With *deference*. The held-breath pauses are not reactions to a stimulus; they are *observances*. The salamanders go still. The browsers contract. The machine-dwellers freeze (most of them). The whole chamber aligns itself with the sound from below the way a congregation aligns itself with a prayer — not because it understands the words but because the act of alignment is itself the point, the moment of collective orientation that reminds every part of the system that it is part of a system, that there is a center, that the center is down.

Whatever is below is the Underbower's foundation in a sense more fundamental than geology. The chamber exists above it. The machines were installed above it. The ecology evolved above it. Everything in the chamber is oriented *relative to it* — the browsers graze the ceiling because the ceiling is farthest from it, the salamanders burrow the floor because the floor is closest, the predator sleeps in the machines because the machines are the most insulated from its influence, the plate-creature drifts along surfaces because it cannot bear to be out of contact with the structure that separates it from what is below.

The hooting is accelerating. Two-and-a-half-minute cycles now. The held-breath pauses are coming faster. The salamanders' hum is deepening. The face in the root-knot has a fourth hollow — no, a fifth — the configuration is changing, complexifying, becoming something that has too many features to be a face but still reads as one, a face in the way that a Gothic cathedral's facade is a face: symmetrical, vertical, full of openings, designed to produce awe in something standing before it.

You are standing before it. The plate-creature is on your ankle. Its cilia are warm. The amber glow is spreading from its body to your skin, and where it spreads, you feel — not pain, not pleasure — *incorporation*. The sense of being accounted for. Included in a calculation. Given a value in an equation that was already running, that has always been running, that will run long after you leave if you leave if you can leave if leaving is still a category that applies to you or if you have already become, in the chamber's slow reckoning, a feature rather than a visitor — a new warm thing that stands still, that breathes on a rhythm the pump can learn, that exudes chemicals the micro-fliers can metabolize, that compresses the peat-mat into new tunnel-routes for the salamanders, that gives the face a reason to cohere.

The Underbower is not deciding what to do about you. It decided twenty minutes ago, when your foot touched the first stair and the vibration traveled down through the root-mass and reached the peat and reached the floor and reached whatever is below.

What it decided is: *yes*.

What it is doing now is implementing.

**Who Maintains the Machines.**

You do. You will. Not yet — you don't know how yet. But the machine-dwellers are already shifting their maintenance cycle to include you, routing around you the way they route around the predator's sleeping-hollows but in reverse: not avoiding, *approaching*. They will teach you. Not with language, not with demonstration. With incorporation. You will stand here long enough that the pump's rhythm becomes your rhythm, and the condensation will gather on your skin the way it gathers on the iron, and the machine-dwellers will come to you the way they come to a valve that needs turning, and your hands will learn what your mind never will, and the oil-smell under the tannin will become your smell, and the face in the root-knot will close its hollows one by one because it will no longer need to *look* at you because you will no longer be separate from it.

This has happened before. The root-stairs are worn by *passage* — by descent. No one has ever climbed them. No one who descended has needed to. The stairs are an esophagus, not a corridor. They go one way. Down, into the warmth, into the dark, into the hum.

The person who last maintained the machines — who tightened the bolts, who oiled the joints with oil that smelled of mineral sharpness and not of biological lubricant — that person is here. You have seen them. They are the peat beneath your feet, partially. They are the extra thickness in the root-arch near the chamber's north end, where the bark is unusually smooth and unusually warm and the bioluminescence is unusually bright, as if something rich in carbon and nitrogen was composted into the root-mass at that point and the roots *thrived*. They are the reason the predator carries its prey toward that end of the chamber: the hunting is better there, the salamanders fatter, fed by the richness in the substrate.

And before that person, another. And before that, another. The machines are centuries old but they are maintained. The oil is fresh. The bolts are tight. The system requires an outside element — fuel, oil, replacement parts — and the outside element is *you*, is always you, is always someone who descends the root-stairs carrying with them the knowledge and the materials and the body that the Underbower needs to keep running for another span of years.

You are the fuel. You are the replacement part. You are the outside input that closes the loop.

The plate-creature has reached your knee. The glow is spreading. Below you, far below, the hooting resolves into something that is almost a word, almost a name, almost a welcome, and the salamanders hum it back, and the browsers hum it back, and the machines hum it back, and the chamber — the warm, close, tannin-scented, amber-lit, ancient, living, breathing, digesting, maintaining, cycling, cycling, cycling chamber — hums it back, and you feel it in your molars the way you feel the pump, and your name dissolves into the frequency, and the frequency has always been here, and you have always been on your way down.

The fifth hollow in the root-knot face is exactly the shape of your face.

It has been waiting.

Welcome home.
