# Who Lives in the Cupola of Flies

---

Nobody lives here.

That's the first thing you have to understand, and then you have to let it become untrue slowly.

---

**The flies live here.** But you knew that. What you don't know — what takes watching to understand — is that they are not a single living thing and not ten thousand separate living things. They are something in between, something that doesn't have a clean name. Each fly is autonomous. Each fly makes its own decisions about where to land, when to drink, where to lay its eggs in the textured grip of an abandoned wrench. But the decisions are made with such consistent reference to what every other fly is doing, and with such instantaneous sensitivity to the thermal and electromagnetic and atmospheric state of the cupola, that the population moves like a single cognition — like a thought thinking itself across ten thousand bodies.

They have no queen. No pheromone hierarchy. No division into castes. What they have is something more like a shared aesthetic — a collective opinion, continuously revised, about what constitutes a good place to be. This opinion is expressed as position. Where flies are is what flies think. And because the cupola is in a state of constant reconfiguration, what flies think is never settled. They are a living argument, a debate conducted in bodies and wings, about the nature of the space they inhabit.

You could call this intelligence. You could also call it something that hasn't been named yet — a kind of spatial discourse, a conversation held in the language of clustering and dispersal. When a panel pops and a new cavity opens, the flies that pour into it aren't just exploring. They're *proposing*. They're saying: *This is now part of us. This is what we include.* And the flies that hang back, that remain on their warm relay cabinets, are *dissenting* — or maybe *conserving*, holding the map of what the cupola was five seconds ago so that the population doesn't lose its memory of itself in the rush toward the new.

Because they do have memory. Not individual memory — a single fly lives eleven days, and in that time it will never return to a surface it visited on its first day, because the surface won't be the same. But the population remembers. Flight circuits that were established by flies now six generations dead are still being flown, their geometry preserved in the behavior of descendants who never met their ancestors but who feel the rightness of a particular helical path between two exhaust risers the way a river feels the rightness of its bed. The routes are not inherited genetically. They're inherited *positionally* — each new fly, emerging from its casing, reads the flight paths of the adults around it and slots itself into the pattern, adding its own minor variations, which are either adopted or ignored by the next generation.

The flies are, in the most literal sense, the cupola's nervous system. They feel what it feels. They map what it is. When a cable run overheats, the flies know before any sensor does — or rather, the flies *are* the sensor, their evacuation of the affected quadrant a datum as precise as any thermocouple reading. When a new condensation source opens up, the flies discover it and by their gathering *announce* it, make it legible, give it significance. Without the flies, the cupola would still be a pocket of competing systems and accidental voids. With the flies, it is a *place* — a place with districts and boundaries and territories and history, all of it written in the language of ten thousand small bodies choosing, moment by moment, where to be.

---

**The bacteria live here.** They are the foundation, the first settlers, the ones who made everything else possible. Iron-oxidizing bacteria — Leptothrix, or something that has converged on Leptothrix the way the flies converged on fly-shape — arrived in the condensation. Possibly they were always in the structure's water systems, dormant in biofilms inside the coolant loops, and when the first crack opened in the first duct and the first drop of water traced its first rust-line down the first transformer housing, they woke up and began to eat.

They eat iron. They breathe oxygen. They excrete rust — or rather, they accelerate rusting, catalyze it, make it happen faster and more thoroughly than it would in their absence. Every orange stain in the cupola is their work. Every rust-line is a road they built. The thin biofilm they form on damp ferrous surfaces is the base layer of the cupola's ecology — the grass of this tiny, absurd, industrially-lit savanna. The fly larvae graze on it. The adult flies drink from the puddles it enriches with dissolved iron. Without the bacteria, the flies would have nothing. Without the flies, the bacteria would still be here, quietly rusting the world, but nobody would notice.

The bacteria do not respond to the cupola's reconfigurations the way the flies do. They are slow. They are patient. When a new wet surface appears, they colonize it over hours, not seconds. When a surface dries out, they encyst and wait. They are the cupola's deep memory, its geological record — the rust stains they leave behind are the closest thing this space has to permanent marks, to history written in something other than behavior. A maintenance worker, if one ever came, could read the history of the cupola's condensation patterns in the overlapping rust lines the way a dendrochronologist reads tree rings. Here, water flowed for six months. Here, it stopped. Here, it started again, shifted two centimeters to the left by a duct that moved under thermal stress. The bacteria recorded all of it. The bacteria are still recording.

---

**The larvae live here**, and they are the most alien presence in the cupola, because they are the only inhabitants that *consume the structure itself*.

They eat paint. That faded orange stenciling — the serial numbers, the temperature warnings, the cryptic maintenance codes — is food. The larvae cluster on the painted characters and devour them over days, leaving bare metal behind, and the bare metal is then colonized by bacteria, which is then grazed by the next generation of larvae, and so the larvae are not just eating — they are *terraforming*, converting the cupola's surfaces from human-legible to fly-legible, replacing text with biofilm, replacing information-for-people with information-for-flies.

This is not vandalism. The serial numbers were never going to be read again. The temperature warnings warn no one. The maintenance codes refer to protocols that expired decades or centuries ago. The larvae are not destroying meaning — they are composting it, recycling it, turning dead language into living substrate. In the places where they have been most active, the walls are mottled: patches of bare, bacteria-furred metal alternating with islands of still-legible text, and the boundaries between them are scalloped and organic, like a coastline, like the edge of a forest advancing into a field. The larvae are the forest. The text is the field. The forest is winning, slowly, and what it leaves behind is more alive than what was there before.

The larvae are translucent. Under the industrial floods, they glow. When they migrate along the grating mesh — driven by a thermal shift, or by the depletion of a paint source, or by some larval calculus too fine to observe — they catch the light and look like animated rice grains, or like living droplets of some slow, purposeful fluid flowing through the grid. They are eyeless. They navigate by temperature gradient and chemical trace. They are perhaps the only inhabitants of the cupola that are completely indifferent to the light.

---

**Something with knees lives here.** Or visits. Or used to visit and has stopped. Or hasn't stopped but comes at intervals longer than anyone has watched.

The scuff marks. The knee impressions. The calibration wrench. These are evidence of a presence that is categorically different from everything else in the cupola — a presence that *chose* to come here, that navigated the maintenance crevice (barely wide enough for shoulders), that crouched at the edge of the space and looked inward, that brought a tool and forgot it or left it deliberately, that came back more than once.

What was it? What is it?

Here is what can be inferred:

It has knees. It is therefore bilaterally symmetrical, probably bipedal, probably something whose body plan originates from a gravity well and a solid surface, something that evolved to walk and developed the ability to crouch as a secondary posture — a posture of watching, of patience, of voluntary stillness. It is small enough to fit through the maintenance crevice, which means it is no larger than an adult human, and possibly smaller. It has hands, or something functionally equivalent, because it brought the wrench and set it down with enough care that the wrench is upright, balanced on the grating, not dropped or thrown. It uses tools. It understands pipe fittings, or at least carries the equipment for adjusting them. It is not afraid of the flies. The knee impressions are deep enough and repeated enough to indicate long periods of immobility, and anyone who sat that still in the cupola would be covered in flies within minutes — flies landing on warm skin, tasting salt, investigating. Whoever crouches here permits this. Welcomes it, maybe. Or simply does not care.

The most telling detail is the wrench. It is furred with egg casings. A hundred layings, the description says, in the textured grip. A fly is standing on it now, grooming. The wrench has become infrastructure — fly infrastructure, part of the cupola's ecology, a surface that is grazed and colonized and incorporated into the living map. Whoever left it here either didn't notice this would happen or noticed and approved. Either they were ignorant of the flies' nature, which seems unlikely given the evidence of long repeated observation, or they understood exactly what would happen to any object left in this space and chose to leave the wrench as a *gift* — a new surface, a new territory, a small addition to the cupola's geography, offered by someone who had watched long enough to understand what the flies would do with it.

This is the most frightening possibility: that the thing with knees is a *naturalist*. That it comes here the way a field biologist visits a study site — repeatedly, patiently, with tools, with the willingness to sit still and be crawled on, with an understanding of the system deep enough to make small deliberate interventions and observe their effects. That the wrench is not forgotten but *placed*. That the scuff marks are not careless but *necessary*, the unavoidable traces of a body that keeps returning to the same observation point because this is where the view is best, where the whole turbulent city of flies can be seen at once, where the systemic inhale and the copper-green suspension and the shimmering renegotiation can be witnessed in their full scope.

If this is true, then the cupola has a god. A small, quiet, crouching god with knees and a calibration wrench, who watches everything and adjusts nothing — or almost nothing — and who understands the flies better than the flies understand themselves, because the flies have no distance from their own behavior, no ability to see the whole pattern, while the thing in the crevice sees all of it, the proposals and the dissents and the territorial micro-boundaries and the generational memory preserved in flight circuits, and finds it — what? Beautiful? Interesting? Useful? Familiar?

Or maybe the thing with knees is something simpler. A maintenance worker. A technician who was supposed to adjust a pipe fitting somewhere in this region of the structure and discovered the cupola by accident and kept coming back because it was extraordinary and because nobody checks where you are when you're this far up in the infrastructure and because watching ten thousand small lives negotiate an unstable world is more compelling than any task on any work order.

Or maybe the thing with knees is something stranger still. Maybe it is another emergent property of the structure, the way the cupola itself is emergent — not planned, not designed, but *happened*, the way a bubble happens in glass. Maybe somewhere in the structure's vast and self-reconfiguring systems, a maintenance protocol fired that said *go here, observe, report*, and the thing that responded was not a person but a process, a subroutine with a body, a function that walks on two legs and carries tools and crouches and watches because that is what it was instantiated to do, and it keeps coming back because the observation is never complete, because the cupola is never the same place twice, because the report it is trying to compile is infinite.

The flies don't care. The flies have incorporated the knee-marks into their geography, the way they incorporate everything. The depressions in the particulate are slightly warmer than the surrounding grating — residual body heat, absorbed and re-emitted by the compacted dust — and a few larvae have clustered in them, grazing on whatever was left behind: dead skin cells, maybe, or synthetic fibers from clothing, or just the faint chemical signature of a body that was here. The larvae don't know they are eating the traces of their observer. They don't know they have an observer. They know only that this patch of grating is 0.3 degrees warmer than the adjacent patch and that there is something organic here that can be metabolized, and that is enough, that is everything, that is the whole of their world.

---

**The structure lives here.** Not in the cupola specifically — the structure lives everywhere, is everywhere, extends in every direction below and to every side farther than anyone in the cupola can perceive. But it lives *here* in a particular way, because this is one of its unplanned spaces, one of its bubbles, one of the places where its competing systems create something that no system intended.

The structure does not know the cupola exists. That is almost certainly true. Whatever intelligence or administrative process governs the structure's operations has no model of this space, no sensor dedicated to it, no maintenance schedule that includes it. The cupola exists in the gaps between jurisdictions, in the overlap between systems that were installed in different construction phases and never fully integrated. The warm duct and the cool duct and the cracked duct and the relay cabinets and the cable runs — each of these belongs to a different system, is monitored by a different protocol, is maintained (or not maintained) by a different subroutine. None of them knows about the others. None of them knows that their convergence has created a void, and that the void is inhabited, and that the inhabitants have built a city.

And yet the structure *feeds* the cupola. The warm air and the cool air and the condensation and the electromagnetic fields and the thermal gradients — all of this is provided by the structure, not deliberately but inevitably, the way a body provides an ecosystem for its gut bacteria without ever deciding to. The structure's reconfigurations — the reversed ducts, the popping panels, the overheating cables — are the cupola's weather, its seasons, its geological events. The flies have adapted not to a static environment but to the structure's *restlessness*, its chronic inability to settle into a stable configuration. Every reconfiguration is a catastrophe and an opportunity. Every reversed duct is a flood and a fertile alluvial deposit. The flies thrive not despite the instability but because of it, because instability creates gradients, and gradients create niches, and niches create diversity, and diversity creates resilience.

The structure, in other words, is the cupola's planet. It is the ground the flies walk on and the sky they fly in and the ocean they drink from and the tectonics that reshape their world. It does not know them. It cannot know them. They are too small and too quiet and too far from any sensor. But they know it — not as a concept, not as an entity, but as the totality of conditions that constitute reality. The structure is not something the flies live *in*. The structure is everything that *is*.

---

**And the light lives here.** The light that never changes. The one constant.

It comes from devices that were installed to illuminate maintenance corridors that no longer exist as planned. The status LEDs report on systems that may or may not still function. The junction box scrolls its perpetual semaphore — green-green-green-amber-green — for no audience, to no purpose, a signal that has outlived its recipient. The light is, in a sense, the structure's dream of this space: the illumination it provides for workers who will never come, the information it broadcasts to monitors that were never installed, the readiness it maintains for a maintenance visit that was scheduled once and then lost in some administrative lacuna and never rescheduled and never canceled, so that the lights stay on, will always stay on, will burn until their elements fail, which may be decades or may be centuries, because they were built to industrial tolerances and have no reason to stop.

The light makes the cupola visible to itself. Without it, the flies would still be here — they don't navigate by light, they navigate by heat and chemistry. But the light makes their wings flash copper-green. The light makes the rust stains legible. The light makes the condensation drip visible as it falls, a tiny silver line in bright air. The light makes the whole turbulent system *seeable*, and therefore beautiful, and therefore something that a thing with knees might crouch at the edge of a crevice to watch.

The light is the cupola's most generous accident. It is what transforms a thriving ecosystem in darkness — which would be impressive but invisible — into a *spectacle*, a display, a performance staged for no one and attended by at least one repeat visitor. The light is not alive, but it is the condition that makes everything else's aliveness apparent. It is the medium in which the cupola knows itself.

---

So who lives here?

Everyone listed above. A bacterial foundation that eats iron and writes history in rust. Ten thousand flies that think collectively in the language of position, that map an unstable world in real time with their bodies, that remember with their flight paths and propose with their clustering and dissent with their stillness. Larvae that terraform the walls, converting dead language into living substrate. A structure so vast it has its own weather, providing everything and knowing nothing. A light that was meant for someone else and ended up illuminating a city it can never see. And something with knees, crouching in a crevice, watching, returning, watching again, leaving small gifts in the form of tools that become territory, and finding in this impossible, unplanned, ovoid pocket of bright competing air something worth the long crawl up through the infrastructure to see.

All of them live here. None of them planned to. The cupola was not built for any of them, was not built at all, was a convergence, an accident, a bubble in glass, a knot in heated metal. And yet it is, in its six bright meters of unresolvable turbulence, as complete an ecosystem as any tide pool, any canopy, any deep-sea vent — a world that invented itself in the gaps between systems, and that reinvents itself every time a duct reverses or a panel pops or a puddle shifts, and that will go on reinventing itself for as long as the structure breathes and the light stays on and the flies keep making their papery, sub-threshold, almost-silent argument about what it means to be alive in a place that was never meant for living.

The cupola doesn't care that it wasn't meant. The cupola is busy. The cupola is full.
