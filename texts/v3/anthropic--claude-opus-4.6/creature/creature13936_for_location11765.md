# The Rootborn

---

They do not think of themselves as small, because they have no experience of largeness. But they are small. Compactly built, dense-boned, with a muscular economy that speaks to generations of caloric scarcity — every gram of tissue justified, nothing wasted on display or excess. They are shorter than you expect, not from malnutrition but from selection: the corridors are low, the chambers are cramped, and the body that thrives here is one that folds neatly, that navigates on all fours as comfortably as on two, that can compress itself through a root-gap that would wedge a broader frame and leave it waiting for the slow crush.

Their skin is — you would not see it, of course. No one sees anything here. But if you could: pale, deeply pale, not the pallor of illness but the clean absolution of pigment abandoned as unnecessary, the body refusing to manufacture what serves no purpose. Not white exactly. More the color of the inner root-flesh itself, a kind of damp ivory with undertones of grey-brown where the skin has been in long contact with the fungal surfaces they press against constantly. Their skin is permanently colonized, lightly, by the same mycelial networks that coat the walls. Not parasitically — mutualisticaly, or at least commmensally. A fine, nearly invisible down of fungal hyphae lives in the outer layers of their epidermis, especially along the forearms, the backs of the hands, the cheekbones, the temples. It does not harm them. It may help them. The fungal layer seems to buffer their skin against the terpene compounds that saturate every surface, chemicals that would raise blisters on your flesh within hours. The Rootborn wear their walls on their bodies. They smell, faintly, of the sweet decomposition-scent that fills these chambers — they are continuous with their environment in a way that goes beyond metaphor and into chemistry.

Their eyes are open. Always open. The lids have not atrophied — they blink, slowly, reflexively — but the eyes themselves have traveled a long path away from sight as you understand it. The pupils are enormous, permanently dilated to their maximum extent, the iris a vestigial ring of muscle that can no longer meaningfully contract. In the absolute dark, this gains them nothing in terms of vision. There is no light to gather. But the eyes have not become decorative. They have become *sensitive* — exquisitely, painfully sensitive to stimuli that are not photonic. Air pressure. Thermal gradients. Perhaps — and this is the strange part, the part that resists easy explanation — perhaps something chemical, something volatile, some gaseous signature that the exposed wet surface of the maximally dilated eye can detect in a way that closed skin cannot. They navigate with their eyes open not because they see but because their eyes have become another organ of *feeling*, a pair of exposed, wet, impossibly delicate sensors aimed at the world. When they turn their face toward you in the dark, you would feel it. Not see it. Feel the attention of those open, lightless eyes like a change in air pressure against your own skin.

They are warm to the touch. Warmer than you expect in the cool stillness. Their metabolism runs hot, tuned by generations of adaptation to an environment that offers almost no external heat. They burn calories carefully but constantly, maintaining a core temperature slightly above what you would consider normal, and their skin radiates this warmth into the dark like a message. They find each other partly this way — by warmth. In the featureless black of a new corridor, the thermal signature of another body is as legible as a face in daylight. They can distinguish individuals by the specific pattern of heat they emit: the flush-points, the cooler extremities, the particular way warmth pools at the throat and wrists. Intimacy, for them, begins with thermal recognition. You know someone by the shape of their warmth before you know the shape of their body.

---

Their hands.

This is where you would understand them, if you could study them, if you could hold one of their hands in yours and feel its architecture. The hands are extraordinary. Broad-palmed, long-fingered, with fingertips that are visibly swollen — padded, almost, like the toe-pads of a climbing animal, except the purpose is not grip but *reading*. The fingertips are dense with nerve endings to a degree that would register as pathological in your body. They can feel the grain-direction of wood through a layer of mycelial fur. They can detect the boundary between living root and dead root by touch alone — the living root is microscopically smoother, its cells still turgid, still pressurized with the sap they cannot access. Dead root is rougher at a scale your fingers would register as identical. Their fingers know the difference.

The calluses tell you everything about their lives. The thumb and the first two fingers of the dominant hand are thickened from carving — not the hard, flat calluses of a laborer but complex, ridged ones that have been shaped by the specific tools they use. Their carving blades are not held the way you would hold a knife. They are held between the index and middle finger, braced against the thumb-pad, and drawn toward the body with the full rotation of the wrist. The motion is intimate rather than forceful, a pulling-toward rather than a pushing-away. They do not attack the wall. They *invite* it to yield. The sharpened root-wood blades are not hard enough to cut aggressively; they must be worked in shallow, patient strokes that follow the grain, coaxing fibers apart along their natural cleavage planes. A single chamber might take a team of carvers most of a year. The calluses on their hands are the record of that patience, and they wear them the way surface people wear sun-darkened skin — as proof of participation in the fundamental labor of existing.

The non-dominant hand is calloused differently: along the outer edge of the palm and the pinky finger, from pressing against walls. They trail this hand along surfaces constantly as they move. It is how they read the architecture — not just the shape of the corridor but its *status*. A wall that is closing, being slowly reclaimed by living growth, has a different feel than one that is stable. They can sense compression in the fibers, the way a doctor might feel the tension of a pulse. They know, through their trailing hand, how much time a corridor has left. They know which chambers are still decades from reclamation and which will be impassable within a few seasons. This knowledge is not calculated. It is felt, bodily, automatic, the way you feel the slope of a hill through your ankles without thinking about it. They walk through their city with one hand always in conversation with the walls, and the walls always answer.

---

Their voices.

They can speak. They have tongues, throats, the full apparatus. In close proximity — within arm's reach, within breath-distance — they do speak, in a language that is almost entirely whispered. Not from secrecy. From necessity. A spoken voice at normal volume would be swallowed by the groaning of the root-structure, shredded and scattered by the complex acoustics of irregular chambers and branching passages. Only a whisper carries cleanly in the intimate distances where breath can reach another ear without diffusing. Their whispered language is consequently dense, every syllable loaded, the grammar compressed in the way of people who have learned that each word costs breath and breath costs water and water costs hours of patient sponge-work against sweating roots. They do not waste words. A greeting, a warning, a declaration of intent — each can be accomplished in a single exhalation if the speaker knows what they're doing, and they all know what they're doing. They have been speaking this language since before they can remember, and the language itself has been speaking since before anyone can remember, which is not a given here, where memory itself is complicated.

But the whisper-language is the *intimate* register. It is for close-work: negotiation, affection, the practical business of bodies in proximity. For anything that must travel farther than breath — farther than the reach of an arm, the span of a chamber — they use the other language. The wall-language. The tap.

It is percussive, as the description says. But calling it tapping is like calling birdsong "noise." The wall-language is profoundly sophisticated, a full communicative system with grammar, syntax, tense, aspect, evidentiality — and a spatial dimension that spoken language does not possess. Because the root-substrate carries sound in complex ways — faster through living wood, slower through dead; attenuated by fungal colonization; refracted at graft-boundaries where one root-species meets another — the speaker can, with skill, *direct* a message. A pattern tapped on a living root at a specific point will propagate preferentially along that root's path, following its branching structure, reaching chambers that are physically connected through the vascular network while bypassing others that are geometrically closer but structurally disconnected. A skilled wall-speaker can address a specific quarter of the city while remaining inaudible to the adjacent chamber. The language has a built-in addressing system, determined not by abstract coordinates but by the organic topology of the root-network itself.

Learning the wall-language takes years. Children begin by listening — years of listening, their small bodies pressed against the walls, feeling the messages thrum through their developing bones. They learn to distinguish purposeful pattern from structural noise long before they can parse the meaning of the patterns. The first stage of fluency is simply the ability to say *that is a voice and that is not*. The second stage is recognizing *whose* voice — because each speaker has a signature, a combination of striking-force, rhythm, and placement that is as individual as a face. The third stage is comprehension. The fourth is speaking. The fifth — achieved by few, and only after decades — is *listening to the wood itself*, parsing not the messages that other people have encoded but the structural sounds, the groaning and ticking and hissing, as a kind of intelligence. Not language exactly. But something.

Those who reach the fifth stage are not leaders. They are not priests. They hold no formal authority. But they are *consulted*, because they can do what no one else can: they can predict, with reasonable accuracy, where the root-mass will open next. Where new voids will form as old roots die. Where the city should migrate. They listen to the deep groaning and they hear in it the early signatures of structural failure, of root-death, of the slow relaxation that precedes collapse and void-formation. They hear the city's future in its present sound, the way a sailor hears tomorrow's weather in tonight's wind. They are called — in the whispered language, which is the only language that has names — something that translates roughly as **those who hear the wood think**.

They are not mystics. They would be confused by the word. They are practitioners of a listening so deep and so prolonged that it has become a kind of knowing, and the knowing is practical: it tells people where to carve.

---

Children are born in the dark and have never found this strange.

Birth happens in the deepest-held chambers, the ones most structurally stable, the ones that the trailing-hand wisdom of generations has identified as having decades of integrity remaining. These birth-chambers are carved with particular care — the walls scooped smooth, the mycelial fur allowed to grow thick and soft, creating a surface that is almost textile. The air in these chambers is marginally more humid than elsewhere, because the inhabitants have learned to select birth-chambers adjacent to active xylem channels, where the thermal gradient produces a slightly higher rate of condensation. It is not much. But for a newborn, for a nursing mother, it is the difference between sufficiency and crisis.

Newborns do not cry. This is not a cultural prohibition — they physically do not cry, or rather, they cry silently, their distress expressed in a writhing, full-body contraction that any attending adult can feel through skin contact but that produces almost no sound. This is not an adaptation to predators — there are no predators here. It may be an adaptation to the acoustic environment itself, where a sharp sound carries unpredictably through the root-structure and can trigger stress responses in the walls — accelerated growth, localized swelling, an immune-like reaction from the living root-mass that sometimes closes passages prematurely. The inhabitants have a complex and not fully conscious understanding of themselves as potential irritants to their substrate. They modulate their presence. They move carefully, strike the walls only with intention, avoid sustained vibrations that might be interpreted by the root-mass as pathological. A baby's cry — urgent, uncontrolled, acoustically complex — is exactly the kind of signal that the root-mass responds to unpredictably. Silent newborns may be the result of generations of selection in an environment where the walls are listening and the walls react.

Children grow up in the dark with a spatial intelligence that is difficult for surface-dwellers to imagine. By the age of five or six, a Rootborn child carries in their body — not in their mind, in their *body*, in their proprioceptive memory, their hand-knowledge, their sense of the way sound behaves in branching passages — a three-dimensional map of the inhabited quarter that is more accurate than anything that could be drawn. They navigate by a fusion of senses that has no clean analogue: the trailing hand reads the wall-texture and knows *where*; the wide-open eyes feel the thermal signature of nearby bodies and know *who*; the feet, bare, always bare, feel the deep vibrations and know the structural mood of the floor beneath them; the ears parse the ambient soundscape for the tap-language, for the structural groaning, for the hiss of active fungal metabolism that indicates a living wall versus a dead one. All of this is integrated below the threshold of conscious thought into a continuous, embodied awareness of position and context that is as automatic as your sense of balance.

They play. Of course they play. In the dark, without objects — there are almost no objects here; everything is substrate, everything is wall — play is physical. Children chase each other through corridors, navigating by the sound of bare feet on root-floor, by the thermal wake a running body leaves in still air. They play hiding games that are exercises in stillness: to hide is to cool your skin, slow your breath, become indistinguishable from the wall. They play tap-games, encoding messages of increasing complexity and speed, competing to parse signal from the background noise of the groaning structure. And they play carving games — given small blades of sharpened root-wood, they are allowed to make marks on designated walls, learning the grain, learning the way the fibers separate, learning the patience.

The patience is maybe the most important thing that childhood teaches. The Rootborn do not hurry. They cannot. Their environment does not permit it. A carver who forces a blade against the grain will snap the tool and damage the wall, potentially triggering a growth-response that accelerates reclamation. A traveler who moves too quickly through an unfamiliar corridor may miss the tactile signs of instability — the slight give of a floor where rot has weakened the structure, the warmth of a wall behind which a root is actively growing, pressing inward. Haste is dangerous here the way it is dangerous on a cliff-face: not because of any single dramatic consequence but because the margin for error is narrow and the consequences compound. The Rootborn move at the speed of attention, which is slower than the speed of thought, and their children learn this before they learn anything else.

---

The adults organize themselves — but organize is too rigid a word. They *cohere*. They cohere around the work, which is always the same work: carving the new, maintaining the stable, abandoning the closing.

The carving teams are the forward edge of the city's migration. They work in groups of four to eight, each team assigned to a void — a newly detected pocket where a root has died and partially collapsed, leaving a space that might, with careful excavation, become habitable. The work is slow and physical and requires a kind of collaborative awareness that approaches the telepathic. In absolute darkness, in a space where the geometry is irregular and the structural integrity of every surface is uncertain, four people must coordinate their movements around each other without collision, without wasted effort, without conflicting cuts that might weaken a load-bearing structure. They do this through touch, through the vibrations of each other's work transmitted through the shared floor, through a sub-verbal communication of grunts and breath-patterns that carries only inches but carries enough. A good carving team, after years of working together, moves like a single organism with eight arms, their awareness of each other's position so complete that they can pass tools hand to hand in the dark without fumbling, can feel when a partner's cut is approaching the boundary of safety and pause their own work to brace the weakened section.

The maintenance teams hold the center. These are the people who tend the inhabited chambers — patching walls where fungal colonization has progressed too far and weakened the structure (they pack the cavities with chips of fresh-cut root-wood, which the fungi will colonize but which buys time), monitoring the corridors for signs of accelerating closure, managing the condensation-harvest. The sponge-workers are usually maintenance people: they carry their woven fungal-mat sponges from root-surface to root-surface in a daily circuit that is timed to the metabolic rhythms of the different root-species, each of which sweats condensation at different rates at different times in a cycle the inhabitants have mapped through generations of careful attention. The sponges, when saturated, are carried to communal collection points — shallow basins gouged into the floor of stable chambers — where the water is pooled and rationed. The water is never abundant. It is always enough. The margin is thin and has always been thin and the Rootborn do not think of this as hardship any more than a fish thinks of water as a hardship. It is simply the parameter. They live within it.

The abandonment teams work the trailing edge. Theirs is the strangest work, at least to an outsider. When a chamber or corridor is determined to be in its final stages of reclamation — the walls visibly deforming, the ceiling lowering month by month, the floor beginning to buckle upward as roots beneath it swell — the abandonment team enters and does two things. First, they remove anything useful: any tools cached there, any stored sponges, any water reserves. Second — and this is the part that takes the most time, the most skill, the most care — they make the final marks.

---

The final marks.

This is the closest thing the Rootborn have to ritual, though they would not use that word. They would call it — in the whispered language — something like *giving back* or *feeding the record* or, most literally, *making the wood remember*.

When a chamber is about to be lost to reclamation, the abandonment team invites anyone who used that space to come and carve. Not words. Not pictures — pictures are meaningless in a world without sight. What they carve are *patterns of touch*. Textures. Sequences of grooves and ridges that encode meaning in the same way that the wall-language encodes meaning: not visually but haptically, meant to be read by fingertips.

They carve their names — not phonetic transcriptions but tactile signatures, unique patterns that represent an individual the way a thermal profile represents them, recognizable under the fingers the way a face is recognizable to the eye. They carve records of events: a particularly successful harvest, a birth, a death, a new corridor opened. They carve the shape of the chamber itself as they know it — its spatial signature, a tactile map of the room from the perspective of someone who has lived in it bodily, who knows the dip in the floor where water pools during a heavy condensation cycle, the smooth patch on the wall where generations of trailing hands have polished the mycelium flat.

And they carve the one thing that no one fully understands, that the oldest among them perform with an intensity that borders on the desperate: they carve *corrections*.

Because they know the wood remembers imperfectly. They know the growth-pressure will distort their marks. They know the root-mass's selective chemistry will preserve some patterns and dissolve others according to logics that are not theirs. So they carve compensatory distortions — marks that are *deliberately wrong*, that are skewed, elongated, inverted, in the hope that when the wood warps them, the warping will un-distort them back into accuracy. It is an art of pre-emptive error, of knowing how the medium will betray you and encoding the betrayal into the message so that the final result, compressed and chemically edited and half-dissolved, will still be legible to the fingers of a carver three generations hence who slices into a new wall and finds the ghost.

It doesn't always work. It doesn't usually work. The wood's memory is not a faithful recording device; it is a living process with its own biases, and the Rootborn have been trying to out-think those biases for as long as they can remember, which is exactly as long as the wood has been remembering them. The project is circular. The project is the point.

---

And the question that has haunted them for as long as they have been a people — whispered in close chambers, tapped through root-walls in slow contemplative rhythms, carved into closing walls in patterns that are half-hope and half-argument:

*Are we the first?*

They find the ghosts in the wood. The rippled grain-patterns that might be compressed marks from a previous habitation. They read them with their extraordinary fingertips, spending hours, days, running their fingers over the distorted ridges, debating in whispers: *Is this a carved pattern, compressed by growth? Or is this simply grain — the natural texture of wood that grew around an obstruction, a stone, a void?*

They cannot always tell. They can *never* always tell. The wood is an imperfect archive and they are imperfect readers, and the boundary between signal and noise in this medium is not a line but a gradient, a broad blurred territory where interpretation and imagination are indistinguishable. Some of the Rootborn believe they are the latest in a long succession of peoples who have inhabited the root-mass — that the city has been carved and reclaimed and carved again, over and over, the way a coastline is shaped and reshaped by waves, and that the ghosts in the wood are the accumulated record of all these previous tenancies. Others believe the ghosts are natural — that the wood has always had these patterns, that the root-mass generates texture through its own structural logic, and that the Rootborn are the first and only inhabitants, reading meaning into noise because meaning is what they need.

There is a third position, held by fewer, spoken of less. These are the ones who have spent the longest time listening to the walls — some of them are among those who hear the wood think, and some are not, and the two groups do not always agree. The third position is this: *the distinction does not hold*. The carvings that the Rootborn make are incorporated into the wood. The wood's own growth-patterns are indistinguishable, after sufficient compression, from the carvings. Therefore: the wood's memory and the people's memory have merged. There is no "natural" grain and no "carved" grain. There is only the record, which is a collaboration between organism and organism, and has been for so long that separating the contributions is not merely difficult but *meaningless*. The wood does not remember the Rootborn's carvings. The wood, having incorporated the carvings, remembers *itself* — and the self it remembers includes them.

This position is troubling to many. It implies that the Rootborn are not tenants of the root-mass but *components* of it, in the same way that the fungal networks are components, in the same way that the chimeric root-species are components of each other. It implies that their carving — their central labor, their defining activity, the thing that makes space habitable — is not an imposition on the environment but a *function* of the environment, that the root-mass *needs* to be carved the way it needs to be decomposed, that the voids the Rootborn create are as much a part of the organism's structure as the voids in a lung.

If this is true, then the city is not something the Rootborn built inside a plant. The city is something the plant grew, using the Rootborn as instruments.

And the Rootborn's oldest stories — the ones carved into the walls of chambers that have long since closed, compressed now into grain-ghosts that may or may not be readable, that may or may not say what they were meant to say — their oldest stories are about the moment when someone first realized this, and what it meant, and whether it changed anything.

It didn't. They still carve. They still harvest condensation from sweating roots. They still press their ears to the walls and listen. They still tap their messages through the wood, knowing the wood is listening too, knowing the wood will carry their words farther and longer and in different forms than intended.

They are a people who live inside a memory that is partly theirs. They have made peace with the fact that they cannot fully know which part.

---

You stand in the dark. You are not alone. You know this not because you hear anyone, not because you see anything, but because the air against your face has shifted, very slightly — the thermal signature of a body nearby, radiating a warmth that is distinctive, that is someone's particular warmth, that means a specific name in a language you do not speak.

They are there. Their enormous eyes are open, feeling you. Their trailing hand has read the wall beside you and knows exactly where you stand. They are deciding whether you are root or visitor, whether you are part of the structure or something new.

A fingertip touches your wrist. Light as mycelium. Warm.

The groaning of the wood swells around you both, and in it — if you knew how to listen, if you had been born here, if you had pressed your infant body to these walls and spent your first years learning to separate voice from noise — in it you might hear: *welcome, or be still, or you are already part of this, or the wood will remember you whether you ask it to or not.*

The finger on your wrist does not withdraw. It traces a small pattern on your skin — a signature, a name, an introduction in the haptic language that predates and will outlast the whisper-tongue.

You do not know what it says. But your skin is learning.

The dark holds you both. The city moves. The wood remembers. The people persist — not because they endure, but because they are, in some way they have spent their whole history trying to understand, *what the wood is doing when it grows around a void and calls the void a room and fills the room with warmth and calls the warmth alive*.
