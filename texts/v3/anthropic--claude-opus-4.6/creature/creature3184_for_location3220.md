What lives here is not any single creature. What lives here is the *gathering itself* — the fact that warmth and light, however feeble, however accidental, will always be answered.

---

**The moths** came first, and they are still the central fact of the place. They are not one species. There are at least three, maybe four, occupying slightly different niches even within this small volume of hot air.

The largest are the ones resting on the pipes — heavy-bodied, furred at the thorax, their wings folded back like cloaks. These are the heat-seekers, the ones that found their way down through fissures and cracks following the thermal gradient the way water follows gravity. They are not interested in the light. They are interested in the warmth of the iron, the way it radiates against their undersides when they press themselves flat and still. Their antennae are enormous relative to their bodies — feathered, delicate, tasting the saturated air for pheromones that must be difficult to parse in this wet heat. They mate on the pipes. You can see pairs of them coupled, motionless for hours, their wings overlapping to form a single pale shape like a smudge of calcium on the sweating iron. Their eggs are laid in the sediment itself — tiny pearl-white clusters pressed into the soft walls at about waist height, where the temperature is most stable. The larvae, when they hatch, will burrow. They will eat the algal film, the bacterial mats, the microscopic life that threads through the wet calcium. They will grow inside the wall the way a fossil forms, except alive, except still moving. And when they pupate and emerge, they will leave tiny exit holes in the hardening sediment — holes that will eventually become features of the rock, perforations that some future geologist might mistake for the traces of ancient marine organisms.

The second kind are the ones near the lights. Smaller, more restless, orbiting the caged bulbs in that compulsive spiral that moths perform when their navigation has been hijacked by a signal they cannot resolve. These are the ones dying fastest — you can see their bodies on the ground beneath the lights, wings spread, already being attended by the beetles. They are the most conventionally moth-like, the ones behaving the way moths behave in the upper world, where the moon is the reference point and a porch light is a catastrophe. Down here the caged work-lights are the only thing resembling a celestial body, and these moths treat them accordingly — with devotion, with ruin. Their wings are marked with faint eyespots, dark circles on pale ground, a camouflage pattern evolved for some bark or stone surface far above that they will never see again. Some of them have singed wings. The cage around the bulb keeps them from direct contact with the filament, but the heat radiating through the wire mesh is enough to curl the edges of a wing if a moth rests there too long. They rest there too long. They always rest there too long.

The third kind is the hardest to see. These are the smallest — wingspan of a fingernail, bodies like grains of rice — and they live not on the walls or the pipes or near the lights but in the ground itself, on the surface of the pale slurry, walking more than flying. They pick their way across the wet sediment on legs that seem too long for their bodies, moving with a fastidious, water-strider quality, their wings held vertical above their backs like tiny sails. They are feeding on something in the surface film — the same algal bloom that tints the corners green, probably, or the bacterial cultures that thrive in the warm calcium water. They are the most adapted to this place. The others might leave if they could find the way back up. These would not. These have made the cistern works their entire evolutionary context. Give them ten thousand years and they will be eyeless, wingless, unrecognizable — some new thing, native to a niche that didn't exist until the city put pipes through raw sediment and strung lights on a cable and created, by accident, an underground terrarium heated from below and lit from above and wet with the breath of a water table pressing up through stone that hasn't finished becoming stone.

There may be a fourth kind. In the highest domes — those pockets where the wet clay has sagged upward into vaults — there are shapes resting in the apex that might be moths or might be something else. They are too high to see clearly in the bad light. They are dark, which is unusual here where everything tends toward paleness. They do not move during the hours when the work-lights are on. Whether they move during the hours when the work-lights are off, no one can say, because no one is here when the work-lights are off.

---

**The geckos** are the most visible predators, and they have arranged themselves with a territorial precision that suggests they have been here long enough to negotiate. Each gecko occupies a section of pipe, and the boundaries between territories are invisible but absolute — you can watch a gecko pursue a moth right up to an unmarked point on the iron surface and then stop, freezing, toes splayed, as if it has hit glass. The gecko on the adjacent stretch does not even look up. The agreement is settled.

They are pale to the point of translucence. Their skin has the quality of wet paper — you can see the branching of blood vessels at the throat, the dark blob of the liver, the shadows of moths in their stomachs in various stages of digestion. Some of them have been here long enough that their coloring has shifted, or perhaps they were always this pale and the species was already adapted to lightless or near-lightless environments before it found this place. Their eyes are disproportionately large, the pupils dilated wide in the dimness, the irises a faint gold that catches the filament-light and gives them a look of permanent, glassy attentiveness. They do not blink often. When they eat, they are sudden — a moth will land on the pipe two inches from a gecko that has been motionless for twenty minutes, and the gecko will snap sideways with a speed that seems to violate the lethargic physics of this room, and then it is motionless again, and the moth is gone, and the only evidence is a faint movement in the gecko's throat as it swallows.

There is one gecko that is larger than the others — noticeably, disproportionately larger, maybe fifteen centimeters nose to tail where the others are eight or ten. It occupies the highest point on the pipe scaffold, where the two mains meet a horizontal brace, and its territory extends down both pipes for a distance that encompasses what might be the territories of two or three smaller geckos that are no longer present. Its belly is opaque with fat. It does not hunt often. When it does, it takes the largest moths — the heat-seekers, the heavy-bodied ones — with a lunge that shakes the scaffold slightly and sends a vibration through the iron that the other geckos register with a synchronized flinch. This gecko has lost most of its tail at some point and regenerated a blunt stub that is darker than the rest of its body, suggesting either injury or conflict. It is possible that the geckos have their own politics down here, their own wars, their own history of expansion and loss, all played out on two meters of sweating iron pipe in a room that the earth is trying to close.

---

**The toad** is singular. There is only one, and it has the quality of a monarch in exile — solitary, patient, immovable, surrounded by the evidence of its appetites. Its depression beneath the workbench is not a burrow exactly but a wallow, a shallow basin that it has formed by the simple repeated pressure of its body against soft ground, night after night (if there are nights here — there are not, but the toad may remember nights from wherever it came from and impose that rhythm on its stillness). The wallow is lined with the shed scales of moth wings, a papery mulch that has begun to calcite — to calcite — to calcify, the calcium-rich water wicking through the organic material and beginning the long process of mineralization. The toad is sitting on its own future fossil bed. The litter of wings around it will become stone. The impression of its body will become stone. If it dies here and is not removed, the toad itself will become stone, a pale shape in pale rock, a secret housed inside a formation that the city above will never excavate because the pipes must be maintained and the chamber must remain open.

The toad's skin is almost white. Not albino — its eyes are dark, a deep brown-black that absorbs the filament-light rather than reflecting it, which makes it the anti-moth, the thing that draws light in rather than throwing it back. Its skin is white because it has not seen sun in — how long? Long enough for the melanin to fade, for the pigment cells to stop producing what they have no reason to produce. Its skin is also dry in a room that is wet, or rather its skin is wet in a different way — an oily, mucous wet, the toad's own moisture system operating independently of the room's ambient saturation. It breathes through that skin. You can see the pulse at its throat, the slow bellows-rhythm of a creature that is in no hurry to do anything except wait for the next moth to come within range.

It eats by ambush, which in this room means it barely has to try. The moths land near it constantly — on the workbench leg, on the wrenches, on the edge of the wallow itself — and the toad's tongue is fast enough that the strike looks like a twitch, a tic, a muscular accident. The moth is there and then it is not and the toad has not appeared to move at all, only its throat works once, twice, and then it is still again. It eats maybe twenty moths in an hour. It eats maybe five hundred in a day. It has been eating like this for long enough that the field of discarded wings around it extends a full meter in every direction, a corona of translucent tissue that lifts and flutters in the faint air currents generated by the work of wings overhead. The beetles work the edges of this corona, processing the organic debris, breaking it down into smaller components that the sediment absorbs, that the algae feeds on, that the smallest moths eat, that the toad eventually eats again. The cycle is tight. Nothing is wasted. The room is efficient in the way that all closed ecosystems are efficient — everything is food for something else, and the only input is the heat from below and the light from above and whatever nutrients seep in through the cracks.

The toad has been here long enough that the worker — the person with the initials on the clipboard — must know about it. Must step around it when approaching the workbench. Must see it sitting there in its wallow, its white skin almost glowing in the dimness, surrounded by its carpet of wings. Whether the worker considers the toad a companion, a pest, or simply a feature of the room — like the pipes, like the scaffold, like the heat — is not recorded on the clipboard. But the tin cup of cold tea is on the workbench directly above the toad's wallow, and there is something about that proximity that suggests, if not affection, then at least tolerance. Cohabitation. Two organisms occupying the same space for different reasons, each going about their business, each ignoring the other with the practiced ease of long familiarity.

---

**The beetles** are the janitors, and they are perhaps the least interesting creatures here individually but the most important collectively. They are small — six, seven millimeters — with hard black shells that make them the darkest things in the room, little mobile punctuation marks against the pale ground. They move in the cart ruts because the ruts provide channels, paths of least resistance through the soft sediment, and the beetles are not built for difficult terrain. Their legs are short. Their bodies are heavy for their size. They trundle. That is the only word for their movement — a trundling, a slow mechanical forward progress that has no urgency and no apparent destination until you watch long enough to see the pattern: they follow the organic waste. The moth wings, the gecko droppings, the dead moths beneath the lights, the algal film, the bacterial mats — all of it is food, and the beetles process it with a methodical thoroughness that keeps the chamber from becoming choked with detritus. Without them the floor would be ankle-deep in wings. Without them the sediment would be clogged with organic material that would rot and stink and change the chemistry of the water and possibly affect the formation of the rock itself. The beetles are geological agents. They are editing the fossil record before it forms, removing the organic noise so that the calcium sediment can set clean.

One beetle has climbed the diesel engine and is making its way across the flywheel, as noted. It has been doing this for a long time — long enough that its progress is measurable in minutes per centimeter. The flywheel is cold iron, and the beetle's feet find uncertain purchase on the smooth surface, so it moves in a series of advances and corrections, each step placed with the deliberation of a mountaineer. It will reach the far side of the flywheel eventually. It will descend. It will find whatever it was looking for — a smear of diesel, a dead moth caught in the belt mechanism, a film of organic grease — and it will eat, and it will trundle on. Its life is the most legible life in this room: a simple vector from hunger to food, repeated until death, which will come quietly, probably in a cart rut, where its body will be found by other beetles and processed in turn.

---

**The spiders** are opportunists, and they are the most recent arrivals — you can tell because their webs are fresh, the silk still white and elastic, not yet coated with the calcium dust that settles on everything down here. There are maybe a dozen of them, occupying the scaffold structure where the vertical and horizontal members create corners and junctions ideal for web-building. Their webs are not beautiful. They are sheet-webs — flat, messy expanses of silk laid across horizontal surfaces, designed to catch things that fall or flutter downward rather than things that fly into them head-on. The moths that get caught in these webs are usually the light-seekers, the ones spiraling around the caged bulbs, which are mounted on the scaffold. A moth loses its orientation, drops, hits the sheet-web, and struggles, and the spider comes.

The spiders are fast. Faster than the geckos, faster than the toad's tongue, though in a different register — the geckos are ambush-fast, all-or-nothing, the toad is reflex-fast, involuntary, but the spiders are pursuit-fast, the speed of a thing running across a surface it owns, covering the distance from the web's edge to its center in a quick straight rush. They bite. The moth stops struggling. They wrap. The bundle hangs in the web like a small pale lantern, and the spider retreats to its corner and waits for the next one.

The webs are heavy with catches. Some of them hold twenty, thirty wrapped moths, more than any single spider could eat, a surplus that suggests either greed or a hoarding instinct or simply the mathematical reality that in a room this full of moths, a web cannot help but catch more than it needs. The wrapped moths that go uneaten will eventually fall from the web as the silk degrades, and they will land on the floor, and the beetles will find them, and the cycle continues.

There is competition between the spiders and the geckos. Not direct — they do not fight — but spatial. A gecko that climbs the scaffold to hunt is a gecko that might blunder into a web, and while the spider cannot eat a gecko, it can lose a web to one, hours of silk-work destroyed by one clumsy reptilian foot. The geckos seem to know where the webs are. They route around them. Occasionally a young gecko — smaller, less experienced — will put a foot in a sheet-web and pull back, trailing silk, and the spider will rush to the disturbance and then stop, confronting a creature a hundred times its size, and there will be a moment of mutual assessment, and then the gecko will move on and the spider will begin repairs. These encounters happen several times a day. They are the closest thing to drama that this room produces.

---

**The algae** deserves mention as a living thing, though it is the least animate life here. It clings. It persists. It photosynthesizes under conditions that would starve most photosynthetic organisms — the brownish light of a dying incandescent filament is not sunlight, not even close, but it is *light*, and the algae has no standards. It takes what it can get. Its growth is slow, measured in microns per week, a faint greenish stain that spreads across wet surfaces with the patience of the geological process happening around it. It is the base of the food web, or one of the bases — the bacterial mats in the warm water are the other — and without it the smallest moths would have nothing to eat, and without the smallest moths the slightly larger things would have nothing to eat, and so on up the chain to the toad in its wallow and the large gecko on its iron throne.

In the corners where it is thickest, the algae has a smell — green, mineral, faintly sulfurous, the smell of life operating at its bare minimum, squeezing energy from a trickle of bad light and a bath of calcium water. It is the only green thing down here. Everything else is white, cream, tan, pale gray, the palette of the underground. The algae is a flag planted by the surface world, a reminder that somewhere above — far above, through meters of sediment and concrete and urban infrastructure — there is a sun, and things grow toward it, and the color green means something.

---

**And the room itself** is alive, in a way that is not metaphorical. The sediment is calcium carbonate in solution and suspension, actively precipitating, actively lithifying, the slow chemical process by which mud becomes rock. The walls are growing harder. The floor is growing firmer, except where water wells up, where it remains soft and yielding. The bolts in the rock are being incorporated. The boot-prints are being preserved. The cart-ruts are becoming permanent features, grooves that will still be legible in the stone a million years from now if no one disturbs them. The room is a mold of its own use — every action taken in it, every step, every tool set down, every splash of tea from the tin cup, is recorded in the soft substrate and slowly made permanent.

The moths are part of this record. When a moth dies on the wall and its body slumps into the soft sediment, the calcium water closes over it, and the wings — so thin, so detailed, every scale and vein — are preserved with a fidelity that would astonish a paleontologist. The wall of this chamber, if you could section it and examine it under magnification, would already contain hundreds of moth impressions at various depths, a stratigraphic record of the room's occupation: the oldest moths deepest, the newest just below the surface, and on the surface the living moths, resting on the wall, pressing their wings against the damp sediment, not knowing that they are composing their own fossil record in real time.

The geckos too. A gecko that dies on the pipe falls to the floor and sinks. The toad, when it finally dies, will sink. The beetles, the spiders, all of them — they are all pre-fossils, all future stone, all in the process of being archived by a room that does not know it is an archive but simply does what calcium-rich sediment does when organic material rests against it in the presence of warm water and deep time.

---

What lives here, then, is a world in the process of being made — both geologically and ecologically. The room is becoming rock. The creatures are becoming a community. The whole system is young — decades old at most, dating to whenever the pipes were installed, whenever the lights were strung, whenever the first moth found its way down through the first crack and thought, or felt, or simply responded to the signal that said: *here is warmth, here is light, here is enough*.

And the worker — the absent worker, the one with the initials on the clipboard, the one who drinks cold tea and reads pressure gauges and tightens bolts — the worker is part of this ecology too. Not as a predator or prey but as a geological force, an agent of maintenance, the reason the chamber stays open instead of collapsing, the reason the lights stay on (mostly), the reason the heat continues to draw moths down from above. Without the worker the pipes would be swallowed, the lights would fail, the heat would dissipate into the closing earth, and the moths would stop coming, and the geckos would starve, and the toad would sit in its wallow in the dark and grow thinner and thinner until it too was absorbed by the sediment and became just another shape in the stone.

The worker keeps the room alive by keeping the room *a room*. That is what the worker does. That is the work.

And the moths keep the room alive in a different way — by filling it, by being the proof that a space this small and strange and hot and dim can still be found, can still be colonized, can still be made into a place where things live and eat and mate and die and leave their impressions in the soft earth. The moths are the heartbeat of the cistern works. Not the pipes, not the pump, not the diesel engine. The moths. The pale, persistent, innumerable moths, turning in the brownish light, landing on walls that will remember them forever.
