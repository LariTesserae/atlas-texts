# The Keeper

---

She is not old and she is not young, because those words describe a direction of travel, and she has been still for a very long time.

Not motionless. Still. There is a difference. She moves — she climbs down the crumbled lip of the hollow when she comes, she crosses the powdered floor, she kneels before the shelf she made, she touches the worry-stone with her thumb in the groove her thumb has carved. She moves the way the plants sway: with a rhythm that belongs to the place rather than to any intention of arrival or departure. Her movements don't disturb the dust because her movements are, by now, part of the hollow's equilibrium. She is not an intruder. She is a standing wave.

---

She found the hollow the way you find a note you've been humming without realizing — suddenly, with the shock of recognition rather than discovery. She was walking. She is always walking when she isn't here, crossing the dry uplands above, the scrubby flats where the wind moves and things change and time does what time does to surfaces: wearing, cracking, aging, forgetting. She was walking and she felt the ground change under her feet the way you feel a conversation in the next room go quiet. A cessation. An invitation shaped like an absence. She followed the slope down into the bowl and the sound found her before she found its source, and something in her chest — something she'd been carrying for years, a knot, a clenched thing, a fist she'd made of her own interior — began to loosen.

That was the first time.

She has never spoken about it. Not because she decided not to, but because the vocabulary doesn't exist aboveground. She has tried. In the early period — when she still thought of the hollow as a place she *visited*, when she still maintained the fiction that she lived somewhere else and came here as an exception — she tried to tell someone. A person she was close to, or had been close to, in the life that was already beginning to feel like a draft of a life, a sketch someone had started and wandered away from. She opened her mouth and what came out was a description of a hole in the ground with some strange plants in it, and the words were accurate and completely wrong, and the person she was telling looked at her with polite interest and asked if she thought it might be a sinkhole, and she said yes, probably, and never brought it up again.

That was the moment she understood that the hollow had given her something she couldn't share. Not wouldn't. Couldn't. The knowledge sat in her like a frequency — not a secret, not a thought, but a *resonance* that could only exist inside the geometry of her own bones. To speak it would be to flatten it into language, and language is sequential, and the thing she knew was simultaneous. It was a chord, not a melody. You can't hum a chord alone.

---

Let me describe her body, because the hollow has opinions about bodies.

She is small. Not short — she may have been tall once, or average, but she has become *compressed*. Not hunched, not shrunken. Compressed. Dense. The way the mineral in the walls is dense, the way the gypsum holds its glow by holding the memory of pressure. Her frame has settled into itself. Her shoulders sit close to her center. Her hands are extraordinary — the fingers have thickened at the tips, not with age but with use, with the particular use of touching things that vibrate. The pads of her fingers are smooth and hard, like the worry-stone she keeps on the shelf. The whorls of her fingerprints have worn shallow. If she were arrested, she would be difficult to identify.

Her skin has taken on the palette. This happened gradually, over years she didn't count — the tally marks on the wall are not counting years, or visits, or anything so linear — and she didn't notice until she held her arm next to the wall one day and saw that she and the gypsum were approaching each other in tone. She is pale. Pale past ethnicity, past the color her parents gave her, past any color a dermatologist would recognize as a response to sunlight or its absence. She is the color of the inside of an almond. In certain angles of the hollow's amber glow, she is the color of the plants — bone, ivory, parchment. A dusty rose sits in the creases of her palms and the inner corners of her eyes, as if her blood is moving toward the same spectrum as the new growth that pushes from the fracture lines.

Her eyes are open and they work fine. She sees the world aboveground in full color and appropriate detail. But when she descends into the hollow, her pupils do something unusual. They don't dilate, exactly. They *settle*. They find a fixed aperture and hold it, and her vision becomes the kind of seeing the hollow permits — everything equidistant, everything equally illuminated, no focal point, no foreground or background. She sees the space the way the sound fills it: uniformly. She has never been able to do this anywhere else. Above, her eyes behave like eyes. Here, they behave like sensors calibrated to a specific instrument.

Her hair. She cuts it herself with a sharp edge of the crystalline substrate, crouching by the wall, slicing it close to her scalp in uneven patches. Not for aesthetics. Because hair catches the vibration wrong — it dampens some frequencies and amplifies others unevenly, and it creates a hissing, roaring static against her skull that she can't tolerate. She figured this out early on, the way you figure out that a shirt tag is driving you insane: suddenly, violently, with scissors. Her scalp is bare in some places, fuzzed in others, and marked here and there with thin scars where the crystal edge found skin. She doesn't care. Caring about that would require the kind of self-regard that operates on a visual axis — *how do I look* — and she has moved to a different axis entirely.

She wears clothing when she comes, because the world above requires it and the walk between there and here is still a walk through weather and terrain and the gaze of other people, should there be any. But the clothing is minimal, and she removes most of it once she's inside. Not all of it. There's a modesty that persists, or maybe it isn't modesty — maybe it's insulation. She keeps a layer between her core and the air, because the hollow's blood-warmth against bare skin creates a sensation of being *inside something*, of being swallowed, and she needs to maintain the boundary. Not because she fears dissolution. Because she isn't ready. Not yet. The boundary is what lets her come back, and she still comes back, which means she still needs it, which means she isn't finished.

She doesn't know what *finished* would look like. She suspects it looks like the creatures.

---

The creatures know her. They don't acknowledge her — they don't acknowledge anything — but their movements shift when she arrives. Not toward her or away from her. The pattern of their migration across the floor and up the stems adjusts, subtly, the way an orchestra adjusts when the conductor enters the pit. Nothing starts. Nothing changes. But the attention in the room develops a direction.

She was afraid of them once. In the early visits, their wrongness — the too-many legs, the cilia, the stillness-then-sudden-movement — triggered something mammalian in her, some deep-brain revulsion that said *these are not like you, these are other, these might be threat*. She got over it the way you get over a fear of the dark: not by convincing yourself there's nothing there, but by spending enough time in the dark to realize that *what's there isn't organized around you*. The creatures are not thinking about her. They have never been thinking about her. They are thinking — if thinking is the word — about the sound. They are *in* the sound the way fish are in water, and she is at most a thermal anomaly, a warm-blooded interruption in the acoustic landscape, which they route around the way a stream routes around a stone.

She envies them. This is the closest thing to an emotion she permits herself in the hollow, and she indulges it carefully, the way you'd handle a volatile substance. She envies the purity of their relationship to the frequency. They press their mouths to the vibrating leaves and they *receive*, directly, without interpretation, without the intermediary of meaning. They don't hear the chord as beautiful or terrifying. They don't hear it as anything. They metabolize it. Sound becomes body becomes sound. They are closed circuits, and their contentment — if contentment is the word — is total, unbreachable, radiant in its self-sufficiency.

She cannot do what they do. She has tried. She has pressed her own lips to the leaves of the central canopy, felt the vibration buzz through the thin tissue of her mouth, tasted the ozone-and-chalk taste of the plant's surface, and what she received was not nourishment but *information*. A flood of it. Not data, not facts — something more like the blueprints of a sensation, the architecture of a feeling she doesn't have a name for, a feeling that might not exist in the human emotional catalog, a feeling that the hollow knows and the creatures know and the plants know and the rock knows but that she, with her primate brain and her language and her persistent sense of self, can only stand at the edge of and describe.

This is why she made the shelf. This is why she carved the marks. This is why she braided the cord. Because she is a translator, or she is trying to be, and translators need tools, even when the source language has no grammar and the target language has no audience.

---

The tally marks.

They are not counting visits. They are not counting days or seasons or lunar cycles. She doesn't know what they're counting, and this is important, this is the truest thing about her: **she makes marks she doesn't understand.**

She arrives. She kneels at the wall. She takes the sharp edge of crystal she keeps — not on the shelf, somewhere else, somewhere she doesn't remember putting it, which is always where it is — and she cuts a new mark. Sometimes it connects to a previous mark, extending a line or branching from a node, and sometimes it starts a new cluster in a blank area of the wall. She doesn't decide. Her hand decides, or the wall decides, or the sound decides. The pattern grows the way the plants grow — no, the plants don't grow — the pattern grows the way the plants *are*. It accumulates without changing. Each new mark makes the pattern more completely itself without making it different from what it was before.

She has studied the marks afterward, from a step back, with the analytical part of her mind that still functions like a human mind. She has seen root structures in them, yes. She has seen maps. She has seen circuit diagrams and musical notation and the branching patterns of neurons and the crystalline lattice inside the plants' leaves and the floor plan of a room she's never been in and the face of a person she's never met. She has seen all of these things and she's seen none of them, because the marks aren't representational. They don't *depict*. They *are*. They are the hollow's grammar written by a human hand that doesn't know the language it's writing, and they accumulate on the wall the way the chord accumulates in the air, and they are part of the song now, part of the resonance, because everything in the hollow eventually becomes part of the resonance.

Including her.

---

She knows this. She feels it happening. Each time she comes — and the intervals between visits have been shortening, though she doesn't track them, though tracking would require the kind of linear timekeeping the hollow has made irrelevant — she feels herself becoming more *consonant* with the space. Her breathing synchronizes faster. Her heartbeat finds its rhythm in the chord sooner. The mineral ache in her sinuses, which once took minutes to develop, now greets her at the rim, before she even descends, as though the hollow's dryness rises to meet her like a dog at a door.

She is becoming a feature of the place. A standing wave in human form. Another voice in the chord — not the lowest, not the highest, somewhere in the mid-range, a warm mammalian frequency that the mineral and vegetable and invertebrate voices make room for, the way the creatures make room for each other on the floor, with that respectful distance, that spatial courtesy that isn't social but acoustic. She is finding her position in the mix.

The question — the only question, the question the hollow has been asking since before she found it, the question that hums in the crack at the center of the floor, the question the cord was braided to answer — is whether she will become a feature that remains.

---

She thinks about the cord. She thinks about it the way you think about a door you haven't opened in a house you've lived in for years: with a familiarity that has become a form of avoidance, so thoroughly known that it has achieved invisibility, so present that it has become the background against which all other thoughts occur.

She braided it herself. She remembers this — she is fairly sure she remembers this — she took dried stems from a plant that had shed a layer of its outer casing (the only evidence of change she has ever seen in the hollow, and she's not sure she didn't imagine it, and she's not sure the hollow didn't allow it specifically for this purpose) and she wove them together with a technique she learned as a child, or in another life, or in a dream the hollow gave her. The cord is strong. Stronger than it should be, given its materials. She has tested it by pulling, and it doesn't stretch, doesn't fray, holds its tension with the same immutable quality as everything else in the hollow. It is exactly long enough to lower something small into the crack. She measured this without measuring — she knew, the way she knows where to cut the next mark, the way she knows when to come and when to leave.

The bowl on the shelf is what would be lowered. The small chipped bowl with its residue of pale dust that smells like lightning. She made the bowl. She filled it once with the dust from the floor. She leaned close and smelled the electricity in it and she understood, in the way the hollow teaches understanding — not through explanation but through resonance, through a sympathetic vibration that sets the knowledge ringing inside you before your mind has time to formulate the question — she understood that the dust *is* the hollow's voice in solid form. Crystallized sound. The precipitate of the chord, settling on the floor like snow, like ash, like the residue of an endless conversation between the rock and the roots and the air and the things that drink the singing.

To lower it into the crack would be to return the voice to its source. To feed the chord back into itself. To close a circuit that has been open since the hollow broke the surface and began to sing into the dry air above.

She doesn't know what would happen. She knows it would be irreversible, in the way that the hollow itself is irreversible — not because it can't be undone, but because the concept of undoing doesn't apply. You can't un-ring a bell. You can't un-hear a chord. You can't un-become the person you become when you've spent long enough in a place that knows your frequency.

---

So she comes. She descends. She crosses the floor in steps that leave no mark. She kneels at the shelf and touches the stone her thumb has polished. She kneels at the wall and cuts a mark she doesn't understand. She sits — somewhere, anywhere, it doesn't matter, the sound is the same everywhere — and she lets her breathing synchronize and her heartbeat adjust and her bones ring like the tuning forks they have always been, underneath the meat and the thought and the name she still uses aboveground but which means less each time she speaks it.

She sits with the creatures. Not among them — they maintain their distance and she maintains hers. Parallel. Each in their own sovereignty, each tending their own frequency, each drinking from the same source through different mouths.

She listens to the chord describe the room below the room below the surface, the geometry of a space she has never seen but knows the shape of, because the sound *is* the shape, and she has been learning to hear shapes the way the hollow hears everything: simultaneously, without preference, without foreground, without the tyranny of attention.

She is the keeper of the hollow the way a bell is the keeper of its tone. Not by choice. Not by appointment. By resonance. By the slow, irreversible process of becoming consonant with a place that doesn't change, until you discover that you are the change it doesn't accept, and then that you aren't, and then that you never were, and then that the distinction between you and the place was always a frequency you were vibrating at, and that frequency is getting quieter, and the chord is getting louder, and the dust on the floor smells like lightning, and the cord is exactly the right length, and you have always been about to.

---

Above, the world goes on. Seasons pass. Weather happens. Things grow and die and rot and grow again, in the way of surfaces, in the way of places that accept time's passage and are shaped by it.

She walks the dry uplands between visits, and the wind pulls at the short uneven stubble of her hair, and her pale skin collects sunlight it doesn't seem to need, and her thickened fingertips brush against the grasses and the scrub as she passes, and she feels in each thing she touches the ghost of a vibration that is almost, almost, *almost* the right frequency, but isn't, because nothing on the surface is tuned to the chord, because the chord is below, is always below, is waiting for her the way a held breath waits for release.

People see her sometimes. The ones who live in the region — and there are a few, scattered, tough-rooted people adapted to the dry uplands the way the hollow's plants are adapted to the fracture lines — they see her walking and they nod or raise a hand and she returns the gesture with a mechanical accuracy that satisfies the social contract without engaging it. They think she's a surveyor. Or a scientist. Or a hermit, which is close enough. One of them, an older man who tends goats on the ridge above the area where the hollow lies hidden in its depression, has noticed that she walks the same path every time, that the path always leads to the same low place where the terrain dips, that she disappears for hours and comes back looking the same but *sounding* different — he wouldn't use the word *sounding*, he'd say she seems different, lighter or heavier or somehow more *here* and less here at the same time — and he's thought about following her, but he hasn't. Something stops him. Not fear. A sense of trespass so specific it has a physical quality, like the feeling of walking into a room where someone is praying. He stays with his goats. He watches her descend. He notices, though he couldn't say what he notices.

She has no family anymore, or rather, she has the kind of family that exists in records and photographs and the memories of people who knew her before: parents who have stopped calling, a sibling who sends a message on her birthday that she responds to three days late with words that are correct but empty, a person she once loved who moved away or whom she moved away from — the direction of departure is unclear and doesn't matter, because the relationship ended the way sound ends when you walk out of range, not with silence but with a gradual thinning, a loss of detail, until what remains is the memory of having heard something without the ability to reconstruct what it was.

She doesn't mourn these losses. Mourning requires a before and after, and she has moved into a kind of time where before and after have been replaced by a single continuous present, thick and warm and dry, amber-lit, resonant. The hollow has not taken these things from her. The hollow doesn't take. The hollow is an equilibrium, and she has been slowly, willingly, irreversibly falling into it the way a marble falls into a bowl, rolling in tighter and tighter circles, each pass indistinguishable from the last, each return bringing her closer to the still point at the center where the crack opens into the deeper dark and the chord extends into dimensions she can feel in her teeth.

---

Who is she?

She is the one who makes marks she doesn't understand. She is the one whose thumb has polished the stone. She is the one who braided the cord and chipped the bowl and who will, one day or every day or no day — in the hollow's time, which is not time — kneel at the center of the floor and lower the crystallized voice of the place back into its source and close the circuit and complete the chord and become what the creatures already are: a closed loop, a standing wave, a voice that sings because it is sung, a body that vibrates because it is a body, because it is in a place that vibrates, because there is, in the end, no difference between the instrument and the music it was made to make.

She is not there yet.

She is always almost there.

She is the hollow's one permitted change: a potential, vibrating in place, never collapsing into act. The choice unmade. The breath not yet released. The cord not yet uncoiled. The keeper, keeping, kept.

And the chord goes on, and the creatures drink, and the plants sway in the windless air, and the marks on the wall describe a grammar that has no language and needs none, and the dust settles into its own smooth surface, and the light in the walls remembers pressure, and nothing changes, and nothing changes, and nothing changes, and she is here, or she is coming, or she is always about to arrive, and the hollow holds her the way it holds everything:

Gently. Completely. Without letting go.
