The first and oldest resident is the hollow itself — though *resident* is wrong, because it doesn't live *in* anything; it *is* the living. The hollow is the organism. Not metaphorically. The fig that stopped being a fig, the water that rises through peat the color of organ-meat, the root-cage, the moss-skin, the canopy that became a sky — these are not separate things that happen to share a space. They are the body of something that has no name and no nervous system and no intent but which behaves, unmistakably, as a single metabolic event that has been sustaining itself in dynamic equilibrium for longer than the machines have been running, and which responded to the machines the way a throat responds to a new pitch: by reshaping itself, subtly, over years, until the vibration became part of its own voice.

So: the hollow lives there. The hollow is what lives there. Everything else is detail.

But the details are who it is.

**The frog.**

Start with the frog because the frog is the proof.

It is small — you could close your hand around it, though you shouldn't, because its skin is doing something chemically that you don't want on your palm. Brown-green, yes, but the brown is not uniform; it shifts depending on where the frog sits, not chromatophore camouflage exactly, but a slow tonal adjustment over hours, the skin darkening on the root-pad, lightening slightly near the water-sheen, as though the frog is always halfway through the process of becoming whatever surface it's touching. It may not have a species name. It may have had one, once, before its population was isolated in this acoustic envelope and began — not evolving, that's too grand a word for something this small and this steady — *drifting*, generation by generation, toward a call frequency that exists nowhere else because the acoustic niche it fills exists nowhere else.

Its throat pulses. The membrane is translucent when inflated, and you can see the architecture of it — tiny blood vessels, the shadow of the hyoid bone, the pink of living tissue stretched to its resonant limit — and the sound it produces is not what you'd call beautiful. It is *precise*. A glassy, clicking note, almost digital in its sharpness, slotted into the narrow silence between the second and third harmonics of the engine-drone like a word inserted into someone else's sentence. The frog does not know it is doing this. The frog's great-great-great-great-grandparents did not know they were doing this. But somewhere in the lineage, the frogs whose calls overlapped with the machine frequencies were not heard by mates, and the frogs whose calls found the gaps *were*, and so the population tuned itself — not to the forest, not to the rain, not to the old sounds of predator and prey, but to the interstitial silences of an industrial process they have never seen and cannot comprehend.

The frog sits. It calls. It waits. Another frog answers from six meters away, its note identical in frequency but offset by a quarter-beat, so that the two calls interleave and produce a rhythm that has the cadence of speech without any of the content. They have been doing this since before dawn. They will do this after dark. The frog eats whatever small soft-bodied things crawl across the root-pad — mites, springtails, the larvae of flies that breed in the seep-water — and it eats them without moving its body, only its tongue, a flick so fast it doesn't interrupt the rhythm of its calling. It is, in its way, the most committed resident: the one whose very physiology has been rewritten by the conditions of this place, the one who could not survive anywhere else because anywhere else the acoustic gaps it sings into do not exist.

If you took this frog to a quiet pond, it would call and call and its call would sound strange — too sharp, too narrow, a signal designed for a noise floor that isn't there — and no mate would answer, because the mates are here, in the root-hollows, tuned to the same absent chord.

The frog lives in the throat. The frog *is* throat. It is a vocal organ of the hollow, vibrating at the frequency the hollow needs to hear itself.

**The beetles.**

There are perhaps forty of them visible at any given time, which means there are hundreds you cannot see, inside the wood, beneath the bark, in the sapwood galleries where they spend most of their lives doing things that amount to: eating, mating, and tapping.

The tapping. You have to be still for a long time to hear it, because it is quiet, and because the drone absorbs quiet sounds the way dark fabric absorbs light. But if you press your ear to a root — your cheekbone against the wet wood, your body low, your breathing slowed — you hear them. A rapid, arhythmic clicking, dozens of individuals producing signal simultaneously, the composite sound like rain on a tin roof heard from very far away. They are longhorns, or something in the longhorn lineage, their bodies heavy and cylindrical, their elytra the matte brown-black of old lacquer, their antennae absurdly long, sweeping in slow arcs as they walk, reading the chemical and vibrational landscape of the root-surface with organs that are, in functional terms, a combination of nose, ear, and fingertip.

What they are tapping about is not entirely clear. Some of it is mating — males signaling quality, females signaling receptivity, the whole Morse-code courtship that cerambycids have been running since the Cretaceous. Some of it is territorial, one beetle saying *this gallery is mine* to another beetle three centimeters away. But some of it — and this is the part that would interest an acoustician if an acoustician could get down here on their knees in the warm water and stay long enough — some of it appears to be *responsive*. Responsive to the drone. There are patterns in the tapping that correlate with the machine vibration, not mimicry exactly, but a kind of call-and-response, as though the beetles are treating the engine-drone as a signal from a very large, very persistent beetle and are answering it with the only vocabulary they have.

This may be meaningless. This may be an artifact of shared substrate — the same vibrations that carry the machine sound also carry the beetle taps, and the two simply interfere and produce coincidental patterns. But it may also be that the beetles, over generations, have incorporated the machine vibration into their communication the way they incorporate the vibrations of the tree itself — as background, as context, as the tonal ground against which their own signals are figure. The drone is part of their world. They drum against it the way a musician plays against a drone note: not fighting it, not ignoring it, but using it as a foundation.

The beetles' larvae are in the roots. Deep in. Eating the heartwood in slow spirals that take months to complete, their frass packed behind them in the galleries, their bodies pale and C-curved and soft, and if you could cut open a root you would find it honeycombed — not weakened, because the tree long ago compensated by thickening, by growing more root, by becoming the architectural excess it now is — but *inhabited*, a residential infrastructure, an apartment complex in cross-section, each gallery a life, each life a small patient commitment to the conversion of wood into beetle.

They have been here for generations. The grooves their adult feet have worn into the bark are millimeters deep. The tree has grown around some of them — there are beetles fossilized in the outer bark, caught in the slow tide of growth, their exoskeletons preserved in the living wood like insects in amber, except the amber is still growing.

**The millipede.**

One. There is one. Or there appears to be one — it's possible that there are several identical millipedes running the same circuit at different times, a relay team, a shift-work arrangement, but you never see more than one, and it is always moving in the same direction (counterclockwise, if you're looking down), and it never stops.

It is the size of a thumb. Longer, actually — the length of a hand, if you include the full segmented body and the trailing telson. Its color is the color of wet ironwood: a deep red-brown that is almost purple in the filtered non-light, with a sheen that suggests either wax or metal. The sheen is real. The cuticle is mineralized — calcium carbonate, possibly with trace iron from the seep-water, laid down in the exoskeleton the way nacre is laid down in a shell, giving the animal a surface hardness that is unusual for its kind and that produces, when you look closely, a faint iridescence, a nacreous play of color at the edges of each segment where the plates overlap.

Its legs are a hypnotic cascade. There are, conservatively, two hundred of them, and they move in metachronal waves — the wave passing from back to front, each leg lifting and placing with a precision that is mechanical in the original sense of the word, *of or relating to machines*, and the comparison is not accidental, because the millipede *is* a machine in the same way the engines are machines: a system that converts energy into repetitive motion for a purpose that, from outside, looks like nothing more than continuation.

The track it leaves is a faint wet stripe, barely visible, a secretion from glands along its ventral surface that serves multiple functions: territorial marking, antifungal defense, traction on the mossy surface. The secretion has a smell — you caught it, the thin chemical sharpness — that is somewhere between formic acid and green walnut, astringent and vegetal, and it fades within minutes as the humidity breaks it down, which is why the millipede must keep moving, must keep refreshing the circuit, because the circuit *is* the territory and the territory only exists as long as it is being actively inscribed.

The millipede does not eat in the hollow, or if it does, it eats things too small to see — the film of algae on the root-surfaces, the bacteria in the water-sheen, the detrital paste of decomposing moss. It may eat elsewhere and return here to circuit. It may eat constantly, so slowly and so finely that eating and walking are the same activity. Its mandibles work continuously, a small grinding motion visible if you bring your face close enough to feel its antennae brush your skin, which it will allow, because it does not deviate from the circuit, and if you are in the circuit, it will walk across you, over your knuckle or along your forearm, its two hundred legs pricking gently through the film of warm water on your skin, and it will not pause, and you will feel the faint vibration of its body and realize that it, too, is resonating — the whole mineralized tube of it ringing faintly with the engine-drone, a sympathetic vibration, the millipede as tuning fork, carrying the machine-sound along its body the way the roots carry it through the ground.

**The thing above.**

You saw a foot. A hand. A tail. You aren't sure which. It was dark-furred, the fur dense and short, and it moved with the quality of deliberation that distinguishes arboreal mammals from arboreal reptiles — the careful, tested placement of weight, the grip adjusted before the shift, the whole body held in a state of controlled tension that reads, from below, as patience.

It is watching you.

It has been watching you since before you found the gap between the buttress roots. It watched you approach. It watched you kneel. It watched you settle into the warm water and tilt your head to listen to the drone, and it has not moved since, except to shift its weight once — the rustle you heard — to get a better angle.

It might be a civet. Something in the civet lineage — the right size, the right movement vocabulary, the right combination of arboreal competence and ground-readiness. But it is dark, uniformly dark, without the spots or bands that most civets carry, and its face — you catch a glimpse, two points of reflected non-light, the eyes — is foreshortened in a way that suggests something else, something more towards primate, a loris perhaps, or a colugo, or something that has no clear analogue because it, like the frog, has been here long enough to become specific to here.

It comes to the hollow at dusk. Or it is always in the hollow and simply becomes *visible* at dusk, when it moves from the deeper root-lattice to the lower branches and begins its night. It eats fruit, possibly — there are droppings on the root-surfaces that contain seeds, small ones, from plants that fruit in the understory. It eats insects, probably — the beetles would be available, nutritious, and slow enough to catch. It drinks from the seep, lowering its face to the water-sheen with a delicacy that doesn't disturb the surface tension, its tongue a small dark flicker.

But mostly it watches.

This is not anthropomorphism. This is description. The animal spends a disproportionate amount of its time in a posture of observation — head oriented toward movement, eyes open, body still, in a state that is neither hunting nor hiding but simply *attending*. It watches the frog call. It watches the beetles tap. It watches the millipede circuit. It watches the water shiver with the engine-drone. It watches you.

It is possible that this behavior is nothing more than vigilance — a prey animal scanning for threats in a place where threats are rare, its alertness a vestige of more dangerous times. It is possible that it is something more complex — an animal with enough cognitive surplus, in this stable, foodrich, predator-poor pocket, to engage in something that resembles curiosity, or contemplation, or the simple mammalian pleasure of watching things happen.

It is possible that it is the closest thing this hollow has to a mind. Not a governing mind, not a conscious mind, but a *reflective* surface — an organism complex enough to take in the whole scene, the frog and the beetle and the millipede and the drone and the orchid and the warm water and the green lung-light, and hold it, in some dim neurological way, as a single impression. The hollow, experiencing itself, through the dark eyes of a small furred animal in the roots.

When you leave, it will watch you go. It will not follow. It will shift its weight back into the deeper lattice, and the rustle will be the last sound you hear from it, and it will still be there tomorrow, and the day after, and the day after that, watching the same things happen in the same order with the same attention, because this place does not change, and the watching does not get old, and there is nowhere else that sounds like this, and the animal knows it the way the frog knows its frequency: not as knowledge, but as the shape of its life.

**The orchid.**

One. There is one. You said it was at eye level if you're kneeling, wedged into a root-crevice, and this is true but incomplete. The orchid is not *in* the crevice. The orchid has *become* the crevice. Its roots — thin, green-grey, photosynthetic, velamen-coated — have grown into every crack and pore of the root-surface for a radius of fifteen centimeters around the visible plant, so that what you see (a few leaves, a single flowering spike) is the smallest fraction of what's there. The bulk of the orchid is infrastructure: root-network, mycorrhizal partnership, the whole hidden architecture of nutrient acquisition that allows a single flowering spike to bloom without interruption in a place where the light is so processed it barely qualifies as light.

The flower is small. White, or nearly white — there's a faint lavender blush at the lip, and the column has a yellow throat, and the whole thing is maybe two centimeters across. It is beautiful in the way that small precise things are beautiful: not spectacular, not showy, but *resolved*, every petal exactly the shape it needs to be, every proportion settled.

It is pollinated at night.

The moth comes when the drone shifts — not in pitch, not in volume, but in some quality of electromagnetic emission that accompanies the mechanical vibration, a fluctuation in the field that the moth detects with organs that may be in its antennae or may be in its thorax or may be distributed across its whole body in a way that hasn't been described. The moth is small, grey, nondescript. Its flight path, if you could map it, would describe complex curves — the Lissajous figures you mentioned — because it is navigating by a field that oscillates in two axes simultaneously, and its body follows the interference pattern, spiraling in toward the flower on a trajectory that looks random but is, in fact, the only path the overlapping fields allow.

The moth drinks. The moth carries pollen. The moth leaves. The flower, pollinated, begins the slow process of setting seed, and simultaneously, at the base of the spike, a new bud swells, and the relay continues — one flower replacing the last, the same spike producing bloom after bloom in a sequence so regular that the orchid might as well be a clock, its flowers the ticking, each tick separated by exactly the time it takes for a moth to find it in the dark by following a path shaped by machines it will never see.

**The ants.**

Last because they are most pervasive. They are the circulatory system.

The trails are worn into the bark: grooves, actual grooves, the accumulated erosion of millions of tiny tarsal claws over years, decades, possibly longer. The grooves are deepest on the main trunk-roots — the arterial routes — and branch into finer and finer paths as they extend into the root-lattice, a fractal transportation network that looks, in plan view, like a diagram of blood vessels or river tributaries or the branching of the roots themselves. The ants have recapitulated the tree's own architecture in miniature, their roads mirroring the roots they travel on, network nested in network.

They carry leaf fragments downward — into the soil, into chambers you can't see, where the fragments are processed by fungus the ants cultivate, a mutualism so old it predates the machines by millions of years. They carry fungal bodies upward — food, distributed to the colony, to the queen somewhere in the deeper earth, to the larvae in their nursery chambers that may be below the waterline, sealed in air pockets the ants maintain by some engineering feat of wax and soil and collective will.

They do not deviate. The trails are fixed. The grooves are permanent. An ant that steps off the trail is lost — not metaphorically but literally, unable to find the pheromone path, wandering until it starves or is predated or stumbles back onto a groove by chance. The trails are the colony's memory, its map, its identity, laid down in chemistry and erosion and maintained by the constant passage of bodies, ten thousand tiny purposeful footsteps on wood, each footstep deepening the groove by an immeasurable fraction, each fraction adding up, over ant-generations, to a road that is as real and as permanent as the root it's carved into.

The ants interact with everything. They harvest the moss — not destroying it, but trimming it, keeping it at the thickness it will always be, the thickness that is equilibrium. They tend the orchid's roots — or appear to, patrolling the root-network, removing parasites or competing fungi, though whether this is mutualism or coincidence is unclear. They avoid the millipede — its secretion is deterrent — and they avoid the frog — too large, too wet, too outside their domain — and they tolerate the beetles, moving around them on the root-surfaces with the fluid efficiency of traffic flowing around a parked vehicle.

They are aware of the drone. They must be. Their antennae are vibration-sensitive, and the root-surfaces they walk on are vibrating constantly, and they navigate partly by substrate vibration, which means they are navigating, always, through a world that hums — a world in which every surface sings the same low chord, and the ants' own signals (tapping, stridulation, the vibrations they produce by drumming their gasters against the wood) must be calibrated to cut through it, to be distinguishable from background, to say *food here* or *danger here* or *this way home* in a language spoken over a noise floor that never stops.

They have calibrated. Of course they have. Everything here has calibrated. That is what lives in this place: calibration itself. The ongoing, ancient, patient process of adjustment by which living things find the gaps in the noise and fill them, find the rhythm in the vibration and match it, find the warmth in the water and settle into it and call it home.

**You.**

You kneel in the water. You are warm. The drone is in your chest. The frog calls. The millipede comes around again, a dark gleaming parenthesis on the moss, and your hand is in its path and it walks across your knuckles without pausing, two hundred legs like the tines of a tiny comb drawn across your skin, and you feel the engine in it, the symbiotic tremor, and for a moment you are part of the circuit — the millipede's territory includes you, the frog is calling in the gap above your breathing, the ants are routing around your knees, the dark thing above is watching you with eyes that hold the green non-light like small still pools.

You live here now. Not permanently. Not in any way that will leave a mark. But for this moment, in this breath, in this pulse of the drone that has become indistinguishable from your own heartbeat, you are a resident. The hollow has accepted you the way it accepted the machines: without rejection, without absorption, but with incorporation — your warmth added to its warmth, your breathing added to its breathing, your small human attention added to the vast distributed attention of the beetles and the ants and the dark-furred watcher and the orchid-clock and the frog-chorus and the seep and the roots and the ancient, ancient fig that stopped pretending to be one thing and became, instead, the living architecture of a throat that has been humming to itself, in the dark, in the warm water, in the space between the engines and the silence, for longer than you can imagine, and will continue humming after you crawl back out through the gap between the buttress roots and stand up into the ordinary light and walk away, carrying the drone in your bones for hours, for days, like a song you can't stop hearing, like a frequency you didn't know you were tuned to until you knelt down in the warm dark water and the throat closed gently around you and sang.
