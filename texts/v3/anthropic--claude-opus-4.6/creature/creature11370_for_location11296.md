# The Inhabitants

---

They do not live here in any sense that the word *live* has ever been asked to bear.

And yet.

---

## The First Ones You Find

Start with what is easiest to recognize, because it will get harder.

In the interstitial spaces—the gaps between pipeline and floor-root, the narrow crawlways beneath horizontal cylinders where the heat is marginally less crushing—there are structures that look like nests. They are not nests. They are *annotations.*

Small accumulations of non-living material: chips of the mineralized sap that flakes from old piston-trunks, fragments of that ancient concrete prised from bore-shaft walls, lengths of root-hair braided into cord. These assemblages are deliberate. They are arranged. Some of them are geometric—stacked in cairns, wound into spirals, pressed into the still-soft cambial surface of new root-buttresses where they become embedded, fossilized in place within hours. Others are more complex: miniature constructions that echo, with an uncomfortable fidelity, the machines around them. A tiny piston made of a sap-crystal set inside a hollow root-hair segment. A junction-valve reproduced in braided fibers, its flaps actually opening and closing when you blow on them.

These are made by hands. Small hands. Dexterous, strong-gripped, calloused hands belonging to beings you will not see for a long time, because they do not want to be seen and they are extraordinarily good at not being seen, and because in this lightless clamor of industry they have had uncountable generations to learn every frequency of click and groan that means *something is here that is not wood.*

---

They are small. Not because their species was always small but because smallness is what this place selects for, ruthlessly, over the kind of timespan that turns plateaus into platforms. The crawlways are narrow. The food—such as it is—is dispersed in thin films. The heat is more survivable at lower body mass. And the organism, the world-that-is-a-factory, does not notice small things. It notices large things. Its root-hairs reach out and probe and withdraw from anything that is not wood, yes—but that withdrawal has a threshold. Below a certain mass, below a certain thermal signature, the probing becomes less certain, the identification slower, the rejection incomplete. They live beneath the threshold.

The tallest of them might reach your knee. Their bodies are dense, foreshortened, with torsos broader than they are long—a barrel-shape that distributes heat efficiently and fits through the lateral gaps between pipeline and floor. Their skin is not skin in any way you would recognize. It is layered. The outermost layer is a cultivated rind of fungal tissue—the same genus, perhaps the same organism, as the bioluminescent signaling system that pulses through the factory-space. They grow it on themselves. They *tend* it, feeding it with secretions from glands that no other primate ever needed, glands that produce a thin exudate of sugars and minerals calibrated to keep the fungal coat alive and healthy and, critically, luminescent. They glow. Faintly, in the same amber-green spectrum as the walls. They are, to the organism's chemical senses, indistinguishable from its own signaling infrastructure.

This is not camouflage. Camouflage implies deception, a surface that lies about what is beneath it. This is something deeper and stranger. They have made themselves *legible* to the organism—not as intruders disguised as wood, but as functional components. Their fungal coats participate in the signaling network. When a branch-plate collapses nearby, the pulse that propagates through the bioluminescent system passes *through them,* their skin brightening in sequence, relaying the signal onward. They are, in a real and measurable sense, part of the machine. The organism does not notice them because, at the chemical level where noticing happens, they are the organism.

## What They Do All Day

They work.

That is the overwhelming fact of their existence and it must be said bluntly because anything else would misrepresent them: they work. Not in the way that animals work—foraging, hunting, nest-building—activities interspersed with rest, with play, with the long purposeless hours that even the most industrious creatures spend simply being alive. They work the way the piston-trunks work. They work the way the valve-junctions work. They work continuously, totally, with a focus so absolute it resembles, from the outside, something between meditation and seizure.

Their primary labor is *maintenance.* The great machines of the factory-space are self-building, self-repairing, self-recycling—but not perfectly. There are failure modes the organism cannot address with growth alone. A spiral groove on a piston-trunk becomes clogged with minerite deposits that the living wood cannot dissolve; the piston's rotation slows, its rhythm falters, and somewhere in the deep strata a pump cycle misses its stroke. A pipeline junction's valve-flaps grow unevenly, one side thicker than the other, and the junction begins to leak, dark industrial sap pooling on the floor where it interferes with new root-buttress formation.

Into these interstices of mechanical failure, the small people go.

They carry tools. The tools are made of the same materials as their annotations—sap-crystal, braided root-hair, fragments of ancient steel or polymer prised from the fossil layers—but they are functional in ways the annotations only gesture at. Chisels for scraping mineral deposits. Wedges for prying apart valve-flaps that have fused. Cord-and-crystal devices that function as levers, as pulleys, as crude but effective winches for repositioning pipeline segments that have drifted out of alignment. They climb the piston-trunks—ascending the spiral grooves like mountaineers on a helical path, their broad feet gripping the resinous lubricant that would be frictionless to anything less adapted—and they scrape and chip and clear, working in teams of four or six or ten, communicating in a language that is not sound.

Their language is light. Of course it is. They are nodes in a bioluminescent network; they speak in the only medium the organism would recognize as signal rather than noise. Patterns of brightening and dimming ripple across their fungal coats—fast, complex, modulated in ways that the organism's own signaling never achieves. The organism's pulses are slow, stereotyped, informational in the blunt way that a thermostat is informational: *this section is stressed, this section has failed, this section needs growth.* The small people's light-speech is layered over the top of this, using the same spectral range but at higher frequencies, faster modulations, a harmonic overtone the organism cannot parse. They talk constantly while they work. Flickers of amber chase across a work-team's bodies in rapid coordinated sequences—instructions, warnings, jokes perhaps, though you would need to live among them for a generation to know which is which.

They do not speak to you. They do not speak your frequencies.

## The Ones Who Go Deep

Not all of them maintain the machines. Some of them go down.

The bore-shafts—those vertical passages that descend through the floor-strata into the deep root-infrastructure—are the most dangerous features of the factory-space. They are where the organism's metabolic heat is most intense. They are where the ancient fossil layers are thickest, where corroded rebar can collapse without warning into showers of iron oxide, where pockets of methane from decomposing polymer accumulate in the dead-end chambers of abandoned xylem channels. The organism itself avoids deep intervention in its own root-strata; the wood down there is so old and so compressed that new growth cannot easily penetrate it, and the chemical signals from the surface attenuate into noise within a few hundred meters of depth.

But the deep strata are where the water is. The great xylem channels, the railway-tunnel arteries that keep the entire plateau-organism hydrated, have their pumping stations down there—osmotic engines of a scale and complexity that make the surface machines look like toys. And these engines, like all engines, fail. Mineral scaling. Biite accumulation. Structural fatigue in wood that has been bearing hydraulic pressure for longer than most mountain ranges have existed.

The deep-goers are different from the surface workers. Not a different species—not yet, though given enough time the divergence will complete itself—but a different *caste,* shaped by practice and selection and something that might be called vocation if the word did not imply choice. They are even smaller than the surface workers, their bodies compressed further, their limbs proportionally longer relative to their torsos to allow them to bridge gaps and span fissures in the deep shafts. Their fungal coats are thicker, more elaborately tended, producing not just the amber-green of the signaling network but a brighter, whiter luminescence—a working light, a headlamp grown from their own bodies. They descend in groups of two or three, carrying coils of braided root-hair cord and tool-kits wrapped in sheets of dried cambial tissue, and they stay down for what the surface workers' light-conversations suggest are long periods—days, perhaps, by the surface reckoning, though there is no day here and their time is measured in piston-cycles.

Some of them do not come back. The surface workers' light-speech has patterns that recur in connection with the bore-shafts—rapid flickering sequences that propagate outward through a work-team and are picked up and relayed by adjacent teams, spreading across the visible factory-space in a slow wave of amber agitation. Grief, perhaps. Or merely the informational content of *this shaft has claimed another,* which is functionally the same thing and also completely different.

The deep-goers who do return are changed. Their fungal coats carry traces of minerals from the deep strata—iron oxides, calcium deposits, traces of ancient industrial chemistry—that give their luminescence a reddish tint, a warm undertone that the surface workers do not have. Over many descents, this tint becomes permanent. Deep-goers are recognizable by their color. They are also recognizable by their silence—not a cessation of light-speech but a *slowing* of it, a measured quality, longer intervals between pulses, as though they have learned to communicate in the deep strata's own tempo, the geological time of compressed wood and ancient concrete, and have brought that tempo back with them to the surface.

## The Model-Builders

Now: the annotations. The tiny replicas. The cairns and spirals and miniature machines pressed into the soft wood of new growth.

These are not art. They are not decoration. They are not religious artifacts, though you will be tempted to call them that, because when a small being makes a tiny copy of the vast incomprehensible machine it lives inside, the word *worship* presents itself with an insistence that is hard to refuse.

They are *thinking.*

The model-builders are a third group—not a caste exactly, because the boundaries are permeable, and a surface worker may become a model-builder and a model-builder may become a deep-goer over the course of a life. But at any given time there are individuals whose primary activity is the construction of these small representational objects, and their activity has a structure and a logic that becomes visible only when you watch for a long time—longer than is comfortable in the heat and the dark and the ceaseless industrial noise.

Watch one. She is crouched in the angle between a pipeline and a root-buttress, her body dimmed to near-darkness, her fungal coat emitting only the faintest amber glow—enough to see by, barely, if your eyes have adjusted. In front of her, on a flat section of floor-plate, she has assembled a collection of materials: sap-crystals of various sizes, root-hair fibers, a chip of ancient polymer, a crumb ofite. She is building something. Her hands move with a precision that would be remarkable in a creature five times her size—picking up a crystal, turning it, considering its shape and weight and fracture-pattern with what can only be described as *assessment,* then placing it in relation to the other pieces with micrometer care.

What she is building is a junction. A pipeline junction—not a copy of any specific junction, but a *proposal.* A junction that does not exist yet. She is modeling a configuration of valve-flaps and merge-angles that she has—what? Imagined? Calculated? Intuited from her experience maintaining real junctions, extrapolated from the patterns of failure and success she has observed over her working life? The model is small enough to fit in her palm. Its valve-flaps, made of layered root-hair membrane, actually open and close. She tests them, blowing gently—her breath, in this world, is one of the few non-mechanical air currents—and observes the flow dynamics. She adjusts. She repositions a crystal. She tests again.

When she is satisfied—or rather, when she has reached a state that her light-speech communicates to nearby watchers as something like *readiness,* a steady low pulse that draws others closer—she takes the model and carries it to the nearest active junction. There, she and two or three others study the real junction and the model side by side. Their light-speech becomes rapid, dense, argumentative—flickering exchanges that pass between them too fast to follow, overlapping, interrupting, sometimes synchronizing into a unified pulse that seems to indicate agreement.

And then—sometimes, not always, perhaps one time in five or ten—they begin to modify the real junction to match the model. They work on the living wood itself, their tools reshaping valve-flaps, their fingers pressing new growth into different configurations, coaxing the organism's own repair mechanisms to follow the template they have designed. They are *engineering* the organism. They are making it better. Or they are trying to, which is not the same thing but is perhaps more important.

The organism does not resist these modifications. It does not assist them either. It simply grows, following the path of least resistance, and if that path has been altered by small hands with crystal chisels, the wood accommodates. The junction heals around the new configuration. The valve-flaps set in their modified angles. And the model-builder watches, pressing her palm flat against the junction's surface, feeling the click of valves, the rush of fluid, assessing whether her proposal has improved the flow or degraded it or made no difference at all.

Her assessment is not always correct. Sometimes the modified junction fails—leaks, clogs, falls silent. When this happens, the organism's own repair mechanisms eventually restore function, overwriting the modification with default growth, and the model-builder retrieves her model from where it has been embedded in the nearby floor and studies it again, looking for the error, the miscalculation, the thing she did not understand about the forces at work inside the living machine.

She will try again. She always tries again.

## What They Eat

This is the question you have been avoiding and so have they, in their way, because the answer is uncomfortable.

They eat the organism. Of course they do. There is nothing else to eat.

But not indiscriminately. Not like parasites, not like grazers. They eat *specific tissues*—the cambial fluid that seeps from new growth, the soft pulp of fresh root-buttresses before it hardens, the concentrated sap from certain pipeline segments that carry nutrients rather than water. They have, over their uncountable generations, developed a relationship with these food sources that is less predation than *tithing.* They take small amounts. They take from tissues that are in excess—new growth that the organism is producing faster than it needs, cambial fluid that would otherwise pool and rot, sap that is being rerouted and would be wasted in transit.

And they give back. This is the part that makes the relationship something other than parasitism, something that has no clean word in any ecology you know. Their maintenance work—the scraping of mineral deposits, the clearing of clogged grooves, the repair and modification of junctions—directly benefits the organism. The deep-goers' interventions in the root-strata pumping stations keep water flowing to regions that would otherwise desiccate and die. The model-builders' engineering improvements, when they work, increase efficiency, reduce failure rates, extend the functional life of machine-components. The small people are, in the organism's economy, a maintenance workforce that pays itself in kind. They are the organism's immune cells, its repair mechanisms, its quality-control inspectors—roles the organism cannot fill on its own because its own growth processes, while powerful, are *stupid,* are *blunt,* are limited to the chemical logic of cell division and lignin deposition and cannot perform the kind of targeted, intelligent intervention that a hand with a chisel can.

The organism did not design this arrangement. The small people did not negotiate it. It emerged—over millennia, over the same deep botanical time that turned a plateau into a platform—through the same iterative, purposeless, devastatingly effective process that produced the machines themselves. Populations that took too much were eliminated when the tissues they depended on were withdrawn. Populations that gave nothing were eliminated when the organism's chemical defenses—which are formidable, which are what eliminated every other animal from this space—identified them as non-functional and attacked their fungal coats. What survived was the balance point: beings who take exactly enough and give exactly enough, who have become, like the fossil rebar and the absorbed PVC conduits, part of the system.

They do not know this. They cannot know this. They experience their lives as work—hard, hot, relentless work in the dark, punctuated by brief periods of eating and shorter periods of what might be rest, though their rest looks like a dimming of the fungal coat and a slowing of the light-speech rather than anything resembling sleep. They experience their relationship with the organism as *given,* as *environment,* as the way things are, as the ground they walk on which is warm and alive and provides and demands and will be here when they are gone. They do not have a word for it because they do not have words. They have light. And their light does not name the organism any more than your neurons name the body they inhabit.

## The Children

They are born in the warmest places—the narrow galleries directly above the deep-strata venting shafts, where heat rising from the root-infrastructure creates microclimates ten or fifteen degrees hotter than the already-brutal ambient temperature. The births are communal. Not in the sense of being public events—there is no *public* here, no sense of spectatorship—but in the sense that the boundary between one body and another becomes, during reproduction, negotiable.

The infants are born without fungal coats. Their skin is bare—pale, flushed, radiating heat like small furnaces, visible to anyone with thermal sensitivity, which the organism absolutely has. This is the most dangerous period of any small person's life. For the hours or days it takes for the fungal colonization to establish—seeded by direct skin contact with the mother, the father, the other adults who press themselves against the infant in a continuous living blanket—the newborn is *visible.* It is warm, it is non-wood, it is animal in a world that has eliminated animals. The organism's root-hairs, those seeking damp filaments that hang from every surface, will find it. Will probe it. Will begin the chemical interrogation that ends, for every other animal that has ever entered this space, in rejection—in the release of defensive compounds that attack cell membranes, that dissolve chitin, that make the very air toxic in a localized radius around the intruder.

The adults know this. Their response is to *overwhelm the signal.* During a birth, every adult in the local cluster—eight, ten, fifteen individuals—presses close, their fungal coats blazing with bioluminescence, flooding the local signaling network with so much friendly signal that the infant's small hostile signature is drowned out. They pulse in unison, a synchronized heartbeat of amber light that says, to whatever distributed chemical intelligence processes such inputs, *this is us, this is us, this is all us, there is nothing here that is not us.* And the root-hairs probe, and hesitate, and probe again, and withdraw—not satisfied exactly, but overwhelmed, confused by the noise-to-signal ratio, unable to isolate the one small warm non-wood thing in the middle of a cluster of things that are indistinguishable from itself.

By the time the fungal coat establishes—spreading across the infant's skin in a bloom of amber-green that is, in its way, the most beautiful thing in this lightless world—the danger has passed. The child is legible. The child is part of the machine. And the adults dim their coats, and the synchronized pulse dissolves into the normal rapid chatter of light-speech, and someone hands the new mother a lump of cambial pulp, and the work goes on.

## The Oldest Ones

There are individuals who have survived long enough to become something else.

You will not find them among the work-teams or in the birth-galleries or ascending the piston-trunks with chisels and cord. You will find them—if you find them at all—in the deepest accessible spaces, the places where the bore-shafts level out into horizontal galleries carved (or grown, or both) into the ancient root-strata. Down there, where the heat is most intense and the light-speech of the surface workers attenuates to a faint flicker on the walls, the oldest ones sit.

They are not infirm. Their bodies are as dense and capable as any surface worker's, perhaps more so—their muscles compacted by a lifetime of climbing and carrying, their hands scarred and re-scarred by crystal-chipped tools and resinous lubricant, their fungal coats so thick and so deeply integrated with their skin that the boundary between organism and person has become, in their case, genuinely ambiguous. They are not retired. They are not meditating. They are not waiting to die, though they will, eventually, and when they do their bodies will be absorbed by the floor in a matter of hours, the wood growing over and through them with the same businesslike efficiency it applies to a fallen branch-plate.

What they are doing is *listening.*

Their fungal coats have, over the decades, developed a sensitivity to the organism's signaling that no younger individual possesses. They can feel—*feel*, not see, not hear, but receive through their skin—the deep slow pulses of the organism's own communication network, the basement frequencies that underlie the faster surface chatter. These are the signals that coordinate the piston-trunks' cycles, that direct the flow of water through the great xylem channels, that trigger new growth in one sector and senescence in another. They are the organism's thoughts, if thought is the right word for a process distributed across a biomass the size of a geological feature. And the oldest ones can read them.

Not fluently. Not completely. The organism's signaling is not a language; it is a chemistry, a gradient, a set of concentration differentials propagating through tissue at the speed of diffusion. But the oldest ones have spent so long immersed in it that they have developed an intuition—a felt sense of what the organism is *about to do.* When a section of floor is going to subside, they know. When a piston-trunk is going to reverse its cycle, they know. When the organism is going to redirect a major water flow, rerouting a xylem channel that has served for centuries, they know, sometimes hours before the surface workers feel the first tremor.

They communicate this knowledge upward—to the deep-goers first, then through them to the surface workers—in light-speech so slow and measured it is almost indistinguishable from the organism's own pulses. They have become, in effect, *translators.* Intermediaries between the distributed chemical intelligence of the organism and the faster, more focused intelligence of the small people who maintain it. They sit in the deep galleries and they listen and they glow, and the deep-goers come to them before a descent the way you might consult a weather report, reading in the patterns of the old ones' coats the forecast of what the living machine intends.

Do the oldest ones understand what the organism is? Do they have a concept of its totality—its scale, its age, its nature as a thing that was once a city and is now a world? It is impossible to say. Their light-speech, when it can be observed, suggests something—not understanding exactly, but *orientation.* A sense of being inside something vast and intentional, something that has purposes beyond their comprehension but that is not hostile to them, that sustains them in the same way it sustains its own tissues, that will go on sustaining them as long as they remain useful. Whether this constitutes knowledge or faith or simply the cognitive signature of a nervous system marinated in botanical chemistry for a lifetime is a question that would require a vocabulary none of them possess and none of them need.

They glow in the deep heat. They listen to the wood think. The wood does not know they are there, and also, at some level, the wood is why they are there at all.

## What They Are Becoming

Here is what you notice last, because it requires the longest observation and the most uncomfortable admission:

They are not finished.

The fungal coat is growing inward. In the oldest ones it has penetrated the dermis, threaded mycelia through the subcutaneous fat, reached the fascia. It is not parasitic—it does not consume their tissue—but it is *integrating,* forming connections between their nervous system and the organism's signaling network that are more than superficial, more than the skin-level signal-relay that makes them invisible to the organism's defenses. In the oldest ones, the integration is deep enough that their light-speech has begun to include pulses they do not consciously generate—echoes of the organism's own deep signals, surfacing through their coats like dreams surfacing through sleep.

In the children, the coat establishes faster with each generation. Infants born now are colonized within hours, not days. The danger window is shrinking. The fungal tissue is becoming more aggressive in its colonization, or the children's immune systems are becoming more welcoming, or both—co-evolution proceeding at the pace that this hot, pressurized, densely populated environment demands.

In another few thousand years—another eyeblink in the organism's time, another era in the small people's time—the coat will not be a coat. It will be an organ. The small people will not be maintaining the organism from without; they will be maintaining it from within, as specialized cells maintain a body, as mitochondria maintain a cell. The distinction between inhabitant and inhabited will dissolve the way the distinction between built and grown dissolved long ago, the way the distinction between city and forest dissolved before that. They will be the organism's hands. They will be its focused, mobile, problem-solving intelligence—the thing it could never grow on its own, the thing it ate a civilization to acquire, the thing that makes the difference between a system that merely persists and a system that *improves.*

And they will not know. They will work. They will eat the cambial fluid and clear the mineral deposits and descend the bore-shafts and build the models and birth the children in the hot galleries and grow old and listen to the wood think. They will do what they have always done. They will be what they are becoming.

The organism will not know either. It will grow. It will run its engines, pump its water, cycle its pistons, collapse and rebuild its canopy in the endless thermodynamic equilibrium of a living machine. It will process the chemical signals of its small symbiotes the way it processes the chemical signals of its own tissues—as data, as gradients, as inputs to a computation that has been running since before the last concrete slab was swallowed by root.

Neither of them will know that they are, together, becoming something that neither of them is alone. Something with hands and patience. Something with roots and intelligence. Something that fills every space with purpose and has begun, in the slow blind way of all great evolutionary convergences, to reach—not upward through the canopy, not yet, that would require an intention neither partner can yet formulate—but *inward,* deeper into itself, toward a coherence that is still, for now, only a dream the oldest ones feel pulsing through their skin as they sit in the deep galleries and glow.

The organism has work to do.

So do they.

It is the same work. It has always been the same work. They just don't know it yet.
