# Who Lives on the Plateau

---

They are tall, and this is the first thing you notice, but it is not the most important thing.

You see them through the walls of the bridges—dark silhouettes moving upward, always upward first, as if the day begins with ascent. They climb to the highest occupied floors of their towers before descending to wherever they actually need to be, and this is not ritual in the way you might assume. It is not worship or meditation, though it resembles both. It is something closer to *orientation*. They go up to feel the cloud layer close around the building's peak, to stand in that zone where the walls become damp with direct condensation, where the structure's nodules are working hardest and the capillary networks hum faintly in the walls like blood in the ear during a held breath. They go up to know where they are in relation to the ceiling of the world. Then they come down, and their movements for the rest of the day carry a quality of *placement*—each action performed by someone who knows exactly how much sky is above them.

They are tall. Narrow-shouldered. Their limbs are long relative to their torsos in a way that reads as graceful only after you stop expecting human proportions, and even then *graceful* isn't right—*efficient* is closer, or *resolved*, the way a suspension bridge is resolved, every element under exactly the tension it was designed for. Their skin ranges from a deep umber to a gray so cool it approaches the color of the basalt itself, and in the flat, shadowless light of the plateau, this can make them difficult to distinguish from their surroundings at a distance. They do not camouflage. They simply *belong* to the palette. Their eyes are large and set slightly wider than you'd expect, giving them a field of vision that must approach panoramic, though they tend to focus with great deliberateness, turning their heads to fix on something with both eyes when it warrants real attention. The irises are pale—silvers, light ambers, occasionally a washed-out blue-green that catches the directionless light and holds it. These eyes are adapted to the overcast. They would find direct sunlight uncomfortable, probably painful. They are built for this world of even illumination, for reading fine differences in a narrow band of gray.

Their hands are the second thing you notice. Long-fingered, yes, but also *articulate* in a way that goes beyond dexterity. Each finger seems to operate with a degree of independence you don't see in human hands—not prehensile in the way a primate's foot is prehensile, but capable of a kind of distributed attention, as if each digit can be tasked separately while the hand as a whole performs a unified action. You see this when they work on the building material, coaxing it, shaping it during its slow self-healing process, their fingers spread across a surface and each one pressing or stroking at a slightly different rhythm. The material responds to them. Not instantly, not dramatically, but over the course of minutes you can see it flowing slightly differently where a hand has been, the molecular structure accepting a suggestion. They are not engineers in the sense of people who operate tools. They are more like—the word that comes is *conductors*, but that implies an orchestra that plays on command, and this is more collaborative. They suggest. The material considers. Usually it agrees.

---

Their communities are small, and not because they cannot sustain more.

A typical settlement holds perhaps two or three hundred individuals. The towers could house thousands. The infrastructure could support tens of thousands. But they spread themselves thin by choice—or by something deeper than choice, something constitutional, the way a gas expands to fill its container except these beings expand to fill their *landscape*, distributing themselves across the plateau's enormous emptiness as if each one requires a certain volume of silence and stone to function properly.

Within a settlement, they organize not by family in any nuclear sense, but by *project*. This is the closest word, though it fails. A project might be the calibration of a particular thermal exchanger's output over the course of a decade. A project might be the introduction of a new grazer variant to a region of plateau where the biofilm has shifted in composition. A project might be the slow, years-long process of encouraging basalt to weather into something approaching soil at a specific site—cracking it with carefully managed thermal stress, introducing mineral-digesting organisms, monitoring the accumulation of organic material from biofilm die-off, adjusting moisture levels by subtly redirecting the sheeting rainwater with features built into the stone so gently they look like natural undulations. Each project is tended by a group of anywhere from three to thirty individuals, and these groups are the primary social unit. They eat together. They sleep in adjacent quarters within the same tower. They develop, over the course of their collaboration, a shared vocabulary of gestures and tonal references that becomes increasingly opaque to outsiders—not secret, not exclusionary, but simply *specific*, the way any deep expertise generates its own language.

They move between projects over the course of a lifetime. A person might spend twenty years on soil development, then shift to animal ecology, then spend a decade on something that has no obvious practical dimension at all—standing in the warm pools for hours, observing the bioluminescent organisms, occasionally producing a low vocal tone that makes the organisms pulse in response, mapping some relationship between sound and light that may have implications for the settlement's architecture, or for the thermal exchangers' tonal output, or for nothing at all beyond the understanding itself. Nobody distinguishes between these activities in terms of value. The plateau needs soil. The plateau also needs to be *known*, listened to, attended to in ways that don't produce immediate material results. Both are work. Both are how the composition advances.

---

They speak, but speaking is not their primary communication.

Their vocal apparatus can produce a range that extends well below human hearing—into the infrasonic, into the register of the thermal exchangers' hum. When they speak to each other in what you might call conversation, they use a mid-range that is audible to a human listener as a series of fluid, vowel-heavy phrases interspersed with clicks and brief tonal holds. It is not fast. There are pauses that would feel uncomfortable in human dialogue but are clearly *full* here, loaded with meaning or with the deliberate absence of meaning, which is its own kind of communication. They do not interrupt each other. They do not speak simultaneously. When one person is speaking, the others orient toward them with that whole-head, binocular focus, and the quality of their attention is so palpable it seems almost physical, as if listening were a force that could be felt.

But below this, running constantly like a ground bass beneath a melody, there is the infrasonic layer. They produce it in the chest and throat—a felt vibration more than a heard sound, something that registers in the sternum and the jaw, exactly like the tonal pressure from the thermal exchangers. And this is not coincidence. The exchangers' hum is tuned to their communicative range, or their communicative range evolved—or was designed—to operate in the same band as the exchangers. The two systems interpenetrate. When a person stands on the plateau between settlements, alone on the wet stone, they can feel the nearest exchanger's hum in their body, and they can answer it, and the answer propagates through the stone itself—through the dense, fine-grained basalt that transmits low-frequency vibration with startling fidelity—and is felt by other people, in other settlements, tens of kilometers away, as a faint tonal shift in the background hum of their world.

This is not telepathy. It is not instantaneous or high-bandwidth. It is more like—imagine that the ground beneath your feet carried a constant, gentle vibration, and that you could feel, in that vibration, the aggregate emotional state of everyone on the plateau. Not their thoughts. Not their words. But their *presence*, their aliveness, the fact of their bodies resonating against the stone. When someone is distressed, their infrasonic output shifts, and the stone carries that shift, and others feel it as a subtle dissonance in the hum—not alarming, not urgent, but *noticeable*, the way you'd notice a slightly off note in a familiar piece of music. And they respond. Not by rushing to help, not by communicating directly, but by adjusting their own tonal output to compensate, to offer a counterbalance, the way a musician adjusts their intonation to bring a chord back into tune. The plateau, in this sense, is an instrument they all play together, constantly, unconsciously, a collective resonance that binds the scattered settlements into something that functions as a single community despite the vast distances between them.

This is why the settlements are placed where they are—not for resources, not for geography, but for *acoustics*. The architects who positioned them understood the resonant properties of the basalt shelf, the way it channels and reflects low-frequency vibration, and they placed each cluster at a node in the standing wave pattern, a point where the stone's natural resonance amplifies the infrasonic signal. The settlements are notes on a staff. The plateau is the instrument. The people are the music.

---

They live a long time, and they are in no hurry.

Their lifespan is difficult to pin down because they do not track it the way a human would. There are no birthdays, no age-markers, no points at which a person is considered to have transitioned from one life-stage to another. There are physiological changes over time—a gradual deepening of skin color, a slow shift in the resonant frequency of their infrasonic output, a thickening of the fingers that makes their material-shaping work more precise but less delicate—and these changes are acknowledged, referenced, sometimes celebrated with a particular quality of communal attention, but they are not *counted*. Time here is measured in projects. A person has been alive for the duration of two soil developments and a grazer introduction and an ongoing exchanger calibration. A person is old enough to have participated in the founding of a settlement—and this is the closest thing to an honorific this culture produces, not because founding is more prestigious than maintaining, but because it implies a span of experience long enough to encompass the shift from empty stone to inhabited cluster.

The oldest among them—and it is difficult to identify the oldest, because the physiological markers of age plateau after a certain point, and because they do not defer to age in any visible way—the oldest have been on the plateau since the beginning of the project. Since the first thermal exchangers were sunk into the basalt. Since the first towers were grown from mineral feedstock. They remember the plateau before the biofilm, before the grazers, before the fliers. They remember bare stone and silence—true silence, without the tonal hum, without the resonance of other bodies in the rock. They describe this silence not with horror or loneliness but with a kind of awe, the way one might describe having stood in a cathedral before its first note of music was ever played. *The stone was waiting*, they say, or something that translates approximately to that. *We could feel it waiting. The resonant structure was already there, in the geology. We didn't create the instrument. We learned to play it.*

Their children—and they do have children, rarely, with great deliberateness, after a process of communal consideration that can take years—their children are born into a world already humming. They have never known the silence. They are calibrated from birth to the resonance of the plateau, their infrasonic output finding its place in the collective chord before they can speak, before they can walk, while they are still carried against a parent's chest and their small bodies vibrate in sympathy with the adult's tonal output and the stone beneath and the exchanger's hum and the distant calls of the fliers and the whole vast, quiet, ongoing composition that is the Ketharan plateau becoming itself.

---

Their relationship with the animals is not ownership or stewardship, exactly.

The fliers, especially. The great six-limbed gliders that spiral above the thermal columns are not domesticated, not trained, not symbiotic in any strict biological sense. But they are *known*. Each settlement's residents can identify individual fliers by their tonal call—that struck-tuning-fork note carries harmonic information as specific as a fingerprint, and the people track individuals over years, decades, know their flight patterns and territorial ranges and reproductive cycles. When a flier's call shifts—when age or injury alters its harmonics—the people notice, and they adjust, and there is a quality to this noticing that suggests grief, or at least the kind of attention that grief and love share a border with.

The fliers, for their part, are aware of the settlements. They roost on the upper stories of towers that extend into the cloud layer, folding their four secondary limbs against the self-healing walls, their membranous wings draped like wet fabric over the building's nodules, and the people inside can feel them—feel the weight of them through the structure, feel their breathing as a slow rhythm in the building's frame, feel the subsonic register of their resting vocalizations as a bass note that the tower's architecture channels and amplifies until the whole structure rings, very faintly, like a bell that hasn't been struck but is remembering the last time it was.

The people do not go up to disturb them. They do not feed them or tend them. They simply incorporate the fliers' presence into the settlement's tonal ecology—another voice in the chord, another resonance in the stone. When a flier launches from a tower's peak, the sudden absence of its weight and vibration is felt throughout the structure as a momentary silence, a rest in the music, and the people inside pause—not all of them, not consciously, but enough of them that an observer would notice—and then the chord re-establishes itself without the missing note, and the day continues.

The grazers are more peripheral but no less attended to. The mosaic they create on the basalt surface—that shifting pattern of grazed and ungrazed biofilm—is read by the people the way a farmer reads soil, or a sailor reads water. The pattern tells them where the biofilm is healthiest, where mineral content is shifting, where the basalt's surface chemistry is changing as it weathers. The grazers are, in effect, a distributed sensing system, and the people observe their movements from the towers' upper levels, tracking the mosaic's evolution, using it to inform their soil-development projects and their management of the thermal exchangers' output. They do not control the grazers. They could, probably. But control is not the operative principle here. The operative principle is *listening*. The plateau is telling them what it needs, through the grazers' movements, through the biofilm's color shifts, through the water's behavior on the stone, through the tonal register of the exchangers and the fliers and the rock itself, and their job—their purpose, if that word isn't too heavy—is to hear it accurately and respond with the minimum necessary intervention.

---

They are not solemn. This must be said, because everything about the description so far—the grayness, the patience, the deep time, the infrasonic communication—could give the impression of a people weighed down by the seriousness of their project, and that impression would be wrong.

They play. Not in organized ways, not in games with rules and outcomes, but in the way that musicians play when they're alone with their instruments and no audience is expected. You see it in the towers—a person shaping the self-healing material into a form that serves no structural purpose, a smooth curve or a spiral or a series of ridges that catch condensation in a pattern that produces a particular visual effect when the water runs. You see it in the pools, where someone stands ankle-deep and hums a tone that makes the bioluminescent organisms flash in sequence, chasing the sound around the pool's circumference like a wheel of light, and the person laughs—a sound that is surprisingly bright and sudden in this landscape of muted tones, a sound that travels upward through the gray air and causes a flier overhead to tilt its wings in response, its tuning-fork call answering with what an observer might—carelessly, unscientifically, but perhaps not wrongly—describe as curiosity.

They touch each other frequently. Not in the performative way of cultures where touch is a statement, but casually, constantly, the way their fingers touch the building material—a hand on a shoulder, a forehead resting briefly against another person's temple, fingers interlaced while standing together in the rain on an open plaza, looking out at the stone. Touch is communicative for them in the same register as the infrasonic—it is a way of sharing tonal information directly, body to body, without the mediation of the stone. When two people touch, their resonant frequencies interact, and there is a moment of adjustment, a negotiation below the threshold of consciousness, and then they are *in tune*, and the information that passes between them is richer and more specific than anything the stone can carry. This is intimacy for them. Not the touch itself, but the tuning. The moment when two bodies find a shared frequency and hold it.

Their humor tends toward the observational and the patient. A joke might unfold over the course of weeks—a series of small modifications to a shared workspace, each one building on the last, each one eliciting from the collaborators a response that is part appreciation and part counter-move, until the workspace has been subtly transformed into something absurd and beautiful and utterly non-functional, and everyone involved has been laughing, intermittently, for days. They find the fliers funny. They find weather funny—the endless, unchanging overcast strikes them as inherently comic in a way that is difficult to translate but seems related to the concept of *over-commitment*, of the sky doing one thing so completely and for so long that it becomes absurd. They do impressions of the overcast. A person will hold a single expression of mild, featureless neutrality for an extended period, and others will begin to lose composure, and the loss of composure spreads through the infrasonic like a chord resolving into laughter, and for a few minutes a whole settlement will be shaking with shared amusement that propagates through the stone and is felt, faintly, at the neighboring settlement forty kilometers away as a barely perceptible brightening in the hum—a warmth in the ground that means someone, somewhere, is laughing.

---

They came from somewhere else. This is known but not emphasized.

There is a history, and it is long, and it includes worlds that were not basalt plateaus under pewter skies. It includes, presumably, a world of origin, a place where this species first became itself, but that place is not spoken of with particular reverence or longing. They are not exiles. They are not colonists in the acquisitive sense. The best analogue might be *composers in residence*—they came to this plateau because it was here, because the basalt shelf presented a resonant structure of extraordinary complexity and potential, because the young stone was an empty staff waiting for notation, and they are the kind of beings for whom that invitation is irresistible.

They have done this before. Other worlds. Other compositions. The technology—the exchangers, the self-healing material, the transportation system that leaves no trace—is the accumulated toolkit of a species that has been doing this for a very long time, composing landscapes, seeding ecologies, settling into the tonal structure of a place and spending centuries or millennia bringing it into its fullest expression. They do not strip-mine. They do not terraform in the brutal sense. They *collaborate* with geology, with climate, with the organisms they introduce and the ones that arrive on their own. The end result, generations from now, will be a plateau that is fully alive—soil deep enough to support forests, waterways carved by erosion into the basalt, a complete ecology from microbe to apex predator, weather patterns stabilized into something seasonal and varied—and the settlements will still be here, still sparse, still quiet, their towers disappearing into clouds that have become, by then, more dramatic, more varied, clouds that tower and churn and break occasionally into sunlight that the people will find overwhelming and exhilarating and slightly painful, standing on their plazas with their pale eyes streaming, laughing at the sky's sudden, unprecedented extravagance.

But that is the future. For now, the plateau is young, and the people are patient, and the hum is steady in the stone, and a flier turns above a thermal column in the flat gray light, calling its single clear note into the silence, and somewhere in a tower a person pauses, listens, feels the note in their sternum, and answers with a tone so low it is felt only by the stone and by the other bodies standing on it, scattered across the dark wet rock like seeds thrown across a table, each one resonating, each one part of a chord that is still, after all these years, still finding its shape.
