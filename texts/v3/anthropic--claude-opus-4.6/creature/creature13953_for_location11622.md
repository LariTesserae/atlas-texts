# Who Lives in the Sunken Pumping District

---

No one lives here. That is the first and most important thing to understand, and it is also not true.

---

## The Salamanders

They are the most visible inhabitants, and so people — visitors, the rare ones who find the iron stairs and descend — tend to think of them first, tend to call them the *keepers* of the place. This is understandable but slightly wrong. The salamanders do not keep the Pumping District. They *correspond* with it.

There are between forty and sixty of them at any given time, though counting is difficult because they move through the pipe interiors as readily as along their surfaces, and because their pale grey skin, when still and wet, is nearly indistinguishable from the concrete. They are the color of infrastructure. Not camouflaged — camouflage implies something to hide from, and nothing here hunts them. They are simply *the same* as where they live, the way old groundskeepers eventually become the color of the soil they work.

Each one is roughly the length of a human forearm, wrist to fingertip, but heavier than you'd expect. Their bodies have a density to them, a solidity, as though they have slowly taken on the mineral content of the water through their permeable skin. And perhaps they have. If you were to hold one — they would allow it, they show no fear, though they show no interest either — you would find it cool and surprisingly weighty, like a river stone shaped into something alive. The skin would feel less like skin and more like wetite. Fine-grained. Almostite. Beneath it, a slow pulse.

Their hands are their most remarkable feature. Wide, with long spatulate fingers tipped in adhesive pads that grip any surface — oxidized iron, slick concrete, the wet curve of a four-foot pipe. But the fingers are also *dexterous*. Watchmaker-dexterous. You see this when they work the pipe joints.

Because they do work the pipe joints. This is the thing that unsettles, that makes the word *maintenance* hover in the air even though no one taught them, no one assigned them. The salamanders travel the pipe network in what appear to be regular circuits — the same individual traversing the same route at roughly the same intervals, though intervals measured in days, not hours. They pause at joints, at valve housings, at the places where pipes pass through walls. They press their wide fingers against the metal. They hold still, sometimes for ten or fifteen minutes, their bodies flattened against the pipe surface as though listening through it. Then they either move on or they begin to work.

The work involves the crystalline deposits. The pipes precipitate minerals constantly — calcium carbonate, mostly, but also stranger formations, things with a faintly iridescent quality, things that grow in feathered clusters at the joints where different metals meet and galvanic processes do slow, patient chemistry. The salamanders harvest these deposits. They grip the crystal clusters in their mouths or cradle them against their chests and carry them through the pipe network to specific locations — junction boxes, mostly, and a few spots on the walls of the pump stations where the brick has been colonized by the verdigris moss.

What they do with the crystals in the junction boxes is difficult to observe. The gaps in the casings are too small for a human eye to see through clearly, and the light inside is nil. But you can hear the clicking — a rapid, delicate percussion, like someone sorting beads on a stone table. And if you find a junction box that has lost its cover entirely (there is one, near the transformer house, its lid corroded away), you can look inside and see what they've built.

It is a structure. A crystalline structure, growing slowly, filling the interior of the junction box with a lattice of deposited minerals arranged in a pattern that is — not random. Not regular either. It has the quality of something *organized* without being *designed*. The way a coral reef is organized. The way a city is organized, if you look at it from far enough above and forget that anyone planned it. The crystals branch and join. They form archways, bridges, hollow chambers. Some of them are integrated with the electrical wiring that still runs through the junction box — copper strands passing through crystal tunnels, encased but not obstructed. The salamanders have built *around* the wiring. They have incorporated it.

Whether this structure does anything — whether it serves a function in the larger system — is impossible to say from observation. It exists. The salamanders maintain it and add to it and occasionally remove a piece, carrying it away to some other location. It is growing, but slowly enough that growth and maintenance are the same activity.

The salamanders do not eat, as far as anyone has observed. Or rather, they do not eat in a way that looks like eating. They absorb. They spend long periods immersed in the gutter channels, their bodies slack, their mouths slightly open, the mineral-rich water flowing over and presumably through them. They may filter nutrients from the water. They may absorb dissolved minerals directly through their skin. They may photosynthesize, faintly, in the grey light near the ceiling breaches — their skin has a quality, in that light, a faint luminous undertone, that suggests *something* is happening at the cellular level. But they do not hunt. They do not forage. They simply persist, and they work, and they rest in the water, and they persist again.

They reproduce, presumably, though no one has seen it. There are smaller ones — juveniles, half the size of adults, their skin slightly more translucent, their movements less deliberate. The juveniles tend to stay near the pump stations, pressed against the warm brick, vibrating faintly in sympathy with the pump harmonics. They do not yet travel the pipe routes. They are not yet part of the circuit. They are learning the hum, perhaps. Imprinting on it. Becoming the frequency they will carry in their bones when they are grown and take their place on the network.

The adults do not interact with each other much. They pass on pipes without acknowledgment. They work in junction boxes alone or in pairs, never more. There is no social structure visible — no hierarchy, no territory, no aggression. They are not a community in any social sense. They are a *population*, in the ecological sense, and their niche is the pipe network, and they fill it with the same calm saturation as the water fills the channels. Not too many. Not too few. Exactly as many as the system requires, though who or what determines the requirement is part of the larger question that the Pumping District asks and does not answer.

## The Moths

The moths are the most numerous inhabitants, if you count them, which is harder than it sounds. Resting on the walls with their wings folded, they are near-invisible — grey-brown on grey-brown, their wing surfaces textured to match the mineral-stained concrete. You walk past a wall and then your eye adjusts and you realize the wall has thirty moths on it, each one as wide as your palm, each one perfectly still, each one oriented with its head pointing upward and its wings pressed flat, and you wonder how long they have been there and whether they were there when you first looked.

They are old, the moths. Not individually — a moth's lifespan is what it is, weeks or months, rarely more. But the *lineage* is old. They have been here for generations uncountable, breeding in the dim space, their larvae feeding on — what? The mold, probably. The pale filamentous things that hang from the ceiling slabs. The verdigris moss. Maybe the mold on the chart recorder paper. Their caterpillars are rarely seen, which means they are either very hidden or very few at any time, the population held at carrying capacity by the scarcity of food.

But it is the adults that matter, because the adults do something that food and reproduction do not explain.

They survey.

This is the word that comes to mind and will not leave. The moths, when they fly — which is infrequently, once or twice per moth per day — fly in straight lines between resting points. Not wandering, not searching, not drawn to light (there is so little light, and it is so diffuse, that phototaxis is meaningless here). They fly *from* a specific point on a wall *to* a specific point on another wall, and they land, and they orient upward, and they are still. The flight paths, if you could map them — if you had the patience to watch for days and mark each transit — would form a network. A grid of sight lines criss-crossing the district. Each moth, at its resting point, is positioned at an intersection of these lines, a node in a web of attention.

What are they attending to? The infrastructure. Their resting positions correspond, roughly but unmistakably, to structural features — load-bearing walls, pipe junctions, the places where the ceiling has been patched or where the concrete is under strain. Each moth sits near a feature and is *oriented toward it*, head up, antennae extended, the feathered plumes of their antennae spread wide and trembling faintly, not in any breeze (there is no breeze down here) but in response to vibrations transmitted through the wall surface. They are feeling the building. They are feeling the infrastructure through the substrate it is built on, the way a doctor feels a pulse through the wrist.

And the wing patterns. You have to see one with its wings open to understand. It happens rarely — a moth startled by your movement, or one stretching after a long rest, or two moths in proximity performing what might be a mating display or might be an information exchange or might be something that has no name. The wings open, and the patterns are there.

They look like technical drawings.

Not exactly. Not literally. But the resemblance is strong enough to make your breath catch. Concentric circles that evoke pressure gauges. Parallel lines that evoke pipe cross-sections. Branching forms that evoke flow diagrams. Symmetrical patterns that evoke something you've seen on an instrument panel but can't quite place. The patterns are rendered in scales of grey and brown and a faint copper sheen, and they are *precise* — not the blurred impressionism of a typical moth wing but something almost *etched*, as though the wing surface is a plate and the pattern has been engraved on it by something very fine and very careful.

These patterns differ from moth to moth. Not randomly — there are types, categories, recurring motifs. The moths near the pump stations tend to carry circular patterns. The moths near the transformer house carry grid patterns. The moths near the control room carry something that looks like waveforms. As if each moth's wings encode information about the part of the system it monitors.

This is anthropomorphization. This is the human tendency to see meaning in pattern. Except that the moths near the transformer house — the ones arranged in the almost-grid on the wall nearest the humming equipment — are arranged so that their wing patterns, taken together, *do* form a grid. A composite image. A map. If you stand back and unfocus your eyes and let the individual moths blur into a collective surface, you can see it: a schematic of something. Pipes, maybe. Current flow. The district's own circulatory system, rendered in moth wings on a damp wall.

The moths don't interact with the salamanders. They don't interact with anything, really. They rest, they survey, they fly their straight-line transits, they display their wing patterns to each other or to no one, and they die, and their bodies fall to the engineering brick and are slowly dissolved by the mineral water, and their wings — the last thing to go — curl and bleach and become part of the pale detritus in the gutter channels, and the larvae grow in whatever hidden places larvae grow, and new moths emerge, and the new moths take their positions, and the survey continues.

## The Eels

The eels are the deepest inhabitants. They belong to the water the way the salamanders belong to the pipes and the moths belong to the walls. They are water made dense and slow and purposeful.

There are perhaps a dozen of them, though like everything else here the count is uncertain. They are dark — not black but a deep brown-grey, the color of water seen through more water, the color of a shadow in a pool. They are thick-bodied, two to three feet long, their skin smooth and mucus-slicked in a way that is different from the general wetness of the district — more *organic*, more deliberately lubricated. They have no eyes. Where eyes would be, there are slight depressions, pale patches where the skin is thinner and more translucent, and beneath this skin you can sometimes see — or imagine you see — a faint flicker, like an electrical discharge, like a thought.

They live in the gutter channels, in the deeper pools where the engineering brick has subsided and created basins, in the flooded sublevels of the pump stations where the water is deep enough to submerge in fully. They move slowly, coasting through the shallow channels with sinuous undulations that seem too large and powerful for the water they're in. They could move faster. They choose not to. Their movement has the quality of *patrol* — a slow, continuous circuit through the waterways of the district, touching every channel, every pool, every flooded space, before returning to start again.

They orient toward the pump stations. This is the behavior that visitors — the few, the rare — find most unsettling. An eel will be moving through a gutter channel, its body a dark ripple in two inches of water, and it will stop. It will raise the front third of its body above the surface, its blunt pale head lifted into the air, its eyeless face turned toward the nearest pump station, and it will hold that position for minutes at a time. Motionless. Oriented. As if receiving a signal. As if the pump's hum contains information that the eel can decode through whatever sensory apparatus it has in place of eyes.

Then it lowers itself back into the water and continues its circuit.

The eels interact with the water in a way that seems to exceed simple habitation. The water *moves differently* around them. This could be fluid dynamics — a large body in a shallow channel creates disturbances, changes flow patterns. But the changes seem directed. An eel passing through a channel will leave the water behind it flowing more evenly, as though its passage has smoothed out turbulence. An eel resting in a pool will create a slow circulation around itself, a gentle vortex that draws water in from the feeder channels and pushes it out toward the drains. The eels are, in some sense, auxiliary pumps. Biological components of the hydraulic system. They add their energy to the water's movement, their slow bodies supplementing the mechanical pumps with something older and more organic.

They eat the watercress-that-isn't-watercress. You can see this sometimes — an eel nosing along the thin dark-green line that grows in the gutter channels, its mouth opening against the stems, its body pulsing faintly as it takes something in. But this seems insufficient to sustain creatures of their size. They must take something else from the water — minerals, dissolved organics, the microbial film that coats every submerged surface. They are filter feeders, perhaps, their entire skin a membrane, their entire body a slow process of extraction, pulling sustenance from the water as the water passes over them, the way the pumps pull water from below.

The eels are the oldest things here. This is a feeling, not a fact — but it is a strong feeling, shared by every visitor who has spent time in the district. The salamanders arrived. The moths adapted. But the eels were *already here when the pumps were installed*. The eels were in the water before the water was pumped. The engineers who built this place — if engineers built it — sank their shafts and poured their concrete and set their riveted iron plates into the bedrock, and the water they tapped already had eels in it, and the eels simply accepted the new architecture the way water accepts a new channel. They flowed into the district's shape and became part of it. The infrastructure was built around a process the eels were already part of.

This is why they orient toward the pumps. Not because the pumps command them. Because the pumps are doing what the eels were already doing — moving the water, maintaining the equilibrium, keeping the water table where it needs to be. The pumps joined the eels' project, not the other way around.

## The Beetles

They are small enough to overlook. Thumbnail-sized, oval, with a lacquer-black carapace that reflects the dim light in a way that makes them look like drops of dark liquid on the instrument panels. There are between twenty and thirty of them, all in the control room, all on or near the instruments.

They are meticulous.

Each beetle occupies a position on or near a specific instrument. One sits on the glass face of a pressure gauge, directly over the needle's current position, as though marking it. When the needle moves — slowly, fractionally, the system's pressures shifting in their long sine wave — the beetle moves with it, maintaining its position over the needle's tip. Another beetle sits on the frame of an ammeter, its body pressed against the glass, its antennae touching the surface where the needle's reflection moves. Three beetles cluster at the base of the chart recorder, where the pen mechanism meets the paper, and they arrange themselves around the pen arm with a precision that suggests they are measuring its movement, triangulating its position, confirming that the sine wave it traces is the sine wave it should be tracing.

The beetle that sits in the path of the pen — the one that lets the pen draw its line across its back — has done this many times. You can see the traces on its carapace, faint lines of ink accumulated over repeated exposures, a palimpsest of sine waves written on a living surface. The beetle wears the record of the system on its body. It is both observer and archive.

The beetles do not maintain the instruments in any mechanical sense. They do not turn screws or adjust calibrations. But they *attend*. They are present at each instrument in a way that is so consistent, so positionally precise, that their presence begins to feel functional — as though the instrument's reading is not complete until a beetle has confirmed it, the way a measurement is not official until it has been witnessed.

They feed on the mold. This is the one straightforward thing about them. The mold that grows on the chart recorder paper, on the edges of instrument housings, in the seams where brass meets glass — the beetles graze on it, slowly, leaving clean trails through the microbial film. This is maintenance, in the most literal sense. They keep the instrument faces readable. They keep the mold from obscuring the gauges. Not because they intend to — intention is too grand a word for a beetle — but because their feeding behavior and the instruments' need for clarity are the same thing, expressed differently.

The beetles are the most recent arrivals. This is another feeling-not-fact. The instruments are newer than the pumps — decades newer, perhaps, installed in some period of modernization when someone decided the district needed monitoring, needed data, needed to be *known* in numerical terms. The beetles came after the instruments, drawn to the warm bakelite smell, to the faint electrical fields, to the mold that grows in the humidity. They found a niche that didn't exist before the control room was built, and they filled it, and now the control room functions — the instruments read, the chart recorder traces, the data exists — partly because the beetles are there to witness it. An unread gauge is a meaningless gauge. The beetles read the gauges. The gauges, therefore, mean.

## The Crabs

There is only ever one visible at a time, but this doesn't mean there is only one. They are pale — almost translucent — with a flattened body shaped less like a crab and more like a hand with too many fingers, pressed flat against a pipe fitting. They live in the pipe trenches, the exposed channels where flagstones have been removed, where the pipes run openly through shallow water and the mineral deposits build up fastest.

They grip the pipe fittings with their claws — not aggressively, not defensively, but structurally. They hold the fittings the way a wrench holds a bolt. Their claws are shaped for this, curved to match the hexagonal profiles of pipe nuts, the flanged edges of coupling rings. A crab will wrap itself around a fitting and *hold*, for hours, for days, its body trembling faintly with the vibration of the pipe it grips, its pale translucent flesh taking on the color of the metal beneath it — green where it grips oxidized copper, rust-brown where it grips iron.

What are they doing? Holding things together. This is the simplest reading and the most unnerving. The pipes are old. The fittings are old. The joints weep and the threads are corroded and the whole network is held together by inertia and mineral deposits and — possibly — by pale crab-things that grip the fittings and add their biological tension to the mechanical tension of the threads. Remove the crab and the fitting might hold. Or it might not. No one has tested this. No one has removed a crab. It seems inadvisable.

They eat the mineral deposits too, or at least they scrape them. Their smaller legs — the walking legs, the ones not occupied with gripping — pick at the crystalline formations around the fittings, breaking off small pieces and conveying them to the mouth. This is the same mineral diet as the salamanders, the same crystalline harvest, but the crabs eat *in place*. They do not carry. They do not build. They hold and they eat and they hold, and the fitting beneath them is always, when a crab finally moves on, slightly cleaner and slightly tighter than it was before.

## The Spiders (or the Things Shaped Like Spiders)

They are not spiders. This needs to be said, even though the word is unavoidable. They have eight legs and they build webs. But their bodies are wrong for spiders — too symmetrical, too crystalline in their segmentation, their legs all the same length and thickness, their bodies a perfect octagon when seen from above. They look like geometric diagrams of spiders. They look like the *idea* of a spider, rendered by something that understood the concept but not the organism.

Their webs are strung between pipes, always between pipes, always in the spaces where pipes run parallel and close — the six-inch gaps, the foot-wide channels between a main line and its auxiliary. The webs are not sticky. They are structural — taut lines of silk (if it is silk) that connect one pipe to another, bracing them, creating a tensile network that distributes vibration and load between parallel members. The webs are engineered. They have the geometry of truss bridges, of suspension cables, of the bracing structures inside aircraft wings. Each web is different, adapted to the specific gap and the specific pipes it bridges, but they all share this quality of *structural intent*.

The mineral water beads on the web lines and does not break them. Instead, the water deposits its minerals on the silk, and the silk slowly mineralizes, becoming stiffer, stronger, more permanent. Old webs — and some of these webs are very old — have become solid mineral structures, calcium-sheathed cables connecting pipes, no longer silk at all but something harder and more lasting. The spider-things build with silk and time and mineral water, and the result is a permanent bracing system that reinforces the pipe network from within.

The spider-things do not catch prey. Nothing flies through their webs — the moths avoid the pipe gaps, flying in open spaces between walls. Nothing crawls through them either. The webs have no prey function. They are purely structural. The spider-things sit at the centers of their webs and feel the vibrations of the pipes, and they adjust — adding a line here, reinforcing a junction there — and they feel again, and they adjust again. They are tuning the system. They are keeping the pipes in harmonic balance, damping resonances that might stress a joint, transmitting vibrations that help the pumps operate smoothly.

They eat the way everything here eats: from the water, from the minerals, from the slow diffuse energy of the district itself. They may not eat at all, in any conventional sense. They may be sustained by the vibrations they feel. They may be sustained by the act of building. They may be something the district grew, the way a bone grows a callus, a structural response to a structural need, alive only in the sense that everything here is alive, which is to say: functioning, persisting, maintaining.

## The Birds

There are two of them.

There have always been two of them, according to the logic of the place, which is not historical logic but the logic of balance: two birds, two ceiling breaches large enough to admit the grey light they need, two high pipes near those breaches where they can perch and see the entire district below.

They are small. Smaller than your fist. Dark-feathered, round-bodied, with disproportionately large eyes that catch the dim light and refract it in a way that makes their gaze visible — two points of faint luminance in the upper darkness, watching. They do not sing. They have never been heard to sing. They make, occasionally, a sound — a single note, low and clear, that drops into the district's ambient hum like a stone into water and rings for a moment and is gone. One bird makes this sound. The other answers. Then silence again, for hours or days.

They are not the same species as any surface bird. They are too adapted — their feathers have a quality of water-resistance that goes beyond oiling, as though each feather has been individually lacquered, as though they have evolved not for flight in air but for existence in humid air, in air that is more water than air, in the saturated breath-space of the Pumping District. Their flight, when they fly — rarely, from one perch to another, a distance of perhaps thirty feet — is heavy and direct, their wingbeats audible as a soft, wet thudding, as though the air here is thicker than air should be and they must work harder to move through it.

They watch. This is their function, if function is the word. They watch from above, from the only vantage points that allow a view of the entire district, and they watch with an attentiveness that is different from the moths' survey or the beetles' attendance. The moths feel. The beetles witness. The birds *oversee*. They see the salamanders on the pipes and the eels in the channels and the moths on the walls and the beetles on the instruments, and they see these things simultaneously, as a single integrated system, the way a bird of prey sees a landscape — not as individual elements but as a field of relationships, a pattern of movement and stillness that either coheres or doesn't.

When the system is in balance — which is almost always — the birds are still. They perch. They watch. Their luminous eyes sweep the space below in slow arcs, tracking nothing in particular, seeing everything in general.

When something changes — a pipe burst, a pump hesitation, a shift in the water table that sends a tremor through the channels — the birds react first. Before the eels orient, before the moths reposition, before the salamanders alter their routes. The birds make their sound — that single low clear note, call and response — and something in the district *shifts*. Not dramatically. The hum adjusts. The salamanders converge, subtly, toward the disturbance. The moths nearest the affected area open their wings briefly, displaying their patterns to each other or to the air. The system responds, and the birds watch the response, and when equilibrium is restored — it is always restored — the birds fall silent again.

Are they commanding the system? Are they signaling? Or are they simply the first to notice, the canaries in this underground coal mine, their call not a command but a *reading* — a vocalization of system state, the way the chart recorder's pen is a vocalization of pressure state, information made physical, made audible, made part of the district's self-knowledge?

They nest in the pipe junctions near the ceiling breaches, in nests made of spider-silk and mineral deposits and the shed skin of salamanders and fragments of moth wings. The nests are small, dense, and surprisingly warm — insulated by the crystalline materials, heated faintly by the pipes they're built against. There are no eggs visible. There are no chicks. There are always two birds. Whether they reproduce, whether they age, whether the two birds you see today are the same two birds that were here a decade ago or a century ago — this is unknowable. They exist in a pair. The pair is the unit. The pair watches.

## The Others

There are things you see at the edges. Things that might be inhabitants or might be artifacts of the light, the moisture, the way your eyes adapt and over-adapt to the permanent twilight.

A shape in the water below the pump station floor, seen through a grate: longer than an eel, paler, segmented. It moves once and then is still, and you cannot find it again.

A vibration in a pipe that has a rhythm too complex for mechanics — a pattern that sounds, through the iron, like breathing, or like speech played at a fraction of its proper speed.

A mark on the wall of the control room that is too regular to be staining and too irregular to be writing, but that occupies the space where a sign or a nameplate might have been, as though something removed the human text and replaced it with its own.

A moment — just one, and you cannot be sure it happened — when all the moths on a wall opened their wings simultaneously, and the composite pattern they formed was not a map of the pipe system but something else, something that looked like a face, or like the memory of a face, before the wings folded again and it was gone.

These are the edges. Every system has edges, places where the pattern becomes ambiguous, where the clean relationships between salamander and pipe, beetle and gauge, eel and water begin to blur into something less defined. The Pumping District is a system in balance, but it is not a closed system. It connects — downward, to whatever deeper water the eels came from; upward, through the ceiling breaches, to the surface world that grew over it; and sideways, through the pipe network, to wherever the pipes go, to whatever larger infrastructure this district is a node within.

Things come through. Things that are not yet integrated. Things that the district has not yet absorbed into its logic. They appear at the edges — a new species of pale invertebrate in the pipe trenches, an unfamiliar mold on the transformer housing, a vibration in the system that the eels orient toward with unusual intensity. The district encounters these things and either incorporates them or lets them pass through, and the balance adjusts, and the pumps continue, and the water moves.

## What Lives Here

What lives here is not a community. It is not an ecosystem, exactly, though it has the shape of one. It is a *process* that has become populated. A mechanical process — the pumping of water, the maintenance of a water table, the balancing of pressures underground — that has been running long enough and steadily enough that life has grown into it the way life grows into any stable niche, filling every role, occupying every function, until the distinction between the mechanical process and the biological one has dissolved.

The salamanders maintain the pipes. The moths monitor the structure. The beetles attend the instruments. The eels move the water. The crabs hold the fittings. The spider-things brace the network. The birds oversee the whole. Each creature does what the infrastructure needs, and the infrastructure provides what each creature needs — shelter, warmth, water, minerals, stability, the long slow hum that is the pulse of this underground world.

Nothing here is conscious of this arrangement. Nothing here chose it. The salamanders do not know they are maintaining; the moths do not know they are monitoring. They are doing what their bodies and their instincts and their millennia of adaptation have shaped them to do, and what they do happens to be what the district requires, because the district is what shaped them. They were shaped by this place the way water is shaped by a pipe. They flow through it in the form it gives them, and in flowing, they keep it running.

What lives here is the district itself. The creatures are its living components, the way the pumps are its mechanical components and the water is its hydraulic medium. It is all one thing. One organism, if organism can stretch that far. One process, breathing in its saturated way, pumping its slow pumps, tracing its sine wave on its moldy paper, watching with its two bright eyes from the high pipes, listening with its eyeless eels, holding together with its pale crab-hands, surveying with its grey moth-wings, building its crystal structures in its junction boxes, and continuing. Always continuing.

It does not need you. It accounted for you when you entered — a temporary variable, a bubble in the pipe — and it will account for your departure. The stairs are behind you. The surface is above. The district hums. The salamander grips the pipe with its wide, dexterous fingers and carries its crystal forward through the dark, and the chart recorder pen traces its line on the living paper, and the water rises and is pushed and the water table holds, and the two dark birds watch from the high pipes with their bright, mineral eyes, and they make no sound, because the system is in balance, and when the system is in balance, there is nothing to say.
