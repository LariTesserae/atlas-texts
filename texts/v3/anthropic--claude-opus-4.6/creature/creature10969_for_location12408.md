# Who Lives in the Sunken Greenhouses

---

Start with the ones you'll never meet face to face, because they are the truest inhabitants, the ones whose tenancy is least contested.

**The canopy dwellers.** Up in the warm layer, in the dense woven mass where scaffold has become armature and armature has become living architecture, there are small pale birds — or things that were once birds, or things that function as birds do. They are the size of large moths. Their feathers, if they are feathers, have lost all pigment; they are the white of root tips, the white of things that grow under stones. Their eyes are large and dark and poorly suited to bright light, which is fine, because there is no bright light here. They navigate by sound — not echolocation exactly, not in the sophisticated chiropteran sense, but something simpler: they have learned the drone. They know its texture the way a fish knows current. The mechanical roar is not noise to them. It is landscape. It has geography. Where the air moves faster near a duct outlet, the sound shifts, and they feel it in their breast bones and adjust. Where a compressor housing creates a pocket of turbulence, the harmonic is different, and they avoid it. They flit through the vine curtains in short, precise arcs, feeding on the soft-bodied insects that also live in the warm layer — tiny translucent things, aphid-derivatives or mite-kin, that have proliferated in the canopy's compressed ecosystem and that the birds pluck from the undersides of leaves with quick sideways motions of their heads.

They nest in the thickest root masses, where the tangled fibers form cavities. Their nests are made of shed root hairs and fragments of insulation pulled from the pipes — pink fiberglass, grey foam, the frayed edges of rubber gaskets. The nests are surprisingly warm. The birds press into them and go still, and from below, if you happened to look up through a gap in the canopy at the right moment, you might mistake a roosting bird for a clump of pale fungal growth. You would not hear them. Nothing that lives here sings. The drone has eaten all the small sounds. If these birds vocalize, they do it in frequencies below or above the mechanical spectrum, in private ultrasonic conversations carried out beak-to-beak in the sheltered pockets between root and steel.

They have been here long enough to have become something specific. Not a species — nothing so formal, nothing so named. A local phenomenon. A population finding its shape.

---

**The floor creatures.** Down in the cold, in the drainage channels and the thin films of water that sit on the limestone like something the basin is reluctant to swallow, there is a sparse and patient ecology. The translucent flatworms are the most visible members — visible being relative, since they are essentially colorless ribbons a centimeter long, discernible only by the faint shadow they cast on the pale stone beneath them and by their slow, deliberate gliding. They feed on the biofilm that coats the wet rock: a thin, living skin of bacteria and microalgae that subsists on the nutrients that drip down from the canopy — leaked hydroponic solution, organic residue from decomposing root tips, the slow rain of detritus that filters through the warm-cold boundary and arrives on the basin floor as a barely perceptible organic dust.

The flatworms are not alone. There are the amphipod-like creatures — humpbacked, segmented, smaller than a grain of rice, scooting along the edges of the water films where surface tension is strongest. They are scavengers and scrapers. There are nematodes in the wet moss crusts, threadlike, invisible to the naked eye, cycling through generations in the thin moisture between stone and lichen. There are fungi — not the kind you'd notice, not mushrooms or brackets, but networks of hyphae threading through the rubble, breaking down whatever organic matter finds its way to the floor: a fallen leaf that drifted through a canopy gap, a fragment of root, the frozen clippings in the abandoned wheelbarrow. The fungi are patient in the way only cold, dark organisms can be patient. They measure progress in molecules.

Nothing down here is warm-blooded. Nothing down here moves fast. The cold enforces a metabolism of contemplation. The flatworms glide. The amphipods scoot and pause. The nematodes coil and uncoil in their films of moisture. It is an ecosystem operating at the lowest viable energy level, like a fire reduced to its last ember — still alive, still consuming, but so slowly that you could watch for an hour and see nothing happen.

And yet it persists. It has carved out its niche in the thermodynamic leftovers of the system above. The canopy gets the light, the warmth, the engineered nutrition. The floor gets what falls through. And what falls through is enough.

---

**The gradient dwellers.** Between floor and canopy, in that shimmering thermal boundary three meters up where your breath stops fogging and the air begins to soften, there is a zone that belongs to almost nothing. It is too warm for the floor creatures, too cold and exposed for the canopy birds, too far from both the stone substrate below and the dense growing mass above to offer shelter. It is the no-man's-land of the basin. But "almost nothing" is not nothing.

Spiders live there. Small ones, drab ones, the kind that build webs in structural corners and wait. They anchor their webs to the lowest-hanging root tips and to the scaffold legs that descend from the canopy to the floor, and they sit in the transitional air, in that liminal warmth, and they wait for whatever blunders into their silk. Mostly what they catch are the tiny flying insects that sometimes descend from the canopy — gnats, fungus flies, things with wings too small to see individually but which, in aggregate, form a barely perceptible haze in the air near duct outlets where warm currents push downward. The spiders are solitary. Each one occupies its own web, its own structural corner, and there may be hundreds of meters between them. They are the loneliest things in the basin. They sit in a zone that isn't quite anything, catching food that isn't quite abundant, in a silence that isn't quite silence because the drone fills everything, and they persist because persistence is what spiders do.

Some of their webs are old. You can tell because the silk nearest the anchor points has gone grey with the same fine organic dust that coats the basin floor — years of accumulated detritus, the web becoming a record of the air it's hung in. The spider at the center is always new. Spiders don't live long. But the web endures, repaired and extended by successive tenants, and some of those webs have been in continuous use longer than any single creature in the basin has been alive. They are the oldest structures here that aren't made of metal or stone.

---

**The machines.** This must be said directly: the machines live here. They are not alive in any biological sense and I will not pretend otherwise, but they are inhabitants. They occupy space. They consume resources. They maintain themselves through ongoing processes and they modify their environment in ways that make their own continued operation possible. The compressors breathe — pulling in basin air, compressing it, using the heat of compression to warm the canopy layer, exhausting the cold remainder to the floor. The pumps circulate — drawing nutrient solution from mixing tanks, pushing it through kilometers of tubing to the hydroponic racks, recovering the runoff, re-enriching it, sending it again. The fans move air in patterns that are not random but are not precisely planned either — they were designed once, but decades of ductwork modifications, blocked passages, failed dampers, and unauthorized patches have altered the airflow into something emergent, something that no single blueprint describes. The machines have settled into their own ecology of operation. They balance against each other. When one compressor runs hot, its neighbors compensate. When a pump fails, the nutrient flow redistributes along paths of least resistance, finding alternate routes through the plumbing the way water finds alternate routes down a hillside.

They are loud and they do not apologize for it. The drone is their collective voice and it is the dominant sound of this place and it will continue to be the dominant sound of this place for as long as the power supply holds and possibly longer, because some of these machines have been running for so long that their bearings have worn into shapes that are uniquely adapted to their current loads, and they would resist being stopped the way a river resists being dammed — not with intelligence, not with volition, but with the accumulated momentum of a system that has found its groove and wears it deeper with every passing hour.

The machines do not know they are keeping anything alive. They are keeping everything alive.

---

**The people.** There are people. They are the least present inhabitants but they are the reason everything else exists, the initial cause that set the whole cascading system in motion, and so they deserve attention even though — especially because — they are so rarely here.

They come in small groups. Two, three, sometimes four. Never more. They arrive through one of the access tunnels that pierce the basin wall at floor level — narrow, concrete-lined passages that connect the basin to the surface plateau above — and they come dressed for the cold, because whatever is up on the plateau is cold too, and the basin floor is no warmer. Heavy jackets, layered. Boots with thick soles against the metal grating. Gloves that they remove only when they need fine dexterity — to turn a valve, to test an electrical connection, to collect a sample from one of the canopy racks using a long-handled cutting tool that reaches up into the warm layer and snips a leaf or a fruit or a cluster of something and brings it back down to be examined in gloved hands.

They are maintenance workers. Technicians. They might be called something else — tenders, keepers, engineers — but their function is maintenance. They check the machines. They read gauges mounted on the sides of compressor housings, tapping them with a knuckle to make sure the needles aren't stuck. They open fuse boxes and peer at indicator lights. They climb ladders welded to scaffold legs and ascend into the canopy to inspect the racks, disappearing into the warm green mass above and emerging twenty minutes later with their jackets unzipped because up there it is a different world, warm and humid and close, and they are sweating even though their boots are still cold from the basin floor.

They do not speak to each other often. The drone makes conversation difficult; you would have to shout to be heard clearly, and shouting requires more energy than the information is usually worth. Instead, they communicate in gestures, in pointed fingers and nodded heads and hand signals that have evolved through use into a local shorthand. *That valve needs attention.* A pointed finger, a turning motion. *The pressure's low in this section.* A palm held flat, pushed downward. *I'm going up.* A thumb jerked toward the canopy. *I'll be back.* A hand held up, five fingers spread. Five minutes, or five hours — the gesture is the same, because in this place the difference barely matters.

They carry tools but not many. A belt pouch with wrenches, a multimeter, a knife. Pruning shears in a holster on the hip. A headlamp, though most of them leave it off while walking the floor, navigating by the amber glow of fuse box lights and the dim green-filtered daylight from above. They know the walkway grid by feel, by the number of steps between intersections, by the particular sound the grating makes under their boots in different sections — looser here, tighter there, a slight rattle where a bolt has worked free.

They are not young and not old. They have the bodies of people who do physical work in cold environments: thick through the shoulders, slow-moving but sure-footed, hands that are rough and permanently slightly swollen from the cold. Their faces are hard to see in the dimness, but what you notice is the set of their mouths — closed, compressed, the expression of people who have learned to breathe through their noses to conserve warmth and who have carried that habit into every other moment of their lives. They do not smile often, not because they are unhappy but because smiling requires opening the mouth and opening the mouth lets the cold in and the cold is always there, on the floor, pressing against the skin, waiting.

How many of them are there, total? It is difficult to know. You see two or three at a time, and their visits seem to follow a rough rotation — the same faces reappearing at intervals, though the intervals are irregular. Perhaps a dozen, spread across shifts. Perhaps fewer. The basin is vast and the workforce is thin, and there are always sections that haven't been visited in weeks, where a failed indicator light glows red unnoticed or a collapsed scaffold section has spilled root mass across a walkway that no one walks down. The work is triage. The workers go where the need is most acute and they trust the rest of the system to hold, and mostly it does, because the system was overbuilt — more redundancy than was strictly necessary, more capacity than the current canopy demands — and the margins of safety, while narrowing year by year, have not yet narrowed to zero.

Where do they go when they leave? Up the access tunnels to the plateau. To a surface that is never described and does not need to be, because the basin is self-contained in a way that makes the outside world feel theoretical. They live up there, presumably. They have lives up there. But their lives down here, in the basin, are the lives that matter for this telling, and those lives are defined by the work: check the machines, tend the canopy, keep the system running for another day, another week, another season. The purpose — what is grown here, and for whom, and why it must be grown in this particular way, underground, in a basin, in the cold — is never stated. Perhaps they know and don't discuss it. Perhaps they have stopped asking. The system runs. The canopy grows. The machines drone. That is enough.

---

**What doesn't live here.** No large animals. No dogs, no cats, no rats even — the floor is too cold and too bare to support anything warm-blooded that isn't wearing a jacket. No earthworms in the stone. No bees in the canopy — pollination, if it's needed, is done by hand or by the tiny insects that serve as accidental surrogates. No trees — the canopy is vines and broad-leaved hydroponic cultivars, things with flexible stems and aggressive root systems, things that can be trained along cables and pruned back from machinery, not things with trunks and deep roots and the structural ambition of trees. No standing water deeper than a centimeter or two, which means no fish, no frogs, no mosquito larvae, no pond ecology. No soil. This is important. There is no soil anywhere in the basin. The plants grow in hydroponic medium — mineral wool, expanded clay pellets, something synthetic and inert — and the floor is bare stone. The transition from biology to geology is absolute. Where the roots end, the rock begins. There is nothing in between.

---

**What lives here, then, in the truest sense.** The system. The whole integrated thing: the machines and their drone, the canopy and its layered green mass, the thin cold ecology of the floor, the gradient spiders in their ancient webs, the pale birds flitting through root curtains, the workers ascending and descending and checking and pruning and never quite keeping up. It lives as a single organism lives — not because it has a single will or a single purpose, but because its parts are so deeply interdependent that removing any one of them would cause the rest to stutter, compensate, reorganize, and eventually settle into a new configuration that would be recognizably the same place but slightly diminished, slightly less itself. The canopy needs the machines. The machines need the workers. The workers need whatever the canopy produces. The floor ecology needs the canopy's leavings. The birds need the insects. The insects need the plants. The plants need the light, the heat, the nutrients, the water — all of it manufactured, all of it cycled, all of it held in the basin like water in cupped hands.

It is a living thing that was built. Or a built thing that has become living. The distinction dissolved somewhere in the decades since the first rack was bolted to the first scaffold and the first seedling was planted in the first cube of mineral wool and the first compressor was switched on and added its voice to what was then silence and is now the drone, the constant, the foundation note of everything that happens here.

And beneath it all — beneath the machines, beneath the walkways, beneath the drainage channels and the thin water and the moss and the limestone rubble — there is the basin itself. The rock. The geologically young depression that was here before any of this and will be here after all of it, patient and indifferent, holding everything the way a bowl holds whatever is placed in it, without opinion, without preference, without care.

The basin does not live. But everything that does live here lives because of its shape, its depth, its shelter from whatever is above and beyond. It is the condition that makes the rest possible. It is the cupped hands. And the water it holds is green, and warm at the top and cold at the bottom, and loud — always, always loud — with the sound of the machines that keep it from spilling.
