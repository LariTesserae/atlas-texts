# The Inhabitants

---

The fish are the easiest to name and the hardest to know.

They are not truly fish — or rather, they are fish in the way that the room is a room: technically, by the most generous available classification, because no better word has survived. They are vertebrates. They are aquatic. Beyond that, they have become themselves so completely that comparison to anything above feels like a translation that loses the original.

The largest one — the one you first noticed against the far pipe — is the oldest. You can intuit this without evidence, the way you can intuit that a stone at the bottom of a river has been there longer than the stones on top of it. She (and it is a she, though sex in this species has become a muted, almost vestigial distinction, a quarterly hormonal whisper rather than any visible dimorphism) has been grazing the biofilm on that particular pipe for years. Perhaps decades. Her route is fixed: she begins at the point where the pipe enters through the ragged concrete, works her way along its underside to where it bends upward toward the ceiling, then drifts back to the start with a few slow strokes of her tail and begins again. The biofilm regenerates faster than she consumes it. This is the fundamental equation of her existence, and it is solved. It has been solved for her entire life. She does not explore. She does not need to. The world is forty meters long, and everything in it that matters grows on the surface of things.

Her body is the color of the water — not transparent but translucent in a way that suggests she has simply stopped bothering with pigment, the way you might stop maintaining a room in a house you no longer use. In the amber light, you can see the shadow of her spine, the slow pulse of her gills, the dark comma of her liver. Her mouth, wide and ventral, is fringed with soft tissue that acts as a combination of lip and sieve and sensory organ, tasting the biofilm before ingesting it, selecting for the bacterial strains that provide the most energy, rejecting the calcium-ite crusts and the dead material with a delicate lateral motion of her jaw that looks almost like distaste. She has no eyes. The sockets remain — shallow depressions on either side of her head, slightly paler than the surrounding tissue — but whatever occupied them has been reabsorbed over generations, the cells repurposed for more useful things. Lateral line sensitivity. Electroreception, maybe. The ability to feel the pressure wave of the pump cycle and know, with the certainty of a body that has never known any other rhythm, exactly where she is in the room at all times without seeing any of it.

She is not aware of you in any way you would recognize as awareness. You are a pressure disturbance. You are a thermal anomaly. You are a source of unusual water displacement that briefly disrupts the laminar flow along her pipe, and she adjusts her position by a few centimeters, and that is the full extent of your significance to her. She has encountered disruptions before. The water level once shifted — rose by perhaps a centimeter over the course of a week, then settled back. A pipe bracket failed and dropped into the water with a clang that sent pressure waves careening off every surface for nearly a minute. These events are logged in her body not as memories but as calibrations. She is not an organism that experiences things. She is an organism that has been so perfectly fit to her circumstance that experience, in the mammalian sense, would be a wasteful extravagance, like installing windows in a submarine.

The others are her descendants, probably. There are eight of them — you undercounted at first because three were holding station in the shadow of a column where the current from the pumps creates a small dead zone, a pocket of relatively still water where the energetic cost of maintaining position drops to almost nothing. They hang there the way a person might lean against a wall: not resting, exactly, but not expending effort, either. Conserving. The economy of this room is tight. The biofilm grows; the fish graze; the fish excrete; the bacteria consume the excrement and grow. The loop is closed, or nearly so. The only external input is whatever the pumps bring in from elsewhere in the system — dissolved minerals, organic traces, the chemical signature of rooms and pipes and reservoirs that none of these fish will ever see. They taste those distant rooms in the water. The water from the north pipe is slightly warmer, slightly more alkaline. The water returning through the floor grate is cooler, carries a faint sulfurous note. These are the horizons of the world. These are the rumors of elsewhere.

---

The luminescent drifters are simpler and more alien.

They have no brains. They have no centralized nervous system of any kind. Each one is a small, soft machine — a few millimeters of gelatinous tissue organized around a single imperative: persist. They feed on dissolved organic matter, absorbing it directly through their outer membrane. They reproduce by budding — slowly, reluctantly, only when caloric surplus accumulates beyond a threshold that is rarely met. Their bioluminescence is not communication. It is not a mating display. It is a metabolic byproduct, a chemical overflow, the biological equivalent of a pressure relief valve venting excess energy as light. They glow because to not glow would require a mechanism for suppressing the glow, and no such mechanism has been worth the cost of evolving.

They are, in aggregate, the most important organisms in the room. Not because of what they do but because of what they indicate. Their presence means the water is oxygenated. Their presence means dissolved organic carbon is circulating. Their presence means the system — the vast, unseen plumbing of which this room is a single node — is still moving water, still cycling nutrients, still functioning at a level sufficient to support life. If the drifters died, the fish would have no warning. The biofilm would thin. The fungus on the valve wheel would contract. The room would become what it looks like it should be: dead infrastructure, a flooded mechanical space, water and iron and nothing else. The drifters are the canary. They drift, and they glow when disturbed, and they persist, and their persistence is the room's proof of its own continued viability.

They do not know this. They do not know anything. They are the simplest form of alive that this room contains, and they are everywhere, suspended in the upper water column in a loose, three-dimensional cloud that shifts and reshapes with every current, every pump cycle, every movement of every body. When you walk through them — slowly, because walking quickly in sternum-deep water is impossible — they part around you and then close behind you, and the light they produce outlines your passage the way a contrail outlines a plane, a temporary bright scar in a dark medium, fading as the disturbance fades, until you are simply a warm body in cool water again, and they have forgotten you in the only way an organism without memory can forget: by never having registered you in the first place.

---

The fungus is the loneliest organism in the room.

This is anthropomorphism. The fungus does not experience loneliness. The fungus does not experience. But the word persists because of the fungus's position: it is the only photosynthetic — no. It is not photosynthetic. There is not enough light for photosynthesis, and the caged amber bulb does not emit the right wavelengths. The fungus is chemotrophic. It is eating the iron. It is eating the rust, slowly, converting iron oxide into energy through a metabolic pathway so ancient and so marginal that it barely registers as life at all. It grows perhaps a millimeter per year. It has been growing for — well. Look at it. The main body of the colony covers an area roughly the size of your palm, centered on the hub of the third valve wheel from the left, sending pale filaments along the spokes like roads radiating from a city. The filaments are fine enough that they are invisible unless you bring your face close and let the amber light catch them at an angle. Then they appear: a network, a web, a small and patient architecture of threads, each one mining the oxide beneath it, each one advancing outward at a rate that would be imperceptible even if you watched for a year.

It is soft. You touched it — earlier, without meaning to, your hand finding the valve wheel in the dark and your fingers brushing the growth before you realized it was alive. It felt like damp velvet. It felt like the only gentle thing in a room made entirely of hard surfaces and hard water and hard sounds. You pulled your hand away, not from disgust but from a sudden, irrational sense of trespass, as if you had touched something private, something that had earned its small softness through decades of patient chemistry and did not deserve to have a strange warm hand laid upon it.

The fungus is the room's only record of biological time. The fish could be any age. The drifters bud and die in cycles too short and too uniform to mark. But the fungus grows outward from a center, and its center is its origin point, and if you could measure its radius precisely and knew its rate of growth you could calculate how long it has been here, how long since a spore arrived — carried in on the water, presumably, from elsewhere in the system — and found the rust and began the slow work of converting inorganic death into organic life. It is a clock. A very slow clock, ticking in millimeters, marking the years since the room's biology began.

---

There are other things. Smaller things. Things you sense rather than see.

In the biofilm itself — that slick, living skin that coats every underwater surface — there are billions of organisms, bacteria and archaea, the true citizens of this room, the ones who were here first and will be here last. They are the ones who built the biofilm. They are the ones who feed the fish and the drifters. They are the foundation of everything. They live in the boundary layer between water and surface, in a world measured in microns, and they experience the pump cycle as a tidal force, a rhythmic shear stress that selects for organisms that can adhere, that can flex, that can survive the endlessly repeating mechanical heartbeat of this room. They have been evolving in here. Not toward anything — evolution does not move toward — but in response to the specific, unchanging, relentless conditions of this exact room, this exact water chemistry, this exact pump rhythm. They are, by now, organisms that exist nowhere else. They are endemic to SEC-7 CIRC-N, adapted to its particular cocktail of dissolved iron and calcium and trace sulfur, adapted to its temperature, adapted to its light (or lack of light), adapted to the four-to-five-second cycle of the pumps that has been their selective pressure for longer than they have had the same genome. They are the room's own species. If you took a sample and cultured it in a lab, in different water, at a different temperature, they would die. Or they would grow, but slowly, unhappily, like a dialect spoken far from home.

There may be things in the pipes. The system extends beyond this room in every direction — up, down, laterally, through walls and floors and ceilings — and the water that circulates through it carries biology with it. What lives in the pipes between nodes? What has adapted to the flow itself, to life in transit, to the permanent current of a system that never stops cycling? You cannot know from here. But the fish sometimes orient toward the pipe openings and hold still, mouths slightly open, tasting whatever arrives. And sometimes — not often, but sometimes — one of the drifters that enters through the north pipe is slightly different from the ones already here: slightly larger, slightly more luminous, carrying the chemistry of a different room in its tissues. It joins the local population and is absorbed. Or it fails to thrive and sinks, slowly, through the turbid water, past your thighs, past your knees, past the point of visibility, down to the smooth concrete floor where it will become food for the biofilm and, eventually, for the fish. The system circulates. The system provides. The system does not distinguish between life and nutrient.

---

And you.

You are also here. You are also alive in this room. You are the warmest thing in it, the largest thing in it, the only thing in it that is aware of being in it. Your body heat is slowly leaching into the water around you, creating a faint thermal plume that rises from your shoulders and disperses against the sweating ceiling. The drifters respond to your warmth — they are slightly denser near you, drawn by the convection current, orbiting your torso in a loose, glowing halo that makes you, from a distance, look like something sacred. Or something bioluminescent yourself. Or something the room has grown.

You are breathing the air above the waterline. It is humid enough to taste: iron, ozone, the faint vegetable sweetness of the fungus, the mineral blankness of the water. Your breath fogs slightly. Your exhalations join the moisture on the ceiling, adding your own small contribution to the film of condensation that has been migrating across the iron plate for decades. You are already part of the water cycle. You are already part of the room.

The fish do not regard you. The drifters orbit you. The fungus does not know you are here. The bacteria are already colonizing the surface of your skin — not aggressively, not harmfully, but inevitably, the way they colonize every surface, because every surface is a habitat and you are no exception. You are warm and you are carbon-rich and you are standing very still, and in the logic of this room, that makes you a substrate. A surface. A place where things might grow.

You will leave, of course. The room does not assume you will stay. The room does not assume anything. But while you are here, you are the newest organism in SEC-7 CIRC-N, and the most complex, and the most temporary, and the only one that wonders what all of this is for. The fish do not wonder. The drifters do not wonder. The fungus on the valve wheel, soft and pale and patient, eating its way through iron oxide one molecule at a time, does not wonder.

The pumps cycle. The water vibrates. The reflections crawl across the ceiling. And the room's inhabitants — all of them, including you — continue the work of living in a place that was never meant for life, that no longer serves whatever purpose it was built for, that has become, through the slow accumulation of accident and adaptation, a world.

Not a good world. Not a bad world. Not a world that cares what you call it.

A world that works, in the dark, in the water, to the rhythm of the pumps, and will go on working long after the last warm-blooded visitor has climbed out and dried off and forgotten it was ever here.
