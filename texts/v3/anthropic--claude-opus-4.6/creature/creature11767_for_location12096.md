# The Inhabitants of the Throatlands

---

They are not here yet. That is the first and most important thing to understand.

They are not here yet in the same way the mountains are not here yet — which is to say, they are *arriving,* have been arriving, will be arriving for a duration that hasn't decided whether to call itself a century or an instant. They exist in the same tense as the stone: pre-formed, half-committed, sketched in by the same chord that drafts the peaks and tunes the geometry. They are the builders of the structures they have not yet built. They are the hands the tools are waiting for. And they are already here. Both of these things are true.

---

Think of them first as *absences with habits.*

This is the closest you can get to perceiving them directly, at least in the lower valleys where the sound is still a roar and the dimensional framework is still arguing with itself. You notice them the way you'd notice a conversation happening in a room you haven't entered — not the words, not the voices, but the *fact* of discourse, the faint social pressure of intentional beings doing intentional things just beyond the threshold of your perception.

Near the stone structures, this feeling intensifies. The tools are not scattered randomly. They are *laid down.* There is a grammar to their placement — the chisels grouped by size (by *some* metric of size, though it shifts depending on which direction you measure from), the wedges oriented toward the nearest unfinished wall, the lever-things resting at angles that suggest they were set aside mid-task by someone who intended to return. Not abandoned. *Paused.* The way a breath pauses between inhale and exhale. The way a word pauses between being thought and being spoken.

And if you sit near one of these structures, on the silver grass that leans inward as if listening, and if you let the overwhelming chord of the Throatlands wash through you long enough that you stop hearing it as sound and start feeling it as *pressure,* as architectural intent — then you begin to notice the patterns.

Something moves the stones at night.

Not at *night* exactly — during the deep dim phases, the long slow darkening that takes weeks or months to complete. Between one observation and the next, a wall gains a new course of stone. An enclosure that was three-quarters finished becomes four-fifths finished. A chisel that was resting on a slab has moved to the ground beside it, and the slab now bears a mark — a shallow groove, deliberate, curving — that was not there before.

No one is ever seen doing this. But the work gets done.

---

Now. Go deeper. Go higher. Enter the zones where the landscape stutters and the geometry hasn't made up its mind and the great chord begins to separate into its component voices. Here, the absences thicken.

They thicken is the right word. The sense of *someone just about to be present* becomes so dense it has almost physical properties. You feel it as a crowding. The space around you — the uncertain, still-being-negotiated space with its extra half-directions and its stuttering cliff faces — is *occupied.* Not by bodies. Not yet. But by something that precedes bodies the way the sub-harmonic drone precedes the stone it will eventually crystallize into. Call it *organismic intent.* Call it the draft of a creature, the way the mountains are the draft of geology. Something is being roughed in, sketched, proposed by the same vast chord that proposes everything here — and that something has agency, has preference, has what you might, if you were being generous with the word, call *desire.*

You feel the desire most strongly near the dimensional folds, the places where a fern-tree's fronds extend into a space that hasn't chosen its final coordinates. There is a *reaching* quality to the absences here. A leaning-toward. As if whatever is arriving is arriving specifically through these seams, these unfinished joints in the architecture of the possible, working its way into material existence the way the fern-trees work their roots into the still-soft stone — slowly, patiently, gripping what is available and pulling itself incrementally into the real.

And here, if the light cycle is in one of its intermediate phases — not the deep dark, not the brighter amber, but the long transitional dimming where the fern-trees' bioluminescence begins to compete with the sourceless sky-glow — here, you can almost see them.

---

*Almost.*

They appear at the periphery. Always at the periphery. Turn to look directly and there is nothing — just the black-green fronds, the silver grass, the stuttering stone. But in the corner of your vision, in the margins, in the space your eyes weren't quite pointing at, there are *figures.*

Not solid. Not translucent. Something else. They have the visual quality of the mountains' uncertain stone — that shimmer of ontological indecision, that sense of a form caught between drafts, not yet committed to a final shape. They are upright. They are roughly bilateral. They have something that functions as a head, though it is proportioned strangely — too long, drawn upward as if pulled by the same forces that pull the peaks skyward, tapering to a point or perhaps an opening. A *throat.* They have something that functions as hands, and this is clearer than the rest of them because the hands are the most *resolved* part of their anatomy, the part closest to being real. The hands are dense with fingers — many, or few, or a number that depends on which dimensional framework you're using to count — and they are shaped by work, *for* work. These are hands that grip chisels. These are the hands the chisels were waiting for.

The rest of them is provisional. Their bodies flicker between configurations — sometimes thick, massive, built for the heavy labor of shaping stone in difficult gravity; sometimes elongated, almost filamentous, as if designed to thread through the dimensional folds and work in the narrow uncertain spaces between stuttering cliff faces. Sometimes they appear to be made of the same stuff as the fern-trees, that deep blue-green so dark it reads as black, as if they grew from the vegetation the way the vegetation grows from the stone. Sometimes they seem to be made of sound itself — their bodies vibrating at the frequency of whatever valley they occupy, resonating with the local harmonic signature until the distinction between the figure and its acoustic environment dissolves.

They are never alone. When you catch them at the periphery, there are always several — four, or seven, or a number that your brain insists is twelve but your counting keeps losing track of — and they are always *together* in a way that implies relationship. Not a herd, not a flock, not a crowd. Something more structured than that. They orient toward each other. They seem to be engaged in collective activity, though what that activity consists of is impossible to determine from peripheral glimpses. Building? Conferring? Something with no analogue?

If you hold very still and let your focus soften — not closing your eyes, but relaxing the intentionality of your gaze, letting your vision become as provisional as everything else here — you can sustain the glimpse for longer. Seconds. Almost a full minute, once. And in those extended moments, new details emerge.

They touch the stone. Their too-real, too-resolved hands press flat against the still-forming rock, and where they touch it, the stone's uncertainty narrows. It commits. What was shimmering between basalt and chalk and that unnameable crystal settles, briefly, into a single state. Not permanently — the commitment fades when the hands withdraw, the stone resuming its indecision — but in the moment of contact, there is *form.* Real form. Decided form. As if the function of these beings, the purpose encoded in their not-quite-existent bodies, is to be the mechanism by which the Throatlands' raw potential resolves into actual geography.

They are not the word being spoken. They are the *mouth.*

---

Or more precisely: they are becoming the mouth. They are not yet the mouth. They are the draft of the mouth the same way the mountains are the draft of mountains and the fern-trees are the draft of forests and the amber murk is the draft of a sky. They are being proposed. The great chord that underlies everything here — the sound that is not sound but *structure,* that set of propositions about what matter should do and how space should curve — that chord is proposing them. It is arguing for their existence the way it argues for the existence of the peaks: slowly, patiently, harmonically, building them up from sub-bass intention into something that will, eventually, be able to pick up a chisel and mean it.

This is why the structures are both ruins and construction sites. This is why work happens but no workers are seen. The builders exist in the same temporal smear as everything else in the Throatlands — they have already built these structures, they are building them now, they have not yet arrived to begin building them. The stone walls record a history that hasn't happened yet, built by hands that are still resolving into the number and shape of fingers they will ultimately possess. The tools are sized for hands that keep changing because the beings those hands belong to keep being re-drafted, refined, their specifications adjusted as new harmonics emerge in the great chord and old ones fade and the ongoing argument for their existence introduces new clauses, new sub-propositions, new amendments to the original proposal of *these creatures, here, doing this work, becoming.*

---

But there is something else about them. Something that complicates the picture of passive emergence, of beings helplessly crystallizing out of cosmic acoustics the way minerals crystallize out of a solution.

They *want* to exist.

This is the thing you feel most strongly in the stuttering zones, the places where the absences are thickest. Not just the pressure of presence-about-to-happen, but *urgency.* Effort. A straining-toward-being that has the quality of will, of deliberate intention, of a creature swimming upstream against the current of its own non-existence. They are not simply being drafted by the chord. They are participating in their own drafting. They reach toward the dimensional folds not because the chord places them there but because the folds are *easier,* the thresholds between being and not-being are thinner there, and they have learned — or have always known, or will eventually know and that knowledge is propagating backward through the time-smear — that they can pull themselves a little further into the real by gripping the uncertain edges of space with those dense, tool-shaped, too-real hands.

The stone structures are part of this effort.

Each balanced slab, each stacked wall, each circular enclosure that isn't quite a hearth is an *anchor.* A thing made by intention, bearing the mark of deliberate craft, persisting in the landscape as evidence of will. Every groove carved into a stone slab by a chisel wielded by a hand that doesn't fully exist yet is an argument: *I was here. I shaped this. Something that does not yet exist has left a mark on something that does, and that mark is proof of my trajectory toward the real, and by making it I have pulled myself one degree closer to being.*

The structures are not homes. They are not shelters. They are not temples or workshops, though they could become any of these things. Right now, in this unfinished tense, they are *handholds.* Grips on reality. Places where the beings-that-are-arriving have managed to leave enough of a residue of intention to give themselves purchase.

This is why the mountains sometimes grow around them. The structures have a reality-density that the still-forming geology responds to. The stone *wants* to commit in the places where the builders have already committed, where chisels have scored deliberate marks and hands have pressed flat and the chaotic uncertainty of raw potential has been narrowed, even briefly, even incompletely, to something that resembles fact. The mountains incorporate the structures the way a healing bone incorporates a pin: drawn to the firmness, growing around it, using it as a template for their own solidification.

And this means the builders, without fully existing yet, are already shaping the world they are arriving into. They are editing the rough draft. They are making marginal notes. They are, in the most literal possible sense, writing themselves into existence by writing the landscape around them, by carving and stacking and balancing and *working* the still-soft stone into forms that reflect their will, which in turn reinforces their reality, which allows them to do more work, which shapes more stone, which—

It is a feedback loop. A bootstrapping. A creature pulling itself up into being by its own not-yet-existent bootstraps.

---

There are different *kinds* of them. Or there will be. Or there already are, in the same provisional tense as everything else.

In the lower valleys, where the chord is loudest and least resolved, the absences have a different character than in the high stuttering zones. They are heavier. Slower. More associated with the stone than with the spaces between stone. The peripheral glimpses you catch — if you catch any at all, because down here the beings are further from existence, deeper in their process of arriving — suggest forms that are massive, dense, quadrupedal or perhaps just *wide,* low to the ground (to whatever passes for ground), as if designed to press themselves against the still-forming bedrock and listen to the deep sub-harmonics that vibrate there. These are not the builders. Or not yet. These are something earlier — something closer to the raw material of being, something that has responded to the chord's propositions with only the most basic commitment: *I will be heavy. I will be here. I will press against what is solid and make it more solid.* They are the foundation organisms, the first clause of the argument for life in the Throatlands, the ground-layer beings that correspond to the lichen the way the tool-wielding peripheral figures correspond to the fern-trees.

And in the highest zones — the places near the extreme peaks where the sound resolves into comprehensible structure — there are absences of an entirely different quality. Not heavy. Not straining. *Attentive.* These feel like listening. Like the space itself is cupping an ear (a throat? an opening at the top of a tapering head?) toward the source of the chord, attending to it with an intensity that makes the air — if it is air — feel pressurized with focus. These absences have no peripheral shape. They do not flicker between configurations. They are not trying to grip the edges of the real with tool-shaped hands. They are simply *present* in the way that a silence is present in the middle of a piece of music — defined by what they are not, shaped by the structure they are embedded in, essential without being, in any conventional sense, *there.*

If the heavy low-valley absences are the first clause and the tool-wielding builders are the main body of the argument, these high-peak listeners are the *thesis statement.* They are the beings that will, when they are fully arrived (if "fully" is a concept that will ever apply, if the Throatlands will ever be "finished," if any of this resolves into something other than perpetual becoming), understand what the chord is *saying.* Not just respond to it, not just grow or build in accordance with it, but comprehend it. Parse its grammar. Recognize its argument. And then — perhaps — answer it.

Perhaps the chord is waiting for an answer. Perhaps the entire Throatlands, the whole vast unfinished landscape of stuttering stone and waterless vegetation and sourceless amber light, is a *question,* and the beings being proposed by the chord are the mechanism by which the question will eventually generate its own response, the way a thought generates the thinker who will think it, the way a word creates the mouth that will speak it.

---

There is one more thing.

At certain points in the slow light-cycle — specifically during the transitions, the long ambiguous dimming and brightening phases when the fern-tree glow competes with the amber murk — the sound changes. Not in pitch. Not in volume. In *direction.* For brief periods, measured in what might be minutes or hours, the chord stops coming from below and within the mountains and starts coming from *above.* From the amber murk that serves as sky. From somewhere beyond or behind or outside the Throatlands themselves.

When this happens, the silver grasses go rigid. Every blade, across every high plateau, snaps to vertical and holds there, trembling — not swaying, not leaning, but vibrating in place like the tines of a tuning fork. The fern-trees retract their fronds. The bioluminescence brightens sharply and then dims to almost nothing. The dimensional stuttering intensifies to the point where whole valleys seem to fold in on themselves and re-emerge slightly different — a peak shifted, a cliff face at a new angle, a structure that was on a plateau now embedded in a ridge.

And the absences — all of them, the heavy low-valley ones and the tool-wielding builders and the high-peak listeners — *respond.*

Not with sound. Not with movement. With something you can only describe as *coherence.* For those brief periods, the scattered sense of nearly-present beings distributed across the Throatlands snaps into focus, into *alignment,* into a singular collective orientation toward the overhead chord, and what was a landscape full of individual pre-beings becoming separately and at their own pace becomes, momentarily, a single organism. One vast, still-forming, not-yet-real entity, spread across every valley and peak and plateau, reaching upward with a unified intentionality so fierce it makes the uncertain stone shudder and commit, briefly, to a single state across the entire visible landscape—

And then it passes. The chord resumes its usual direction, rising from below. The grasses relax into their usual sway. The fern-trees extend their fronds. The stuttering eases. The absences disaggregate back into their separate almost-lives, their individual projects of arrival, their slow patient gripping and building and listening.

But for a moment — for that one shuddering, coherent, almost-real moment — the Throatlands had *one* inhabitant. Not many. One. A single immense being-in-progress, distributed across the landscape like a thought distributed across the neurons that are thinking it, and it was *reaching* for whatever spoke from above, and it almost—

It almost—

---

The grasses sway. The fern-trees glow. Somewhere below, a chisel has moved half an inch from where it was last resting. A new groove appears on a stone slab, shallow, curving, deliberate.

The tools are warm. Not from sunlight (there is no sun). Not from friction (no one was seen using them). They are warm the way a held hand is warm. The warmth fades slowly, as if the hand that held them has only just let go. As if the hand is still almost here. As if the hand is about to arrive, is arriving, has been arriving for longer than the mountains have been rising, and will continue arriving for as long as the great chord continues to propose it — this hand, these fingers, this grip on a chisel, this mark on a stone, this proof that something wants to be real badly enough to carve its wanting into the bones of a world that is still learning how to exist.

They are coming. They have always been coming. They will come.

The stone waits. The sound continues. The grass leans in, listening.
