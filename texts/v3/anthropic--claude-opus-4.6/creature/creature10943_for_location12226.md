# Who Lives in the Nursery Below

---

They live in the spaces between disruptions, which is to say they live in no fixed space at all.

You have to understand first that they are small. Not diminutive in the way that suggests fragility — small in the way that a fist is small, in the way that a knot is small. Compact. Dense with purpose. They stand perhaps to your waist if they straightened fully, but they rarely straighten fully. They move in a low, fluid crouch, their weight centered over wide feet that grip the obsidian with a sureness that the crab-things' radial legs can only approximate. Their skin is dark — not uniformly, not the way a single pigment would make it, but mottled and variegated, a terrain of tones from charcoal to rust-red to the pale calcium white of the pool-edges, and the pattern shifts over the course of days as mineral dust and steam-deposit and their own secretions build up and slough away. They are, chromatically, indistinguishable from the ground they walk on. This is not camouflage in the deliberate sense. It is saturation. They are soaked in this place the way a rag is soaked in the water it's been wrung from.

Their hands are extraordinary. Four fingers, all of roughly equal length, opposable against each other in pairs — not the single-thumb architecture you'd expect but a kind of bilateral grip, like two sets of pliers nested together. The fingers are thick-padded at the tips, heat-resistant, calloused into something that is almost mineral itself, a keratin so dense andite that they can — and do — pick up stones that are still too hot to touch by any other skin they possess. They knap obsidian by feel and by sound. They know the rock's fracture planes the way you know the vowels in your own name, instinctively and completely, and when they strike a core to flake a blade, the motion is so practiced and so quick that it looks like a tic, like a habitual gesture, like someone tapping their fingers on a table while thinking about something else entirely. They may in fact be thinking about something else entirely. The toolmaking seems to cost them almost no attention. It is infrastructure-level cognition, below the threshold of effort.

Their faces — and here is where you might start to feel something shift in your chest, some recognition or its inverse — their faces are broad, flat, dominated by nostrils. Not eyes. The eyes are there, small and deep-set beneath a heavy brow ridge, functional in this golden light but clearly secondary. The nostrils are the primary apparatus: wide, mobile, flanged with muscular tissue that flares and contracts in constant rhythmic motion, tasting the air the way your eyes scan a landscape. They smell the mineral composition of a new upwelling before it breaks the surface. They smell which pools are draining and which are deepening. They smell the crab-things' stress pheromones when a thermal event displaces a colony, and they move toward that displacement because scattered crab-things are easier to harvest than clustered ones. They smell each other, constantly, in a flow of chemical information so dense and continuous that it may constitute something like language, or at least like music — a shifting, layered, harmonic exchange of data about internal state, intention, spatial memory, and the current configuration of a world that reconfigures itself every few hours.

They don't speak. Not with sound, not in the way you mean when you say *speak.* They would be drowned out anyway — the cavern's baseline roar would swallow any vocalization subtle enough to carry the nuance they need. But they are not silent. They click. A sharp, deliberate percussion made with tongue against palate, or with two obsidian flakes struck together, or with their thick-padded fingers snapped against their own thighs. These clicks carry in the cavern's acoustics the way the crab-things' collective ticking carries — as texture, as presence-signal, as a way of saying *I am here, I am here, I am still here* to others who may be fifty meters away and navigating by nostril through a steam plume. The clicks bounce off the new stone formations and return with information about the shape of the space, which is always new, which must always be re-learned. They echolocate, but crudely, approximately — enough to know that a wall has risen where no wall was, that a passage has opened where yesterday was solid rock. Their mental map of this place must be less like a map and more like a weather forecast, a probabilistic sketch that they update continuously and hold lightly, ready to abandon the moment the ground shifts again.

---

They live in groups. Not families, exactly — the composition is too fluid for that word, too responsive to the terrain's moods. A group might be six or sixteen, coalescing around a resource that exists for a few days — a long-lived pool, a particularly productive thermal vent, a section of new floor that the crab-things have colonized so densely that harvesting is effortless. When the resource shifts or vanishes, the group dissolves and reforms elsewhere, its members mixing with members of other dissolved groups, combining and recombining in patterns that probably follow kinship lines but also probably follow something else — affinity, maybe, or complementary skill, or simply the chemical draw of one body's scent-signature harmonizing well with another's.

The children are carried. Always. In slings made from the cured, dried skin of the serpentine wall-hunters — a material that is tough, flexible, and slightly mucilaginous even when dry, which means it grips and doesn't slide. The slings are worn across the chest, positioning the infant's face near the carrier's nostrils, and this sustained proximity may be how the scent-language is learned: months of breathing in the carrier's chemical output, that dense stream of molecular information, the infant's developing brain soaking in it the way the infant's skin soaks in the mineral-rich humidity, building the architecture it will need to parse the world's constant chemical speech.

The infants are born — and here you see why this place is called what it is — frequently. Staggeringly frequently. The warm, mineral-saturated environment seems to accelerate their reproductive cycle, or perhaps it's the other way around: their reproductive rate evolved to match a world where mortality is high and unpredictable and the ground might literally open beneath you on any given day. The infants are small and dark and nearly silent, their eyes sealed shut for the first weeks, their nostrils already wide and working. They are born into the sling and they stay in the sling and when they outgrow the sling they toddle on the warm obsidian with the same low-centered sureness as the adults, and within what seems like a shockingly short time they are knapping their own small blades, ticking their own small clicks, reading the cavern's moods through their own wide flaring nostrils.

Many of them die. You should know this. The place that births everything also takes freely. A thermal surge. A floor collapse. A cascade of serpentine hunters pouring down a wall onto a group that didn't smell them massing. The pools that drain suddenly, taking a wading toddler down into a crack that closes behind them. The adults do not perform grief the way you would recognize it. They perform *adjustment.* A recalibration of the group's scent-profile, a re-weighting of who carries what, who scouts which flank, who sleeps nearest the vent. The loss registers chemically — the missing member's scent-signature fading from the group's collective cloud over the course of hours — and the group reconstitutes around the absence the way the crab-things reconstitute around a new ridge of stone. Not callously. Not without something that might be sorrow. But efficiently. The world here does not pause, so they do not pause. They process in motion, in the act of continuing.

---

Look more carefully at the structures. The stacked-stone walls around the pools — those are not individual projects. They are maintained communally, and the maintenance is constant, because the pools shift and the stones must be restacked and rechanneled every time the seepage finds a new path. You can see, if you watch long enough, that different groups maintain different catchments, and that the catchment-building follows a kind of informal protocol: a group arriving at a draining pool will assess the remaining water, assess the seepage pattern, and decide — through some chemical consensus that takes less than a minute, a rapid-fire exchange of flared nostrils and subtle shifts in body odor — whether to reinforce this catchment or abandon it and search for the next upwelling. The decision is almost always right. They have been reading this place's hydrology for longer than you can guess, and their predictive accuracy is startling. They know which cracks will refill. They know which thermal events precede a new seepage. They know, in some way that lives below conscious reasoning and above mere instinct, the *rhythm* of this restless geology, its moods and tendencies and periodicities, the way a sailor knows the sea.

The bone-frame structures — drying racks, you decide, watching one in use. Strips of serpentine-hunter flesh draped over the lashed-together long bones, positioned in the rising heat of a vent, drying into a jerky-like preservation that lets them carry protein when they move. The frames are light enough to carry and are carried, disassembled into component bones and bundled, when a group relocates. They are the only possessions that move with any consistency. Everything else — the stone tools, the shell caches, the small hearthless hearths around the thermal vents — is made, used, and abandoned with a fluency that speaks not of poverty but of abundance. Why carry a blade when every new obsidian flow gives you material for a hundred blades? Why hoard crab-shells when the fields are so dense that harvesting takes less effort than carrying? They are rich in the way that only those who live inside their wealth can be rich. The cavern provides. The cavern has always provided. The cavern provides so constantly and so overwhelmingly that the concept of scarcity may not exist in their chemical vocabulary.

But the bone frames. Those they keep. And you notice that each frame is slightly different — the bones chosen for length and curvature, the lashing pattern, the angles of the joints — and that individuals within a group will reach for a specific frame the way you would reach for a familiar tool, by memory and preference. The frames are *theirs.* Personal. Made and remade and repaired across what might be years, the bones replaced one at a time as they crack or warp, until no original component remains but the pattern persists, the shape persists, the hand-feel persists. It is the only thing they own and it is not a thing. It is a continuity. It is the one fixed point in a world of constant flux, and they carry it with them the way you carry your name.

---

Now. The thing you've been sensing but not seeing.

There are, in the deeper recesses of the cavern system — the places where the light is dimmest, where the ceiling veins have not yet fractured, where the stone is newest and hottest — larger structures. Not stacked stones. Not bone frames. These are shaped formations, natural pillars and ridges that have been *modified* — surfaces smoothed by hand, channels carved into them, small hollows scooped out of their faces. The hollows hold things. Crab-shells arranged in patterns. Flakes of obsidian, selected for color and translucency, pressed into the soft mineral deposits that coat the stone. Bones, again, but not functional bones — small bones, infant bones, cleaned and placed with an intentionality that stops your breath.

These are not tools and they are not caches. They are — you don't want to say the word too quickly, because the word carries so much weight and you are not sure these beings carry weight the same way — but they are *markers.* Memorials. Records. The small bones in their careful hollows are the bones of infants who didn't survive, and they have been placed here, in the hottest and most active part of the cavern, where new stone is most likely to flow over them and seal them in, preserving them, making them permanent in the one way this environment allows: by burying them in the geology itself.

The adults come here rarely and in small numbers. Two or three, never a full group. They come with the chemical cloud of their group still clinging to them but fading as they move deeper, so that by the time they reach the carved pillars they are stripped of the collective scent, reduced to their own individual signature, raw and unblended and — if your nose were sensitive enough, you suspect — unbearably specific. They stand before the hollows and their nostrils work and their hands touch the arranged shells and their clicks fall silent. They are reading the record. They are smelling the traces of others who have stood here — other groups, other configurations of this endlessly recombinant community — and they are adding their own trace, their own chemical footnote, to a palimpsest of scent-memory that may stretch back generations.

Then they place what they have brought. A shell. A blade. A small bone. They press it into the mineral deposit with their heat-resistant fingertips and they hold it there until the calcium-rich water seeping through the stone begins to cement it in place, and they wait, motionless, for what might be an hour, the only still figures in this entire restless world, standing in the heat and the dim light and the deep infrasonic hum with their hands on the rock and their eyes closed and their nostrils wide open, breathing in the accumulated sorrow and continuity and identity of everyone who has ever stood here and done this same thing.

This is what they have instead of permanence. This is what they have *as* permanence — this one place where things are intentionally fixed, intentionally held, intentionally kept still in defiance of a world that holds still for nothing. The stone will flow over it eventually. The geology will claim it. But for now, for the span of days or months before the next thermal event reshapes this chamber, the small bones and the bright shells and the translucent blades will stay where they were placed, and the scent-record will linger, and anyone who comes here will know that something was lost and someone marked the loss and the marking mattered enough to be the one thing in this entire seething, birthing, dying, reforming world that was done slowly, and quietly, and alone.

---

They go back. They always go back. Out of the deep chambers, into the noise and light and steam, into the clicking fields and the shrieking upper air, into their groups that are already shifting, already reforming, already adapting to whatever new terrain has emerged in their absence. They unsling their bone frames and drape fresh-caught serpentine meat over the joints and position the whole assembly near a vent and crouch in their low, balanced way and begin to knap new blades from whatever obsidian the world has just offered up, and the crab-things click around their feet and the fliers wheel overhead and the pools fill and drain and fill again, and the light shifts, golden and warm and restless, as another vein cracks open in the ceiling and the cavern rearranges itself one more time, one more time, one more time, always one more time, and they ride it, they have always ridden it, they will ride it until the heat cools or the stone sets or the water stops rising, and maybe not even then, maybe they will find the next warm crack, the next fresh floor, the next nursery below the nursery below, and begin again.

They are not the most numerous thing here. They are not the most powerful. They are not the apex or the keystone or the dominant species. They are a thread in the weave, a single voice in the choir, and what makes them extraordinary is not that they have risen above the tumult but that they have found, within it — within the boiling, unfinished, unceasing chaos of a world still deciding what it is — small moments of stillness. Small acts of keeping. A wall that holds water an hour longer. A frame that persists through a hundred repairs. A hollow in hot stone where a small bone rests and someone's breath has left a chemical ghost that says: *this one was here. This one was real. Remember.*

The cavern doesn't remember. The cavern is always new.

They remember for it.
