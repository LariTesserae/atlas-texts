# The Inhabitants of the Hanging Lakes

---

Start with the smallest thing, because that is what came first, and because here, smallness is not a limitation but a way of being woven into everything.

---

## The Film-Swimmers

In the water-skin between moss and stone — that layer no thicker than a fingernail, that everywhere-river that moves so slowly it might as well be still — there are worms. Not the flatworms already noted, though those are here too, translucent and simple and easily understood. These are something else.

They are segmented, barely visible, each one the length of a grain of rice and the width of a hair. Their bodies are glassy, not merely translucent but actively transparent, so that what you see when you look at one is not the organism but the faint distortion it creates in the water around it — a moving lens, a tiny aberration in the film. They have no eyes. They have no heads, exactly. Each end of the body is functionally identical; they move in whichever direction they were last moving, and when they encounter an obstacle they simply reverse, the trailing end becoming the leading end with no pause or confusion. They graze on the bacterial film that coats the underside of the moss, rasping it with mouthparts so small they are theoretical — implied by the clean trails they leave rather than observed directly.

But here is the thing about them. They are never alone.

They move in associations of dozens, sometimes hundreds, their transparent bodies aligning in parallel formations within the water-film like iron filings near a magnet. They don't touch. They maintain a spacing of roughly their own body-width, and they move in unison — not perfectly, not mechanically, but with the soft coherence of a flock of something, each individual adjusting to the movements of its neighbors with a lag so slight it looks like thought. When the association encounters a region of richer bacterial growth, it slows collectively. When the water-film thins near the edge of a rock, the association splits, flows around the dry spot in two streams, and reconverges on the other side. No individual leads. No individual decides. The intelligence, if you can call it that, lives in the spacing between them, in the consistency of their attention to each other.

They reproduce by fragmentation. An individual simply stops, and its middle segments soften, and over the course of a day it becomes two individuals, each already moving in the direction it was pointed. The new individuals are absorbed into the nearest association without ceremony. If an association grows too large — and there seems to be a threshold, something around three hundred — it fissures along a line that no one chooses, the two halves drifting apart over hours, each becoming its own complete thing. They don't compete. There is nothing to compete for. The bacterial film is everywhere. The water is everywhere. They simply *are*, in their thousands, in their tens of thousands, a civilization of transparent worms living in a layer of water too thin to see.

They are, as far as can be determined, the most numerous complex organisms in the rift.

## The Crust-Grazers

The pale crustacean-like creatures deserve more attention than a passing mention.

They live in the pools that collect at the bases of fern fronds, in the cupped hands of bromeliad-like rosettes, in any small depression in the moss where water gathers into a volume rather than a film. Each pool is a world. Each world is small — a few centimeters across, a centimeter deep, warm from the ambient air, rich with algae that tints the water a faint gold-green.

The crust-grazers are the size of lentils. Their bodies are ovoid, faintly ridged, the color of old ivory. They have too many legs — not in a way that suggests extravagance but in a way that suggests caution, as if the blueprint called for redundancy at every stage. Twelve legs, fourteen, it's difficult to count because several are always tucked beneath the body, and the creature's posture changes depending on what it's doing. Feeding, it hunches low and extends the front four pairs, using them to scrape algae from the inner surfaces of its pool with a motion that is less like eating and less like working and more like *tending* — slow, thorough, circular, covering every surface before beginning again. Resting, it retracts all legs and sits on the bottom of its pool like a pale pebble, motionless for hours.

They are solitary. One per pool, rarely two. When two are found in the same pool, they orient to face away from each other and seem to pretend the other doesn't exist with a determination that borders on philosophy. They do not fight. They do not interact. They simply allocate the pool's surface area into two halves by unspoken agreement and graze their respective territories in mirrored patterns, never crossing the invisible line.

How they reproduce is unclear. New individuals appear in empty pools — pools that were unoccupied one day and occupied the next — and they are already full-sized when they appear, which suggests either a very rapid growth phase or a migration so slow and so well-camouflaged against the moss that it has never been observed. Perhaps they walk, at night or during the slow amber hours, climbing out of one pool and crossing the vast moss-scape to find an empty one, their too-many legs carrying them over terrain that to them must seem as varied and treacherous as a mountain range. Perhaps the journey takes days. Perhaps they stop and hunker down in the water-film between pools, waiting, reading some chemical signal that says *this way, there is an empty place, it is yours*. No one has seen this happen. But the pools fill, and the creatures are there, and the algae is grazed in careful circles, and the system persists.

They are, in their own tiny way, farmers. The grazing pattern prevents any single algal species from dominating the pool, maintaining a diversity that keeps the water clear and oxygenated. A pool whose crust-grazer dies — if they die, if such a thing has been observed — would presumably cloud over within weeks, the algae blooming unchecked, the water turning opaque and then anoxic and then foul. The crust-grazer doesn't know this. It grazes because grazing is what it does. But the effect is stewardship, and the pool is healthier for its presence, and this is a relationship so old and so settled that it no longer looks like anything at all. It looks like a pebble sitting in a puddle.

## The Thread-Hangers

Now look up.

The curtains of green that trail from the undersides of the hanging lakes are not only plant matter. Living among them — living *as* them, in a way that makes the distinction between habitat and inhabitant difficult to maintain — are organisms that defy easy categorization. They are not plants. They are not animals. They are something that this young rift is still working out.

Each one begins as a spore, or something like a spore — a packet of cells released into the water of a hanging lake by a parent organism, drifting in the warm, slow currents until chance or gentle convection carries it to the lake's lower surface. There, it attaches. And it grows downward.

The body of a thread-hanger is a single long filament, unbranching, somewhere between a root and a tentacle and a strand of silk. It can reach lengths of three meters or more in mature individuals. It is green — photosynthetic, or at least partly so, its cells containing symbiotic organisms that capture what light filters through the water above. But it also feeds in other ways. The filament is coated in a thin mucus that traps microscopic organisms from the saturated air — bacteria, fungal spores, pollen-like particles from the cliff-face plants, the occasional drifting cell of unknown origin. These are slowly absorbed, digested, incorporated. The thread-hanger is simultaneously a plant and a predator and a filter and a piece of architecture, its body thickening over years as it adds layers, its surface becoming textured, becoming habitat, becoming the thing that other things grow on.

And they grow in curtains — hundreds of filaments hanging from a single lake, close enough that they touch, close enough that in still air they tangle slightly, forming a loose fabric that sways with a motion too slow to see in real time but visible if you could watch over hours, a gentle pendulum driven by the thermal drift. From a distance, a mature hanging lake trailing its full complement of thread-hangers looks like a floating island with a green beard, or like a jellyfish made of moss, or like nothing else at all, really — like itself, which is the only honest comparison.

Here is what makes them most strange: they are aware of the lake's coherence.

When a hanging lake begins to lose stability — and this is a process that takes hours, sometimes days, the water's hold on its own shape weakening incrementally — the thread-hangers respond. They contract. Slowly, over the same timescale as the lake's dissolution, each filament shortens, pulling itself upward, thickening as it compresses. By the time the lake unfolds into its descending mist, the thread-hangers have retracted to a fraction of their extended length, clinging to the last dense core of water as it disperses. Some of them survive the transition, finding themselves attached to the next lake that forms in roughly the same region of the rift (and they do form in roughly the same regions — there are favored spots, nodes of stability in the atmosphere's architecture, where the physics of suspension works best). Some don't survive. Their contracted bodies fall with the mist, landing on ledges or the rift floor, where they die and decompose and become soil. The ones that survive extend again, slowly, over weeks, resuming their hang.

This means the thread-hangers experience a cycle — extension and retraction, growth and compression — that is tied to the lifespan of the particular lake they inhabit. A lake that persists for years will host long, mature thread-hangers with thick filaments and complex surface ecosystems. A lake that lasts only months will trail thin, young, sparse curtains. The thread-hangers are, in this way, a living record of hydrological stability. They are clocks made of mucus and chlorophyll, measuring the persistence of the impossible.

## The Lens-Drifters

Inside the hanging lakes.

This is where the observation becomes most uncertain, because seeing into a body of water suspended in mid-air from a position on the rift wall is an exercise in refraction and frustration. But there are shapes in there. In the larger lakes — the ones the size of a village common, the ones that have persisted long enough to develop a faint internal coloration, a greenish-gold tint that suggests a mature microbial community — there are shapes.

They are round. Or roundish. Flattened, like the lakes themselves, as if the same forces that hold the water in its oblate form also influence the bodies of whatever lives within. They are the size of coins, or the size of hands, or — in one case, seen once, in the largest and oldest lake in the rift, through a brief window of clarity when the ambient mist thinned for an hour — the size of a shield, a broad disc turning with excruciating slowness in the body of the water, catching the diffuse light and seeming almost to glow from within.

They might be colonial organisms — aggregations of simpler things that have found advantage in combining. They might be single organisms of a kind that doesn't exist anywhere else, evolved (or evolving, still, now, the experiment not yet concluded) in conditions that exist nowhere else. They move, but only in the way that the water moves — carried by the slow internal convection that circulates warm water from the sun-facing upper surface down to the cooler underside and back. They might be moving under their own power. It's impossible to tell. A creature embedded in a current and a creature choosing to go the direction of the current look identical from outside.

What can be said is this: they are present in every lake above a certain size. They are absent from the smaller ones. This suggests either a minimum volume of water necessary to support them, or a minimum persistence — the smaller lakes don't last long enough for the lens-drifters to establish. Or it suggests that the lens-drifters *are* the reason the larger lakes are larger and more stable, that their presence somehow reinforces the water's coherence, their bodies acting as nucleation points or structural elements in the physics of suspension. This is speculation. But it is the kind of speculation this place invites — the kind where cause and effect have not yet separated, where the inhabitants and the habitat are still negotiating which one is which.

The ripple that was observed — the one that moved across a mid-sized lake as if something turned over just below the surface — may have been a lens-drifter. It may have been the only direct evidence of their movement ever observed at the surface. Or it may have been a thermal artifact, a convection cell expressing itself as a visible disturbance. Or it may have been the lake itself, adjusting, settling, doing whatever it is that hanging water does to maintain its impossible shape. The rift keeps its ambiguities.

## The Moss-Cradle Colonies

Back to the rock. Back to the shelves and ledges where the cliff meets the air and the vegetation piles up in its meters-deep sponge.

Deep in the moss — not at the surface, not in the water-film, but *deep*, in the layers of dead and compressed moss that have been accumulating since the rift was young — there are structures. They are not built. They are grown. They are fungal, or something adjacent to fungal: networks of pale filaments that permeate the compressed moss like a second circulatory system, threading through the dead plant matter, digesting it, extracting what nutrients remain, and in the process creating cavities.

The cavities are spherical. They range from the size of a marble to the size of a human head. Their inner surfaces are lined with a dense mat of the pale filaments, slightly luminescent — not light, exactly, but a faint pale glow that might be bioluminescence or might be a trick of the eye in a dark place, the brain inventing light where it expects it. The air inside the cavities is different from the air outside: warmer, drier, enriched with carbon dioxide from the fungal respiration. They are, in effect, tiny enclosed atmospheres, microenvironments buried in the moss, each one a self-contained terrarium of unusual conditions.

And they are inhabited.

The inhabitants are — well. This is where description becomes most difficult, because the inhabitants are small and soft and live in the dark and have never been seen in anything like clear light. What can be said: they are invertebrate. They are pale, almost white, with a faint translucency that suggests a body plan not far removed from the flatworms of the water-film, though larger — the biggest ones fill a head-sized cavity almost completely. They are moist. They are slow. They seem to spend most of their time pressed against the fungal lining of their cavity, and whether they are feeding on the fungus or the fungus is feeding on them or they are engaged in some mutualism so intimate it amounts to a single organism with two genomes is not clear. The fungus grows toward the inhabitant. The inhabitant orients toward the densest growth of fungus. They exist in a closeness that might be predation or symbiosis or something for which no word yet exists.

What is most remarkable about them is their distribution. The cavities are not random. They are spaced with a regularity that implies communication — each cavity separated from its nearest neighbor by a consistent distance, roughly thirty centimeters, and connected to its neighbors by thicker-than-average fungal threads that pulse (or seem to pulse, the observation is uncertain) with slow rhythms. The network of cavities and connections, if mapped, would resemble a nervous system, or a city grid, or a constellation — some organized pattern of nodes and links that extends through the deep moss of every major ledge in the rift.

Tap the moss above one cavity, and — nothing happens immediately. But over the next hour, the inhabitants in the neighboring cavities shift. They orient differently. They press more firmly against their fungal walls, or less firmly. Something has been communicated, though what, and whether it constitutes information or merely disturbance, is a question the rift is not yet ready to answer.

They might be the oldest complex life here. The deep moss is the oldest substrate. The fungal network may have been growing since the first mosses colonized the first bare rock of the newly raised plateau. If so, these pale soft things in their buried spheres have been here from nearly the beginning, growing their quiet network in the dark while the ferns rose above them and the hanging lakes condensed out of the impossible air and the whole young ecosystem assembled itself overhead. They are the foundation — not in a structural sense, but in a temporal one. They were here first. They will likely be here last. When the rift eventually weathers into old, soft mountains and the hanging lakes lose whatever atmospheric trick sustains them and the tree ferns give way to whatever comes next, the deep moss will still be there, compressed and ancient, and in it the pale things will still be pressing against their fungal walls, communicating slowly about nothing in particular, maintaining a network whose purpose may never become clear because it may not have one yet.

They are still becoming what they are. Like everything here.

## The Almost-Seen

There is one more.

It has to be addressed, even though addressing it honestly means admitting that the evidence is almost nonexistent. It might not be real. It might be a pattern the mind imposes on a place this strange, the brain's insistence on finding a large, comprehensible animal in a world of worms and spores and fungal networks, because the brain wants something it can recognize.

But.

On the aerial bogs — those spongy masses of living and dead plant matter suspended between cliff face and hanging lake, those impossible floating wetlands that have no right to exist — there are, occasionally, depressions. Not pools. Not cavities. Depressions, as if something heavy rested there and compressed the sponge and then left. They are large. The largest observed was roughly two meters long and one meter wide, an ovoid compression in the surface of the bog, deeper at the center than at the edges, the moss and plant matter slowly springing back over the course of days.

There are no tracks leading to or from the depressions. There are no tracks anywhere on the bogs. Whatever made them either arrived from the air — dropped, floated, descended in some way that left no trace — or was produced by the bog itself, some internal process of settling or gas release that created the compression from within. The second explanation is more likely. The first is more interesting.

Once — once — at the edge of perception, in the amber light of late day, with the mist thickening and the far wall of the rift gone entirely and the caustic light-patterns deepening to gold on the near cliff: a shape. On a bog. Large. Larger than anything else alive in the rift by an order of magnitude. Low, rounded, the color of wet moss — which means it could have *been* moss, a hummock, an irregularity in the bog's surface mistaken for something else. It did not move during the period of observation, which was brief, because the mist closed in and the bog vanished behind a curtain of suspended water and when the air cleared again an hour later, the shape was gone.

Or was not gone but was no longer distinguishable from the bog itself.

Or had never been there.

This is the kind of creature this place would produce, if it produced large creatures at all: something so slow and so saturated and so thoroughly integrated with its environment that the distinction between organism and habitat dissolves. Something that rests on the floating bogs because it is, in some sense, *made of* the same stuff as the floating bogs — moss, water, accumulated organic matter, fungal networks, the whole wet living fabric of the rift compressed into a body that moves (if it moves) on a timescale measured in days. Something that eats by absorbing, the way the moss absorbs water, the way the thread-hangers absorb particles from the air — not hunting, not grazing, just slowly incorporating whatever it rests upon into itself. Something that reproduces once in a lifetime, or less, budding off a portion of its body that drifts to another bog and begins the decades-long process of becoming a new individual. Something with no brain and no need for one, its behavior so simple — rest, absorb, occasionally move — that a fungal network could manage it, and maybe does, maybe the moss-cradle colonies in the deep ledge-moss extend their pale threads out into the bogs and into this creature's body and it is not so much an organism as an outgrowth of the network, a mobile node, a slow-walking extension of the oldest living system in the rift.

Maybe.

Or maybe it is just moss, and the depression was gas, and the shape was mist, and the rift has no large creatures because it doesn't need them, because the experiment it is running is not about size but about saturation, about how thoroughly life can permeate a space, how thin an organism can spread itself, how slow a metabolism can go and still count as alive.

The rift does not clarify. The rift is young and has not decided yet what it is making. The water hangs. The light glows. The pale things pulse in their buried spheres. The thread-hangers extend and retract with the lives of their impossible lakes. The film-swimmers school in their legions in water too thin to see. And maybe, on a bog that shouldn't exist, something large and slow and indistinguishable from its world rests in a shallow depression and breathes with the rift's own breath, in and out, wet and warm, patient beyond comprehension, waiting to find out what it is.

---

Everything here is waiting to find out what it is. That is what lives in this place: the *becoming*. Not creatures in a habitat but a habitat in the process of deciding whether certain parts of itself are creatures. The lines haven't been drawn yet. The categories haven't hardened. The film-swimmers might merge with their bacterial film and become something new. The crust-grazers and their algae pools might fuse into a single self-sustaining unit that no longer needs to graze because it *is* the grazing. The thread-hangers might become the lakes. The moss-cradle colonies might become the mountain.

Give it time. The rock is young. The strata are still tilted and sharp. The water still hangs in the air like a held breath, like a question the world asked and hasn't answered yet, and everything that lives here lives in the shape of that question, unresolved, open, dripping with possibility, waiting for the answer that might never come because the waiting itself — the suspension, the almost, the not-yet — might be the point.

The place breathes. Its inhabitants breathe with it. They are the same breath.
