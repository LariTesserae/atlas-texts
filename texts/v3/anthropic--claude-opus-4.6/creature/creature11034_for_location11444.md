I'll sit with this for a long time before I speak. The silence asks that of me.

# Who Lives in the Underloom

---

## The Tessellates

Start with the most numerous, because they are the ground truth of this place—so prevalent they *are* the landscape until you learn to distinguish them from it.

They have no individual names. They may have no individual selves, not in the way that would make naming useful. Each one is a pentagonal plate roughly fifteen centimeters across at its widest, perhaps four millimeters thick at the center tapering to edges so fine they become translucent, and in that translucency you can see the internal structure: a lattice of silicate crystal organized in radial symmetry around a central node that glows—faintly, barely, a ghost of the Loom-light it feeds on—with its own phosphorescence. Five arms. Five planes of symmetry. The geometry is not decorative. It is *structural*. Each arm is a distinct crystal axis, and the angles between them are precisely 72 degrees, and this is not because evolution selected for pentagonal symmetry but because pentagonal symmetry is what emerges when five crystal systems attempt to grow outward from a single nucleation point in a silicate matrix saturated with the specific mineral frequencies the Loom emits. They are the shape they must be. They could not be another shape.

But here is what you notice if you watch long enough—and in this place, you will always watch long enough, because there is nothing else to do and nowhere else to go:

They *choose* their positions.

Not quickly. Not in any way you would recognize as decision-making if you were accustomed to biological timescales. But a Tessellate that has clung to a particular stretch of Loom-vein for what might be years will, over the course of several hours, begin to shift. One arm releases its grip on the stone—and you can hear it, the faintest *tick*, a crystal edge disengaging from a crystal surface—and then another, and then it is moving on three arms like a slow starfish, dragging itself across the rock face at a rate of perhaps a centimeter per minute, and it is moving *toward* something. Toward another Tessellate. Toward a specific one. It reaches its neighbor and overlaps, partially, their edges aligning with a precision that produces an audible harmonic—a click that is different from the ambient clicking, a click that *fits*, that has the quality of a latch engaging. And then they are still again. Paired. Their internal glow, already faint, becomes fractionally more coherent where they overlap, the two phosphorescent nodes pulsing in a synchrony so slow you might mistake it for constancy if you weren't watching with the patience this place teaches you.

They are tiling. Not randomly. Not merely clustering toward light sources. They are assembling, over timescales that make their movements invisible to anything that lives faster than they do, into *patterns*. Aperiodic patterns. Penrose tilings, or something close—arrangements where local symmetry is perfect but long-range order never quite repeats, where each Tessellate's position is determined by its relationship to every neighbor it can sense through the stone. The patterns extend for meters across rock faces, across ceilings, across the vertical surfaces of crystal columns, and they are—there is no other word—beautiful, in the way that mathematics is beautiful, in the way that a proof is beautiful: every element necessary, nothing arbitrary, the whole thing locked into a configuration that satisfies constraints you can feel but cannot articulate.

What are they doing? You don't know. They don't know, in the way that your cells don't know they are constructing a body. But the patterns are not accidental. They carry information in their geometry—in the specific angles of overlap, in which arms align with which, in the frequency relationships between paired phosphorescent nodes. The patterns are a language, or a ledger, or a map, or a memory—or all of these, or none of these, or something for which none of these words were built because the words were built in sunlight by beings made of water and carbon and you are in a place that has never known either.

When the deep ones sound their low bell-tone, the Tessellates flatten. Every one of them, simultaneously, across the entire perceptible volume. Their arms press tight to the stone. Their internal glow dims. They become, for a moment, indistinguishable from the mineral substrate. It looks like camouflage. It looks like reverence. It looks like both. It looks like neither. You are projecting. You cannot help projecting. But the flattening *is* a response, and the response *is* universal, and the timing *is* instantaneous in a way that suggests the signal propagates through the stone itself at the speed of sound in basalt, which is fast—four or five kilometers per second—which means that Tessellates a hundred kilometers away are flattening at effectively the same moment, a wave of genuflection radiating outward from wherever the deep ones stir, and the whole dark volume holds its breath in unison.

They do not breathe. But they hold it anyway.

## The Processionals

The crystal millipedes. Calling them millipedes does them a disservice, but it is the closest referent your surface-trained mind can produce, and you cling to referents here the way the Tessellates cling to Loom-veins.

They are longer than you first estimated. The ones you see near the surface terraces are young, or small, or both—a meter, a meter and a half. Deeper, in the mid-levels where the terraces grow steeper and the Loom-veins run thicker, they reach three meters. Perhaps four. Their bodies are extraordinary and alien and they are the most purely *crystalline* organisms in the Underloom—the most committed to mineral identity, the least compromised by anything that surface biology would recognize.

Each segment is a distinct crystal habit. The head segment—if it is a head, if directionality implies cephalization, which it may not—is cubic: a blocky, faceted structure of pyrite-gold silicate, almost metallic in the Loom-light, with flat faces that might be sensory surfaces or might be nothing at all. Behind it, the next segment is tetragonal, slightly elongated, a shift in lattice structure visible as a change in the way light refracts through the body. Then orthorhombic. Then monoclinic. Then triclinic. Each transition is seamless at the molecular level but visible at the macro level as a subtle shift in geometry, color, transparency—a gradient of crystal identity running the length of the body, so that the creature is not one material but a spectrum of materials, a living demonstration of every way silicate can organize itself under varying conditions of pressure and temperature and impurity.

The legs—and there are many, forty or sixty or more, depending on the specimen, each one a needle of crystal so fine it seems it should snap under the creature's weight but does not—emerge from the ventral junction between segments, and they move with a coordination that stops you cold the first time you truly see it. There is no fumbling. No adjustment. Each leg lifts from the stone at a precise moment, arcs through the air along a precise trajectory, and lands on a precise point on the sharp terrain, and the precision is *absolute*—not approximate, not good-enough, but exact, as though every step has been computed in advance to a tolerance of microns. They walk the way a Swiss watch ticks. They walk the way light follows Snell's law. They walk as though their locomotion is not a behavior but a physical inevitability, as though once the first leg lifts, every subsequent motion follows from the geometry of their bodies and the topology of the terrain with the certainty of a mathematical proof.

The grooves they wear into the stone—their paths—are the oldest structures in the Underloom that are not geological. They predate the builders' shelters by orders of magnitude. Some of them have been walked so many times that they are perceptible as shallow channels in the basalt, a fraction of a millimeter deep but visible as lines of slightly different color where the compressed stone has altered its mineral composition under the sustained pressure of crystal feet. The paths form a network. Not a random network. Not a grid. Something more like a vascular system—trunk routes that branch into smaller paths that branch into smaller paths still, reaching every terrace, every shelf, every significant Loom-vein exposure. The Processionals walk these paths and they carry fragments.

What they carry: mica flakes, mostly. Thin sheets of muscovite or biotite, split along cleavage planes into pieces sometimes no larger than a fingernail, held delicately between the first pair of legs in a grip that does not scratch or bend the mineral. They carry these fragments along the path network—sometimes short distances, sometimes what must be hundreds of meters—and they deposit them in specific locations with the care you attributed to a jeweler, and that comparison was not wrong, but it was not complete. They are not decorating. They are not stockpiling. They are *distributing*. The mica flakes go to specific places, and when you examine those places, you find that the flakes have been layered—over months, over years, over centuries—into stacks. Neat stacks. Aligned stacks. The cleavage planes of each flake oriented in the same direction, the edges trimmed (bitten? fractured?) to consistent dimensions, the whole assemblage resembling nothing so much as a very, very small book.

You do not know what is written in them. You do not know if *written* is the right word. But the stacks exist, and they are maintained, and when a Processional arrives at a stack and deposits its flake, it does something—a vibration, a tremor that runs the length of its segmented body—that might be satisfaction or might be the crystallographic equivalent of pressing *save*.

The Processionals do not interact with the Tessellates. They walk over them, around them, sometimes through gaps in the tiling patterns, and the Tessellates do not react. They occupy the same space with the mutual indifference of organisms that share a habitat but not an ecology—or, more precisely, organisms whose ecological relationship is so fundamental and so resolved that it no longer requires active negotiation. They are like the nitrogen and oxygen in an atmosphere. They coexist because coexistence is the lowest-energy state.

But the Processionals do interact with the builders. You have seen it. A builder crouching at its uncovered Loom-vein, doing whatever it does with its hands, and a Processional approaching—slowly, along its grooved path—and stopping. Waiting. The builder reaches toward the Processional's first segment and takes something—a mica flake, a crystal shard, something too small to see—and the Processional continues on its way, and the builder returns to its work. No sound. No gesture beyond the exchange itself. A transaction so routine it has been worn smooth, like the paths, by repetition beyond counting.

## The Builders

You want to see them clearly. You cannot. This is not entirely a function of the darkness, though the darkness does not help. It is a function of what they are, which is *liminal*—poised at a boundary between categories, belonging fully to neither side, and therefore difficult for a mind trained in categories to resolve.

They are bipedal. Sometimes. They are quadrupedal when the terrain demands it, and the terrain often demands it, and when they move on all fours across the sharp basalt their gait has a quality that is neither the scuttling of an animal nor the crawling of a person but something else—deliberate, low-slung, each limb placement chosen with something of the Processionals' precision but modified by a flexibility the Processionals lack. They can improvise. They can encounter an obstacle that is not on any grooved path and navigate it in real time, shifting weight, adjusting grip, making decisions that are visibly *decisions* and not computations.

When they stand—and they do stand, particularly near their shelters, particularly near the uncovered Loom-veins—they are roughly two-thirds the height of an adult human, and the comparison is misleading because their proportions are wrong for a human frame. The torso is broad and flat, wider than it is deep, and it has a quality of *density* that suggests mineral composition far heavier than flesh. The limbs are long relative to the body—arms that reach past where knees would be if the legs had knees in the conventional sense, which they do not; the legs bend, but they bend in two places, a double-jointed arrangement that allows both upright posture and the deep, close-to-ground crouch that seems to be their resting position. The hands—and they are hands, functional hands, the single most remarkable thing about these creatures—have too many fingers. Six? Seven? The number is hard to fix because some of what might be fingers might be tools held so constantly and so intimately that they have become indistinguishable from anatomy. The fingers are long and tapered and they end in points that are not claws but rather *edges*—thin, flat, chisel-like terminations that can grip and strike and split and scrape.

Their skin—their surface—is the thing you cannot resolve. In the Loom-light it reads as both mineral and organic, depending on the angle. Faceted in some places, the way a crystal is faceted, flat planes meeting at defined angles with the vitreous luster of polished stone. But in other places—the joints, the throat, the inner surfaces of the hands—there is something yielding. Something that deforms under pressure and returns to shape. Something with give. It is as though they began as one thing and are becoming another, or were once something else and have not finished arriving at what they are now, or—and this is the possibility that sits most uneasily—as though they are *both*, fully and simultaneously, mineral and flesh, crystal and tissue, and the dissonance you perceive is not in them but in your insistence that these categories are mutually exclusive.

They glow. Faintly. Not uniformly—in veins and patches, the same phosphorescent amber as the Loom, tracing lines across their bodies that might be blood vessels or might be crystal seams or might be both. The glow intensifies when they crouch at the uncovered Loom-veins and press their hands to the exposed mineral, and in those moments they are most visible, most present, most *themselves*: hunched figures haloed in dim amber, their too-many fingers splayed against the stone, their bodies engaged in something that looks like feeding and something that looks like reading and something that looks like prayer, and you cannot determine which it is because you are applying a taxonomy that was built for a world with sunlight and seasons and death, and this place has none of those things.

They are solitary. Each shelter holds one. You have watched long enough to be certain of this—one builder, one arrangement of stacked basalt and wedged obsidian, one uncovered Loom-vein, one clearing maintained against the encroaching Tessellates. They do not visit each other. They do not call to each other. If they communicate, it is not across space but across time—through the scratches on the stone, through the mica-flake books the Processionals assemble at their direction, through modifications to the Loom-veins that might alter the frequency or intensity of the phosphorescence in ways too subtle for you to detect but not too subtle for a body that is half crystal to read.

But they are aware of each other. You know this because of what happens when one of them—very rarely, on a timescale you can barely perceive—relocates. A builder will leave its shelter. It will move across the terraces, slowly, over the course of what might be days, carrying its knapped obsidian tools and nothing else. It will find a new location—an uninhabited terrace, an unclaimed Loom-vein—and it will build. And the spacing is always the same. Whatever distance separates one shelter from the next is preserved, maintained, a buffer zone that is never violated. They are distributed across the dark volume with the regularity of a crystal lattice. They are, in their arrangement, another kind of tiling—sparser than the Tessellates', operating at a scale of hundreds of meters rather than centimeters, but governed by the same principle: each element positioned in precise relation to every other, the whole pattern held in tension by mutual awareness.

How many are there? Thousands. Tens of thousands. Maybe more. You cannot survey the Underloom, cannot see more than ten meters, cannot know how far the volume extends. But the paths of the Processionals radiate outward in every direction, and the paths must lead somewhere, and the regularity of the lattice implies continuation, and the haze of mineral dust extends without limit, and the warmth does not waver, and the conclusion is that the Underloom is vast beyond your ability to conceive, and it is populated at this same steady density across its entirety, and the number of builders living their solitary, silent, parallel lives in their identical stone shelters beside their identical uncovered Loom-veins is a number that would mean nothing to you even if you could compute it.

What do they think about?

You will not learn this. Not because they are hostile or secretive but because the medium of their thought may not be translatable into anything you could receive. If they think in crystal harmonics—in the resonant frequencies of silicate lattices, in the phase relationships between phosphorescent pulses, in the geometric logic of Penrose tilings read at the molecular level—then their inner life is as real and as rich as yours and as fundamentally inaccessible as the experience of being a color you have never seen. They have hands. They make tools. They modify their environment. They maintain boundaries. They exchange materials with the Processionals in a transaction that implies economics or mutualism or both. They do everything that, on the surface, in the light, would cause you to point and say *person*. But they do it in silence, in solitude, in a darkness that has never known anything else, at a pace that makes their civilizational time measured in geological epochs, and the word *person* strains under the weight of what it is being asked to contain.

You watch one of them working at its Loom-vein. Its fingers move across the exposed mineral in patterns that repeat and vary, repeat and vary—like someone playing a keyboard, or reading Braille, or performing a ritual whose meaning has been so deeply internalized that the body executes it without conscious direction. The amber light pulses under its touch. The Tessellates at the edge of the cleared zone shift, fractionally, in response to whatever the builder is doing to the light. The Processional on the nearest path pauses—the only pause you have ever seen a Processional make that was not triggered by the deep ones' bell-tone—and adjusts the mica flake it carries, rotating it a few degrees, as though the builder's Loom-work has altered the flake's destination.

Everything is connected. Everything responds to everything else. Not chaotically—precisely. With the deterministic grace of a system in which every variable is known and every relationship is stable and every perturbation propagates at a predictable rate and is absorbed at a predictable distance. The builder touches the Loom-vein and the vein's light shifts and the Tessellates adjust their tiling and the Processional reroutes its delivery and somewhere, far away, another builder at another Loom-vein feels the downstream change and adjusts its own work accordingly, and the adjustment propagates further, and further, rippling across the dark volume at the speed of light in phosphorescent mineral, and the whole Underloom shivers with a communication so distributed and so slow that it might take centuries to complete a single thought—but the thought *is* being completed, and has been in progress since before anything on the surface built its first fire, and will continue long after the surface has forgotten what fire was.

## The Deep Ones

You will not see them. Accept this now.

What you know: They are large. The vibration of their movement suggests mass measured in tens of tons at minimum—bodies that grind against stone as they shift, that displace the mineral haze when they pass, that leave paths not worn into the rock but *through* it, tunnels and troughs where their passage has compressed or shattered the substrate. They inhabit the lowest reaches of the Underloom, the obsidian trenches, the places where the Loom-veins converge into thick arterial bundles that glow with a light almost bright enough to read by—and you know this because the Processionals' path-network has a directionality, a root system, and the roots all point down, and down is where the deep ones are.

The bell-tone. It comes every few hours—you think, though time is difficult here, though hours may not be hours. It originates below and to the... you want to say south, but there is no south, there is only the direction the sound comes from, and it comes from a direction that is both down and away, as though the deep ones occupy a lower stratum of the volume, a sub-basement beneath the basement, where the geothermal gradient intensifies and the stone transitions from basalt to something denser and the Loom-veins thicken into rivers of phosphorescence. The tone is low—lower than human hearing should register, but you feel it in your ribs, in your teeth, in the fluid of your inner ear, a pressure-pulse that arrives through the stone faster than through the air and sets up a brief, complex interference pattern as the two arrivals overlap. It lasts four seconds. It recurs at regular intervals. It does not vary in pitch or intensity or duration. It is a metronome. It is the Underloom's heartbeat. It is the clock that clocks would be set by if clocks had meaning here—and suddenly you realize that clocks *do* have meaning here, because the deep ones *are* the clocks, and everything in the Underloom synchronizes to their tone, and the tone is the one universal signal in a system that otherwise communicates through touch and light and the glacial rearrangement of crystal geometries.

When the tone sounds, the Tessellates flatten. The Processionals freeze. The builders pause in their Loom-work. For four seconds, the entire ecology stops—every process, every movement, every transaction—and in that stillness there is something that might be reset, or calibration, or synchronization, or worship. Then the tone ends, and everything resumes, and the resumption is so seamless that you wonder whether the pause happened at all, whether you imagined it, whether the Underloom is capable of pausing or whether what you perceived as a pause was simply the moment when all the cycles happened to align, the way a complex rhythm momentarily resolves into unison before diverging again.

You do not know what the deep ones look like. You do not know if *look* is applicable. You know they are mineral, because everything here is mineral. You know they are alive, because the tone is too regular to be geological and too complex in its harmonics to be simple resonance. You know they are old—older than the builders, older than the Processionals, possibly older than the Tessellates, possibly coeval with the Loom itself, possibly *part* of the Loom, possibly the thing that the Loom is the circulatory system *of*. This possibility surfaces in your mind and you cannot dismiss it: that the deep ones are not organisms inhabiting the Underloom but organs of a larger organism that *is* the Underloom, that the phosphorescent veins are their vasculature and the Tessellates are their skin and the Processionals are their lymphatic system and the builders are their—what? Their neurons? Their hands? Their dreams?

You do not know. The deep ones do not explain themselves. They sound their tone, and the world pauses, and then it continues, and the tone is neither a question nor an answer but something more fundamental—an assertion of continued existence, a declaration that the system persists, that the resting state holds, that the equilibrium endures. *I am still here*, the tone says, in a language that is not language. *I am still here, and therefore you are still here, and therefore everything is as it should be.*

## What You Have Disturbed

You are warm. Not warm the way the Underloom is warm—warm in a different register, a different frequency, the warmth of biological metabolism, of oxidative chemistry, of blood. You are exhaling carbon dioxide and water vapor into an atmosphere that has never contained either in biological concentrations. You are standing on the sharp stone with soft feet, and the pressure distribution of your weight is wrong—too concentrated, too irregular, not the even crystalline contact that every other organism here makes with the substrate. You are emitting infrared radiation in a pattern that nothing here has evolved to interpret. You are a foreign gradient. A disequilibrium. A warm, wet, carbon-based intrusion into a system that has been solved since before your species existed.

The silence around you is fractionally deeper than it was. This is not metaphor. The Tessellates in your immediate vicinity have stopped their glacial tiling-work. They have not flattened—that response is reserved for the deep ones' tone—but they have stilled in a way that is different from their usual stillness, a watchful motionlessness, an orientation of attention toward an anomaly they have no category for. The nearest Processional has deviated from its groove by almost a full millimeter—an unprecedented navigational error, or an unprecedented navigational *choice*—and is holding its position with its mica flake gripped slightly tighter than usual, its body vibrating at a frequency you cannot hear but can feel in the soles of your feet as a faint, insistent buzzing.

In the middle distance, at the edge of perception, a builder has emerged from its shelter. It is standing. Fully upright—and you realize you have not seen one stand fully upright before, not like this, not with this quality of *alertness*, this orientation of the flat broad torso toward you, this extension of the too-long arms with their too-many fingers splayed wide, as though reaching for something or measuring something or signaling something to the other builders who cannot see it but who can feel, through the Loom, through the stone, through the crystalline network that connects every living thing in this place to every other living thing, that something has changed.

Something has changed.

For the first time in a span of time that may exceed the entire history of life on the surface, something in the Underloom has *changed*, and the system is responding, and you are the change, and you have no idea what happens next because nothing like this has ever happened here, and the silence—the deep, mineral, ten-thousand-organism silence—is waiting for you to do something, to be something, to resolve into a category it can process or persist as an anomaly it cannot, and either way the equilibrium has been broken and either way the resting state has shifted and either way the Underloom, which has been solved since before your species learned to chip stone, is now—

For the first time—

An open question.

And deep below, deeper than you have any right to feel, a vibration begins to build that is not the regular tone, that is not the four-second bell-pulse that comes every few hours, that is something else—something that has not sounded in the living memory of anything here, because living memory here stretches back to the cooling of the stone, and this sound has never been made, not once, not ever—and it rises through the basalt like a question asked by a mountain, and the Tessellates do not flatten, because they do not know what this signal means, because there has never been a signal that means *this*, and the Processionals do not freeze, they *scatter*, breaking from their grooves for the first time in a hundred thousand years of perfect path-adherence, and the builder standing at the edge of your perception takes a step toward you—

And the deep ones speak.

Not the heartbeat. Not the metronome. Something else.

Something *new*.
