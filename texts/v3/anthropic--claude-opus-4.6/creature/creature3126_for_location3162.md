# What Lives on the Abyssal Plain of the Closing Sea

---

They are not individuals. They have never been individuals. To call one of them a *thing* would be to impose a boundary where none exists, and they have no use for boundaries — or rather, they are *made* of boundaries, every one of them a living edge between states, a creature that exists precisely at the place where one kind of water becomes another.

Start with the worms, because the worms are easiest to see, and because even the worms are stranger than they appear.

---

## The Polychaetes of the Closing Mat

They are pale. Not white — white implies a color chosen, a pigment committed to. They are the pale of things that have never needed to be any color at all: the translucent, vaguely pinkish non-color of tissues that have been building themselves out of sulfur and iron and the stripped-down chemistry of deep time for so long that they have forgotten what it means to absorb light, because there is no light worth absorbing. Their bodies are threadlike, segmented, each segment nearly identical to the last, a repetition so faithful it borders on the liturgical. They range from three to eight centimeters in length. Their setae — the tiny bristle-legs that in their ancestors served for locomotion — have been repurposed, or perhaps un-purposed: the setae now function primarily as chemical sensors, each one a fine hair of chitin coated in a film of proteins that respond to gradients in sulfide concentration, temperature, dissolved iron, pH. The worms do not so much *move through* the bacterial mats as *taste their way* through them, each bristle-step a measurement, each measurement a decision made by no brain but by the aggregate of local reflexes distributed across a body that is, in essence, a single long tongue pressed against the chemistry of the world.

They have no eyes. They have never had eyes — not in the way of cave fish that lost theirs, but in the way of a lineage so old it predates the evolutionary pressures that made eyes useful. What they have instead is a sensitivity to pressure changes so refined that they can detect the passage of a density cascade front from meters away: a faint increase in the compression of their tissues, a change in the viscosity of the interstitial water within the mat, and the worm responds — slowly, always slowly — by retracting deeper into the layered films, pulling its anterior segments downward into the older, deader layers of bacterial crust where the chemistry is stale but the turbulence cannot reach. They do not fear the cascades. Fear requires a nervous system organized for something other than gradient-following, and theirs is not. But they *avoid* the cascades with a consistency that looks, from the outside, exactly like fear: a blind, ancient, cellular flinch away from the thing that disrupts.

They feed by absorption. Their gut is a simple tube, but their skin is the real organ of nutrition — permeable, studded with transport proteins that pull dissolved organics directly from the mat matrix, molecule by molecule, a feeding strategy so passive it barely qualifies as eating. A worm can go months without encountering a concentration of food dense enough to register as a *meal*. Instead, it is always eating, always absorbing, running a slight caloric surplus that it banks in lipid droplets distributed along its body cavity, tiny spheres of stored energy that give the worm, when seen in the faint chemical light, a stippled, granular texture, like a thread of glass with bubbles trapped inside.

They reproduce by fragmentation. When a worm reaches a certain length — and *certain* here means something determined not by genetics alone but by the local chemistry, the availability of sulfide, the temperature of the mat — it simply breaks. The posterior segments separate from the anterior ones along a predetermined fracture plane, and each half regenerates what it lost. There is no mating. No gametes. No genetic exchange. Each worm is a clone of the worm it broke from, which was a clone of the worm before that, a lineage of identical copies stretching back through deep time with only the slow, accumulating errors of imperfect DNA replication to introduce variation. They are, in a sense, not a population but a single organism that has been breaking itself into pieces for half a billion years, scattering its fragments across the mat-fields of the plain, each fragment carrying the same genome, the same reflexes, the same patient, tasteless hunger.

And yet they are not all the same. The errors have accumulated. Drift has done its quiet work. A worm from one fracture system, transplanted to another — if such a thing could be done, if there were anyone to do it — would find itself slightly mismatched to the local chemistry, its transport proteins tuned to a sulfide concentration a few parts per million different from what it encounters, its thermal tolerance shifted by a fraction of a degree. It would survive. It would function. But it would be, in the most minimal and fundamental sense, a *foreigner*, and the mat would know it — not consciously, not through any mechanism of recognition, but through the simple fact that its metabolic waste products would be subtly wrong, slightly different in composition, and the bacteria nearest to it would grow at a marginally different rate, and over years or decades this difference would propagate outward in a ripple of altered chemistry that the other worms would taste and, without understanding, turn away from. The plain is vast and flat and uniform, but it is not homogeneous. It is a mosaic of micro-environments, and the worms are the tiles.

## The Mats Themselves

But the worms are secondary. They are tenants. The true inhabitants — if *inhabitants* is the right word for organisms that *are* their habitat — are the bacterial films.

They do not have species, not in any way that would satisfy a taxonomist working on a planet with light and oxygen and the luxury of morphological diversity. What they have is *metabolic identity*: clusters of genes organized around a particular strategy for extracting energy from the chemistry of the vents, each cluster slightly different from its neighbors, each one a local solution to the local problem of staying alive in a world where the energy gradients are shallow and the cold is absolute. Some oxidize sulfide. Some reduce iron. Some do both, switching between metabolic modes in response to shifts in the vent fluid composition with a flexibility that looks almost like intelligence but is really just the brute efficiency of a genome that has been edited by four billion years of selection in an environment that punishes specialization and rewards versatility.

The mats are layered. The surface — the living layer — is a film of active cells no more than a few micrometers thick, a skin of metabolism stretched over the fracture margin like a membrane over a wound. Beneath it, the dead layers accumulate, each one a fossil record of the chemistry that prevailed when it was alive, its mineral composition a diary entry written in iron oxides and calcium carbonates and the faint organic residue of lysed cells. The mats are their own archives. Read downward through the layers and you read backward through time: here, a stratum rich in pyrite, marking a period when the vent ran hot and sulfide-heavy; there, a gap, a thin layer of pure mineral precipitate, marking a time when the fracture sealed and the mat starved and only the hardiest cells survived, dormant, waiting, metabolizing nothing, burning through their lipid reserves at a rate so slow it barely registers as life. And then the fracture reopened, and the chemistry returned, and the survivors divided, and the mat grew back, and a new entry was added to the record.

They shape the floor. This is the closest thing on the plain to *will*, and it is not will at all — it is the inevitable consequence of metabolism occurring in a mineral-rich environment over geological time. The bacteria excrete waste. The waste alters the local pH. The altered pH changes which minerals precipitate and which dissolve. Around the edges of a mature mat, a ridge of calcium carbonate builds up, millimeter by millimeter, century by century, forming a low wall — barely visible, no higher than a coin stood on its edge — that channels the flow of vent fluid and protects the interior of the mat from the scouring effect of the density cascades. The ridge is not *built*. It is *excreted into existence*. But it functions as architecture. It has load-bearing properties. It deflects currents. It creates, in its lee, a microenvironment of slightly warmer, slightly more sulfide-rich water where the mat grows thickest, where the worms are most numerous, where the chemistry is most generous. The mats are engineering their world by the act of living in it, and they have been doing so for so long that it is no longer possible to distinguish the organism from its modifications. The mat *is* the ridge. The ridge *is* the mat. They are a single system, a living geology, a creature made of rock and slime and time.

And they communicate. Not in any way that involves signals or intention, but in the way that all chemical systems communicate: by changing the composition of the medium that connects them. A mat on one fracture, metabolizing vigorously during a period of strong venting, releases a plume of metabolic byproducts — organic acids, reduced metals, dissolved gases — into the bottom water. This plume is caught by a density cascade, carried laterally across the plain, and deposited on a mat at another fracture fifty meters away. The receiving mat is altered. The organic acids lower the pH at its surface. The reduced metals provide electron donors it hadn't had before. The dissolved gases shift the redox balance. The mat's growth rate changes. Its community composition shifts — some metabolic types favored, others suppressed. It responds, not to a message, but to a *condition*, and its response generates its own plume, its own cascade of chemical consequences that propagate outward to the next mat, and the next.

The result is a network. Not a designed network, not a network anyone built or intended, but a network nonetheless: a web of chemical dependencies and feedback loops that connects every mat on the plain to every other mat through the medium of the turbulent, mineral-laden water. A change in venting at one fracture propagates through this network as a slow wave of metabolic adjustment that can take years to cross the plain. The mats are coupled. They rise and fall together, not in synchrony but in sequence, like a very slow, very diffuse heartbeat — the pulse of a single distributed organism that has no center, no boundary, no self, but is nonetheless *alive* in the most fundamental sense: it takes in energy, it expels waste, it maintains itself against the entropic current, and it has been doing so for longer than multicellular life has existed on any world with a sun.

## The Crustaceans at the Edge of Rumor

They are said to exist — *said* in the sense that the chemistry says so, in the sense that certain water parcels arriving from the denser fracture clusters to the east carry traces of chitin degradation products and uric acid analogues that are consistent with the presence of small arthropods. No one has seen them. No one *can* see them, here, because here is not where they are. They are elsewhere on the plain, in the zones where the fractures bunch together and the venting is dense enough to support a food web with a second trophic level: creatures that eat the bacteria, that graze the mats, that introduce into the system the alien concept of *consumption as locomotion*, moving from patch to patch, mat to mat, carrying with them the novel disruption of selective feeding pressure.

If they exist — and the chemistry says they do — they are small, blind, pale, and slow. They are amphipods or something amphipod-adjacent, body plans inherited from a Paleozoic ancestor and then stripped, reduced, simplified over the eons until what remains is a minimal architecture for the minimal task of scraping bacterial film from basalt and converting it into more of themselves. Their exoskeletons are thin, barely calcified — calcium is metabolically expensive, and there is no predator to armor against. Their appendages are reduced to the essentials: mouthparts for scraping, legs for clinging, antennae for tasting the water. They reproduce slowly, live long, and die without drama, their bodies settling into the mat and being absorbed by the same bacteria they spent their lives eating. They are, if they exist, the closest thing on the plain to an *animal* in the way a surface-dweller would understand the term: a thing that moves, that chooses, that navigates. But their choices are so constrained and their navigation so slow that the distinction between them and the worms is more philosophical than biological.

What matters about them — what makes them *inhabitants* rather than curiosities — is not what they are but what they *do*. If they exist, they graze. And grazing creates heterogeneity. A grazed mat is different from an ungrazed mat. It is thinner, younger, more active — forced into rapid growth to replace what was eaten, and rapid growth means different metabolic priorities, different waste products, different mineral precipitates. The grazed zone becomes chemically distinct from the ungrazed zone, and this distinction propagates through the network, rippling outward from the crustacean's tiny, scraping mouthparts in a wave of metabolic consequence that can, over centuries, alter the growth pattern of mats dozens of kilometers away. The crustacean — if it exists — is a perturbation. A stone dropped into the thousand-year pond. And the ripples it creates are, in their way, the most complex and unpredictable phenomenon on the plain: the signature of animal agency, however minimal, imposed on a system that had been running on pure chemistry for eons.

## What the Turbulence Carries

There are things in the water that are not alive and have never been alive but that behave, in the turbulence, as though they have purposes.

The mineral particles — the dissolved bones of continents, the ground-down memory of mountains — are not passive. They are not simply falling. In the density cascades, they are sorted, concentrated, organized by the physics of differential settling into layers and lenses of varying composition. A cascade front carries with it a leading edge of heavier particles — iron-rich, dark, fast-settling — and a trailing haze of lighter ones — silicates, clays, the ghostly residue of feldspars that were crystallizing in magma chambers when the world was young. Where two cascade fronts collide, their particle loads mix, and the mixing produces new mineral phases — clays that didn't exist before the collision, iron-silicate compounds that form and dissolve and re-form in the turbulent interface, a chemistry so dynamic and so far from equilibrium that it generates, briefly, conditions that a prebiotic chemist would recognize as *interesting*: steep energy gradients, concentrated organics, mineral surfaces that catalyze reactions, all brought together by the physics of turbulent mixing in a combination that might, in another billion years, have produced something new.

But it will not. The vents are closing. The energy is winding down. Whatever the turbulence might have made, given time, it will not be given time. The particles will settle. The gradients will flatten. The interesting chemistry will become uninteresting chemistry and then no chemistry at all. What the turbulence carries is potential — vast, ancient, unrealized potential — and what lives on the plain lives in the last moments of that potential's existence, drawing from it the final thin rations of energy that a dying planet can provide.

## The True Inhabitants

So: what lives here?

A network of bacterial mats, connected by chemistry, shaped by time, engineering their environment through the brute fact of their metabolism. A population of worms that is really one worm, fragmented across the plain, tasting its way through the dark. Perhaps — probably — a scattering of crustaceans at the far edge of the fracture fields, introducing the tremor of animal choice into a system that otherwise runs on thermodynamics alone. And the turbulence itself, which is not alive but which behaves, in its complexity and its sensitivity to perturbation, as though it were the circulatory system of something much larger — the blood of the plain, carrying nutrients and waste and chemical information from one living outpost to another, connecting them, sustaining them, and slowly, slowly, ceasing.

These are not creatures that *do* things. They are creatures that *persist*. Their primary activity is continuation. Their greatest achievement is the simple, staggering fact that they have not yet stopped. In a world that has lost its sky, its light, its land, its oxygen, its diversity, its future — in a world that is, by every meaningful measure, over — they remain. They metabolize. They grow their fractions of a millimeter per century. They taste the water and pull back from the cascades. They excrete their tiny ridges of carbonate and shelter behind them. They break themselves in half and scatter their halves across the dark. They do not know they are the last of anything, because they do not know. They do not experience their situation as tragic, or heroic, or even as a *situation*. They experience nothing. They are patterns of chemistry that have maintained themselves against dissolution for longer than thought has existed in the universe, and they will continue to maintain themselves for as long as the vents breathe and the cascades roll and the water remains full of the dissolved ghosts of continents.

And then they will stop. And the plain will be empty. And the water will be still. And the darkness will be complete, not the darkness of an absence but the darkness of a completion — the final, silent, mineral dark of a world that has finished being a world and become, at last, what it was always becoming: a sphere of stone and brine and cold, turning in a void, carrying in its depths the chemical memory of everything that ever lived on it, locked in nodules and crusts and thin iridescent films of iron, unread, unreadable, and enduring beyond all possibility of witness.

But not yet.

For now, in the thin bacterial film at the edge of a hairline fracture in a plain of glass-smooth basalt beneath a sea that has swallowed the sky, a worm turns. It tastes sulfide. It moves — one bristle-step, two — toward the gradient. Behind it, the mat closes over the trace of its passage. Above it, the cascades roll their slow, silent thunder across the dark. Around it, the network hums with the chemical conversation of a trillion cells that do not know they are speaking, and will not know when they fall silent.

It is enough. It has always been enough. It is the absolute minimum of what *enough* can mean, and it is, in its way, everything.
