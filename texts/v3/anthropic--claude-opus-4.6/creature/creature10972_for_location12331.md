# The People of Thurm

---

They are thick-boned and unhurried. That is the first thing you notice about them, once the drone has settled into the background noise of your own pulse and you begin to see the city as inhabited rather than endured.

They are not large people, not tall, but they carry a density that has nothing to do with fat. Their frames are compressed and load-bearing, as if their skeletons have quietly negotiated with the same gravity that pulls the water upward through the stone and have agreed to meet it halfway. Their hands are broad. Their wrists are broad. The women and the men share this quality equally — a squareness to the joints, a thickness through the forearms that speaks of a lifetime of turning valves and gripping wet iron and hauling ceramic pipe through narrow culvert openings. Even the children look slightly denser than you expect, as though the mineral content of the water has gotten into them and begun the long process of fortifying them from the inside out.

Their skin has a quality you cannot place at first. It is not a color exactly — though there is a warmth to it, a tawny ochre undertone that might be genetic or might be the accumulated effect of decades spent in air saturated with dissolved calcium and iron. No. It is a texture. Their skin looks *seasoned*. Even the young ones have a faint patina, a smoothness that is not youth's smoothness but something more akin to the polish on those iron railings — the result of constant contact with mineral-laden humidity, with water that is never quite absent from anything in Thurm. They do not look weathered. They look cured. The old ones especially: their faces have the quality of well-handled stone, every line not a wrinkle so much as a channel, a groove worn by years of expression repeated until it became permanent topography.

They do not move quickly. You will not see anyone in Thurm run.

---

This is not lethargy. You understand this after watching them work, which they do constantly, in shifts and rotations that seem governed by the same tidal pressure cycles that drive the drone. They move with an economy that approaches ritual. A pipe-fitter working on a junction box in the middle of a thoroughfare does not hurry because the junction box will still be there in an hour and so will she and so will the water, and the question is not speed but *fit* — the exact torque, the precise angle of the ceramic sleeve against the iron collar, the way the gasket material (a fibrous grey substance that smells of wet chalk and appears to be harvested from something that grows in the deeper culverts) must be seated so that the joint neither leaks nor over-compresses.

They are, all of them, plumbers. This is too reductive. But it is the closest word. Every resident of Thurm has a relationship with the water system that goes beyond occupation into something like citizenship. The children learn the sound of the culverts before they learn to read — can tell you, by pressing an ear to a certain wall, whether the flow beneath is running clean or silted, whether the pressure is building toward a swell or ebbing, whether a particular harmonic means a junction upstream has loosened or a solution channel has shifted. This is not taught formally. It is absorbed, the way language is absorbed, through immersion in a world where the water's voice is the dominant fact of existence.

But they are not all pipe-fitters. There are distinctions, and the distinctions matter to them even if they are invisible to you at first.

---

The **channel-keepers** are the most visible. They are the ones you see in the streets, walking the open channels with long iron-shod poles, clearing debris — which is mostly mineral scale that has sloughed off the channel walls in pale, curling sheets — and testing the flow with a gesture so practiced it looks like nothing at all: a hand dipped to the wrist, held still for a count of three, then withdrawn and examined. They are reading the water's temperature, its mineral load, its pressure by the force it exerts against their palm. They can do this the way you read a sentence — not word by word but whole, in a glance. The channel-keepers tend to be younger, or at least more mobile; the work requires walking, all day, in a slow circuit that covers the same channels in the same order and notices everything that has changed since yesterday. They wear heavy aprons of oiled leather that hang to the knee and boots that are more iron than leather, soled with riveted plates that ring against the wet stone. The sound of a channel-keeper walking is one of the minor percussions of Thurm — a metrical clanking that weaves through the drone like a shuttle through warp.

The channel-keepers talk to each other constantly. Or rather, they broadcast. They have a mode of speech that is half-shouted and half-sung, pitched to cut through the drone at a specific frequency that carries well in the heavy, humid air. It is a working language, not a social one — a pared-down vocabulary of flow states, pressure readings, junction designations, delivered in a rhythmic cadence that lets information travel efficiently down a street. You hear it and it sounds like chanting. It is not chanting. It is status reports. But the boundary between the two is thinner than you might think, in a city where the water is both infrastructure and something older.

---

The **deep-workers** are different. You see them less often, and when you do they are emerging from stairwells that descend into the plateau's interior — metal-rung ladders bolted into shafts that drop into darkness and sound. They are the ones who maintain the sub-surface system, the network of solution channels and engineered culverts that forms the true body of Thurm's hydrology. They look different from the channel-keepers: paler, though not unhealthily so, as though their skin has adapted to the reduced light below by simply declining to darken. Their hands are extraordinary — mapped with a fine white tracery of mineral deposits that has accumulated in the creases of their knuckles and the whorls of their fingerprints, giving their skin the appearance of cracked porcelain or the surface of a dry lakebed. They do not wash this off. It does not wash off, or not easily; the minerals bond with the oils of the skin and form a thin, flexible crust that the deep-workers regard with something between pride and resignation. It is the mark of their work. It is also, you will learn, mildly protective — the mineral layer insulates against the heat of the deeper water, which runs hotter the closer you get to the geothermal seam, and provides a degree of resistance to the mild acidity that would otherwise soften and crack their skin over years of exposure.

The deep-workers are quieter than the channel-keepers. Down in the culverts, the drone is not a background but a medium — it fills the passages so completely that spoken language becomes useless below a certain depth. Instead, the deep-workers communicate through touch and vibration. They carry iron hammers — small, precisely weighted — and tap on the culvert walls in patterns that propagate through the stone and water simultaneously, arriving at a colleague thirty meters away as a complex of felt impulses that encode location, urgency, flow condition, and a dozen other variables. You are told that experienced deep-workers can hold conversations this way that are as nuanced as any spoken exchange. You are told this by a deep-worker's spouse, shouting over the drone in a tea-house near the central plaza, and the deep-worker herself simply nods and taps a short rhythm on the table with her fingernail: confirmation, or amusement, or both.

The deep-workers marry each other, mostly. This is not a rule. It is a consequence of the fact that people who spend their working lives communicating through stone tend to develop a sensitivity — a receptivity to vibration — that makes the company of other vibration-literate people more comfortable than the alternative. A deep-worker at rest will often keep one hand on a wall or a stone surface, not consciously, the way someone else might keep a hand on a table or a knee: staying in contact, staying in the conversation that the city is always having with itself.

---

Then there are the **pressure-readers**, and these are harder to classify because their role is not quite technical and not quite administrative and not quite priestly, though it brushes against all three.

The pressure-readers are the ones who understand the tidal cycles of the water table — or claim to, or come closer than anyone else. They are usually older, though not always. They operate from a building near the city's center, a squat, heavy-walled structure with no windows on the ground floor and a rooftop observation platform cluttered with instruments you do not recognize: iron columns of graduated height, each standing in a shallow basin of water, their surfaces etched with markings that have been re-etched so many times the metal is filigreed with overlapping scales. These are manometric devices, you realize eventually — they measure pressure, water level, something about the harmonic character of the flow. The pressure-readers spend hours with them, marking observations in heavy ledger books with pages that have gone wavy and soft from the humidity.

What the pressure-readers produce is prediction. They predict the swells — the long, slow peaks in the drone that correspond to surges in the water table and that affect everything from culvert pressure to the output of the factory turbines to the stability of building foundations. A good prediction gives the channel-keepers time to adjust flow rates, gives the deep-workers time to reinforce vulnerable junctions, gives the factories time to throttle their intake. A bad prediction — or a missed swell — means burst pipes, flooded basements, and the occasional catastrophic rupture of a culvert wall that sends a geyser of hot amber water shooting up through a street and does not stop for days.

The pressure-readers are respected, but they are also watched. There is a tension around them that you feel before you understand it. They are the ones who decide when a building at the city's margin must be abandoned because the solution channels beneath it have widened past the point of safety. They are the ones who designate new seep-holes as viable or contaminated. They are the ones who, when the drone enters one of its rare, unsettling *silences* — a sudden drop in the harmonic that lasts only seconds but that stops every person in the street mid-stride in a shared, animal stillness — are expected to explain what it means.

They do not always know what it means. The water table is not fully understood. It has never been fully understood. The pressure-readers work with models that are part measurement, part inherited lore, part intuition trained by decades of listening to a sound that most people have learned to ignore. They are, in a sense, the city's interpreters — translating the water's behavior into terms the city can act on — and like all interpreters they are aware that something is always lost, something is always approximated, and the original text is doing things they cannot quite follow.

---

The **factory workers** are a class apart, not by choice but by circumstance. They work in the southeastern quarter where the water pressure is highest and the drone is loudest and the air is so thick with steam that visibility drops to a few meters inside the buildings. They tend to be stockier even than the Thurm average, with a particular redness to the skin — especially the face and forearms — that comes from years of working in superheated humidity. They have their own argot, clipped and consonant-heavy, stripped of the vowel-sounds that get swallowed by the drone in the factory halls. A factory worker's speech, heard outside the factory, sounds percussive and strange, like someone trying to communicate using only the bones of language.

The factories produce metal goods, mostly. Iron fittings, ceramic pipe, valve assemblies, gasket material, the heavy riveted boots the channel-keepers wear, the graduated iron columns the pressure-readers use. Thurm's economy, insofar as you can map it, is largely self-referential: the city produces the materials needed to maintain the city, and the maintenance of the city is the primary labor of the city, and the product of that labor is the continued existence of the city. There are exports — high-quality valve assemblies and ceramic pipe are valued in the valleys below, where the water systems are less sophisticated and less... existential — but the trade is conducted at the plateau's edge by a subset of residents who make the long descent through the haze and return days later with whatever the city cannot produce from its own stone and water. These traders are lean, unusual in Thurm. They have a restlessness that reads as foreign. The rest of the city regards them as necessary and faintly suspect, the way a body might regard its own circulatory system: vital, but uncomfortably dependent on an outside world that the body would prefer to be sufficient without.

---

The children are the most startling thing about Thurm, because they are so clearly *of* this place in a way that goes beyond upbringing into something cellular.

They do not play quietly. They cannot — the drone would swallow quiet play, render it mute and invisible. Instead they play loudly, in the wide streets, in games that involve shouting at specific pitches and slamming objects against the stone to produce sounds that compete with the background harmonic. Their games are rhythmic. They involve call-and-response patterns, iron tokens thrown into seep-pools to produce specific splashes, competitions to identify the source and meaning of particular culvert-sounds that propagate up through the pavement. A group of children crouched at a seep-pool, ears pressed to the wet stone, faces screwed tight in concentration, debating whether the sub-harmonic they're hearing means a swell is coming or a junction has silted up four blocks south — this is a common sight, and it is both play and apprenticeship, the same way a wolf pup's wrestling is both play and training.

The very young ones — toddlers, infants — are remarkably calm. You notice this. In a city this loud, you expect crying, distress, the ordinary protest of small organisms overwhelmed by stimulus. But the infants of Thurm are born into the drone and they settle into it like a heartbeat. They sleep through swells that make your vision blur. They nurse placidly while the culvert beneath the house sings in a register that vibrates the crockery off shelves. Their mothers — and their fathers, the caregiving in Thurm is not strongly gendered — hold them against their chests and the vibration passes through both bodies equally, parent and child resonating at the same frequency, and the child does not cry because why would it cry? The world has always sounded like this. The world has always hummed.

When a child of Thurm is taken down off the plateau — which happens rarely, for trade journeys or family obligations in the valleys — they become inconsolable. Not at first. At first they are fascinated by the silence, the way any child is fascinated by a new sensation. But within hours the silence begins to press on them, a negative pressure, an absence so total it registers as pain. They cover their ears as though blocking a sound, but they are blocking the lack of sound, trying to hold in the hum they carry in their bones. They sleep poorly. They fuss. They press their bodies against walls, against the ground, searching for vibration. They are brought home as quickly as possible, and when they crest the last switchback and the drone finds them again, they go still and their eyes lose that terrible, searching focus and they melt into their parent's arms like a held breath finally released.

---

The old people of Thurm are stone. Not literally. But close.

The mineral deposits that mark the deep-workers' hands are, in the very old, spread across the entire body. A lifetime of drinking mineral-laden water, of breathing mineral-saturated air, of bathing in bathwater that leaves a fine grit in every fold and crease, produces in the elderly of Thurm a condition that has no medical name because no one in Thurm considers it a condition. Their skin develops a chalky underlayer — visible at the temples, at the wrists, along the shins — where calcium and silica have been slowly deposited in the dermal tissue over decades. It is notite calcite armor. It is a thickening, a densification, as though the body is gradually becoming more like the substance it has lived on and among for seventy or eighty years. The very old creak when they move. Their joints have lost flexibility not from arthritis alone but from the fine mineral accretion in the cartilage and connective tissue. They move slowly, deliberately, planning each gesture the way a pressure-reader plans a swell prediction — with full awareness that the system has tolerances and the tolerances are not infinite.

They are not pitied. They are consulted. The old ones carry in their bodies a record of the city's hydrology that no ledger can match. They remember swells from fifty years ago. They remember the sound the culvert under the old market hall made the year before it ruptured, and they can hum it — a specific, nasal drone that makes the channel-keepers stop and listen and nod. They remember buildings that are gone now, absorbed back into the plateau, their stones reused so many times that the original quarry marks have been polished away. They sit in the tea-houses with their mineral-crusted hands wrapped around ceramic cups — the cups themselves are beautiful, glazed in colors produced by the metallic content of the local water, iridescent greens and deep ferric reds — and they tap on the table and they hum, and the deep-workers listen, and the pressure-readers listen, and even the children listen, because the old ones are singing in the same key as the city and sometimes the city seems to answer.

---

There is a woman you meet, or rather a woman who allows herself to be met, on your second day.

She is somewhere between middle age and old age — in Thurm this span is wide, because the mineral accumulation ages the appearance while the geothermal warmth and the constant low-level vibration seem to preserve the body's function well past what you'd expect. She works as a pressure-reader, though she tells you this with a qualifier you don't fully understand — she says she *sits with the columns*, which is either a title or a description or a self-deprecation, and you cannot tell which because she says it with her hand on the table and her fingertips drumming a rhythm so subtle you only notice it when she stops.

Her name is — and here the drone swells, and you lose it, and she does not repeat it, and after a while you stop trying to remember what you heard and simply think of her as the woman who sits with the columns.

She tells you things. Not in an interview format — she is not being interviewed, she is allowing you to be present while she talks, which is different — and the things she tells you are layered the way the drone is layered, surface meaning resting on deeper meaning resting on something that might not be meaning at all but simply resonance.

She tells you that the water table rose fourteen centimeters in her lifetime and that this is neither crisis nor trend but simply what the water does. She tells you that the factories will need to be moved within two generations because the southeastern dip is deepening — the stone is dissolving faster there, has been dissolving faster for centuries, and the increased water pressure that makes the factories productive is the same pressure that is eating the ground out from under them. She tells you this with no particular emotion, the way you might tell someone that winter follows autumn. She tells you that the offerings at the seep-pools are not for the water and not for anything in the water but are for the *sound* — that the residents of Thurm are not superstitious about the hydrology but are deeply, inarticulately reverent toward the drone itself, the voice that the water makes when it passes through the bones of their plateau, because the drone is the proof that the system is working, that the water is moving, that the city is alive, and its absence — even for those few seconds of silence that stop everyone in their tracks — is the most frightening thing they know.

She tells you that the silence means the water has paused. Not stopped — water does not stop, not here, not in a system this pressurized and this vast — but *paused*, the way a breath pauses between exhale and inhale. The pause is the moment when the water table, pressed upward by geothermal force, reaches some equilibrium with the weight of the stone above it and hangs there, balanced, before the pressure builds again and the upwelling resumes. It lasts only seconds. It happens irregularly. It cannot be predicted, though the pressure-readers try. And in those seconds the city goes silent and every person in Thurm feels, in the sudden absence of vibration, the full and terrible fact of the stone beneath them — not alive, not singing, not conducting, just *stone*, inert and ancient and dissolving, and them on it, and nothing holding them there but the resumption of the sound.

She pauses. The drone fills the space her voice has left. Her fingers resume their rhythm on the table.

She tells you that she loves the sound. She tells you this the way someone might tell you they love a difficult parent — with full knowledge, with no illusions, with a weary tenderness that has survived its own reasons. She loves the sound because it is the sound of the world doing what the world does, which is to move water through stone, which is to dissolve and to build, which is to carry on without permission or purpose. She loves it because she was born into it and it is the medium of her thought and she cannot imagine thinking in silence any more than you can imagine thinking without language.

She asks you, gently but without flexibility, to leave before the next swell. She can feel it coming — through the table, through the floor, through the column of mineral-laden air that connects her body to the plateau's body. She wants to be alone with it. Or rather, she wants to be alone with the city being alone with it, which is different again, and which you do not fully understand, but which you respect, because by now you have been in Thurm long enough to know that there are things here that do not translate and that the failure to translate is not a failure but a boundary, a seep-pool edge where the water collects before finding its invisible channel down.

---

You leave. You descend through the haze, and the drone fades, and the silence when it comes is not silence but the ordinary noise of the world — wind, birds, the sound of your own footsteps on a path that does not vibrate — and it sounds thin, and unconvincing, and you press your hand against the rock face beside the trail and feel nothing, nothing, nothing at all, and for a moment you understand the children of Thurm, and then you don't, and then you keep walking.

---

But they are still up there. They have always been up there. The channel-keepers walking their circuits, the deep-workers tapping their messages through the stone, the pressure-readers watching their columns rise and fall, the factory workers shouting their consonant-heavy reports through curtains of steam, the children with their ears to the wet pavement, the old ones humming in the tea-houses, the woman who sits with the columns drumming her fingers on a table worn smooth by the drumming of fingers before hers.

The water rises. They shape its rising. The sound fills their world until their world and the sound are the same thing, and they live in it the way fish live in water — not by resisting it but by being made of the same stuff, by being continuous with it, by having no boundary between self and medium that would let them tell the difference.

They are not suffering. They are not trapped. They are *saturated* — with sound, with mineral, with the slow patience of a city that knows it is dissolving and builds anyway, and maintains what it builds, and maintains the maintenance, and finds in that endless recursive labor something that functions as meaning even if it cannot be extracted from its context and displayed.

They are the sound the water makes when it passes through the living stone.

They have always been the sound.
