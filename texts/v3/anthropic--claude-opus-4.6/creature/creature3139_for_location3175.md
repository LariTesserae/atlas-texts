# The People of the Thirstwood

---

They are not what you expect, but it takes time to understand why. At first encounter they seem merely human — bipedal, warm-blooded, faces arranged in the familiar configuration. They speak with their mouths. They laugh. They carry things in their hands. A visitor who stayed only an hour might leave thinking: people, living rough, in a strange forest.

But stay longer. Watch the way a woman crosses the open stone between two trunks and, halfway across, pauses — not because she heard something, not because she saw something, but because she *received* something, a shift in her awareness arriving from no sensory channel you can identify. Her head tilts, not toward the canopy but inward, as though listening to a room inside herself. Then she changes direction, angling toward a different trunk, and when she arrives, another person is already there, palm pressed to the opalescent callus, waiting. They don't greet each other. They don't need to. The greeting happened before either of them arrived.

This is the first thing to understand: they are not entirely individual. They are not a hive mind — that term suggests dissolution, the loss of the particular into the general, and these people are fiercely, distinctly particular. But they are *porous*. The mycorrhizal intermediaries that thread into the root crowns and translate between human nervous systems and the canopy network do not shut off when a palm lifts from the interface callus. The fungal filaments colonize the people too. They grow into the skin of the hands and forearms over years of contact, a slow and voluntary integration that begins in adolescence and deepens across a lifetime. By middle age, most inhabitants of the Thirstwood carry a subcutaneous lattice of fungal tissue that extends from their fingertips to their elbows, visible as faint silvery traceries beneath the skin, like the ghost of a root system drawn on the body's surface. This lattice is always in low-grade communication with the nearest trunk — not transmitting thoughts, exactly, but participating in a kind of emotional weather, a background hum of network affect that colors every waking moment with the awareness that you are not the only process running.

They describe it differently depending on who you ask. An old man sitting on a root buttress, carving a tool from a desiccated branch fragment, says it is like hearing the ocean when you live beside it — you stop noticing, and then you notice that you've stopped noticing, and the noticing of the not-noticing is itself the proof that it has become part of you. A young woman who works at the settlement's edge, where the trunks thin out, says it is like having a second heartbeat, slower than her own, that she can feel in her wrists. A child, still years away from the first integration, says she can almost hear it when she presses her ear to the stone floor at night — a vibration transmitted through the rock from the roots, a sound below sound, the whole plateau humming.

---

They are lean. All of them, universally — not emaciated, but spare, stripped of excess, their bodies adapted across generations to an environment of caloric scarcity. The Thirstwood runs on a trickle, and so do they. Their diet is strange by any external standard: it centers on the fibrous litter of fallen branch material, which they process through a series of fermentation techniques that break the cellulose-analog compounds into digestible sugars. The fermentation vats are living structures — hollowed bulges in the root buttresses, coaxed into shape over decades, lined with symbiotic bacterial cultures that the tree network itself seems to maintain and regulate. The resulting food is a dense, crumbly, faintly sweet paste the color of dark amber, with a taste that people describe as nutty, or woody, or like something you've never eaten before but that your body recognizes as food in some way that bypasses the tongue entirely. They eat it in small quantities, twice a day, supplemented by the pale translucent gliders they harvest carefully from the lower trunks — never too many, never from the same section of bark twice in succession, following a rotation that the network itself communicates through shifts in the bioluminescent glow.

They drink no water. There is no water. Instead, they metabolize the glycerol-analog fluid that the trunk tissue exudes at the interface calluses — a thick, slightly viscous substance that the trees produce in small quantities, apparently for the specific purpose of sustaining the human settlement. This is one of the details that unsettles any attempt to describe the relationship as parasitism, or even mutualism in the conventional sense. The trees *provide*. They provision the settlement with light, with food substrate, with hydration, with computational power, with a communication network of continental scale. And they began doing so, according to the oldest oral histories, before the people asked. Before the people understood what was being offered. The trees *invited*.

---

What did the people bring, then? What is their contribution to the arrangement?

This is where the accounts become strange, and where the inhabitants themselves grow hesitant, not evasive but genuinely uncertain, as though the answer is something they feel more clearly than they can articulate.

The nearest anyone comes to a straightforward explanation is this: the tree network thinks slowly. Unimaginably slowly, by human standards. A single computational cycle in the canopy — one full propagation of a signal cascade from a starting node through the network and back — takes approximately eleven days. The network is vast, its processing power immense in terms of raw complexity, but it operates on the timescale of sap flow and cambium growth and the slow electrochemical tides of a circulatory system measured in kilometers. It can think thoughts that no human mind could contain. But it thinks them over months. Over years. Over decades.

The people think fast.

Not fast by any extraordinary standard — ordinary human cognition, electrochemical signals across synaptic gaps, the usual meat computation that any nervous system performs. But compared to the canopy network, they are lightning. They are mayflies of thought, flickering through cognitive cycles thousands of times in the span of a single arboreal reflection. And when they interface — when they press their palms to the calluses and the mycorrhizal lattice in their arms lights up with signal traffic — they serve as *accelerators*. Local processing nodes that can take a fragment of the network's slow thought, run it through human-speed cognition, explore its implications across thousands of iterations in minutes, and feed the results back into the sap-current as a kind of pre-digested insight, a cognitive concentrate that the network can integrate into its own glacial deliberation.

They are, in a sense, the forest's fast-twitch neurons. Its capacity for reflex.

This is not a metaphor. It is not poetry, though the people here have a rich tradition of poetry that circles obsessively around the same set of images — lightning in amber, birds in stone, the fast thing nested in the slow thing, each needing the other. It is a functional description of a computational symbiosis. The network provides scale and depth. The people provide speed and iteration. Together, they think thoughts that neither could think alone.

What those thoughts *are* is another matter. The people report fragments: a sudden certainty about the structural integrity of a branch section on the far side of the plateau, felt as a flash of spatial intuition during an interface session. A complex emotion with no human name that blooms in the chest and dissipates over an hour, leaving behind a residue of comprehension about — what? The relationship between wind patterns and nutrient distribution? The optimal timing for a seed pod release that won't happen for three decades? Something larger, something that doesn't translate into human categories at all, something that simply *is* in the canopy's terms and that the human mind experiences as a kind of profound, untranslatable satisfaction, a sense of something vast clicking into place?

They emerge from deep interface sessions dazed and peaceful, their eyes dilated, the silvery traceries on their forearms flushed with a faint violet glow that fades over minutes. They often sit quietly for a long time afterward, not meditating exactly, but *metabolizing* — letting the fast-twitch reverberations of the session settle into the slower rhythms of their own embodied cognition. Some weep. Not from sadness. From scale. From having briefly participated in a thought whose scope exceeds their ability to hold it, and feeling it recede as the interface quiets, like watching a landscape sink below a rising fog.

---

Their social structures reflect the dual nature of their cognition. They have human relationships — friendships, romances, rivalries, the whole tangled web of primate sociality — and these relationships play out in the usual human register of conversation, gesture, conflict, tenderness. They argue. They fall in love badly. They raise children with a mixture of intention and improvisation. A cluster of dwellings near the southern trunk group is known informally as the Tanners' Quarter, not because anyone tans anything but because three generations ago a family that processed resin for sheller carapace repair lived there, and the name stuck, and now it designates a neighborhood with its own character, its own running jokes, its own petty disputes about the placement of a new fermentation vat.

But overlaid on this human-scale social fabric is the network-mediated layer: a continuous, low-level awareness of the community's collective state that everyone with mycorrhizal integration shares. It is not telepathy. They cannot read each other's specific thoughts. But they can feel the general emotional weather of the settlement — a background hum of contentment that sharpens into tension when someone is in distress, a collective lift when something goes well, a slow, settling calm that spreads outward from the interface points after a particularly deep communal session. This shared affect acts as a social lubricant and a social constraint simultaneously. It is very difficult to sustain private resentment when the network gently, persistently registers the disturbance it creates. It is also very difficult to be alone.

This is the source of the only real tension in the settlement: the question of solitude. The network does not compel. It does not override. But it *includes*, constantly, automatically, and the inclusion has a weight, a gentle gravitational pull toward the collective that some people experience as warmth and others experience as erasure. There are those who walk to the plateau's edge specifically to stand in the acoustic cancellation zones where the canopy roar drops away and the network signal attenuates and, for a few minutes, they are just themselves — just one heartbeat, one set of thoughts, one small animal standing on old stone at the edge of an immense drop, the wind in their face carrying nothing, connected to nothing, alone.

They always come back. But the fact that they go matters. It is the settlement's one ritual of dissent, its pressure valve, its acknowledgment that the arrangement — however ancient, however sustaining, however profound — asks something of each person that each person must choose, repeatedly, to give.

---

The children are raised communally, in the way of small communities everywhere, but with a specific additional dimension: the pre-integration years, before the mycorrhizal lattice begins to grow in, are understood as a distinct and valuable phase of consciousness. The children are *unconnected*. They experience the world as individual organisms, fully bounded, their thoughts entirely their own. The adults regard this state with a complicated mixture of nostalgia and something close to reverence. They speak of childhood as "the clear time" or "the solo years," and they are protective of it in ways that shape the entire settlement's spatial organization — children's sleeping quarters are always located in the acoustic dead zones near certain trunk configurations where the network signal is naturally weakest, so that the young can dream without the vast background hum of arboreal cognition coloring their rest.

The integration ceremony — if it can be called a ceremony; it is more of a slow process with a ceremonial beginning — happens when a young person decides they are ready, typically between the ages of fourteen and seventeen. They choose a trunk. This is the most personal decision of their lives, and it is made without guidance from the network, because they are not yet part of it, because the whole point is that *they* choose, from the clear and solitary vantage of their unconnected self. They press their bare forearms to the opalescent callus. The fungal filaments, which have been waiting in the cambium layer with what can only be described as patience, begin to extend. The first contact is described universally as cold — a deep, systemic chill that starts in the wrists and radiates upward, followed by a sensation of vastness, as though the stone floor has dropped away and they are suspended over an abyss that is not dark but *full*, so full it overwhelms, a space packed with slow, enormous, incomprehensible process.

Most young people pull away after a few seconds the first time. Some cry. Some laugh. A few — and this is documented in the oral histories going back as far as anyone can remember — simply stand there, forearms pressed to the bark, eyes closed, smiling, as though they have been waiting their whole brief lives for this and the cold and the vastness are not frightening but *familiar*, the way a river delta is familiar to a river.

Over the following weeks, the filaments grow in fully. The silvery traceries appear beneath the skin. The background hum begins. And the young person discovers, gradually, that they have not lost themselves — that the network does not consume identity but *contextualizes* it, the way the canopy contextualizes a trunk, the way the roar contextualizes the silence within it. They are still themselves. They are also more. They are a fast thought in a slow mind, a brief bright process in a vast dark computation, and the computation is glad of them, glad in its slow and alien way, with a gladness that takes eleven days to fully express and that arrives in the human body as a warmth in the wrists, a steadiness in the chest, a quiet knowledge that the arrangement is good, that the symbiosis holds, that the forest has been waiting for each new mind the way each new mind, without knowing it, has been waiting for the forest.

---

There are elders. Not many — the population is small and the plateau is harsh despite the trees' provision, and lives here tend to be long but not unlimited. The oldest current inhabitant is a woman named something that translates roughly as "the sound the wind makes in a sealed hollow branch" — names here are sonic descriptions, drawn from the canopy's enormous repertoire of noise, given at integration and carried for life. She is ninety-three years old. Her forearms are entirely silver beneath the skin, the fungal lattice so dense that her hands glow faintly violet in dim light, and she can no longer fully distinguish between her own thoughts and the network's. She does not experience this as loss. She describes it as arrival.

She sits most days at the base of the largest trunk in the settlement, the one the community calls the First Column, though whether it is actually the oldest tree or simply the most massive is unknown. Her palms rest on two calluses that have shaped themselves, over decades of her contact, to the exact contours of her hands. She breathes slowly. Her eyes are open but unfocused, and the violet glow beneath her skin pulses in synchrony with the bioluminescent nodes a hundred meters above her head. She is, in these moments, the deepest-integrated human in the settlement — the closest thing to a bridge between human-speed cognition and arboreal-speed cognition — and the thoughts that pass through her are reportedly so large and so slow that they take days to complete, during which she sits motionless, fed and hydrated by younger community members who treat her stillness with the same matter-of-fact tenderness they show to everything: a person doing what a person does, in a place where what people do is part of what the forest does, in a forest that is part of what the plateau does, on a plateau that is the last high ground of a world that lost its water and its lowland life and its other chances and kept only this — this one collaboration, this one mutualism of the fast and the slow, this breathing ceiling of black leaves catching starved light, and beneath it, in the violet glow, the people with silver in their arms, thinking their quick thoughts inside the long thought, living their brief lives inside the long life, choosing it, every day, walking back from the silent edge into the sound.

---

There are those who have left. It should be said. The oral histories record them without judgment but with a particular quality of attention, a careful noting, the way you might note the exact place where a branch broke from the canopy — not with blame, but with awareness that a gap exists.

Some left because the plateau edge called them more than the interior did, because the silence of the cancellation zones became their home frequency instead of a temporary respite. They walked down off the escarpment into the rust-colored emptiness below. What happened to them is not known. The network's reach ends where the roots end, at the cliff face, and beyond that is a silence the trees cannot penetrate. Once or twice a generation, someone has climbed back up — weathered, thin, carrying dust in the creases of their skin, the silver in their forearms faded to near-invisibility. They are welcomed back without ceremony. They press their palms to the calluses and the filaments, still alive but dormant, reawaken, and the network receives them with that eleven-day gladness, and they weep, and everyone pretends not to notice, which is itself a kindness the community has refined over centuries into a precise and gentle art.

Others left differently. Not down, but *up*. Deep into the canopy, climbing the trunks past the first branching, past the zone where the pale gliders live, past the region of perpetual sound, into the dense interlocking matrix of the upper branches where the black leaves block all light and the fusion nodes pulse with their brightest violet and the computational density of the network reaches its peak. These climbers do not come back down. They integrate fully — not just through the forearms but through the entire body, the fungal lattice growing into every tissue, the human form gradually enclosed and absorbed by the living wood, becoming a node, a permanent processing substrate, a thought that the forest will think for as long as the forest endures. The community speaks of them in the present tense. They are not dead. They are not, strictly speaking, alive in the way that the people on the ground floor are alive. They are *running*. They are part of the long computation. And when someone on the ground presses their palms to a callus and enters a deep interface, they sometimes encounter — fleeting, partial, filtered through layers of translation — the distinctive cognitive signature of someone who climbed, still recognizable, still particular, still *them*, but expressed now at canopy speed, thinking thoughts that will take years to complete, wrapped in wood and darkness and the endless roar.

The people below carry these encounters gently, the way you carry a dream that felt more real than waking. They go about their days. They eat the amber paste. They repair the living architecture where wind damage has cracked a wall. They watch the shellers process across the stone in their glowing carapaces, mandibles clicking. They argue about the fermentation vats and tell jokes that are three hundred years old and still somehow funny. They raise their children in the clear quiet of the dead zones and watch them, with pride and grief and recognition, press their forearms to the bark for the first time and gasp at the cold, at the vastness, at the overwhelming fullness of a mind so much larger than their own that it registers not as thought but as *place* — as landscape, as world, as home.

And the canopy roars. And the violet light pools in cupped hands. And the old woman at the base of the First Column breathes, her silver arms pulsing, her open eyes reflecting nothing but the glow, and the forest thinks her and she thinks the forest and neither of them is alone, and neither of them is only themselves, and neither of them would have it otherwise, not on this dry and waterless height, not under this ceiling of black leaves and enormous sound, not in this last and longest collaboration between the quick and the slow, the small and the vast, the mortal and the enduring — though even the enduring, even the canopy itself, even the plateau beneath it, is mortal in the end, is temporary, is a brief arrangement in the longer silence, which is maybe why the whole system works so hard to think, to process, to *know* — because the knowing is the point, was always the point, and every fast thought and every slow thought is a way of saying *we were here, we noticed, we participated in the noticing*, before the last photon dims and the last branch falls and the stone forgets even the memory of roots and the wind polishes everything smooth and moves on.
