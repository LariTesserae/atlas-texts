# Who Lives in the Flats at the End of Wearing

---

They are not easy to see. That is the first thing to understand about them — not because they hide, but because your eye has to learn a new grammar of scale and motion before it can pick them out from the stone and the shimmer. They are low to the ground, as everything here is low to the ground. They are the color of what they live among. And they are still for long periods — so still that the first one you notice will likely be one you've been looking at for some time already, mistaking it for a rounded stone or a strange spreading tussock, until it shifts its weight and you understand all at once that it is alive, that it has been watching you with a calm, untroubled awareness the entire time.

---

They are amphibian. Or rather — they are what amphibians become when given enough time and enough of this particular world, a world that never asks them to choose between water and land because the distinction has been erased. Their skin is the dark ruddy brown of the gneiss, mottled with bands of mineral grey so precise that when one lies flat on a stone shelf it genuinely vanishes, its outline dissolving into the rock's own banding. The skin is not scaled, not furred — it is smooth and faintly glistening, always damp, the surface busy with gas exchange. They breathe through it as much as through the low, wide nostrils set into the blunt front of the face. Their lungs exist but are modest, supplementary. The skin does the deeper work. This means they cannot dry out, but here, in this place, nothing dries out. The negotiation between wet and dry was settled, as the landscape's own negotiation was settled, long ago.

They are broad. Built low and wide, the way the land is low and wide, their bodies shaped less like any single animal you might name and more like a general principle of distribution — of spreading weight across the largest possible area. An adult, fully settled onto a stone shelf, might cover a space the size of a large platter, but it would stand no higher than your mid-shin. The limbs are short and thick, heavily muscled in a slow-twitch way that suggests endurance rather than speed, each foot broad and flat with long, splayed digits that grip the wet stone with an almost adhesive sureness. They do not slip. They have never needed to learn to recover from slipping. Their relationship with this stone is too old for that.

The head is wide and flat-skulled, continuous with the body in a way that makes it hard to say where the neck ends and the head begins — there is no sharp demarcation, just a gradual widening, a slow unfurling of bone into the broad triangular platform of the cranium. The mouth is wide, lipless, curved into something that looks like patience. The eyes are set far apart and slightly raised, like a crocodilian's, so that when the body is submerged in the shallow water-films — which is often, which is the default state — only the eyes and the crown of the head break the surface. The eyes are extraordinary. Large, round, the irises a deep amber-gold with flecks of darker rust, they catch the light and hold it. They are not sharp, predatory eyes. They are watching eyes. Observing eyes. Eyes made for taking in wide, low, horizontal landscapes and reading the small shifts within them — the passage of a shadow that means a wading bird has moved, the trembling of a water surface that means rain is coming from beyond the haze, the subtle darkening of a cyanobacterial mat that means it has been grazed recently and will not reward further attention.

They see color. They see motion. They see, with particular acuity, the quality of light on water, and they can read the sky's condition by its reflection in the shallows. This is not intelligence, exactly — not in the way you might frame it — but it is a profound, embodied attentiveness. They notice everything within their radius. They simply don't react to most of it.

---

There are many of them here, scattered across the flats the way the tussocks are scattered — widely spaced, each one centered in its own territory, none of them urgent about the boundaries. You might have walked past dozens before you saw the first. Now that you know what to look for — the too-regular oval of a body on an otherwise smooth stone shelf, the faint glint of an eye at water level in a shallow pool, the nearly imperceptible rise and fall of breathing flanks — they begin to resolve out of the landscape one by one, like stars appearing as your eyes adjust to darkness.

Some are solitary. A single adult draped across a warm pavement, forelimbs tucked, hindlimbs splayed behind, utterly motionless except for the rhythmic pulsing of the throat. These solitary ones tend to be in the best spots — the broadest, warmest stone shelves with the thinnest films of water flowing across them, positioned where they can feel the warmth from below and the sun from above simultaneously. They have earned these spots not through aggression but through persistence. Through simply being there, day after day, season after season, until the idea of that particular shelf without that particular body on it has become geologically implausible. Some of the older solitaries have been in their places so long that the cyanobacterial mats have grown up and around them, tracing the outlines of their bodies in blue-green film, so that even when they shift to feed or to follow the sun, they leave behind a shadow of themselves — a body-shaped imprint in living microbial growth that slowly fills in, slowly becomes stone-colored again, but which for a time marks where someone ancient has been resting.

Others are found in loose aggregations of three or four, usually in the wider shallow basins where the water deepens to hand-breadth and the amber-brown tint suggests a richness of dissolved nutrients. These groups are not families in any obvious sense. They do not touch. They do not orient toward each other. They simply coexist in a shared space, each one facing a different direction, their combined visual fields covering the full horizon. Whether this is cooperation or coincidence is a question that has no answer here, because the distinction between those two things has been worn down, like everything else, into something smooth and indeterminate. They tolerate proximity. Proximity is tolerated. The result is the same either way: a loose constellation of watchful bodies, distributed across a basin, each one still, each one breathing through its skin, each one listening to the frogs.

---

Because they listen. This is important. The frog-song is not background to them. It is information — a continuous, densely encoded field of data that tells them everything they need to know about the state of the world.

When the frogs call in their full layered chorus — the trills, the clicks, the metallic drones all present and overlapping — the world is as it should be. Water levels are right. Temperature is right. No large disturbance has passed through recently. The ones who live here relax into this sound the way you might relax into the warmth of a familiar room. Their breathing slows. Their eyes half-close. They sink a little lower against the stone, surrendering a fraction more of their body to the water-film, letting the evaporative moisture settle on their backs.

But the frogs are sensitive. When the wind shifts, certain species fall silent before others. When a shadow passes — a rare large bird soaring far overhead, or an unusual cloud formation — the chorus modulates, drops in density, recovers. When the warm rains come hammering across the flats, the frogs explode into a different kind of song, ecstatic and percussive, and the ones who live here respond to this too — they lift their heads slightly, open their mouths, let the rain strike the broad surfaces of their skulls and run down into the corners of their wide, patient jaws. This is how they drink: not by seeking water, but by letting water find them. Everything here is like that. The strategy, if it can be called a strategy, is one of receiving.

They eat, of course. They must eat. But the eating is so slow, so infrequent, so thoroughly integrated into the texture of their stillness, that you could watch one for a full day and see it feed perhaps twice. The strike, when it comes, looks almost involuntary — a sudden sideways snap of the wide mouth, faster than anything else they do by an order of magnitude, catching a midge or a small fly that has drifted too close over the water surface. The jaws close. The throat pulses once. The body resettles. If you blinked, you would think nothing had happened. Some of the larger ones, the truly old ones whose backs are encrusted with a fine patina of mineral deposit from decades of water flowing over them, also eat the cyanobacterial mats themselves, rasping them slowly from the stone with broad, ridged palates. This is a meal that takes hours. They move forward a centimeter at a time, their flat mouths working against the rock with a sound like a whisper, like a secret being told to the ground. The mats grow back. Everything grows back here, slowly, in its own time.

---

The young are almost impossible to find. They are tiny — the size of the last joint of your smallest finger at first — and they are even more perfectly camouflaged than the adults, because they have not yet accumulated the mineral patina and cyanobacterial colonization that gives the older ones their slightly raised, textured appearance. A juvenile is pure stone-colored. It sits in a crevice between exposed bedding planes and it is, for all visual purposes, a fleck of gneiss. Its eyes — proportionally larger than an adult's, a brighter gold — are the only giveaway, and even those are usually half-lidded, half-hidden.

They are born in the water-films, laid as eggs in the thinnest flowing sheets that cover the warmest stones, and the eggs themselves are nearly transparent, each one containing a dark speck of developing life that pulses faintly with a proto-heartbeat. The females — larger than the males, broader, their skin slightly smoother — lay them in long gelatinous ribbons that adhere to the stone and undulate gently in the water's slow movement. There is no nest. There is no guarding. The eggs are left to the warmth and the water and the sun, and most of them survive, because there is very little here that eats them. The predation pressure in this landscape is astonishingly low. Everything is so slow, so broadly distributed, so marginal in its energy budget, that the concept of a dedicated egg-predator has never been worth the metabolic investment. Things die, of course. Eggs wash away in heavier rains. Juveniles are occasionally taken by the wading birds, snatched up in that sharp *tok* of beak against stone. But the losses are gentle, statistical, impersonal. No one hunts them with intent. The landscape is too worn down for intent.

The juveniles grow slowly. Of course they grow slowly. Everything here is slow. A young one might take fifteen or twenty years to reach what you'd call full adult size, and many of them never do — they simply find a crevice, or a shallow pool, or a warm stone shelf, and they settle, and they stop growing, and they begin the long project of staying. The growth is not the point. The arrival is the point. The finding of a place and the becoming part of it.

---

How long do they live? This is hard to say, and the difficulty is itself revealing.

The older ones — the ones with mineral deposits on their backs, the ones whose outlines have been traced in cyanobacterial mat and retraced and traced again until the layers of biological graffiti form a kind of halo around their resting places — these ones are old in a way that challenges easy measurement. Their metabolisms are so slow, their energy expenditures so minimal, their bodies so thoroughly integrated into the thermal and chemical rhythms of the stone they rest on, that the normal markers of aging don't apply in any straightforward way. They don't weaken. They don't decline. They simply become more of what they already are — more still, more stone-colored, more encrusted, more patient. An old one might not move from its shelf for a full season. Rain falls on it. Sun warms it. The water-film flows over and around it. Midges are caught. Cyanobacteria are rasped. The frogs sing. And the old one persists, its heartbeat so slow that you'd need to press your ear against its flank for a long time before you heard it — a deep, resonant *thud*, once every thirty seconds or so, felt as much as heard, a sound like a stone settling.

Some of the oldest ones have been in their places so long that the stone beneath them is slightly more worn than the stone around them — a barely perceptible depression, a fraction of a millimeter deeper, where the constant small friction of their bodies against the rock over decades, over what might be centuries, has contributed its own tiny increment to the great project of erosion that defines this place. They are wearing the stone down. They are part of the wearing. They are geological agents, patient and marginal, and the flats are fractionally flatter for their presence.

When one of them dies — and they do die, eventually, though the event is so quiet and so gradual that it hardly registers as a transition — the body remains where it was. It does not fall or collapse because it was never raised. It simply stops. The breathing stops. The heartbeat stops. The eyes, which were half-lidded already, close fully or don't close at all, and the gold of the irises fades to a clouded amber. The water-film continues to flow over and around the body. The cyanobacterial mats begin to grow across it rather than around it. Slowly, over months, the body is incorporated — its minerals leaching into the stone, its organic matter feeding a local bloom of microbial life that is briefly, subtly greener than the surrounding mats. A season later, there is a low mound where the body was, capped in blue-green film, indistinguishable from the dozens of other low mounds scattered across the flats. Two seasons later, even the mound is gone, smoothed and spread by rain, and the stone shelf has a faintly enriched patina where someone lived for a very long time and then became part of what they were living on.

This is not tragic. It is not even particularly notable, here. It is simply what happens when the distinction between the living and the ground has been negotiated down to almost nothing. They are made of the same minerals as the stone. They are wet with the same water. They are warmed by the same sun. The difference between a living one and the rock it rests on is real but narrow — a slow heartbeat, a half-open golden eye, the occasional snap of a jaw. Take those away and what remains is still, in some sense, continuous with what was there before.

---

But they are alive. Let that be clear. They are alive in a way that is entire and sufficient, lacking nothing.

When the warm rain comes — and it is coming now, you can see it approaching across the flats, a darkening of the haze-horizon, a subtle shift in the frog-song — the ones who are here do something that might be the closest they come to expression. They lift their heads. Not far. A centimeter, two centimeters. They orient toward the rain. Their wide mouths open slightly. And they wait for it, faces tilted up, eyes open, as the first heavy drops strike the stone around them and send up tiny explosions of spray, and then the rain is on them, drumming on their broad flat skulls, and their throats pulse — not to eat, not to breathe, but to *vocalize*. They call. A sound you haven't heard until now because it is rare, because it only happens in the rain — a low, resonant, almost subsonic hum that rises from their bodies and enters the stone beneath them and propagates outward through the wet rock in every direction, a vibration you feel in your feet and your chest and the back of your teeth. One starts, and then another, and then another, across the flats, until the rain and the frogs and the deep ancestral hum of these old, old creatures merge into a single sound that is indistinguishable from the sound of the place itself — the sound of warm water on ancient stone, the sound of a landscape that has finished becoming and is now simply, fully, patiently being.

They do not have a name for themselves. They do not name. But if the stone could speak for them — the stone that has known them for so long that their weight is part of its wearing, that their minerals are part of its composition, that their slow persistent warmth is part of its memory of the sun — the stone might call them something that translates, roughly and inadequately, as *the ones who stayed*. Not the ones who arrived. Not the ones who adapted. The ones who stayed. The ones who were here when the hills were still hills, who watched the valleys fill and the ridges dissolve, who settled lower as the land settled lower, who became flatter as the world became flatter, who were never in a hurry because there was never anywhere else to be.

They are still here. They are always still here. The rain is warm on their backs, the stone is warm beneath them, the frogs are singing, the light is gold, and they are humming their low hum into the rock, and the rock is carrying it outward to the horizon, and the horizon is five or six kilometers of shimmering wet flatness in every direction, and every centimeter of it is alive, and every living thing in it is the same temperature as the ground, and the ground is the same temperature as the water, and the water is the same temperature as the air, and everything is warm, and everything is low, and everything is here, and has been here, and will be here, after the last hill has been worn away and the last sharp edge has been rounded and the only thing left with any voice is small and flat and patient and pressed against the oldest stone in the world, singing.
