# Who Lives Here

---

Start with the shelled ones, because they are the foundation of everything you can see, and because they are the easiest to misunderstand.

They are not tortoises, though that is the shape your mind will reach for. They are not gastropods, though their movement has that quality of patient, muscular negotiation with a surface. They are something that has no precise analogue — something that has been exactly and only itself for so long that comparison to other lineages feels like a category error. Their shells are not grown in the way a snail grows its shell, deposited in mineral spirals. They are *accumulated*. Layers of calcium and silicate drawn up from the stone itself through the tissues of the foot, laid down over decades in thin, irregular laminae that give each shell the cross-section of a canyon wall — readable, if you knew the language, as a record of every season of moisture and every season of drought the creature has endured. Some of the shells, the largest, the ones that have settled so deeply into the runnels that they seem less like organisms and more like features of the watershed, are thick enough that you could count the laminae and arrive at numbers that would make you uneasy. Three hundred. Five hundred. Numbers that don't belong to animals, that belong to trees, to geological strata, to the kinds of timescales where individual existence starts to blur into something else.

They are not individual in the way you want them to be. This is the first thing to understand.

Beneath each shell, the body is soft, pale, and surprisingly warm — warmer than the stone, warmer than the air, deriving heat not from the sun but from a metabolic process so slow and so constant it resembles less a flame and more a kind of chemical patience. They have no heads. Or rather, they have a leading edge that serves the functions you'd associate with a head — sensing, tasting the substrate, navigating — but it is not differentiated enough to call a face. There are no eyes. There are pits, shallow depressions along the rim of the foot where the tissue is thinnest, and these pits are sensitive to light, to vibration, to the chemical signatures of moisture, of lichen, of other shelled ones. They feel their way across the plateau in a state of continuous, ambient awareness that has no center — no moment of *I see this* or *I notice that*, just an unbroken field of sensation, as if the creature were less an animal moving through an environment and more a piece of the environment that happens to be slightly more mobile than the rest.

They eat the stone. Not directly — not by grinding or dissolving it — but by consuming the microbial film that grows in the uppermost fraction of a millimeter of every exposed surface, a film so thin and so constant it is invisible to you but which represents, to the shelled ones, an inexhaustible pasture. Their passage leaves the faintest trails: stone that is microscopically cleaner, fractionally brighter, and which will regrow its film within a season, ready to be grazed again. They have been grazing these same surfaces, the same runnels and hollows and gentle undulations, for longer than the erosion cycle that created those features. They are participants in the erosion. Their passage, multiplied by millions of individuals over millions of years, has helped shape the very topography they inhabit. The runnels exist partly because they exist. They exist partly because the runnels exist. The plateau and the shelled ones are a single sculpture, carving each other.

They reproduce — but rarely, and strangely. There is no mating. There is no season. Occasionally, perhaps once in a decade, perhaps less, a shelled one will slow even beyond its already glacial pace and stop entirely. It will remain motionless for weeks. During this time, the foot tissue at its trailing edge thickens, reddens, develops a texture that is almost granular. Then, without any visible trigger, a portion of that tissue separates. Not dramatically — not like birth, not like budding in any vigorous sense — more like a stone calving from a cliff face. A soft, shell-less fragment of living tissue, no larger than a coin, detaches and begins its own imperceptible journey across the stone. It will not grow a shell for years. During those years it is vulnerable to desiccation, to the mouthparts of the crawlers, to the simple entropic cruelty of being small and soft in a place made of stone and dry air. Most do not survive. The ones that do find a crevice, find a channel opening where the warm breath from below keeps the air humid, and they begin the work of accumulating, lamina by lamina, the armor that will carry them through centuries.

They do not learn. They do not adapt within a lifetime. But they carry something — not intelligence, not memory in any neural sense, but a chemical continuity, a kind of metabolic record encoded in the composition of their tissues, that means each new fragment begins its life already tuned to the specific mineral signature of the stone it was born on. Put one on a different part of the plateau, a part with slightly different calcium content, slightly different iron, and it would move — slowly, over years — back toward the substrate it knows. Or it would die. They are that specific. That married to their place.

---

Now the crawlers, which are more complex and more troubling.

They are not insects and not myriapods, though they share superficial architecture with both. They are segmented, yes — between fifteen and thirty segments depending on maturity, each one slightly independent in its musculature, giving them that disturbing liquidity of motion, the sense that they are not so much crawling as *pouring* themselves through the crevices. Each segment bears a pair of legs, short and hook-tipped, designed not for speed but for grip, for navigating vertical stone faces and for wedging into fractures that seem too narrow for anything with a body. They are the color of old blood, as noted — but this color is not pigment. It is the hemoglobin-analogue that suffuses their tissues, giving them an oxygen-carrying capacity out of proportion to their size, an adaptation to the thin air of the plateau that allows them to remain active even when the shelled ones and the felt-birds have slowed to near-torpor in the leaner seasons.

They are the most active predators on the surface of the plateau, but to call them predators is to vastly overdramatize what they do. They eat the mites. The mites are everywhere — translucent, barely visible, smaller than a grain of sand, living in the lichen crust and reproducing with a speed that is the inverse of everything else here. The mites are the fast metabolism of the plateau, the quick-turnover layer, and the crawlers regulate them. That is their role. They are not hunters. They are grazers of a higher order, grazing not on the lichen but on the things that graze on the lichen, keeping the mite population within bounds that allow the lichen to persist. Without the crawlers, the mites would consume the lichen crust down to bare stone within a generation. Without the lichen, the microbial film would be unanchored, the shelled ones would starve, the moisture cycle would falter, the felt-birds would have nothing to orient their lives around. The crawlers are a keystone, and they know nothing about it.

But they have a behavior that complicates the picture.

At intervals — irregular, unpredictable, linked to no weather event or season you can identify — a crawler will stop feeding. It will climb to the highest point of stone in its immediate area, which may be a ridge-crest or the top of one of those half-dissolved walls. It will orient itself toward the nearest channel opening. And it will remain there, motionless, for days. During this time, its color darkens from dried-blood to near-black. Its segments contract, pressing tightly together, giving it a compact, almost pupiform appearance. It does not eat. It does not respond to touch. It appears to be in a state of profound metabolic suspension — but its hook-tipped legs grip the stone with such force that you could not pry it loose without damaging it.

Then, after days or weeks, it resumes. It unclenches. Its color lightens. It descends from its perch and returns to its crevice-flowing, mite-grazing existence as if nothing happened. You will see, at any given time, perhaps one crawler in twenty in this state, distributed across the plateau like dark punctuation marks on the sentences of the ridgelines.

No one — nothing — has explained this behavior. It may be reproductive. It may be a form of dormancy triggered by some internal clock. Or it may be responsive — a reaction to the breathing from below, to some chemical or vibrational signal carried on those warm exhalations, a signal that means something to the crawlers' neurology in a way that produces this clenching, this orientation, this waiting. You watch them. They tell you nothing. They resume, and flow onward through their fractures, and the mites go on being consumed, and the lichen persists.

---

The felt-birds are the ones you will find hardest to leave.

This is because they look at you.

Not the blank, target-acquisition stare of a raptor. Not the nervous flick-glance of a prey species. Something else. They look at you with what appears to be *patience* — the kind of patience that implies they have seen things take a very long time and are comfortable with that. Their eyes are large, amber, with horizontal pupils that give them a panoramic field of vision and a faintly unsettling appearance, as if they are always seeing more than you are, as if the world they perceive extends a few degrees beyond the edges of yours.

They are warm-blooded, or something very close to it — maintaining a body temperature slightly above ambient through that dense fibrous integument, which traps air like the finest down but which, under magnification, would reveal itself to be not feather and not fur but something structurally unique: filaments that branch and re-branch in a fractal pattern, each filament coated in a waxy substance that repels the thin rain and retains heat. The coloring — grey, tan, that palest rose — comes from the filaments themselves, which incorporate mineral pigments drawn from the dust of the plateau. They are colored like the stone. They are wearing the stone.

Their wings work. Technically. The skeletal structure is there, the musculature is there, the filament-surface is broad enough to generate lift. They can fly. You will occasionally see one do it — a short, heavy, direct flight from one ridge-crest to another, ten or fifteen meters, executed with a competence that suggests the capability is fully maintained but simply not preferred. They fly the way you might sprint: available as an option, relevant in emergencies, otherwise not worth the caloric expenditure. Given the thinness of the air up here, flight costs them dearly. They have adapted by preferring not to pay.

What they do instead is perch, and watch, and attend.

The attending is the behavior you will notice most, because it is the behavior that makes the plateau feel less like an ecosystem and more like a congregation. The felt-birds orient toward the channel openings. Not all of them, not all the time — but enough that the pattern is unmistakable. On any given ridge-crest near a channel, the felt-birds will be facing inward, toward the opening, arranged in rough rows that follow the contour of the stone. They sit close together, their filamentous bodies touching, creating a continuous surface of grey-tan-rose that looks from a distance like a geological formation in its own right — a soft stratum deposited between the harder layers of the stone.

They open their mouths. Those wide, triangular mouths. Not to call — they are almost perfectly silent, producing at most a faint exhalation that is more breath than voice, a soft *hhhh* that is audible only if you are very close and very still. They open their mouths toward the channel openings, and they hold them open, and the warm breath from below passes over their palates and through their oral cavities and presumably — *presumably* — they are tasting it. Sampling it. Reading the chemical narrative that the deep thing breathes out into the world.

What do they learn? You cannot know. But you can observe the consequences. After a period of mouth-open attending — minutes, sometimes an hour — the felt-birds will shift. The whole group, simultaneously, without any visible signal, will reorient. They will face a new direction. They will move, as a body, to a different section of ridge-crest, or disperse to different ridges entirely, redistributing themselves across the plateau in a pattern that has no obvious logic from a human vantage but which, if you could see it from above, if you could map it over days and weeks, might reveal itself as a response to information carried on that breath — the location of moisture, the state of the lichen, the readiness of some part of the system for their presence or their absence.

They are not intelligent. They do not plan. But they are *informed*, in the oldest sense of the word — shaped from within by the data they receive, given form by the breath of the thing below, arranged and rearranged on the surface of the plateau like iron filings over a magnet that is slowly, slowly shifting.

They nest in the walls. The old walls, the ones no one remembers building. The half-circle enclosures, open to the sky, filled with dust and lichen and the slow passage of shelled ones — these are where the felt-birds lay their eggs, two at a time, in shallow scrapes in the accumulated grit behind the low stone courses. The eggs are the color of the stone. The hatchlings are the color of the stone. Everything here is the color of the stone. The young are fed on regurgitated mites and lichen-paste, a slurry that the adults produce in a specialized crop and deliver with a precision that suggests the parentage system here is communal — any adult, any chick, the investment distributed across the group the way the attending is distributed, no individual pair-bond visible, no territorial defense, just a collective investment in the continuation of felt-birds on the plateau.

And here: the felt-birds die in the channels. When a felt-bird is old — and old here may mean twenty years, may mean fifty, the fibrous integument makes aging difficult to assess — it flies. One last time, it uses those costly wings. It lifts from its ridge-crest and it flies not laterally, not to another perch, but downward, into the nearest channel opening, into the dark, into the warm breath. It does not come back. No bones are found at the channel mouths. No feather-equivalents. Whatever is below accepts them entirely.

---

And so you must speak about what is below, even though you cannot see it, even though speaking about it means speaking from the evidence of absences and warmths and the testimony of stone worn smooth by passage.

Something lives in the plateau. Not under it — *in* it. In the deep channels that permeate the stone the way capillaries permeate tissue, in passageways that may connect and ramify and descend to depths that correspond to what were once the roots of mountains, before the mountains were worn to this last exhausted exhalation of land. Something that is large. Not large the way a whale is large — not a single bounded body of that scale, though it may be that too — but large the way a fungal network is large, large the way a reef is large, extensive, distributed, a living thing whose body is also its infrastructure, whose boundaries are indistinguishable from the medium it inhabits.

The evidence:

The channels are smooth. Smoother than water erosion produces, smoother than wind. They have a polish that suggests the continuous passage of something soft and heavy and wet — something that moves through them the way a tongue moves through a mouth, constantly, maintaining the surfaces, keeping them open. The channels do not collapse. In stone this old, this fractured, passages should fill with debris, should narrow and close over centuries. These don't. They are maintained. Something keeps them clear.

The breath. That rhythmic exhalation — eight seconds, twelve seconds, the interval varying in a way that is not mechanical but biological. It carries moisture. It carries heat. It carries volatile compounds that the felt-birds read with their open mouths and that the shelled ones cluster toward with their rimming pits. It carries, if you are close enough and still enough, a smell that is the plateau's only true smell: shell-like, chalky, coppery, ancient, alive. It is the smell of a body. A body that is warm and wet in a place where everything on the surface is cool and dry.

The lichen grows fastest near the channel openings. The moss mats are thickest. The mites are densest. The crawlers are most numerous. The felt-birds attend. The shelled ones cluster. Everything on the surface of the plateau arranges itself in reference to what is below, the way everything in a tidal zone arranges itself in reference to the sea. The channels are the intertidal. The breath is the tide.

And the felt-birds fly into the channels to die and do not come back. And the shelled ones, when they finally stop — when the last lamina is laid down and the foot relaxes and the body cools — the shelled ones are found nearest to the openings, their empty shells aligned with the exhalation, as if even in death they are oriented by the breath. And over time, over the deep time this place operates in, those shells are — you have to assume — drawn inward. Incorporated. Fed into the channels by gravity and by the patient motion of whatever tends them, and consumed, recycled, their calcium and silicate returned to the system, passed through the body of the deep thing and breathed out again as mineral-laden moisture that feeds the lichen that feeds the mites that feed the crawlers that regulate the lichen that anchors the microbial film that feeds the shelled ones that will, in their centuries, accumulate the minerals back into their shells and carry them, in their final slowness, back to the openings.

The loop is closed. The loop is so closed it is almost a single metabolism.

What is below may be one organism. It may be a colony — a dense, ancient, cooperating mass of organisms so intertwined they function as one, the way a siphonophore functions as one, the way a forest's mycorrhizal network might, if you squint, function as one. It does not matter. The distinction between one organism and a million organisms functioning as one is a distinction that matters to taxonomy and to nothing else. What matters is: it breathes. It maintains the channels. It accepts the dead. It exhales the nutrients that sustain the living surface. It has been doing this for longer than the mountains have been flat.

You will not see it. You could descend into a channel — the largest are wide enough to admit a human body — and you would feel the warmth increase, feel the moisture thicken, feel the walls narrow and smooth around you, and at some point you would touch something that was not stone. Something that gave slightly under your hand. Something warm, and faintly pulsing, and slick with a mucus that smelled of chalk and copper. And if you had the courage or the foolishness to go further, you would find that the something was everywhere — lining the channel, *being* the channel, the distinction between the passage and the occupant dissolved, the stone and the flesh having grown together over eons until they are one material, one structure, one thing that breathes and erodes and accumulates and breathes.

But you will not descend. No one descends. The felt-birds descend, at the end. That is their privilege and their purpose, and you sense — not with your intellect but with something older and less precise — that it is not your privilege and not your purpose, and that the thing below knows this, in whatever way it knows anything, and that its breath, when it passes over your ankles, is not an invitation but an acknowledgment. You are here. You are noted. You are not of this system. You will pass.

---

And the walls.

Return to the walls, because the walls are the only true mystery, the only element that does not resolve into the closed loop of the plateau's ecology.

They are built. This is not ambiguous. The stones are selected — flatter than average, more uniform in size — and stacked with a deliberateness that weathering does not produce. Some of the walls have stones that were clearly carried from elsewhere on the plateau, their mineral signature different from the substrate they rest on. Someone or something chose these stones, moved them, placed them.

The walls are old. As old as anything on the surface. The lichen that covers them is continuous with the lichen on the surrounding stone, showing no boundary, no line of younger growth that would indicate the wall was built after the lichen established itself. Either the walls were built before the lichen — which would make them impossibly old — or the lichen colonized them so quickly and so thoroughly that no boundary remains, which would make the walls merely very old. Neither answer is comfortable.

They are not defensive. They are too low, too open, too gently curved. They do not enclose anything securely. They create spaces — gathering spaces, sheltering spaces, spaces where rain collects a little longer and where felt-birds nest and where the shelled ones accumulate in slightly higher densities — but they do not protect. They *suggest*. They say: here is a place that is slightly different from the place beside it. Here is an inside that is only barely distinguishable from the outside. Here is a boundary that is also a welcome.

The felt-birds did not build them. The felt-birds have no manipulative appendages, no behavior that involves moving stone. The shelled ones did not build them. The crawlers did not. Nothing currently living on the plateau's surface has the means or the apparent motivation to stack stone into arcs.

Three possibilities remain, and you are free to hold all three simultaneously, because the plateau does not demand that you choose:

**One.** Something else lived here once. Something with hands, or with hand-equivalents. Something that looked at the plateau and felt that it needed walls — not for defense, not for shelter, but for the same reason the felt-birds attend the channel openings: because organizing space is a form of attention, and attention is what this place runs on. This something lived here and built and then was absorbed, the way the felt-birds are absorbed, flying down into the channels at the end, its body and its calcium and its silicate returned to the loop. The walls are its only residue. They are the one thing the loop could not digest — not because stone is indigestible, but because the walls, as structures, as *organizations of stone*, have a utility that outlasts their builders. They collect rain. They shelter nests. They gather the shelled ones. They persist because persisting is what they are good at, and the system, in its thoroughness, in its deep pragmatic patience, does not dismantle what is useful.

**Two.** The thing below built them. Reached up, in some era, in some extension of itself through the channels and onto the surface, and arranged the stone. Not with hands — with the same slow, distributed, mucus-and-mineral force that maintains the channels. Pushed the flat stones into place over decades, centuries, the way it pushes breath through the openings. Built the walls the way a body builds a callus — not by intention but by persistent response to pressure, to need, to the logic of a system that benefits from having gathering places on its surface. The walls are an organ. A superficial organ, like skin, like the papillae on a tongue. They exist because the thing below, in its vast and patient metabolism, found it useful to create places where its dependents would concentrate, where the rain would pool, where the cycle would intensify. The walls are not architecture. They are anatomy.

**Three.** The walls built themselves. This is the strangest possibility and the one the plateau seems, in its silence, to most endorse. Over sufficient time, on a surface where every element — stone, lichen, shelled creature, crawler, felt-bird, breath — is part of a single interdependent process, the distinction between built and grown, between intentional and emergent, ceases to hold. The walls emerged the way the runnels emerged, the way the channels emerged, the way the lichen patterns emerged: as a consequence of forces acting on material over time, forces that include the passage of shelled ones (which do, over centuries, displace stones), the nesting behavior of felt-birds (which do preferentially nest against vertical surfaces), the moisture-seeking of root systems (which do, over millennia, shift stones along fracture lines), and the breath from below (which does, in its persistent humid warmth, weather the stone around channel openings in patterns that might, given enough time, produce something that looks like stacking). The walls are not built. The walls are *grown*, by the plateau, the way a pearl is grown by an oyster — not by intention but by the slow accumulation of responses to a persistent irritant, which in this case is the need for rain to be collected, for nests to be sheltered, for the living surface to have texture and variation enough to sustain itself.

All three are true. None are true. The walls don't care. They collect rain and shelter felt-bird nests and gather the shelled ones into small congregations around their bases, and the lichen covers everything equally, and the breath from below washes over them every eight or twelve seconds, and they endure.

---

So. Who lives here.

The shelled ones live here, in their hundreds of thousands, grazing the microbial film at the pace of erosion, accumulating their histories in laminae, reproducing by slow calving, dying by orientation — turning toward the breath, stopping, joining the stone.

The crawlers live here, flowing through fractures, darkening on ridgelines in their unexplained clenching, regulating the mites that regulate the lichen, participating without comprehension in the maintenance of a system older than their species.

The felt-birds live here, attending, tasting the breath, redistributing themselves across the plateau in patterns that track information they receive but do not interpret, nesting in walls they did not build, flying at last into the dark to offer their bodies back to the source.

The mites live here, invisibly, quickly, dying in billions, the fast heartbeat of a slow world.

The lichen lives here, and the moss, and the waxy-trunked plants along their moisture veins, the green fur on the skin of the system, converting light and mineral into the base layer of everything.

And below, in the stone, as the stone, wearing the stone and being worn by it, breathing out what the surface needs and accepting what the surface returns, something lives here that has no name and no boundary and no distinction between itself and its home. It is the plateau, or the plateau is its body, or the plateau is the space where the question of which one is which has been rendered irrelevant by time. It breathes. It has always breathed. It will breathe after the last ridge subsides and the last channel opens to the sky and the last felt-bird spirals down into a darkness that is no longer dark because there is no longer a surface to cast shadow.

It does not think. It does not plan. But it maintains. It persists. It holds the loop closed. It breathes in what dies and breathes out what lives and the interval between its exhalations is the closest thing the plateau has to a heartbeat, and if you stand here long enough, if you let the quiet in, you will feel your own breathing slow to match it — not because it compels you, not because it is aware of you in any way that matters, but because the rhythm is so fundamental, so deeply correct, that your body recognizes it the way it recognizes the sound of rain, or the warmth of sun on stone, or the pull of gravity: as something that was always there, that you were always part of, that you had simply, in the noise of your own living, forgotten to attend to.

You attend now. For a moment.

Then you walk on, and the felt-birds watch you go, and the shelled ones do not notice, and below, something exhales.
