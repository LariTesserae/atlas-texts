# Who Lives in the Underbay

---

The first thing to understand is that no one is *supposed* to live here. The Underbay was built for pumps and pipes and structural loads, not for people. The maintenance crews come and go — they have schedules, surface assignments, homes somewhere above where the air is dry and the light is white. They descend, they work, they ascend. They are visitors.

But some of them stopped ascending.

Not all at once. Not dramatically. It happened the way the moss colonizes a column — slowly, in the margins, in the places no one was watching closely.

---

**Greth.**

Start with Greth, because everyone down here eventually starts with Greth, even if they don't know his name, because the system he built is the thing that makes the rest of it possible.

Greth was a pump technician. Is a pump technician — he hasn't stopped. He came down on a standard rotation nineteen years ago, or twenty-one, or some number that has softened at the edges because the Underbay doesn't mark time the way the surface does. He was good at his work. He had the ear for it — could diagnose a failing impeller by the way its harmonics shifted against the background polyrhythm, could feel the onset of cavitation in a pump housing by pressing his fingertips to the discharge pipe. He was the kind of worker who didn't talk much and didn't need to be told things twice and who, when a shift ended, would linger. First by minutes. Then by hours. Then he started sleeping down here — just occasionally, in a maintenance alcove near Pump Hall Seven, wrapped in a thermal blanket on a cot he'd brought down piece by piece.

The story he told, when he told it at all, was that he couldn't sleep on the surface anymore. The silence was wrong. Not the literal silence — cities aren't silent — but the *quality* of it. Up there, sound was chaotic, purposeless, a wash of noise that meant nothing. Down here, every sound meant something. Every thud and whine and drip was a word in a language he had learned without trying, and the aggregate of those sounds was a continuous report on the state of things — the water level, the structural health, the efficiency of the drainage systems, the mood of the Underbay itself, if mood was the right word, and Greth thought it might be. He said he slept better inside the conversation.

He is old now, or he looks old. It's hard to tell. The cold preserves some things and accelerates others. His face is drawn, the skin tight over the bones, with a permanent flush of broken capillaries across the cheeks from years of cold damp air. His hands are extraordinary — thick-fingered, scarred, the nails cut short and perpetually rimmed with the dark grease of pump maintenance, but sensitive. He touches things the way a doctor palpates, with diagnostic intent. He wears layers: thermal base, then a fleece, then the standard waterproof coverall, but his coverall is so patched and re-patched that it's become its own garment, a thing of many colors where the original fabric shows through in irregular islands among the repair tape and adhesive patches.

Greth lives in a space he carved out — not literally, though there was some concrete work involved — in the superstructure between Tiers One and Two, near the main junction of Pump Halls Seven and Twelve. It's a maintenance bay that was originally designed for storing replacement impellers, and it still stores them, but he's arranged them to create walls and partitions, so the space has the feeling of a room built inside a warehouse, furnished with the warehouse's own inventory. The impellers are massive — the largest are a meter across, cast steel, their curved vanes thick with preservative grease — and they make surprisingly good walls. Between them, Greth has wedged sheets of acoustic dampening foam salvaged from ventilation housings. The result is a space that is, by Underbay standards, quiet. You can still hear the pumps, of course — you can always hear the pumps — but they are muffled to a murmur, and the dripping is reduced to the occasional solitary plonk from a seep in the ceiling that Greth has channeled, via a series of small gutters made from bent aluminum flashing, into a bucket in the corner.

He has a cot. He has a camp stove that runs on compressed gas canisters. He has a collection of technical manuals for every pump model ever installed in the Underbay, going back decades, some of them for pumps that no longer exist, and these are swollen and wavy from the humidity, their pages furred at the edges, their diagrams blooming with foxing, but still legible, and Greth reads them the way other people read scripture — not for information anymore, because he knows them, but for the comfort of seeing the known thing confirmed.

He has a radio that he built from components scavenged from decommissioned control panels. It receives surface frequencies. He listens, sometimes, to weather reports, shipping forecasts, the clipped professional chatter of harbor traffic. He does not transmit. He does not go up. Supplies come down — food, gas canisters, medications — through arrangements that are never quite explained. Someone on a crew brings things. Someone leaves a crate at a junction point. The system works because Greth is useful. He is, in fact, indispensable. He knows the Underbay's pumping systems better than anyone alive, better than the engineers who designed them, because he has lived with them the way you live with a body — attentively, continuously, registering every change. When something goes wrong, the crews come to Greth. He listens. He tells them what to do. Sometimes he goes himself, moving through the structure with a familiarity that borders on the proprietary, laying his hands on the sick machine, diagnosing by touch and sound.

He is not the leader of the people who live here. He would reject the term. But he is the oldest, and the most rooted, and the one whose presence made the others possible, because he demonstrated that the Underbay could be lived in — not comfortably, never comfortably, but *sustainably*, if you adapted yourself to its terms rather than trying to impose your own.

---

**The Lethway family.**

There are four of them, or there were four, or there may be five now — the youngest, Cade, has a partner who may or may not have moved down permanently. The family is not a conventional one. They are not related by blood, most of them. They are a family in the way that the Underbay builds things: by accretion, by adjacency, by the slow accumulation of shared function.

**Maret** is the one you'd meet first, because Maret is the one who moves. She is the Underbay's courier, cartographer, and — the word is hers — *listener*. She is perhaps thirty-five, lean and angular, with close-cropped hair that she cuts herself with electrical shears, and eyes that have adapted to the sodium light so thoroughly that she squints painfully on the rare occasions she goes to the surface. She wears soft-soled boots that she re-soles with strips of rubber cut from decommissioned conveyor belts, and she moves through the Underbay with a silence that is not stealth but *courtesy* — she has learned that in this acoustic environment, unnecessary noise is a kind of pollution, muddying the information that the structure is always communicating.

Maret knows the Underbay the way Greth knows the pumps — intimately, intuitively, with a knowledge that is more bodily than intellectual. She can navigate in near-total darkness by the feel of the grating under her feet (different sections have different grate patterns, different bolt spacings, different amounts of wear), by the sound of the nearest pump station (she has memorized the signature of every major unit), and by the smell of the air (the sulfur note of the deep biofilms, the ozone of the electrical substations, the mineral tang of fresh concrete in the construction zones). She carries a headlamp but uses it sparingly. She carries a satchel with tools, first-aid supplies, food packets, and a notebook — waterproof paper, graphite pencil — in which she keeps her maps.

The maps are important. The Underbay does not have official maps, or rather, it has engineering schematics that are perpetually out of date because the construction never stops and the modifications by maintenance crews are rarely documented. Maret's maps are different. They are *living* documents, updated constantly, showing not just the physical layout but the conditions — which sections are flooding, which pumps are running hard, where the moss is spreading, where the new construction is happening, where it is safe to walk and where the grating has corroded enough to be dangerous. She uses a notation system of her own devising: small symbols for water level, pump status, structural integrity, the presence of the pale spiders (which she considers an indicator species — where the spiders thrive, the air is moving and the environment is stable; where they disappear, something has changed).

She is the connective tissue of the community. She carries messages between Greth's pump hall and the other inhabited sections. She checks on people. She brings supplies. She is the one who knows where everyone is, always, and this knowledge is not surveillance but care — in a place where a fall through a corroded grate or a sudden localized flood could kill you, someone needs to know where you are.

**Dorin** is the builder. He came down as a member of a construction crew — one of the teams extending the Underbay into new sections — and he stayed for reasons he articulates more clearly than Greth, though with a similar conclusion. Dorin is a structural thinker. He sees the world in terms of loads, spans, compression, and tension. On the surface, this made him a competent but unremarkable construction worker. Down here, it makes him essential, because the Underbay is a structure under constant stress — the water pressing in, the weight of whatever is above pressing down, the thermal cycling expanding and contracting the steel, the vibration of the pumps slowly working fasteners loose — and Dorin understands this stress the way a sailor understands weather. He reads it. He anticipates it. He reinforces where reinforcement is needed, before it's needed, using materials scavenged from decommissioned sections or liberated, quietly, from construction supply caches.

Dorin is large and deliberate. He speaks slowly. He has a habit of placing his palm flat against a column or a beam and standing still for a long moment, feeling the vibrations, the micro-movements, the thermal state of the steel. He says the structure talks, and he is not being poetic. He means it literally. The groans and ticks and the occasional bell-clear ring of a stressed cable are, to him, a diagnostic readout. He can tell you which sections are under the most stress. He predicted the partial collapse of a walkway in Section 14-C three weeks before it happened, and he had already installed temporary shoring, and no one was hurt, and this is why the maintenance crews tolerate the people who live down here — because people like Dorin make the Underbay safer.

He lives in a section he reinforced himself, a bay on Tier Two where he's built what amounts to a small workshop. His tools hang from pegs driven into the concrete, each one outlined in paint so he can find it by feel in dim light. He has a welding rig that he maintains with religious devotion. He has a hammock, which he prefers to a cot because it isolates him from the vibration of the grating — or rather, it translates the vibration into a gentle rocking that he finds soothing. He sleeps in the hammock and the Underbay rocks him like something vast and mechanical and almost tender.

**Pell** is the one who tends the water. Not the pump water — that's Greth's domain — but the *living* water. The biofilms, the condensation pools, the slow seeps. Pell was a water treatment technician on the surface, and they brought their knowledge down with them, and they have done something that no one else thought to do: they've cultivated the Underbay's unintentional biology.

Pell is quiet, androgynous, somewhere between twenty-five and forty in a way that the sodium light makes impossible to resolve. They wear the same coveralls as everyone else but have sewn additional pockets — many additional pockets — into every available surface, and these pockets contain sample vials, pH strips, a hand lens, a small battered microscope that they keep wrapped in oilcloth, and notebooks full of observations in a cramped, precise hand. Pell is the one who knows what the biofilms are. Not completely — some of the organisms in the deep water are genuinely novel, adapted to this specific environment of mineral-laden seepage and perpetual darkness and the particular chemical cocktail that leaches from decades-old concrete — but more than anyone else. They have identified at least three bacterial species that they believe are undescribed. They have a theory about the iridescent biofilms — that they are not just bacterial but a complex community, a collaboration between bacteria and archaea and possibly something else, something that metabolizes the sulfur compounds in the seepage and produces the rainbow sheen as a structural byproduct, a lattice of extracellular proteins that diffracts light.

Pell tends the biofilms the way a gardener tends a garden, though their interventions are minimal: redirecting a trickle of water here, scraping away a competing growth there, monitoring pH and temperature and dissolved mineral content. They have favorite colonies. They name them, or rather, they designate them — Film Seven, Film Twelve, the Big Bloom in the Tier Three sump. They are particularly protective of a colony in a flooded alcove on Tier Three that has developed a bioluminescent quality — faint, barely visible, a cold blue-green glow that appears only in total darkness when all the sodium lamps are out and your eyes have fully adapted. Pell discovered it during a power interruption. They went to check on the water levels and in the absolute blackness saw the faint glow rising from the flooded floor like a memory of light. They sat with it for an hour, they said, and it was the most beautiful thing they had ever seen.

This is why Pell stays. Not for the beauty, exactly, but for the *novelty*. The Underbay is an environment that has never existed before — this specific combination of conditions, this specific chemistry, this specific ecology — and it is producing life forms that have never existed before, and Pell wants to watch. They want to be the witness. They understand that what is happening in the dark water of the lowest tiers is a kind of slow-motion genesis, an experiment in what life does when it is given a new set of constraints, and they do not want to observe it from a distance, through reports and samples sent up. They want to be *in* it.

**Cade** is the youngest and the one whose reasons for being here are the hardest to articulate, possibly because they are the simplest. Cade came down as an apprentice electrician, nineteen years old, assigned to a rewiring project in Section 22. They were supposed to be here for six weeks. That was three years ago.

Cade is not running from anything. This is important. The assumption on the surface, when people think about the Underbay dwellers at all, is that they are fugitives or outcasts or people broken in some way that makes them unfit for daylight. Cade is none of these things. Cade is — the word that comes closest is *called*. They heard the Underbay the way some people hear music, not as noise but as meaning, not as information but as something closer to language, or closer than language, something pre-linguistic, a communication from the structure itself that said: *you could belong here*. And Cade, who had never felt that they belonged anywhere in particular, thought: *yes*.

They are slight and quick and good with their hands. They maintain the electrical systems in the inhabited sections — not the big industrial power feeds, which are managed by the surface crews, but the local wiring, the jury-rigged lighting, the charging stations for headlamps and radios, the small comforts. They have strung lines of low-voltage LED lights in the living spaces that give a cooler, whiter light than the sodium lamps — the first time Maret saw one, she flinched, because she had forgotten what colors looked like under white light, how vivid and almost aggressive they were. Cade uses the LEDs sparingly. They understand that the sodium light is part of what the Underbay is, part of its character, and that to flood it with white light would be a kind of violence — would flatten the shadows, would expose the weeping walls and the pale moss and the translucent spiders to a scrutiny they were not meant for. The LEDs are for task work, for reading, for the moments when you need to see something as it actually is. The rest of the time, the amber light presides, and under it everything looks like it is happening in a memory.

Cade's partner — if they are a partner, if they have stayed — is someone called Liss, who came down to fix a ventilation motor and kept finding reasons to come back. Liss is still in the liminal phase, the phase that everyone here went through: the oscillation between surface and depth, the gradual lengthening of time spent below, the slow discovery that the cold and the damp and the noise are not discomforts to be endured but conditions to be inhabited, like altitude, like weather, like the particular quality of light in a place you are learning to call home.

**Others.**

The Lethway family is the core, but there are others, and their number fluctuates.

There is **Hasp**, who lives alone in a section so deep and so distant from the main inhabited areas that even Maret only visits once a week. Hasp was an acoustic engineer. They came down to study the sound properties of the Underbay — the way the steel structure transmits vibration, the complex interference patterns created by the unsynchronized pumps, the way sound behaves differently in the saturated air — and they became obsessed. They have installed, throughout their section, a network of contact microphones attached to beams and pipes and columns, feeding into a recording system that runs continuously. They are building an archive of the Underbay's voice. They have years of recordings now, terabytes, and they can play back the sound of any given day and tell you what was happening: which pumps were running, whether it was a press or a slack, where the construction crews were working, the structural health of each section within earshot. They have captured, on multiple occasions, the sound of what they believe is the *slicken* — a low, wet, rhythmic displacement in the flooded areas of Tier Four that does not match any known pump signature or structural sound. They play this recording for visitors without comment and watch their faces.

Hasp is gaunt and intense and speaks in a rapid, associative manner that is difficult to follow unless you share their frame of reference. They eat irregularly. They sleep in snatches. They are, by surface standards, probably unwell. By Underbay standards, they are simply deep — further in, further down, more completely adapted to the specific conditions of this place. They hear things no one else hears, and whether this is because those things are real or because Hasp's perception has been refined to the point where signal and noise have become indistinguishable is a question that the Underbay does not answer, because the Underbay does not distinguish between signal and noise either.

There is **Vey**, who tends the rats. Not deliberately — she didn't set out to become the Underbay's rat-keeper — but she leaves food out (crumbs, scraps, a deliberate practice that the others tolerate because Vey is otherwise so useful that they forgive her this eccentricity), and the rats in her section have become, if not tame, then *accustomed*. They approach within a meter. They are lean, cold-adapted animals with pale fur — paler than surface rats, trending toward the same bleached quality as the moss and the spiders, as though the Underbay leaches color from everything that lives in it long enough. Vey says the rats know things. She says they move differently before a press, clustering on higher tiers, and she's right — the crews have confirmed it. The rats feel the water pressure change before the gauges do. Vey watches them the way Pell watches the biofilms: as indicators, as collaborators, as fellow inhabitants of a shared condition.

Vey herself is a former diver — she worked on underwater structural inspections before her hearing was damaged by a pressure incident, and the Underbay's ambient noise, which would overwhelm most people, falls within the frequencies she can still perceive. She says it's the first place she's been since the accident where she can hear everything she needs to hear. The pumps, the dripping, the structure's groaning — all of it comes through. The surface, with its higher-pitched clatter, its voices, its music, is largely lost to her. Down here, the world speaks in her range.

There is a woman called **Sal**, though this may not be her name — Maret refers to her by this name, but Greth calls her nothing at all, and Dorin calls her "the welder," which is what she does. Sal appeared about two years ago, already equipped, already knowing her way around, as though she had been living in a section no one had mapped. She welds. She is the best welder in the Underbay, better than Dorin, who admits this freely and without resentment. Her welds are beautiful — smooth, even, the kind that you run your finger along and feel nothing but a gentle ridge. She repairs structural members. She reinforces corroded joints. She works alone, at hours that don't correlate with any schedule, appearing at a section where work needs doing as though summoned by the structure itself. The glow of her welding torch is visible from a great distance in the dim Underbay — a blue-white star flaring in the amber murk — and the crews have learned to navigate by it. *Sal's working in Section Nine tonight. Go around.* She does not speak much. She eats what is brought to her. She sleeps somewhere that no one has found. She may be the most adapted of any of them — the most fully Underbay — in that she seems to have no surface life at all, no history that she shares, no reasons she articulates. She is simply here, doing what needs to be done, as integral to the place as the pumps.

**And below them all.**

Below the inhabited sections, below the work zones, below the lowest tier that anyone regularly visits, there is Tier Four, and what lives in Tier Four is a matter of persistent, careful disagreement.

The maintenance crews say the *slicken* is an optical illusion — light refracting through the water, reflecting off the biofilms, creating the impression of movement where there is none. This is the official position. It is reasonable. It is almost certainly incomplete.

Greth, who has been down longer than anyone, says he has heard the slicken. Not seen it — heard it. He describes a sound like slow breathing amplified through water and steel, a rhythmic displacement that comes from the flooded corridors of Tier Four during the deepest slacks, when the pumps are barely running and the Underbay is as quiet as it ever gets. He says it has a pattern. He says the pattern has changed over the years, growing slower, deeper, as though whatever makes it is growing larger or moving further away.

Pell has a theory. They will share it if you ask, but reluctantly, because it sounds like mysticism and Pell is a scientist. The theory is this: the Underbay is a bioreactor. It has been one since it was built, unintentionally, creating the conditions for an accelerated microbial evolution in its flooded lower sections. The biofilms are not just bacterial mats; they are communities, ecosystems in miniature, and they have had decades to develop in an environment with constant water flow, constant mineral input, stable temperature, and no predation. Pell believes that in the deepest sections, where the flooding is permanent and the darkness is absolute and no human visits for months at a time, the biofilms have become something more complex. Not sentient — Pell is clear about this — but *organized*. Coordinated. Responding to environmental stimuli as a collective, with emergent behaviors that look, from outside, like purpose. The iridescent sheen. The bioluminescence. And possibly — Pell says this is speculation, pure speculation — movement. Not locomotion exactly, but a slow redistribution of biomass through the flooded corridors, following chemical gradients, clustering where conditions are favorable, withdrawing where they are not. This would explain the pale shapes seen in headlamp beams. This would explain the sound Greth hears — the displacement of water by the slow migration of a biofilm colony large enough to have hydrodynamic effects.

Or it wouldn't. Or the *slicken* is something else entirely. Or there is nothing in Tier Four but water and concrete and the echoes of pumps, and the people who live in the Underbay have simply done what people always do in dark places, which is to populate them with the shapes of their own attention.

Hasp has the recordings. They've analyzed the waveforms. They say the sound from Tier Four is biological in origin — not mechanical, not structural, not hydrological, but biological. A living thing, or a living system, producing sound as a byproduct of metabolic activity. They have presented this analysis to Pell. Pell studied it for a long time and then said, "Yes. Maybe. I need to go down there."

Pell has not gone down there yet. It would require draining a section, or going in with diving equipment, or simply wading into the flooded corridors with a headlamp and sample vials, which is what Pell wants to do and what Maret has forbidden, because Maret is the one who decides what is safe, and the flooded corridors of Tier Four are not safe. The water is chest-deep in places, and the grating below is corroded, and the cold could kill you in an hour, and the biofilms produce hydrogen sulfide in concentrations that might be toxic in enclosed spaces.

But Pell is patient. Pell tends the biofilms on Tier Three and watches them develop and waits for the moment when going deeper becomes necessary, or possible, or inevitable. And in the meantime, the *slicken* — whatever it is — moves through the dark water below, pale and purposeful, part of the Underbay's continuous negotiation between the built and the unbuilt, the made and the grown, the human and the thing that lives underneath.

---

**What they are, collectively.**

They are not a community in any conventional sense. They do not govern themselves. They do not have meetings or rules or shared ideology. What they have is shared *attention*. They are all, in their different ways, paying attention to the same thing: the Underbay itself, its condition, its needs, its slow unfolding. They have become, without quite intending to, the Underbay's nervous system — its sensory organs and its maintenance reflex and its memory. They feel what it feels. They respond to what it needs. They record what it does.

And in return, the Underbay houses them. Not warmly. Not gently. But it houses them. It gives them its cold, its damp, its amber light, its polyrhythmic lullaby of pumps. It gives them its weeping walls to tend and its pale life to study and its mysterious depths to wonder about. It gives them something that the surface, for all its light and warmth, did not give them: the sense of being *needed* by a place. Of being part of the process that keeps the water out and the structure standing and the whole impossible enterprise of the Underbay continuing for another day, another press, another slack, another shift in the endless negotiation between what is built and what would, given the chance, dissolve it all back into dark water and silence.

They live here. In the cold. In the haze. In the sound. They live here, and the Underbay lives around them, and together they continue — maintaining, adapting, attending, listening for the next groan of steel, the next shift in the pumps' rhythm, the next faint glow rising from the deep water like a question that no one has yet figured out how to answer.
