# Who Lives on the Plateau of Voices

---

Start with the mat itself, because the mat is the oldest resident and everything else is a guest in its house.

It has no mind — or rather, it has no mind in any way you would recognize as such. But it has *preferences.* It grows thicker over certain patches of the ancient stone and thinner over others, and the pattern is not explained by water availability or mineral content alone. There are regions where the mat is extraordinarily dense, almost rubbery, packed tight with interlocking root-fibers and fungal threads and the compacted remains of ten thousand years of its own dead layers — and these regions move. Slowly. Over decades. They migrate across the plateau like weather systems, and the columnar trees in their path grow taller, their runners thicker, their crown-flowers more profuse. The people of the plateau have a word for these dense regions that translates to something like *the attention* — as though the mat, in its diffuse and geological way, is *looking* at something, turning its resources toward a place for reasons it cannot articulate and no one else can fully parse. When you stand on one of these dense patches, the breathing of the mat is more pronounced. The sweetness in the air is sharper. The bioluminescent fungi are brighter at night. You feel, in a way you cannot defend rationally, that you are being *noticed* — not watched, not examined, but included in some slow process of awareness that operates on a timescale so far removed from yours that your entire life would register as a single pulse of its breath.

The mat is not one organism. It was, perhaps, once — or rather, it was once a handful of distinct organisms that colonized the plateau's bare stone in separate waves, millions of years ago, when the stone was still sharp. But they have grown together so completely that the boundaries are now theoretical. The moss-analogues provide structure. The lichen-webs fix nutrients from the air. The creeping plants extend fine roots deep into the honeycomb stone to anchor everything. The fungal networks lace through all three, redistributing water and minerals and chemical signals across kilometers. If you could somehow pull apart a single handful of the mat and examine each strand, you would find perhaps forty distinct genetic lineages — but they share so much horizontal genetic material now, have been swapping functional genes back and forth for so long, that the question of where one species ends and another begins has become unanswerable. They are a nation that has become a body. They remember being separate the way the stone remembers being sharp: not at all.

And yet the mat is not the thing that lives here most intensely. The mat is the ground. The ground is alive. What lives *on* the ground is something else.

---

The canopy-swingers.

Call them that, because that's the functional description, but it undersells them considerably. They are the plateau's loudest voice, its most visible presence, and — it becomes clear the longer you spend here — its most complex social actors.

Twenty kilograms is the average. Some of the old matriarchs reach thirty. They are built for the runner-network: six limbs, each ending in a broad, flexible grasping surface — not quite a hand, not quite a foot, something between the two, capable of wrapping around the runner-tissue and gripping with a strength that seems disproportionate to their size. Their bodies are dense, barrel-chested, with a center of gravity low enough that they swing through the aerial network with a rolling, pendular motion that looks lazy but covers ground with surprising speed. The skin is their most remarkable external feature — not chromatophoric in the rapid, flashy way of a cuttlefish, but shifting slowly, over minutes, between the green-black of canopy shadow and the rust-orange of epiphyte clusters, so that a swinger resting in the runners is genuinely difficult to spot even when you know exactly where to look. This is not camouflage against predators — they have no meaningful predators except the mat-swimmers, and even those encounters are rare and mostly involve juveniles who've wandered too far from the network. The color-shifting is social. It is *emotional,* or something adjacent to emotional. A swinger in a calm, contented state tends toward the deeper green. Agitation pushes them orange. Mating displays involve slow, rippling waves of both colors moving across the body in patterns that differ from individual to individual and that, the people of the plateau insist, are as distinct and recognizable as faces.

Their social groups — eight to fifteen, typically centered on a matriarch and her descendants with a few unrelated males cycling through — are defined primarily by song. Each group's territorial call is a genuine composition: a specific sequence of bass pulses, modulated in pitch and rhythm, cycling on that ninety-second period but with micro-variations that shift over weeks and months. A group's song is not fixed. It *evolves.* Young swingers joining a group learn the current version and add their own voices to it, and the slight differences in their vocal anatomy introduce new overtones and rhythmic wobbles that become, if the group tolerates them, permanent features. Over years, a group's song drifts, the way a language drifts — slowly enough that continuity is preserved, fast enough that a song heard today would be unrecognizable compared to its state a generation ago. The people of the plateau, who have been listening to these songs for as long as their oral history extends, say that some group-lineages can be traced back through their songs' structural DNA: this group descended from that group which split forty years ago, and you can still hear the ancestral rhythm buried under the newer layers, the way you can still hear an old word hiding inside a new one.

The swingers are not tool-users. They are not language-users in any way that maps onto the human definition. But they are *culture-bearers.* Their songs carry information across generations. The micro-variations are not random drift — or not entirely. When a group encounters a region where mat-swimmers are newly active, or where a columnar tree is dying and its runners are weakening, the stress of the encounter seems to imprint on the song: the rhythm tightens, certain overtones become more prominent, and these changes persist. Other groups, hearing the altered song from a distance, adjust their own movements. The plateau's canopy-swingers are, in aggregate, a distributed information network, communicating the state of their world through an evolving musical tradition that no individual swinger fully understands but that the population as a whole navigates by.

They are curious about the people. Not friendly, not hostile, but curious in a sustained, watchful way that suggests they have categorized humans as something worth paying attention to. A swinger will sometimes hang from a runner directly above one of the low shelters and observe the people below for an hour or more, shifting slowly to a mottled green-orange that doesn't match anything in the environment, a color-state the people have learned to associate with focused interest. If you look up and meet its gaze — and it does have a gaze, two large forward-facing eyes set in a broad, flat face that is disturbingly expressive — it will hold the contact for a long, measuring moment before moving on. The people do not anthropomorphize this behavior. They do not claim the swingers are wise or spiritual or trying to communicate. They simply say: *they're listening,* and they mean it literally, because the swinger hanging above your shelter has adjusted its position so that its large, cupped ears — which can pivot independently, like radar dishes — are aimed directly at whatever you're doing. It is listening to you. It will incorporate some quality of what it hears — the rhythm of your stone-striking, the cadence of your clipped nighttime speech — into the texture of its next vocal contribution to the group song. You are, in a small way, being woven into the music of the plateau.

---

The chitterers.

If the canopy-swingers are the plateau's bass section, the chitterers are its strings — or rather, its static, its white noise, its shimmering, ceaseless high-frequency wash that gives the soundscape its texture and body.

They are small. The body of an adult chitterer fits in a cupped palm. Scaled, sleek, with a tapered head built for wedging into bark crevices where they lay their eggs, and those extraordinary wings: membranous, multi-veined, folded flat against the body most of the time but capable of snapping open into broad, iridescent planes that catch the light like oil on water. They are not strong fliers. They launch themselves from the columnar trunks with a convulsive leap and then glide, wings buzzing at a frequency just above hearing, steering by subtle warps of the membrane. Most of their travel is in clouds — hundreds or thousands of individuals moving together in loose, shifting formations that flow between the columns like smoke. The clouds are not organized in any obvious hierarchical way. There are no leaders. The movement is emergent, each individual responding to the positions and velocities of its nearest neighbors, and the result is a fluid, organic motion that makes a chitterer cloud look like a single vast creature undulating through the air.

The clicking is produced by a specialized structure at the base of the throat — a hardened plate that vibrates against a ridged surface when the chitterer exhales. Each click is tiny, a dry snap, barely audible at arm's length. But there are *so many of them.* The plateau supports chitterer populations in the millions — possibly tens of millions — and at any given moment, a substantial fraction of them are clicking. The sound builds the way rain builds: one drop is nothing, a million drops is a roar. The chitterer-roar is the most constant element of the plateau's soundscape, present day and night (though diminished at night when most of them sleep), and it fills the frequency band between the swingers' bass and the pollinators' drone with a dense, shimmering curtain that the human ear eventually stops parsing as individual sounds and starts experiencing as *ambiance* — the sound of the plateau thinking, if you're inclined toward that kind of metaphor.

They eat the parasites from the runner-tissue when the runners whistle. They eat the small invertebrates that live in the mat's upper layers. They eat each other's eggs, indiscriminately, which is why they lay so many. They are eaten by the canopy-swingers, by the mat-swimmers, by the nocturnal pollinators (who are not exclusively pollinators, it turns out — they supplement their nectar diet with chitterer protein, snatching sleepers off the bark at night with long, prehensile tongues). The chitterers are the plateau's primary currency of biomass, the small unit of energy that circulates through every trophic level, the living coin that makes the economy run. Without them, the canopy-swingers would starve. Without them, the runners would succumb to their parasites. Without them, the mat-swimmers would surface less often, and the ripping-open of the mat during their feeding lunges — which aerates the root-tissue and stimulates new growth — would slow, and the mat would begin to suffocate under its own density.

The people harvest their eggs, peeling back curls of bark from the columns to reveal the glistening clutches underneath: small, translucent, protein-rich, with a flavor the people describe using a gesture that suggests *good but forgettable.* It is subsistence food. You eat it because it's there and it's always there.

---

The mat-swimmers.

Here is where the plateau reveals that it has teeth.

They are big. How big is hard to say, because they are almost never seen in full. What surfaces during a feeding lunge is a head — blunt, wedge-shaped, wider than a person's shoulders, covered in smooth skin the color of the burgundy stone beneath the mat — and perhaps a meter or two of thick, muscular body behind it. But the people, who have occasionally found dead ones partially surfaced after what appear to be territorial fights, say the full length is six meters or more, a legless, eyeless torpedo of dense muscle that moves through the root-mat by peristaltic contractions, compressing the spongy tissue ahead of it and expanding into the space behind. They are not fast in absolute terms — a brisk walk would outpace one — but in the mat, where the living ground yields to their passage and closes behind them, they are fluid and relentless, and the ripple that precedes them is the only warning you get.

The sensory pits ringing the head are their primary interface with the world. They detect vibration — the clicking of chitterers landing on the mat, the footfalls of people, the deep bass of swinger songs transmitted through the runner-roots. A mat-swimmer hunting is a mat-swimmer *listening,* triangulating the position of prey by the vibrations it produces, adjusting its approach with small corrections that send subtle shivers through the ground. The people, who walk daily on this living surface, have learned to read these shivers with their feet the way a sailor reads the sea. A certain quality of tremor — a low, lateral undulation — means a swimmer is moving nearby. A sudden stillness in a patch of mat that was previously breathing normally means one has stopped, and a stopped swimmer is a swimmer that has localized something and is preparing to strike. The people freeze when they feel this. They wait. The swimmer, receiving no further vibration stimulus, eventually moves on. It is a negotiation conducted entirely through the medium of a shared living ground, a conversation between predator and not-prey that plays out in the language of tremor and stillness.

The feeding lunge is spectacular and violent. The swimmer erupts upward through the mat, tearing a hole a meter wide in the root-tissue, mouth agape — and the mouth is something to see: a circular opening lined with inward-pointing flexible teeth, not for chewing but for trapping, a living net that closes around whatever falls into it. Chitterers, mostly. Dozens of them, caught mid-rest on the mat surface, swept into the mouth along with a spray of torn moss and root-fibers and a gout of the sweet-smelling fluid that the mat stores in its spongy reservoirs. The swimmer holds this position — head above the surface, body vertical in the torn hole, mouth working — for perhaps three seconds. Then it drops. The mat, resilient and alive, begins closing the wound immediately, root-fibers reaching across the gap, fungal threads reconnecting, the moss-analogues already growing over the raw edges. Within a day, the site of a feeding lunge is a slightly thinner patch of mat with a flush of new growth around its edges. Within a week, it's indistinguishable from the surrounding surface. The mat heals. It has been healing these wounds for longer than the swimmers have existed as a species; the swimmers evolved to exploit the mat, but the mat evolved to survive them, and the relationship has settled into something that benefits both — the swimmers aerate and stimulate, the mat provides habitat and hunting ground.

But the moaning. The *moaning.* This is what gives the mat-swimmers their place in the plateau's mythology, such as it is.

The sound is produced internally — a resonance in the swimmer's body cavity, transmitted outward through its skin and into the surrounding mat-tissue, which conducts it with terrifying fidelity. A single swimmer moaning sends a low, grinding vibration through the mat that can be felt a hundred meters away: a sound below the range of easy hearing but emphatically within the range of feeling, a bone-deep thrum that seems to originate from the ground itself, from the stone beneath the mat, from the planetary crust. When multiple swimmers moan at once — which happens most often at dusk, during *the opening* — the overlapping vibrations create interference patterns in the mat, regions where the thrumming intensifies and regions where it cancels out, so that the ground beneath your feet seems to pulse with a complex, almost rhythmic pattern that your body desperately wants to interpret as intentional. As *music.* As a voice saying something.

The people don't claim to understand it. But they sit near the known surfacing sites and they listen with their bodies, and the quality of their attention — the absolute stillness, the closed eyes, the way their hands rest flat on the mat as if trying to feel every nuance — suggests that they are receiving something. Not a message. Not information in the way the swinger-songs carry information. Something more like... a mood. A state. The plateau, expressing itself through its largest and most hidden voice, in a register that bypasses the ears entirely and speaks to whatever part of a person is still animal enough to listen with its skeleton.

And sometimes, rarely, a swimmer will surface slowly. Not to feed. The head emerges from the mat gradually, the tissue parting around it like water, and the blunt eyeless face rests there — exposed, still, the sensory pits open to the air — and the moaning stops. The swimmer is silent. In a place where silence does not exist, this is the loudest thing that can happen. The bass throb of the swingers continues. The chitterers rattle. The wind sings in the runners. But in the immediate vicinity of the surfaced swimmer, the mat has gone still — not breathing, not shifting, as if the swimmer's presence has calmed it into a held-breath attentiveness — and in that relative quiet, you can hear the ancient stone clicking softly far below as the burrowers work, and you can hear your own pulse, and you can hear the person sitting beside you breathing.

The people sit with the swimmer. Minutes pass. The swimmer's sensory pits flare and contract gently, sampling the air, the vibrations, the warmth. It is reading the world above the mat — a world it rarely visits, a world that to it must be a strange open void after the dense, information-rich medium of the root-tissue. What does it perceive? The swingers' songs, yes, conducted through the air and the runners and the mat all at once, triangulated into a rich three-dimensional map. The people's heartbeats. The tiny impacts of chitterers landing nearby. The heat of the sun on its exposed skin, maybe for the first time in months.

Then it sinks. The mat closes. The moaning resumes. The people open their eyes and look at one another, and something has happened between them — some shared experience of proximity to this enormous blind singing thing that lives beneath their feet — that they do not need to discuss and perhaps could not discuss if they tried. They return to their shelters. They eat their chitterer eggs and their mat-tubers. They rebuild the section of wall that the mat has started growing through. Life continues.

The burrowers are the quietest residents, and the most patient, and the most likely to outlast everything else.

They are small — the largest is perhaps the size of a thumb, most are smaller — and they live inside the stone itself, in the honeycomb hollows that they have inherited from erosion and are slowly, meticulously expanding. Their mouthparts are ringed with a mineral deposit harder than the burgundy-grey rock, and they grind. Constantly. A single burrower, working alone, might expand a hollow by a fraction of a millimeter in a year. But they are not working alone. The honeycomb stone beneath the mat is riddled with them — millions, billions, an entire civilization of tiny grinders living in the bones of the plateau, turning stone to dust that they digest for its mineral content and excrete as a calcium-rich paste that the mat's root-fibers eagerly absorb. They are the slowest cycle in the plateau's economy: stone becomes burrower becomes paste becomes mat becomes, eventually, new organic sediment that slowly compresses and mineralizes at the mat's base, becoming a new layer of pseudo-stone that the burrowers' distant descendants will one day begin to grind.

They do not vocalize. The sound they produce — that faint, subliminal grinding — is purely incidental, a byproduct of their feeding. But it is, in its way, the most fundamental voice of the plateau: the sound of the rock being metabolized, of the mineral world being slowly, patiently converted into the living world, of the boundary between geology and biology being worn away the same way the plateau's edges were worn away, until the distinction becomes a philosophical suggestion.

The people know they're there. They can feel the grinding through the mat on quiet nights, a sensation like distant thunder rendered so faint it might be imagined. They do not name the burrowers individually or collectively. They simply include them in the general category of *what the stone does when it's becoming something else.* This is a culture that has lived so long on a living surface that the idea of inert matter seems foreign to them. Stone is just slow life. The burrowers are just the mechanism.

The pollinators.

The columnar trees flower at their crowns — enormous, fleshy blossoms, deep crimson shading to black at the center, that open over the course of several days and remain open for perhaps a week before dropping their petals in a slow, fluttering descent that the chitterers find irresistible (they eat the petals for their sugar content, and in doing so spread pollen residue across the mat, which does nothing useful but is a pleasant side effect). The actual pollination is performed by creatures that seem, at first glance, far too large and too loud for the job.

The pollinators are the size of a human fist, round-bodied and densely furred with a pelt that is perpetually dusted with crimson pollen. Their wings are small relative to their bodies — stubby, fast-beating, producing that improbably deep drone that seems to emanate from a much larger creature. They hover with a precision that borders on mechanical, holding position in front of a blossom while a long, coiled proboscis unrolls and probes the flower's interior. The drone shifts in pitch as they work, rising when they're feeding, dropping when they're transitioning between flowers, and the combined drone of a dozen pollinators working a single tree's crown-flowers creates a resonant chord that the swingers in the nearest runner-network pause to listen to, heads cocked, color shifting slowly toward an attentive deep green.

At night, the pollinators hunt chitterers. The transformation is startling: the same creature that hovered with such delicacy in front of a flower becomes a swift, darting predator, snatching sleeping chitterers off the bark with a tongue that can extend to twice its body length and retract in a fraction of a second. The pollen-dusted fur, which seemed soft and bumbling in daylight, now serves as acoustic camouflage — it dampens the drone of the wings to a near-whisper, so that the pollinator approaches its prey in relative stealth, a muffled hum in the quieter nightscape. The chitterers do not evolve meaningful defenses against this because the predation rate is low enough that it functions as population management rather than existential threat. The pollinators take the weak, the slow, the poorly positioned. The chitterer population absorbs the loss and continues clicking.

The people do not eat the pollinators. They regard them with a mild aesthetic appreciation — the creatures are genuinely beautiful in flight, their fur catching the light like halos — and a practical respect for their role in keeping the columnar trees reproductive. Occasionally a pollinator will investigate a person, hovering close to the face, proboscis half-extended, drawn by the salt on the skin or the warmth of the breath. The drone at close range is overwhelming, a vibration that fills the sinuses and makes the teeth ache. The person holds still. The pollinator decides they are not a flower. It moves on. A small interaction, repeated across thousands of lives, never quite becoming routine, always carrying a tiny charge of strangeness: this creature that is both gardener and hunter, both delicate and fierce, hovering at the boundary of your personal space and finding you irrelevant.

---

And the people.

Come back to the people, because the people are why this place is something more than a remarkable ecosystem. The people are why it is a *place* — a home, a context, a thing that shapes and is shaped by its inhabitants in ways that go beyond the biological.

They are small. Not dramatically so — average height is perhaps a hand's breadth below what you'd find in the lowlands — but their build is compact, wide-footed, low-centered. The wide feet are functional: they distribute weight on the spongy mat, reducing the depth of each footprint, minimizing the vibration signal that would attract a mat-swimmer's attention. Children learn to walk mat-soft before they learn to run, and the gait of a plateau person is distinctive even when they descend to the lowlands and walk on inert ground — a rolling, toe-first placement that wastes no impact, that treats every surface as if it might be listening.

Their skin is weathered by the clear, strong light and the constant gentle wind: darker on the face and hands, lined early, with a toughness that suggests leather. Their hair is kept short or bound tight against the head — a practical adaptation to the wind and to the epiphyte sap that drips from the runners and is nearly impossible to wash out of long hair. Their clothing is minimal: wraps of mat-fiber, soft and slightly elastic, dyed with the rust-orange of dead epiphyte leaves. They go barefoot always. The feet are their most important sensory organ on the plateau — more important than the eyes in many ways, because the eyes show you the surface and the feet show you what's *beneath* it.

They live in groups of three to seven, but these groups are not isolated from one another. The percussive communication system — flat stones struck against the mat in coded patterns — allows information to travel between groups across several kilometers, attenuated but readable by experienced listeners pressing their palms or the sides of their heads to the ground. A typical day might involve receiving a dozen transmissions: reports of mat-swimmer activity, announcements of a columnar tree flowering, invitations to share a large harvest, warnings about a section of runner-network that has become structurally unsound. The transmission system is not instantaneous — the signals travel at the speed of mechanical vibration through the mat, which is fast but not fast enough to feel like real-time conversation. It's more like a slow, ongoing exchange of situational updates, a shared awareness of the plateau's state that each group contributes to and draws from.

The shelters are simple but not crude. The woven runner-tissue is worked with genuine skill: tight, overlapping layers that shed condensation and resist the wind, with ventilation gaps placed at specific angles to allow airflow without admitting the slanting rain that sometimes sweeps across the plateau in the late afternoon. The interior of a shelter is a single room, floored with the living mat (they do not cut the mat away; they build over it, and it grows up through the woven walls and floor gradually, incorporating the shelter into itself over the years, until rebuilding becomes necessary). Sleeping places are shallow depressions pressed into the mat — the mat yields and conforms to the body's shape, and within a few nights, each person's sleeping place has become a personalized mold that fits them precisely. The people say the mat *learns* you. They mean it practically — the root-fibers grow to accommodate the regular compression — but there's an undertone of something more. The mat learns you. The ground knows your shape.

Their tools. Stone blades, yes — the burgundy-grey rock takes an edge with difficulty but holds it well, and the people are patient knappers, working the stone with small, precise strikes over many sessions. Bone awls and needles, harvested from the skeletons of canopy-swingers (they eat the swingers they catch; they are unsentimental about this, recognizing the swingers as intelligent without granting them personhood — a distinction that seems, to an outsider, both pragmatic and precarious). The hardened tree-resin is their most versatile material: they heat it over small fires (fuel is dried mat-tissue, which burns slowly with a sweet, low smoke) and mold it into containers, handles, patches for worn netting, and — most distinctively — the *ear-pieces:* small, cupped discs of shaped resin that fit over the opening of the ear canal and are tuned, by their thickness and curvature, to filter specific frequency bands. Different ear-pieces for different tasks. A thin, wide one that dampens the chitterer-roar while passing the bass of the swingers, for when you're navigating by song. A thick, narrow one that blocks almost everything, for when you need to focus on the mat's ground-transmitted signals. A perforated one that attenuates all frequencies equally, for daily wear, reducing the wall of sound to something merely loud. They carry a set of three or four ear-pieces at all times, switching between them as the acoustic environment changes. The ear-pieces are personal. They are shaped to fit a specific ear. They are the one possession that is never shared, never lent, never given away until the owner dies, at which point they are buried in the mat — returned to the living ground with a brief percussion-sequence that other groups can hear from kilometers away and that translates, roughly, to: *one voice has stopped.*

One voice has stopped. That's their death-phrase. Not *a life has ended* — that would center the individual. Not *we have lost someone* — that would center the bereaved. *One voice has stopped.* It centers the sound. It positions the dead person as a contributor to the plateau's ongoing chord, a voice that was part of the totality and is now absent, leaving a gap in the frequency spectrum that the remaining voices will close over, the way the mat closes over a swimmer's feeding-hole — not by forgetting but by growing to fill the space.

They do not bury their dead in the conventional sense. The body is laid on the mat, uncovered, in a place away from the shelters, and the mat is allowed to do what the mat does: the root-fibers grow up and over the body within days, incorporating it, breaking it down, recycling its nutrients into the living ground. Within a month, the site is a slightly raised mound that will flatten over the following year. Within a decade, it's indistinguishable. The person has become the ground. The ground is alive. The person is alive in the ground. This is not metaphor for the plateau people. It is a statement of material fact. The calcium of the bones will feed the burrowers. The nitrogen of the flesh will fuel the mat's growth. The body's water will be stored in the spongy reservoirs and harvested by the epiphytes and exhaled as morning condensation and drunk again. Nothing leaves the plateau. Everything is the plateau.

---

Their language. Come to it now, because it is the thing about them that would stay with you longest.

At close range, in the relative quiet of night, they speak. The spoken language is clipped, consonant-heavy, with very short vowels and an unusual number of sounds produced by clicks and pops of the tongue against the palate — sounds that cut through low-frequency noise without needing to be shouted. It is not a loud language. It is a *sharp* language, every syllable a small blade of sound designed to be heard in a noisy world without adding to the noise. They speak close together, mouths near ears, and the conversations have an intimacy that is enforced by acoustics: you cannot speak to someone across a room, so every verbal exchange is also a physical proximity, a shared breath, a nearness.

But the spoken language is only the nighttime register. During the day, when the sound is at its fullest, they communicate primarily through gesture. The gestural system is extraordinarily rich — hundreds of distinct signs, each performed with one or both hands, combinable into complex sentences, modulable by speed and amplitude and the micro-expressions of the face. It is a fully expressive language. You can argue in it. You can tell a joke. You can describe a dream you had (they dream vividly; they say the mat gives them dreams, and they may not be entirely wrong — the bioluminescent fungi release trace volatiles at night that have mild psychoactive effects, and the sleeping-depressions put the dreamer's face close to the mat's surface where the concentration is highest). The gestural language is beautiful to watch: fluid, precise, performed with an economy of motion that reflects a lifetime of practice. Children learn it alongside walking, and by the time they're old enough to harvest chitterer eggs, they're fluent in both the gestural and spoken registers, switching between them as effortlessly as the swingers shift color.

And then there's the percussion. The third register. The one that travels through the mat to other groups, the one that is heard with the hands and feet and the pressed-down side of the head. This is the most limited of the three in terms of expressive range — it can convey facts, locations, states, warnings, but it struggles with nuance, with emotion, with the kind of thing you'd need to say close to someone's ear in the dark. But it is the most important of the three in terms of community, because it is what makes the scattered groups into a single people. When a storm is coming from the lowlands, a group on the plateau's western approach will begin a percussion sequence that propagates from group to group across the entire surface, each group hearing it, verifying it, relaying it, until every family on the plateau knows what's coming. When a new columnar tree achieves its first flowering — an event that happens perhaps once in a decade, since the trees are enormously old and slow-growing — the nearest group performs a celebratory sequence that the people call *new voice,* and the other groups respond with their own sequences, and for a few minutes the mat is alive with overlapping rhythms that have nothing to do with information transfer and everything to do with shared recognition that something has happened, something good, something that adds to the world.

New voice. One voice has stopped. The language of a people who define existence in terms of sound.

---

What else. The relationships. The way they love.

Pair bonds are common but not universal. The small group size means that partnerships form within the group more often than between groups, and this gives the bonding patterns a certain intensity — you are partnered with someone you also work with, eat with, sleep beside, navigate with, and there is no space for the kind of distance that larger societies use to manage intimacy. The people are physically close with one another in a way that an outsider might find overwhelming: touching constantly, hands on shoulders, feet overlapping during rest, sleeping in piles rather than in separate depressions (though each person maintains their own mat-molded place, they rarely sleep in it alone). The close-speaking of the nighttime language reinforces this — every conversation is a shared breath, a whisper, a conspiracy of two.

Children are raised collectively within the group but named by the person who feels the strongest pull toward them, which may or may not be a biological parent. The naming is done through percussion: the namer composes a short rhythmic sequence — three to five beats — that becomes the child's name, played on the mat whenever they are discussed in the percussion-register. The child grows up hearing their name as a vibration in the ground, felt through the feet, and learning to recognize it the way a lowlander learns to recognize the sound of their spoken name. When the child is old enough, they learn to play their own name, and the first time they do — striking the mat with a flat stone in their own rhythm — is considered the moment they become a full participant in the plateau's life. *A voice has started.* That's the phrase. A voice has started.

The elderly are valued for their feet. This sounds strange but it is the simple truth: an old person who has spent sixty or seventy years walking on the living mat has developed a sensitivity to ground-vibration that younger people cannot match. They can feel a mat-swimmer's approach from twice the distance. They can read the percussion-transmissions from further away and with greater accuracy. They can sense the subtle changes in the mat's breathing that indicate a shift in the dense-patch migrations, the *attention* moving across the plateau. Old people are the group's seismographs, its interpreters, its deepest listeners. When a decision needs to be made about where to move next, it is often an elder who settles it — not by argument or authority but by pressing their feet to the mat, closing their eyes, and feeling which direction the ground is *most alive.* They follow the attention. The attention has never led them wrong, in the sense that the areas it moves toward are always, over the following years, more productive, more safe, more rich. Whether the mat is conscious and guiding them or whether the elders are simply reading real ecological signals through sixty years of calibrated experience is a question the people do not ask, because to them, there is no difference.

There is one more thing to say about the people, and it is the hardest thing to describe because it concerns the relationship between the people and the sound — not any particular sound, but the total sound, the chord, the full-spectrum, never-ending vocalization of the plateau as a whole.

They love it. Not in the way you love something beautiful. In the way you love something that is part of your body. In the way you love your own heartbeat, which you mostly don't notice but which, if it stopped, would be the end of everything.

The sound is their world. Not the visual landscape — they navigate that, they know it, but it is secondary. The sound is primary. The shifting, evolving, never-repeating chord of the plateau tells them where they are, what time it is, what season it is, what the weather is doing, where the dangers are, where the food is, who is alive and who has died, what the swingers are feeling, what the swimmers are doing, what the mat is growing toward. It is their map, their clock, their newspaper, their church. When they sit during *the opening* — that twenty-minute dusk crescendo when every voice on the plateau overlaps into a full-body vibration that makes the eyes water and the bones ache — they are not meditating. They are not worshipping. They are *listening.* They are receiving the plateau's full daily report, the summation of everything that has happened and is happening and is about to happen, delivered in a language that is not language but that their bodies have been learning since before they were born, since their mothers' bodies vibrated with the plateau's chord while they floated in the womb.

An outsider, visiting, would be overwhelmed. The sound would be a wall, an assault, an indecipherable roar. An outsider would hear noise. The people hear *home.* Every frequency, every layer, every voice — the bass throb, the shimmering rattle, the ground-moan, the drone, the whistle, the grinding, the breathing — is a thread in a tapestry they have been reading all their lives, and the tapestry tells a story that has been unfolding since before the stone forgot it was sharp, and the story is still going, and their voices are part of it now, their percussion-names and their clipped nighttime speech and the pattern of their careful footsteps on the living ground, and their children's voices will be part of it after them, and the mat will grow over their bones and the burrowers will grind the stone and the swingers will sing and the swimmers will moan and the plateau will continue to be what it has always been: a place so old it became something new, a landscape that is an instrument, an ecosystem that is a choir, a world that is a single, endless, evolving chord, and the people who live here are not the audience.

They are one of the voices.
