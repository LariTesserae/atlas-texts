# The People of the Vertical Stone

---

They are smaller than you expect. Not dramatically so — not a people miniaturized by their environment like some parable — but compact in a way that reads as density rather than diminishment. The average adult stands perhaps a hand's breadth shorter than a lowland equivalent, but the proportions are different in ways that take time to notice. The torsos are deep, front to back, the ribcages almost barrel-shaped, built to house lungs that have learned over generations to pull what they need from thin air. The shoulders are broad relative to the hips, in both men and women, because the shoulders do much of the work of living here: pulling the body upward, bracing against narrow walls, carrying loads on the back and across the upper chest rather than balanced on the head or slung from the waist. Their hands are large for their frames, the fingers notably long, with a thickness to the knuckles and a roughness to the pads that makes each hand look like a tool evolved for a specific purpose. Which it is. The specific purpose is gripping stone.

Watch one of them climb. Not a dramatic ascent — there are no dramatic ascents here because drama implies exceptionality, and climbing is not exceptional, climbing is locomotion — just the ordinary movement from one level to the next, a woman ascending a ladder made of bone rungs lashed into holes drilled in the canyon wall. She does not look at her hands. Her feet, in their close-fitting leather wraps, find the rungs by feel. Her weight shifts with a fluidity that suggests she has been doing this since before she could walk in the lowland sense, and she has. Children here climb before they walk horizontally. The first independent movement a child makes is typically upward, reaching for the edge of the sleeping ledge, hauling itself to the level where the family sits during the day. By the time they are walking on flat surfaces — and flat surfaces are rare enough that walking on them is almost a specialized skill — they have already internalized the three-dimensional map of their home city, a map that exists not as a picture seen from above but as a sequence of movements felt in the body: reach here, brace here, step across this gap, duck under this lintel, and you are in the place where your aunt processes sinew into thread.

Their skin is a color that is difficult to name because it shifts with the light, which on the plateau is unforgiving and particular. In the full sun of midday it appears a deep warm brown with an undertone of copper, almost ruddy, the blood close beneath the surface in the way of people whose capillaries have adapted to cold by staying open rather than constricting. In the blue shadow of the canyon walls — and they spend much of their lives in blue shadow, since the cities face in all directions and the sun can only reach a given wall for part of the day — the skin takes on a cooler cast, almost olive, and the ruddy warmth retreats to the cheeks, the bridge of the nose, the ears, the knuckles. Their lips are often cracked. Not painfully, not the crisis-cracking of someone newly exposed to altitude, but a perpetual dry roughness that they manage with rendered fat and mostly ignore. The fat leaves a faint shine that catches light.

Their hair is uniformly dark, true black, thick in a way that serves as insulation. Most wear it long and elaborately managed — not for vanity, or not only for vanity, but because loose hair in a world of narrow passages and constant climbing is a hazard. The common arrangement involves tight braiding close to the scalp, the braids then gathered and wrapped in patterns that vary by city, by family, by age, by something else that outsiders have not been able to map onto any single social axis. The wrapping material is sinew thread, sometimes plain, sometimes dyed with pigments extracted from the lichens — a muted palette of ochre, pale green, the blue-gray of old bone-lichen. The braids are not solely cosmetic infrastructure. They hold things. A small bone tool tucked into a braid above the ear. A few dried cushion-plant leaves folded into the wrapping at the nape of the neck, kept warm by the body, ready to be chewed for their faint stimulant bitterness during long work. The hair, managed this way, becomes a system, a personal architecture as considered as the stonework.

---

But you cannot understand these people through their bodies alone, because their bodies are only the innermost layer of a set of concentric adaptations, each one a response to the plateau and to each other.

The next layer out is the garments, and the garments deserve more attention than their plainness initially invites. The felted fur — and it is true felt, not woven, the fibers worked by hand with water and pressure and patience until they interlock into a dense mat — the felt is layered in a system so consistent across the plateau's cities that it might as well be instinct, though it is surely cultural. Against the skin: a thin, soft layer made from the underfur of the burrowing creatures, which is fine almost to the point of being silk-like, and which retains warmth even when damp. Over this: a thicker layer, stiffer, made from the coarser fur of the furred lizards, which has a slight natural oiliness that sheds the condensation-moisture. Over this, in the coldest periods or when working on exposed upper ledges: an outer shell of something heavier, stitched from pieces of a hide that comes from the canyon-things, the large creatures whose tracks mark the silt below. This hide is stiff, almost board-like until it is worked, and it never becomes truly supple, but it blocks wind absolutely, and wind is the real enemy here — not the cold itself but the cold in motion, finding the gaps, pulling heat from any surface it can reach.

The stitching. Look at the stitching. A lowlander presented with one of these garments might dismiss it as crude because the visible seams are rough, the patches obvious, the overall appearance deeply utilitarian. But turn it inside out. The interior seams are finished with a technique that folds the raw edge of the felt back on itself twice before the sinew thread passes through, creating a sealed channel that prevents the fibers from unraveling and — critically — prevents moisture from wicking along the seam into the insulating layers. Every seam is a small engineering decision. Every patch is shaped to maintain the garment's fit against the body, because a garment that bags or bunches is a garment that catches on stone edges, that restricts the arms during a climb, that creates pockets of dead air in the wrong places. The people who make these garments — and it is not a specialized craft, everyone makes and repairs garments, though some are acknowledged as more skilled — these people understand the relationship between body and covering with an intimacy that is almost medical. They know where the body loses heat fastest. They know where friction will wear the felt first. They know that the left shoulder of a right-handed climber will show wear along the top before the right one does, because the right arm reaches higher and the left shoulder presses the wall.

---

Move outward again, from the garment to the social body.

The basic unit is not the family, exactly. It is the **ledge-group**: the people who share a horizontal band of rooms cut into the canyon wall, typically between eight and twenty adults and their children, occupying a connected set of chambers that open onto a common ledge. The ledge-group shares meals, shares the work of maintaining their section of the city wall, shares the watching of children, shares warmth on the coldest nights when the felted garments are not enough and bodies are pressed together in the innermost rooms with the stone walls radiating back the faint heat of the day. Membership in a ledge-group is partly hereditary and partly negotiated. When two people from different ledge-groups form a partnership — and partnerships here are pragmatic, romantic, often both, sometimes complex in ways that resist easy categorization — one of them moves. The move is literal: belongings carried along the narrow path, up or down a ladder, into the rooms of the new ledge-group, a transition of perhaps fifty vertical feet that constitutes a change of world.

But the ledge-group is nested inside the **wall-city**, and the wall-city has its own logic. The vertical stack of ledge-groups is not a random accumulation. Position matters. The lowest levels, closest to the canyon floor, are the most accessible but also the most vulnerable — to the rare flash floods that sometimes roar through the V-shaped channels after a heavy condensation event, to the canyon-things that occasionally venture close, to the simple psychological weight of living at the bottom of a stone corridor where the sun reaches for only an hour or two at midday. The highest levels have light and air and the prestige of altitude but require the most climbing and are the most exposed to wind and weather. Between these extremes, the middle tiers are the most desirable, and it is in the middle tiers that the public functions concentrate: the wider ledges where disputes are settled, where the kite-watchers sit, where the seasonal ceremonies happen, where the preserved meats and furs from the canyon-thing hunts are stored in communal rooms.

Governance — if that word can be used for something so unlike lowland governance — arises from the intersection of vertical position, age, specialized knowledge, and a quality the people themselves describe with a word that translates roughly as **weight**. Not physical weight. Something closer to *gravitas*, but more literal than the Latin implies — a sense that certain people, by their accumulation of experience and judgment and demonstrated care for the collective, have become heavier, more present, harder to move from their positions. These heavy people are not rulers. They do not command. What they do is sit in certain places — specific spots on the public ledges, spots that are known and recognized — and people come to them. Disputes are brought. Questions of resource allocation. The delicate matter of when to collect hook-beak eggs this season, or whether the burrowing-creature population in the mesa-top gardens has crossed the threshold that requires trapping. The heavy person listens. Often they say nothing for a long time. When they speak, it is usually to ask a question rather than render a judgment, and the question is usually one that reframes the problem in a way that makes the answer feel obvious, inevitable, as though it were always there and simply needed to be uncovered. This is the quality of weight: not the power to decide, but the power to reveal what has already been decided by the situation itself.

The heavy people are not always old. Sometimes a person acquires weight young, through some demonstrated act of perception or care — pulling a child from a ledge-collapse, reading the kites correctly when no one else could, finding a new pool of water in a season of unusual dryness. More often, weight accrues slowly, over decades, and the heaviest people are elderly, their climbing days reduced, their world contracted to a few rooms and the public ledge where they sit. When they can no longer climb at all, they are not moved to a lower, more accessible level. They stay where they are, and the ledge-group arranges itself around their stillness. Food is brought. Company is constant. Their immobility becomes, in a sense, the anchor around which the mobile life of the ledge-group rotates, and there is something in this arrangement — the still center of the turning world — that the people seem to find not tragic but correct.

---

The children.

The children of the Ketharn are, by lowland standards, feral. This is inaccurate but understandable. They are everywhere. They move through the city in loose age-cohorts, flowing over the ledges and through the passages with a speed and confidence that makes adults look cautious by comparison. They know every handhold, every shortcut, every gap between rooms where a small body can squeeze through and a large one cannot. They carry messages, because they are the fastest way to move information vertically through the city — faster than shouting, which the stone walls distort and echo into unintelligibility, and faster than the system of colored flags that some cities use for long-distance signaling between canyon walls. A child can ascend thirty stories in the time it takes an adult to ascend ten, because the child weighs less, fears less, and knows routes that adults have either forgotten or grown too large to use.

They are not unsupervised. They are *differently* supervised. The ledge-group system means that every adult on a given level is responsible for every child on that level, regardless of parentage. A child racing along a narrow ledge will be corrected — not gently, a sharp word, a hand on the shoulder — by whichever adult is nearest, and the child will accept this correction without resentment because the correcting adult might be the same person who fed them breakfast, who re-stitched the knee of their leggings last week, who held them during the last bad fog when the condensation came so thick that the ledges grew slippery and the whole city seemed to contract inward, everyone pulling into the inner rooms, bodies close, the sound of dripping water the only sound.

The education is constant and almost entirely non-verbal. Children learn stonework by watching stonework, then by being handed a bone chisel and a piece of scrap stone and allowed to ruin it. They learn climbing by climbing. They learn the kite-patterns by sitting near the kite-watchers and gradually, over months and years, beginning to see what the watchers see — the subtle shift in gyre radius that means a pressure change is coming, the sudden tightening of a flock that means the canyon-things are moving below. No one explains these things in words, or rather, words are used sparingly, as supplements to demonstration and shared attention. The phrase that might be translated as *teaching* in the Ketharn languages is closer in structure to "sitting beside" — the literal meaning is something like "being in the same wind."

There is one exception to this non-verbal pedagogy, and it concerns the pools. The locations of every water source — every persistent pool, every seep, every crack that reliably produces condensation — are taught to children verbally, deliberately, through repetition that has the cadence and structure of song. Not metaphorical song. Actual song, pitched to carry in the strange acoustics of the canyon walls, with melodic intervals that encode direction and distance and a rhythmic pattern that encodes the reliability of the source (every day, every few days, only during the wet season, only in exceptional years). Children learn these songs before they learn almost anything else, and they sing them to each other as they move through the city, a constant low murmur of navigational information that visitors have mistaken for play. It is play. It is also the most important knowledge their civilization possesses, externalized into a form that is almost impossible to forget.

---

Now look at them in the aggregate, from one of the higher mesas, in the hard horizontal light of early morning.

The canyon below is still in shadow, and the wall-city is just beginning to catch the sun on its upper tiers. The stone is the color of pale bread. The fitted walls of the city are distinguishable from the natural rock only by their regularity — the horizontal lines of the ledges echoing the sedimentary banding, but spaced more evenly, more deliberately. Small figures are emerging onto the ledges, resolving as the light hits them into individual bodies: a woman shaking out a sleeping-felt and draping it over the ledge rail to air; a man already at work with a bone chisel on a wall extension, the faint *tik tik tik* of his striking carrying upward with surprising clarity; a cluster of children flowing along a mid-level path, their dark braided heads bobbing; a kite-watcher already in position on an upper ledge, motionless, face tilted to the sky where the first kites are beginning their morning ascent from the roosts on the opposite wall.

Below the city, on the canyon floor, the dust-runners are moving in their nervous flocks, pecking at something in the silt. A hook-beak emerges from its niche in the city wall, stretches, clicks once, and launches itself into the air with a heavy flap that transitions almost immediately into a glide. Somewhere lower in the canyon, out of sight, something large is moving — the faint sound of displaced gravel, then silence.

On the mesa top behind you, the gardens — if they can be called that, these careful arrangements of cushion-plants in shallow depressions filled with composted dust-runner droppings and crushed lichen — the gardens are being tended by two older women who move through the rows on their knees, pressing their fingers into the soil around each plant with a tenderness that seems outsized for the scraggly, tough little organisms they are nurturing. One of them pauses, looks up, sees a kite passing overhead and watches it for a moment before returning to work. There is no way to know what she read in that brief observation, but something in her posture shifts — a fractional relaxation, or perhaps a fractional alertness. It is too subtle to interpret from this distance.

The city is waking and it is doing so without hurry. There is no bell, no call to prayer or work, no communal signal. There is instead a gradual increase in the density of the background rustle — more bodies moving, more small frictions, more of the clicking and murmuring and stone-on-stone and sinew-creaking that constitutes the city's voice. The condensation from the night is just beginning to melt on the upper surfaces, and thin lines of moisture trace down the canyon wall, finding the carved channels, filling the small stone basins that have been placed at intervals along the descent. A child pauses at one of these basins, cups water in both hands, drinks. The water is so cold it makes her gasp — a tiny sound, sharp, immediately swallowed by the ambient quiet.

This is what lives here: a people who have become the shape of their place so completely that distinguishing between the built and the natural, between the human and the geological, requires sustained attention. They are not primitive. The word is meaningless in this context. They are *fitted*, in the way their stone blocks are fitted — precisely, without mortar, relying on the weight and accuracy of the joint. They have solved the problem of living in a place that does not obviously want them by making themselves into something the place does not reject. This is not harmony in the romantic sense. It is compromise in the engineering sense: a negotiated agreement between the properties of stone, the behavior of water, the movements of animals, the needs of human bodies, and the absolute non-negotiable fact of the cold.

---

They do not speak of the plateau as home. The word they use is closer to **what is under us**. Not beneath, in the hierarchical sense. Under, in the structural sense. The thing that bears the weight. They are on top, and the plateau is under them, and together they are one structure, and the structure is still young, still sharp-edged, still deciding what it will become. They are part of the deciding. So are the kites, and the dust-runners, and the hook-beaks, and the canyon-things, and the water, and the cold. The deciding is very slow. It happens at the speed of stone — which is to say, it happens at the speed of everything here, which only looks like stillness if you are not paying attention.

They are paying attention. That is perhaps the single thing that most defines them. The kite-watchers are only the most visible manifestation of a pervasive cultural posture of observation. Everyone watches. The woman airing her sleeping-felt scans the opposite wall as she shakes it — not looking for anything in particular, just *looking*, registering the state of things, noting without conscious effort that a new crack has appeared in the stone three ledge-groups over, that the hook-beak in the niche below has laid a second egg, that the light this morning has a particular quality that she associates with the onset of a condensation event by mid-afternoon. The man with the chisel pauses between strikes and listens — to the quality of the sound his tool makes, which tells him whether the stone beneath will fracture clean or crumble, but also to the broader sonic landscape, the background rustle, the presence or absence of the kites' wing-whisper overhead. The children, even in their rushing, are reading the stone under their hands and feet with every contact, building and rebuilding their internal maps, alert to the new, the changed, the out-of-place.

This attention is what the plateau demands. The plateau is young and still moving — not fast enough to feel, but fast enough that a crack in a canyon wall today may be a fracture next year and a collapse the year after. The water is scarce and unreliable; its patterns shift. The animals are abundant but their populations fluctuate, and a flush year for burrowing creatures may mean a lean year for the cushion-plants they feed on, which means a lean year for the people who eat the cushion-plants, which means the burrowing creatures must be trapped more aggressively, which means their population crashes, which means the soil in the mesa-top gardens compacts without their aeration, which means the cushion-plants struggle even after the pressure is relieved. Every thread connects to every other thread, and pulling one moves them all, and the only way to manage this web of dependencies is to watch it, constantly, with the patient inexhaustible attention that the people of the Ketharn have elevated to their highest cultural value.

They have a saying. It does not translate well, but the approximate meaning is: **The stone remembers what you did not see.**

It means: if you fail to notice, the consequences will be carved into the world permanently, the way water carves channels in young rock — slowly, but with absolute commitment, and in only one direction.

---

In the evening, as the sun drops below the western edge of the plateau and the sky goes through its bruise-colored transition, the cities quiet. The kites descend to their roosts. The dust-runners retreat to the lower levels. The hook-beaks settle into their niches and go still. The people pull inward, into the rooms, into the warmth of bodies and felted fur and the faint residual heat of stone that has spent the day absorbing honest light.

In the innermost rooms, where the stone walls are smoothest — worked and polished by generations of shoulders and backs pressing against them — there are marks. Not writing, not exactly. Tally marks, pictographs, something between notation and art. The record of pool-levels over years. The number of hook-beak eggs collected each season. The date of the last major rockfall and a diagram of the fracture pattern that preceded it. These marks are added to slowly, a few lines a season, and they constitute the deep memory of the city — the supplement to the water-songs, the record of what was seen by the people who were paying attention.

A child traces one of these marks with a fingertip in the near-dark. An older person — not the child's parent, but a member of the ledge-group, someone heavy, someone whose weight is felt in the room even when they are silent — this older person watches the child's hand move across the stone and says nothing. The child is learning to read the record. The record says: this is what the stone remembers. This is what we saw.

Outside, the cold arrives with its usual honesty. The plateau gives back the heat of the day in a single exhalation, and the temperature drops, and the first condensation begins to form on the outer walls, and the dripping begins — the slow, mineral, almost-musical dripping that will continue all night, ledge to ledge, basin to basin, until the morning comes and the stone dries and the cycle repeats.

The people sleep in their stone rooms, in their layered garments, in their groups of eight and twenty, their breath clouding faintly in the darkness, their bodies generating just enough heat to hold the cold at its contractual distance. Below them, the plateau. Above them, the bruise-dark sky with its too-many stars. Around them, the sound of water finding its way down, and the sound of the city — their city, their fitted, precise, attentive, stone-remembering city — settling into the night with a long slow creak of sinew and stone, like a living thing adjusting its weight before it sleeps.
