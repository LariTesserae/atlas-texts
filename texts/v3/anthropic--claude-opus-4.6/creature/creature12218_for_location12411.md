# The People of Kesk

---

They are pale. That's the first thing, and it's wrong—it should be the opposite. People under a sun this relentless, on a plateau this exposed, should be dark, should have built up every defense the body knows how to mount against light. But the people of Kesk are pale in the way the stone is pale, in the way the mineral crusts are pale, a washed and almost powdery quality to their skin, as though the plateau has been slowly bleaching them across generations. Or not bleaching. Depositing. You notice, when you're close enough, that their skin has a faint mineral quality—not powdered on, not cosmetic, but intrinsic, as if whatever they've been drinking and bathing in and breathing has been slowly incorporated into the tissue itself. Their complexions vary in the same register the stone does: some nearly white, some faintly ochre, some carrying that deep rust stain along the insides of their wrists and at their hairlines where they perspire, where the mineral-laden sweat dries and is replenished and dries again. They don't burn. They should burn. The sun is merciless here and they wear clothing that covers but doesn't shield—loose, light, draped fabrics in undyed or minimally dyed tones, fabrics that breathe and wick moisture but don't block light. And yet their skin doesn't redden, doesn't blister, doesn't peel. Something in them has accommodated the light, or something in the minerals has.

Their eyes are unusual. Not in color—the color range is unremarkable, browns and greys and the occasional pale hazel—but in the quality of their gaze, the way the pupils sit. They're slightly contracted even in conversation, even indoors, even in the deep windowless rooms of the old district. Pinpoint pupils in low light, which should mean they can't see well in dimness, except they move through those deep rooms with perfect confidence. They see well everywhere. They see well in the hammering glare and they see well in the dark and this is not something they discuss or remark upon because it has always been this way for them and they have no visitors often enough to have developed a vocabulary for explaining it.

---

They are quiet. You expected this—the plateau demands it, or rather, the plateau makes quietness so natural that loudness becomes effortful, almost physically uncomfortable, like shouting into a pillow. But their quietness goes deeper than vocal volume. They are quiet in their movements, quiet in their social patterns, quiet in the way they occupy space. Two people passing each other on one of the curving streets might acknowledge each other with a slight motion—a tilt of the head, a shift in the angle of the shoulders—or they might not. Both are acceptable. Neither is rude. The social calculus of Kesk doesn't run on acknowledgment the way it does in places where people live close and thick and loud. Here, where two hundred meters of wet stone might separate your dwelling from your nearest neighbor's, the assumption is solitude. Company is the exception, and when it occurs it is deliberate, chosen, and therefore doesn't need to be decorated with the small performances that lubricate denser social arrangements.

But they are not cold. This is important to understand, and it takes time, because your initial impression will be of a people so restrained they seem vacant. They're not. Watch a pair of extraction workers adjusting the flow valves on one of the deep-source pipes—the way one holds her hand near the pipe's surface, not touching, reading the heat and vibration, and the other watches her hand and adjusts the valve in response to movements so subtle you can't see what signal passed between them. They've been working together long enough that the communication has been stripped of everything unnecessary and what remains is pure. Or watch a parent with a child near one of the walled pools—the child reaching toward the water, the parent not grabbing, not shouting, but placing one hand on the low wall in a way that draws the child's attention to the wall itself, to the care with which it was built, to the *fact* of the wall, and the child understanding the lesson: *this is bounded, this is maintained, this belongs to something else, and your desire to touch it must reckon with all of that*. The teaching is done in gesture. In presence. In the quality of attention.

They touch each other rarely and with great specificity. A hand on a forearm means one thing. A hand on the back of the neck means something else entirely—something you won't see between anyone who hasn't earned it, earned it across years, across shared cycles of the mineral seasons, across the slow accumulation of trust that accretes in Kesk the way calcium accretes on stone. When they do touch, it carries the weight of everything that hasn't been said in the long silences between. You see a woman in the old district place her palm flat against a man's sternum—just that, just a flat palm, held there for four or five seconds—and the gesture is so loaded that you look away, not because it's erotic, though it might be, but because it's *private* in a way that the quiet and the distances have made sacred. Privacy here isn't about walls. It's about the plateau giving everyone enough space to be fully themselves in, and the social contract that says you don't reach into that space uninvited.

---

The children are different from the adults, but not in the way you'd expect. They're not louder—or rather, they are, sometimes, in the way all children are, but the plateau swallows their noise so efficiently that a child shouting at play sounds like a child whispering, heard from even a moderate distance. What's different is their relationship to the fauna. The stone-skimmers don't scatter from children. The palm-sized ones will cross a child's bare foot and continue on, and the child won't flinch, and the skimmer won't alter its path. The children wade in pools that adults avoid—not the walled pools, never those, but the broader shallow ones where the water is warm and the biofilm is thick enough to feel slippery underfoot. They come out with their skin flushed pink from the heat and stained faintly amber at the ankles, and over weeks and months you can watch the stain become permanent, watch it climb. This is how the people of Kesk come to look the way they look. It starts in childhood. It starts with wading.

They grow up slowly, or perhaps it's that they grow up continuously, without the hard demarcation points that other cultures use to separate childhood from adulthood. There is no ceremony you can identify. There is a gradual shift: a child begins spending time at the extraction works, not working yet, just watching. Then fetching tools. Then holding a pipe steady while a valve is adjusted. Then reading the heat of the pipe with her own hand. Then one day she is an extraction worker, and no one announced it, no threshold was crossed. The same with the other roles—the wall-maintainers, the pool-monitors, the people who work in the old district doing whatever it is they do in the deep rooms. You absorb responsibility in Kesk the way the stone absorbs water: slowly, from below, until one day you are saturated with it and it is simply part of what you are.

---

The extraction workers are the most visible population, and the easiest to understand. They work in shifts that correspond not to time of day—the sun varies so little across the plateau's latitude and season that clock-time feels almost irrelevant—but to the mineral cycles. When the upwelling water shifts composition, the extraction process shifts with it. Different minerals precipitate at different temperatures, different concentrations, and the workers read these shifts the way farmers in other places read weather. There are perhaps two hundred of them, men and women in roughly equal numbers, and they carry the darkest staining—their hands and forearms rust-colored up to the elbows from constant contact with the mineral brine. Some of them have a faint crystalline texture to the skin of their palms, a roughness that catches light. They don't wear gloves. The minerals are part of them. The minerals have *become* them, in the same way the plateau's stone becomes stained—not damaged, not contaminated, but transformed into a record of contact.

They trade the extracted minerals with the lowlands. Caravans descend the plateau's edges—long, slow-moving trains of pack animals (dense, low-slung creatures you see penned at the town's southern margin, patient and dusty) carrying sealed containers of precipitated salts, crystalline concentrates, and one substance that is always packed in dark wrappings and handled with more care than the rest. You never learn what this substance is. The workers refer to it by a word that might be its mineral name or might be something else—the word carries a tonal quality that your ear can't quite parse, a flatness to it, as if it's being spoken not to you but to the plateau itself, reported rather than communicated. The caravans return with what the plateau can't provide: food, mostly. Grains and dried fruits and preserved proteins. The plateau grows nothing. Every calorie consumed in Kesk has been carried up from below.

This should make them dependent. It should make them desperate, even—a town that cannot feed itself is a town at the mercy of its supply lines. But the people of Kesk do not behave like a dependent population. They behave like a population that controls something essential, something the lowlands need badly enough that the caravans have never stopped coming. The dynamic is not: *we are isolated and vulnerable and we must trade to survive*. The dynamic is: *we are the ones who sit on top of what rises from below, and we are the ones who know how to handle it, and the lowlands may set the price of grain but we set the price of everything else*. There is a confidence to the extraction workers that borders on serenity. They are not anxious. They are not competitive, not with each other and not, it seems, with the lowlanders. They do their work. The minerals come up. The caravans come and go. This has been true for longer than anyone alive can remember, and the people of Kesk expect it to remain true for longer than anyone alive will live.

---

The wall-maintainers are harder to understand.

There are perhaps thirty of them, and they are older—not elderly, but weathered, settled into themselves the way the plateau's stone is settled. They move between the walled pools on foot, carrying tools and fresh-quarried stone in slings across their backs. Their work is physical but unhurried. They inspect walls. They press their hands to the stone the way the extraction workers press their hands to the pipes—reading something, feeling for cracks or shifts or some quality you can't perceive. When they find a section that needs repair, they work slowly and with a precision that seems excessive for what is, after all, a low stone wall around a pool of water. They cut the new stone to fit exactly. They seal the joints with a paste made from calcium powder and something viscous—some product of the biofilm, you think, something biological that sets harder than morite. The repairs, once complete, are invisible. The wall looks as though it has always been one continuous thing.

They don't speak about their work, but their work speaks. It says: *these boundaries matter*. It says: *the integrity of the enclosure is not negotiable*. Some of the walled pools, as noted, contain the pale eyeless swimmers. Others contain nothing visible. The wall-maintainers treat both kinds with identical care. You ask one of them—a woman with rust staining up to her shoulders and fingers so mineral-rough they look carved—whether the empty pools are being *kept* empty or whether they're waiting for something to arrive, and she looks at you with those contracted pupils and says a single word that translates, approximately, as "neither," and returns to her work.

Later, much later, you realize the word she used isn't the standard negation. It's a word that means something closer to "the question doesn't apply." Not that the answer is unknowable—that the question itself is misformed. You were asking about the pools as containers, as vessels that either hold something or don't. She was telling you that the pool isn't a container. It's—something else. An aperture. An interface. The wall around it isn't keeping something in or something out. The wall is *defining* something. Making a boundary legible. Saying to the plateau: *here, in this specific place, we acknowledge what you are*.

The wall-maintainers eat alone. They sleep in small structures at the edges of the walled pools, one or two per pool-cluster, and the interiors of these structures are spartan—a sleeping surface, a cistern of clean condensate water, a few tools, and, invariably, a small flat stone set into the floor that is warmer than the stones around it, warmer than it should be, as though it connects to something deeper. They rest their bare feet on these stones when they sit. They don't seem to find this remarkable.

---

The people of the old district.

This is where your understanding begins to fail, not because the people are hostile to observation—they're not—but because what they do doesn't resolve into categories you already possess.

There are perhaps fifty of them, and they live and work in the cluster of low buildings at the plateau's center where the biofilm grows thickest and the stone has that faintly *livelier* quality. They are of all ages, unlike the wall-maintainers, and they include children, which means it's not a role you graduate into but something you're born to, or born into, or born *from*. The children of the old district are indistinguishable from other Kesk children except in one respect: they don't wade in the open pools. They don't need to. The staining that other children accumulate gradually through play, the old-district children are born with—faintly, at first, a slight amber warmth to the skin that deepens over the first years of life without any apparent exposure to the mineral water. They're absorbing it from below. Through the stone. Through the floors of their homes, which are the plateau itself, dressed and smoothed but uninterrupted, continuous with the deep rock in a way that the newer buildings' quarried-stone foundations are not.

The adults of the old district spend much of their time in the deep rooms—the windowless, downward-going rooms with the heavy fitted doors. You are not forbidden from entering these rooms. No one stops you, no one warns you. But the doors are heavy enough that opening them requires intention, and the air that comes through when you do is warm and wet and carries a mineral concentration so intense it coats your tongue and makes you blink. The rooms descend in shallow steps, carved directly into the plateau stone, and they are lit—this is the detail that stays with you—not by any lamp or fixture but by the biofilm itself, which here, in the warmth and moisture and proximity to whatever lies below, has grown thick enough to luminesce. A faint, blue-green light. Not bright enough to read by. Bright enough to see faces. Bright enough to see that the people in the deep rooms are sitting in circles, hands on each other's arms or shoulders, eyes open but focused on nothing, breathing in a shared rhythm that is slower than resting breath and perfectly synchronized.

They are listening.

That is the word that comes closest, though it implies ears and sound and neither is quite right. They are attending to something. Something that comes up through the stone, through their feet and their sitting bones and their flat palms where they rest on the warm floor. Something that the plateau has been carrying upward for longer than the town has existed, longer than the fauna have grazed here, longer than the mineral staining has marked the stone. You feel it too, standing in the doorway—a presence in the subsurface vibration, a quality to the hum that is not mechanical, not geological, not random. It is structured. It is patient. It is *communicating*, or at the very least it is *patterned* in a way that the human nervous system recognizes as communication even if the conscious mind can't decode it.

The people of the old district can't decode it either. Or—they can, partially, in the same way you can partially understand a language you've been immersed in for years but have never formally studied. They feel the shifts. They know when the plateau is about to change its mineral output before the extraction workers' instruments detect the change. They know when a pool needs to be walled before anything visible occurs within it. They know things about the deep geology—fault pressures, thermal anomalies, hydrological shifts—that they have no instruments to measure and no vocabulary to explain, and they communicate this knowledge to the rest of the town in the same way all knowledge in Kesk is communicated: gradually, through gesture and presence and the slow accretion of shared understanding.

You ask one of them—a man of middle years with palms so mineral-stained they're nearly white, the color of fresh calcium deposit—what is below the plateau. He considers the question for a long time. The silence is comfortable. The plateau listens with you.

"Something that was here first," he says. His voice is barely above a whisper, not from secrecy but because the deep room they're sitting in carries even the softest sound perfectly, the way certain chambers are designed to carry sound, except this one wasn't designed by anyone who lives here now.

"Something that was here before the stone?" you ask.

He shakes his head slowly. Not a negation. A correction. "Something that *is* the stone. That *became* the stone. The way we're becoming the stone." He lifts his hands, turns them over. The mineral deposits on his palms catch the biofilm light. "Slowly."

---

This is what the people of Kesk understand that you, as a visitor, must slowly come to accept: the plateau is not a place where people live. The plateau is a process that people have become part of.

The water rises. The minerals surface. The biofilm grows. The skimmers graze. The stilt-walkers sip from the pools. The gliders circle in their unreadable geometries. The pale swimmers move through their walled enclosures. And the people of Kesk extract, and maintain, and listen, and slowly, across generations, become more like the thing they live on. Their skin mineralizes. Their silence deepens. Their perception extends downward, into the stone, into the warmth, into whatever vast and patient thing is expressing itself through the upwelling water and the hum in the ridges and the biofilm light in the deep rooms.

They are not being consumed. That's the wrong frame—it implies loss, victimhood, horror. They don't experience it as loss. They experience it as *completion*. The way a river doesn't lose itself when it reaches the sea. The way the mineral stain doesn't damage the stone—it becomes the stone's visible history, its record of contact with what flows through it.

The lowlanders don't fully understand this. The caravans come and go, the minerals are loaded and carried down, and the traders interact with the extraction workers at the town's edge and find them quiet but competent, unusual but functional, a pale and slightly eerie people who live in a hard place and do useful work. The traders don't go to the old district. They don't see the deep rooms. They don't press their hands to the warm spots in the stone and feel the structured vibration underneath. They take their mineral concentrates and their sealed dark-wrapped packages and they descend the plateau and return to a world of weather and soil and green growing things, and Kesk is a name on a trade ledger, a source of materials, a place you wouldn't want to live.

But the people of Kesk are not living in a place they wouldn't want to live. They are living in the only place that makes sense to them—the only place where the ground speaks, where the water carries messages from below, where the light that hammers down from above is met by a light that seeps up from below, faint and blue-green and alive. They are intermediaries. They are translators. They are the point of contact between the surface world and whatever the plateau is slowly, patiently, endlessly expressing.

And they are changing. Year by year. Generation by generation. The oldest photographs of Kesk—if such things exist in the lowland archives—would show people who looked more or less like lowlanders, just paler, just quieter. The people you see now are further along. The mineral integration is deeper. The pupils contract tighter. The silence comes more naturally. The gestures carry more meaning and the words carry less. The children of the old district are born already stained, already listening, already half-translated into whatever language the plateau speaks.

Where this goes—what the people of Kesk will look like in five generations, in ten, in fifty—no one discusses. Not because it frightens them. Because discussing it would be like asking the biofilm where it's going. It's going where the stone goes. Where the water goes. It's going down and coming up and spreading across whatever surface receives it, and the surface that receives it changes, and the thing that spreads changes too, and eventually the distinction between what was there first and what arrived later is a question that doesn't apply.

*Neither*, the wall-maintainer said.

Not empty. Not full. Not waiting. Not complete. Not consumed. Not preserved. Not human, eventually, and not inhuman either. Something that the vocabulary of the lowlands, with its hard boundaries and its clear containers and its confident distinctions between the living and the geological, cannot accommodate.

Something that is becoming what it has always been becoming, slowly, in the light, in the quiet, on the Boiling Plateau of Kesk.
