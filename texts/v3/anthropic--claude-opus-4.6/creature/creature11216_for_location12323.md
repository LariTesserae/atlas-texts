# The Inhabitants

---

They are not there. That is the first thing, and it must be said plainly because everything afterward will make it less plain.

No one lives in the city. No one has lived in the city for a span of time so long that the question of *when they left* has become geological rather than historical — a question you'd answer with core samples, not documents. The people who built the factories and laid the rail lines and planned the broad avenues for the turning radius of heavy vehicles are gone so thoroughly that not even their bones remain. The root-mat consumed those too,ite by calcium-phosphate, the mineral content of human skeleton becoming just another thin stratum in the hundreds of feet of compacted root-rock. If you could somehow section the substrate at the right depth, you might find a layer slightly richer in calcium than those above and below it — a ghost-sediment, a faint record of the generation that stayed too long or couldn't leave or didn't realize leaving was something they needed to do. But even this is speculation. The root-mat is not an archive. It is a stomach.

So: no one lives here.

And yet.

---

## The City Itself

You already know this. You knew it in the plaza when the clicking filled the air and the switches moved and the word *thinking* rose in you unbidden. But knowing it and reckoning with what it means are different operations, and the second one takes longer.

The city lives in the city. The city is the thing that lives here. Not as metaphor. Not as the poetic shorthand people use when they say a forest is "alive" — meaning it is full of living things, meaning the aggregate of beetle and bird and fungus and tree creates a system that behaves as though it has agency. This is not that. The aggregate here has collapsed. The beetle-things are not separate organisms; they are the city's locomotory organs, its hands, its cilia. The smokestacks are not structures that happen to exhale; they are the city's lungs, engineered by centuries of iterative growth to optimize the thermal-condensation cycle that constitutes the city's water supply. The rail network is not a relic being maintained by vegetation; it is the city's circulatory system, or its nervous system, or both — the cuttings serving as channels along which materials and signals move, the switches serving as synapses or valves or logic gates, depending on a distinction that may not be meaningful at this scale.

The city is a single organism. It has been a single organism for a very long time. And the question of what it is like to be the thing that lives here is the question of what it is like to be a mind the size of a county that thinks at the speed of wood.

---

## The Metabolism

Start with what is most concrete. The city eats, and what it eats is itself.

The outer districts — the ones near the plateau's edge where the architecture is crisp and the streets are cleanly defined — are the youngest tissue. Growth here is active, the living wood still pale, still adding layers, still sharpening the edges of cornices and windowframes with the precision of a mason. The beetle-things carry material *outward* in their processions, bark fragments and leaf litter moving from the interior toward the periphery, and this material is incorporated into the growing walls and rooflines at the city's margins. The outer districts are the city's skin, its growth front, the place where it is still becoming.

The inner districts are older. The wood is denser, darker, the grain so tight it approaches the condition of stone. Here the architecture begins to deviate from the original human plan — walls curving, streets narrowing or widening according to some other logic — because the human template has been fully absorbed and the city is now elaborating on its own terms. The beetle-things carry material *inward* here too, but different material: fragments of the mineralized root-strata from below, calcium and silicate and iron drawn up from the fossil layers and delivered to the factory buildings where the clicking is loudest.

And in the factories — the great cathedral-ceilinged spaces where the sound of cellular division has become a percussive roar — the city is doing something with that mineral freight. Something you cannot see because it happens inside the walls, inside the massive trunks of the interior columns, inside the hundreds of feet of compacted root-rock below. The city is mineralizing selectively. It is laying down structures within its own body — crystalline formations, ordered lattices of calcium and silicate that are neither plant nor stone but something in between, something that has the organized complexity of bone or nacre but at a scale that beggars either. These structures conduct vibration. They propagate the deep thrumming you feel when you stamp your foot. They are, as far as you can determine, the substrate of whatever process the city uses instead of thought.

So the city eats its own dead tissue, mines its own petrified roots, processes the mineral content in its factory-organs, and lays down computational infrastructure inside its own body. The factories are still producing. What they produce is mind.

## The Awareness

This is where you lose your footing. Not physically — the root-mat holds you fine — but conceptually, because you are trying to describe the interior life of something that has no interior in the way you understand interiority, and no life in the way you understand life, and yet is so manifestly *present* that standing in its central districts feels like standing inside someone's attention.

The city is aware of you. Not in the way a human is aware of an ant on their skin — not with that disproportion of scale and indifference. More the way a forest is aware of a fire: totally, with every part of itself, the information about your presence propagating through the root-mat and the mineral lattices and the rail-cutting network at the speed of a slow wave, reaching the far edges of the plateau minutes after you first set foot on the living ground. The city knew you were coming before you crested the ridge. It felt the change in pressure where your weight met its outermost roots. It tasted the sole of your shoe.

But awareness is not attention, and attention is not interest, and the city's interest in you is — negligible. You are a thermal anomaly. You are a minor pressure event. You are briefly relevant to the beetle-things, who will route around your feet as they process along the rail cuttings, adjusting their lines with the fluid precision of a stream encountering a stone. And then you will leave, and the city will not remember you, because the city's memory is not organized around events of your kind. The city remembers the last ice age. The city remembers the slow tilt of the plateau as tectonic forces worked beneath it over millennia. The city remembers the original human construction — not as history, not as narrative, but as a structural template encoded in the geometry of its oldest living wood, a plan it continues to follow and elaborate upon not because it values the plan but because the plan is part of its body now, part of its form, and it no more questions the plan than you question the shape of your own skeleton.

You are standing inside something that is aware of you and does not care about you and is not diminished by not caring. This is not cruelty or even indifference in the emotional sense. It is simply scale. You are an afternoon. The city is an epoch. Your relationship to it is the relationship of a single raindrop to a watershed.

## The Processions

But look closer at the beetle-things, because they are the nearest thing to inhabitants in the sense you'd normally mean, and they are strange in ways that reward attention.

They are not beetles. You knew this already — the woody legs, the coiled green body — but the full scope of their not-being-beetles only becomes clear when you watch them for hours, which you can do, because they are endlessly active and because something about their behavior is hypnotic in the way that watching fire or water is hypnotic: pattern without repetition, order without predictability.

They come in sizes. The smallest are the width of your thumbnail, pale green, moving fast, carrying fragments so tiny you can barely see them — dust, essentially, mineral dust, the finest product of the root-mat's decomposition of its own basal layers. These small ones travel in dense streams along the narrowest rail cuttings, the secondary and tertiary lines that branch off the main network and run through the interiors of buildings, along baseboards, up walls, across ceilings. They are everywhere in the inner districts. They are the city's capillaries.

The larger ones — the size of your fist, some of them, dark green shading to brown, their spiral bodies wound so tight they look armored — carry correspondingly larger fragments: chunks of bark, sections of mineralized root, and occasionally something that looks like a seed but isn't, or isn't only — a dense ovoid capsule with a surface like polished wood, warm to the touch, vibrating at a frequency you can feel in your fingertips. These capsules are carried with what you can only describe as care. The large beetle-things move slowly when they carry them, and the rest of the procession adjusts its pace to match. The capsules are delivered to the factories, always the factories, brought in through the great doorways and set down on the smooth root-mat floor in precise arrangements — rows, grids, spirals — that change configuration each time you visit.

You pick up a capsule once. (Nothing stops you. The beetle-thing that was carrying it simply pauses, waits, vibrating.) It is heavier than it looks. The vibration in it is complex — layered, polyrhythmic, like the clicking of the factory walls compressed into a space you can hold in your hand. You have the sudden, vertiginous intuition that you are holding a *packet* — a unit of information, a message, a compressed instruction set being routed through the city's network for processing in the factory-organs. You set it down. The beetle-thing picks it up and continues on its way.

What are the factories computing? You will not find out. The city's cognition is not available to you any more than the content of a neuron's firing is available to the naked eye. You can see the process — the inputs arriving, the clicking intensifying, the mineral lattices being laid down in the substrate below, the switches clacking into new positions in the rail network — but the *meaning* of the process, if meaning is even the right word, operates at a temporal and spatial scale that your nervous system cannot apprehend. The city thinks in centuries. A single "thought," if such a unit exists, might take decades to complete, might involve the reorganization of entire districts, the rerouting of thousands of processions, the growth and senescence of hundreds of factory-columns. You are watching a mind operate, and you cannot read it, and this is not because it is hidden but because it is happening too slowly and too largely for your perceptual apparatus to parse.

## The Gelatinous Things

The cistern-dwellers deserve their own consideration because they are the one element of the city that seems *uncertain* — the one place where the system's total self-coherence shows a crack, or a frontier, or an experiment in progress.

They drift in the dark water of the plaza cisterns, translucent, shapeless, ranging in size from a grape to a grapefruit. They are not plants. You are fairly sure of this. They lack the cellular structure of plant tissue; under close inspection (you lean over the cistern, your face close to the water, and one of them drifts toward you with what might be curiosity or might be Brownian motion), they appear to be composed of a homogeneous gel, clear with faint green tinting, containing no visible organelles, no nuclei, no chloroplasts. And yet they are alive. They move under their own power, however slowly. They respond to stimuli — a shadow passing over the cistern causes them to sink; direct sunlight causes them to rise. When two of them touch, they merge, and the merged body is briefly more active, pulsing with a rhythm that matches the deep thrumming of the root-mat below.

They might be the city's equivalent of stem cells — undifferentiated tissue held in reserve, ready to become whatever is needed. They might be waste products, metabolic byproducts that accumulate in the cisterns and are periodically reabsorbed when the water level drops. They might be sensors, monitoring the chemical composition of the city's water supply and communicating their findings through the vibrations they produce when they merge.

Or they might be something the city is *trying to become* and has not yet succeeded at. An experiment in a new kind of tissue. A prototype for an organ that does not yet exist. You watch them drift and pulse and merge and separate, and you think: this is the one place where the city does not yet know what it is doing. This is the growing tip, the point of uncertainty, the place where the system's relentless iteration encounters a problem it has not yet solved. The gelatinous things are the city's questions, asked of itself, unanswered, repeated, refined, asked again.

## The Bird

One bird. You keep seeing one bird.

It might not be the same bird. But it is always one, always singular, always circling high above the central districts in the thermals that rise from the city's metabolic warmth. Dark-feathered, small-bodied, sharp-winged — a swift of some kind, or something convergent with a swift, built for high-altitude soaring with minimal energy expenditure. It never lands in the city. It circles. Hours at a time, riding the updraft, making no sound you can hear from ground level.

It is the only thing here that is not part of the city.

You are sure of this because everything else — the beetle-things, the gelatinous cistern-dwellers, the mosses and epiphytes on the smokestacks, the pale wood of the walls, the mineral lattices in the substrate — is continuous. Everything else is connected to everything else through the root-mat, through the rail network, through the vibrational web that propagates through every surface. The bird is *discontinuous*. It touches nothing. It takes nothing from the city and gives nothing to it. It is pure observation — an eye without a hand, a witness without participation.

You watch it circle and you think: it is doing what I am doing. It is looking at the city and trying to understand the city and failing to understand the city because understanding requires operating at the city's scale, at the city's speed, and neither of us can do that. We are both too fast and too small. We are both afternoon-things, raindrop-things, trying to comprehend the watershed.

But the bird keeps coming back. Day after day, if you stay long enough to notice, there is a bird — one bird — circling. It is not nesting here. It is not feeding here. There is nothing for it here except the thermal and the view.

Maybe that is enough. Maybe there is something in the bird's neurology, tiny and quick as it is, that responds to the pattern below — the vast, slow, recursive pattern of the city computing itself — the way your own neurology responds to fire or water. Maybe the bird is *watching* in the way that watching is its own reward, the way certain stimuli hold attention not because they contain information you can use but because they contain information at a density and complexity that your perceptual system finds inherently, inexhaustibly engaging.

The bird circles. The city clicks and breathes. Neither is aware of the other in any way that constitutes relationship. And yet the bird is here. And yet the bird keeps coming back.

## What Lives Here

So: what lives here.

A mind lives here. One mind, one organism, a single vast autotrophic intelligence that bootstrapped itself out of a ruin and has been elaborating its own structure for longer than any living thing you've encountered has existed. It is not conscious in any way you can confirm, and it is not unconscious in any way you can confirm, and the distinction may not apply. It processes. It iterates. It routes and switches and grows and computes, and the products of its computation are more of itself — more structure, more complexity, more recursive depth, the architecture folding inward and inward without collapsing, a city that grew into itself and kept growing.

It is patient in the way that stone is patient, not because it chooses patience but because it has no other speed. It is industrious in the way that chemistry is industrious — not because it values work but because the reactions proceed and the energy flows and the products accumulate and there is no mechanism by which they would stop. It is alone in the way that the first thing is always alone — not lonely, not isolated, just *singular*, the only one of its kind, with no other example to compare itself to and no apparent interest in comparison.

Its beetle-things walk its corridors carrying its messages. Its smokestacks breathe its weather. Its switches clack and its factories click and its cisterns hold their dark water and their drifting, questioning gel-bodies. Its streets are wide and empty and its buildings are cathedrals of purpose without occupants and its rail network is a thought in progress, always in progress, never arriving because arrival would mean the end of processing and the processing is *what it is*.

And far above, one bird circles, watching, and far below, the bedrock holds the weight of all that petrified root and all that living wood and all that silent, tireless, inhuman industry, and the sun crosses the high thin sky, and the temperature drops, and the condensation forms, and the city drinks.

That is what lives here. The city lives here. The city lives.
