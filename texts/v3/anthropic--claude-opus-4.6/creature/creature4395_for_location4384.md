They are small. That's the first thing you'd notice, if the light allowed you to notice anything so quickly. They are small the way old trees in poor soil are small—not stunted, not deprived, but *fitted*. Compact. Densely made. Their frames are narrow through the shoulders, narrow through the hips, built for turning sideways in passages, for folding into sleeping niches, for occupying space efficiently. The adults stand no taller than the mid-chest of an outsider, but they are not fragile. Their limbs are short and ropey with tendon. Their hands are disproportionately large for their bodies—broad palms, long fingers, thick at the knuckle—hands that look like they belong to someone much bigger, someone who works stone. Which they do. Which they all do, eventually.

Their skin has taken on the color of the place. Not pale the way cave creatures are pale, not the translucent white of something that has never seen the sun. It's more like the rock itself has stained them from the outside in. Their skin is a warm grey-brown, the color of wet basalt, smooth and slightly luminous in the fat-light, as though the mineral water they've been drinking and bathing in for millennia has deposited its copper and calcium just beneath the surface. Their coloring is remarkably uniform. You can't easily tell one from another by complexion alone. They would blend perfectly into the walls behind them if they stood still, and sometimes they do stand still, and you realize with a small shock that the shape you took for a carved pillar or a natural protuberance of rock is a person, waiting, watching, having been watching for some time.

Their eyes are large. Larger than they should be. The iris has expanded to fill almost the entire visible surface of the eye, leaving only a thin rim of grey-white sclera, and the irises themselves are dark—so dark they seem pupilless, like polished stones set into the face. In the fat-light, those eyes catch and hold the tiny flame and give it back doubled, and a room full of people looking at you is a room full of small paired fires hovering in the dimness. They see well in this light. They see *everything* in this light. They navigate the quarter's labyrinth of passages with a sureness that suggests their vision has been tuned across generations to extract maximum information from minimum illumination. You watch one of them thread a bone needle in the light of a single wick without squinting, without bringing the work close to the flame, and you understand that what feels to you like near-darkness is, to them, a fully articulated world.

They don't blink often. You notice this and then you can't stop noticing it. The wet air keeps their eyes moist without the need for frequent blinking, and the effect is an unbroken attentiveness, a gaze that settles on you and stays. It isn't hostile. It isn't searching. It is simply *continuous* in a way that you, with your constant flickering interruptions of sight, find profoundly unsettling. They watch the way the reservoir holds its water—still, total, unperturbed. A child will look at you with that same gaze, neither curious nor incurious, just *present*, and you'll feel, absurdly, that you're the one who's been put on display.

---

Their hair is thick and dark and worn long by everyone—men, women, those who might be neither or both (the distinctions, if they exist, are not legible to you in any familiar way). The hair is not styled so much as managed: pulled back, twisted, knotted with strips of the same stiff plant fiber that curtains the sleeping niches. Some of the older ones have hair that has gone the color of the pale cress that grows in the planting troughs—not white, but a wan greenish-grey, as though even in this the body has taken instruction from the only vegetation it knows. In the dampness, every head is slightly beaded with moisture, and you see people absently run their fingers through their hair and flick the water away, a gesture so habitual it's become meaningless, a tic of the species.

Their feet are bare and extraordinary. The soles are thick with callus, a full centimeter of densely layered skin that gives each footfall its characteristic soft *pat* on the wet stone. But the toes—the toes are long, prehensile almost, spread wide and articulated with a dexterity that hands might envy. You see a woman descend a steep, water-slicked passage without using her hands at all, her toes gripping the bowl-shaped depressions that were once steps, curling around lips of stone, finding purchase where you would find nothing. Children climb the walls this way, toes jammed into seams and old tool-marks, scaling the stacked sleeping niches to reach the upper tiers with an ease that looks biological, innate, as though the architecture of the place and the architecture of the foot have been evolving toward each other for so long that they are now interlocking parts of a single system.

---

They are quiet. You already knew the space was quiet, but the people are quiet *inside* the quiet, quiet in a way that goes beyond the acoustic dampening of the wet rock. They move through the passages with an economy of motion that eliminates incidental sound. No scuffing. No bumping of elbows against walls. No dropped tools. Their bodies know the dimensions of every passage the way your tongue knows the inside of your mouth—completely, unconsciously, without the need for active negotiation. You watch a man carry a full basin of water through a corridor so narrow his shoulders nearly touch both walls, and he doesn't spill a drop, doesn't slow, doesn't even appear to be concentrating. His body is a key that fits every lock in the quarter, and the quarter is a lock that was made by his body, and these two things have been shaping each other for so long that the passage through is inevitable, frictionless, perfect.

When they speak, it's in a language that seems to have been designed for this acoustic environment—or perhaps it's the other way around, and the language has been sculpted by the space until only the sounds that work here survive. The consonants are soft: no hard stops, no plosives, nothing that would crack against the wet stone and die. Instead, the sounds flow—sibilants, liquids, nasals, a murmuring susurrus that moves through the passages like the water moves through the channels, finding its way without forcing. The vowels are open and low, chest-resonant, felt as much as heard. Listening to a conversation from a distance, unable to make out individual words, you'd think you were hearing the mountain's own respiratory hum modulated into something almost, but not quite, meaningful.

They don't speak much. That's not quite right—they speak *precisely*, the way they carve. Each utterance seems to be a finished thing, shaped and fitted before it's released, and the silences between statements are not awkward but structural, load-bearing, part of the meaning. You realize after a while that a great deal of their communication is gestural—not a formal sign language, but a dense, nuanced vocabulary of hand movements, head tilts, shifts in posture, changes in the pace of breathing. A woman raises one hand, fingers spread, palm angled slightly downward, and the three people she's with change direction immediately, without discussion, as though she's redirected a current. A child touches the back of an elder's wrist with two fingertips and the elder reaches without looking to a shelf and produces a specific tool. The gestures are so integrated into movement that you can't always tell where practical action ends and communication begins. Picking up a stone, setting down a basin, leaning against a wall—any of these might be saying something, and you'd never know.

---

The children are the most visible inhabitants, not because they're louder or more active—they're not, particularly—but because they're the most likely to be in the open spaces near the reservoir, where the dim light from the shafts is strongest. They play, but their play looks like work and their work looks like play. A group of four children, aged maybe five to ten (though you're guessing; their small frames make age hard to read), sit around a shallow basin of water, taking turns pressing shaped pieces of stone against a flat rock surface, grinding in slow circles, producing a sound like a whispered exhalation. They're shaping tools. The motion is identical to the motion of play—repetitive, absorbing, apparently purposeless from the outside—but the objects they produce are real, functional: small hand-adzes, scraping blades, the careful geometry of a channel-clearing pick no longer than a finger. The oldest child inspects each piece by touch, running a thumb along the edge, and either nods—the smallest possible nod, more a settling of the chin than an upward-downward motion—or sets it aside without comment for more work.

The children's games, when they are games, involve water. Of course they do. They dam the smallest finger-channels with plugs of clay, redirect the trickles, race tiny floating slivers of stone along diverted paths, then carefully undo their changes, restoring the flow to its intended course. It's play the way a river's eddy is play—experimental, responsive, ultimately obedient to a larger system. No adult intervenes. No adult needs to. The children already understand that the water goes where it must go, that the channels are not theirs to permanently alter, that the system is complete and has been complete since before anyone alive. Their play is a rehearsal for maintenance, and their maintenance, when they're old enough for it, will retain something of the quality of play.

You notice something about the children that takes a while to articulate. They are not *supervised*. There is no watchful parent hovering at the edge of each group, no anxious eye tracking their movements near the reservoir or the steep passages. The children move through the quarter with the same unconscious spatial fluency as the adults, their toes gripping the same worn stone, their bodies fitting the same passages. A three-year-old—again, you're guessing—toddles along the rim of the reservoir, a drop of several feet to the warm water below, and no one moves to intercept, no one tenses. The child's footing is sure. It has always been sure. It was sure before the child could walk, when it was being carried through these passages in a sling against its parent's chest, its body learning the rhythms and geometries of the quarter through the medium of another body. By the time a child here walks, it has already spent a year being the passenger of someone who knows every surface by heart, and that knowledge transfers—not as memory, but as something deeper, something muscular, proprioceptive, a knowledge that lives in the inner ear and the soles of the feet.

---

The adults divide labor, but the division is so fluid and context-dependent that it takes a long time to perceive any pattern. For a while you think everyone does everything, which is almost true, but not quite. There are specialists—or rather, there are people whose particular attention has, over a lifetime, settled more heavily on one aspect of the quarter's maintenance than another.

There is a man—old, his hair that pale cress-grey, his hands enormous even by local standards—who spends most of his waking hours at the channels. He moves slowly through the quarter with a set of bone picks and scrapers in a leather roll, pausing at each junction and basin to inspect, to scrape away mineral deposits, to test the flow by wetting a finger and holding it to the stone. His attention is absolute. He traces the entire water system the way a physician palpates a body, feeling for blockage, for irregularity, for the subtle change in flow rate that signals a developing problem somewhere upstream. He speaks less than anyone. His communication is almost entirely with the stone, a constant murmured dialogue of tool-tap and fingertip and tilted head that you could mistake for ritual if you didn't understand it was diagnostics. When he does speak to another person, it is to request assistance with a specific repair—a channel that needs recutting, a basin that has cracked and must be sealed with a paste ofite calciumite andite calcium ite calciumite calcium calcium calcium calcium that must be mixed to the right calcium calcium calcium—

No. Let that go. He mixes something. A calcium paste, chalite calcium calcium calcium calcium calcium calcium calcium calcium calcium calcium calcium calcium calcium calcium that hardens to rock and the formula is in his hands and has been in hands like his for longer than language.

He is not the leader. There is no leader. You look for one because you expect one, but the quarter doesn't work that way. Decision-making here is distributed the way the water is distributed—through a network of channels, each one small, each one feeding into a larger flow, the aggregate emerging without any single point of origin. When something needs to be decided—a wall that needs reinforcement, a sleeping niche that has become unstable, a reallocation of planting trough soil—the people involved simply begin talking, and others join or don't, and at some point a consensus materializes, and at some slightly later point the work begins, and you could not tell if you watched the entire process where the discussion ended and the decision began. It is the same seamlessness you see in their gestures, their movement, their carving. Everything flows.

---

There is a woman who tends the planting troughs. She is younger—middle-aged, maybe, though the sameness of their complexions and the difficulty of reading age in the dim light makes this uncertain. Her hands are always dark with soil, a richer brown against the grey-brown of her skin, and she smells of the astringent herb, its sharp scent clinging to her like a second atmosphere. She moves between the troughs on a slow, daily circuit, pinching back growth here, redirecting a root there, crumbling a small amount of calcium calcium calcium calcium—

Of mineral powder. She adds mineral powder to the soil. She does this by feel, rubbing it between her fingers, tasting it, tasting the soil itself, tasting the water that feeds each trough. Her tongue is a precision instrument, calibrated to the chemical signature of the quarter's hydrology, and when she frowns at the taste of the water in a particular trough, people pay attention. A change in the water means a change in the stone means a change somewhere in the mountain's slow, internal metabolism, and the plants are the first indicator, the canary in this particular mine. She has, you learn over days of watching, an entire taxonomy of water-tastes—a vocabulary for mineral variations so subtle they'd require a spectrometer to detect. She speaks this vocabulary in the same low, flowing language as everyone else, but with additional terms that no one else uses or, you suspect, fully understands. She is the quarter's tongue. She tastes the mountain for them.

---

There are others whose roles you begin to discern:

A young person—truly young, maybe sixteen, with a quick, light way of moving that hasn't yet settled into the adult economy of motion—who seems to be responsible for the fat-wicks. They move through the quarter on a circuit that takes most of a day, checking each small flame, trimming wicks, adding rendered fat from a stone pot they carry slung against one hip. The fat comes from something. You're not sure what. There's no visible animal husbandry in the quarter, no pens, no fodder. The fat may come from outside—from some relationship with the world beyond the crack in the cliff face that you haven't yet been shown. The young person handles the flames with a tenderness that seems personal, cupping each one briefly in their hands as though greeting it, and you notice that they know each flame individually, that they adjust each one differently, that the illumination in the quarter is not uniform but *composed*, brighter here and dimmer there according to a design you can't quite read. There's a logic to it. There's always a logic here. You just haven't lived long enough to learn it.

An old woman—the oldest person you've seen, her face a topography of fine lines, her eyes those same dark polished stones but deeper-set, recessed into the skull as though withdrawing into the mountain behind her—who sits near the reservoir most of the day, doing something with fibers. She cards them, spins them against her thigh with a hand-spindle weighted with a disc of polished stone, and weaves them on a frame so small it fits in her lap. The fabric she produces is the same stiff, sparse material that curtains the sleeping niches—functional, not decorative, every thread a contribution to the quarter's material infrastructure. But occasionally, not often, she weaves something different. You see it once: a small panel, no bigger than your two hands, into which she has worked a pattern that is not quite geometric and not quite representational. It looks like water. It looks like the branching pattern of the seep-channels. It looks like the map of the quarter's passages seen from above. It looks like all of these and none of them, and when she finishes it she holds it up in the fat-light and turns it slowly, examining it from different angles, and then she folds it and puts it away in a niche behind her, where you can see a stack of similar small panels accumulated over what must be many, many years. She doesn't show them to anyone. No one asks.

---

The old woman's panels are the closest thing you find to art, and even that designation feels wrong—too loud, too categorical for what they are. The marks on the ceiling are not art either, or not only art. They are something more like *witness*—each mark a record of a hand that was here, that pressed a tool to the stone, that said *I shaped this place and this place shaped me*. The marks are not signed. They are not dated. They are not arranged in any discernible order. They are simply *there*, the way the mineral deposits are there, the way the polish on the floors is there: evidence of process, not product.

But there is something else, something that might be closer to what you'd call a cultural practice, though even that phrase feels too heavy for it. In the evenings—you think of them as evenings, though the light in the quarter barely changes; it's more a shift in activity, a quieting, a settling—the people gather near the reservoir. Not all of them. Not formally. They just drift in that direction, the way the water drifts toward the lowest point, and they sit on the worn stone rim and along the walls of the adjoining passages, and someone begins to hum.

The hum is low, in the chest, and it picks up the pitch of the mountain's own respiratory drone and weaves around it—not harmonizing exactly, but *relating* to it, entering into a dialogue with the bass note of the air moving through the shafts. Another voice joins, and another, each one finding a slightly different pitch, a slightly different rhythm, and the composite sound builds slowly into something that fills the chamber the way the water fills the reservoir: completely, evenly, without turbulence. It's not a song. It has no melody, no lyrics, no beginning and no end. People join and leave as they choose. The sound evolves continuously, responding to itself, to the dripping, to the hum of the shafts, to some shared sense of *rightness* that you can feel but not define. When it's working—when the voices and the water and the mountain are all in alignment—the entire chamber resonates, and you feel it in your sternum, in your teeth, in the wet stone under your hands. The sound becomes the space. The space becomes the sound. You close your eyes and you can't tell where the mountain ends and the people begin.

This might go on for an hour. It might go on for three. There's no signal that it's over. The voices thin, one by one, until only the dripping remains, and the hum, and then people drift back to their niches and their curtains and their sleep, and the fat-wicks burn lower, and the quarter enters its nighttime configuration—not darker, exactly, but *stiller*, more purely mineral, more purely stone.

---

You begin to understand, after enough time, that the people of the cistern quarter do not think of themselves as living *inside* the mountain. That preposition—*inside*—implies a separation, a container and a contained, and for them no such separation exists. They are not inside the mountain the way you are inside a house. They are inside the mountain the way the water is inside the stone—permeating it, shaped by it, inseparable from it. Their bodies are made of the mountain's water and the mountain's minerals and the pale plants that grow in the mountain's filtered light. Their waste returns to the mountain through channels as carefully managed as the water channels—a whole sanitation system you eventually discover in the deepest, warmest corner of the quarter, where biological waste is broken down by microbial action in stone-lined pits that eventually feed back into the mountain's geology. They are a nutrient cycle. They are a hydrological event. They are what happens when a species enters a pocket of stone and stays long enough to become part of its chemistry.

And the mountain is inside them. The mineral taste of the water is in their bones, literally—their skeletons must be dense with the copper and calcium of the seep-water, their teeth stained faintly green, their fingernails thick and hard as horn from a lifetime of mineral saturation. The mountain's geometry is in their muscles, in the specific pattern of development that comes from a life spent climbing, gripping, turning sideways, folding into niches. The mountain's rhythm is in their sleep cycle, in the slow pulse of their communal life, in the patience—the extraordinary, inhuman patience—with which they approach every task. You watch a man spend an entire day cutting a single channel extension no longer than his forearm, and he is not bored, not frustrated, not hurrying. He is working at the speed of stone. He is working at the speed of water. He is working at the only speed this place recognizes, which is the speed at which mountains become smooth.

---

Do they go outside?

Some of them do. You see evidence of it: the rendered fat for the wicks, which must come from animals hunted or traded for beyond the crack in the cliff face. A handful of materials that don't originate in the quarter—a few pieces of bone from creatures too large to live in these passages, scraps of animal hide used for carrying-slings, and very occasionally, a thing that shocks you with its foreignness: a feather, tucked into a niche beside a sleeping place, its color vivid and impossible against the quarter's muted palette. A bird feather. Red. Like a shout in a library.

The ones who go outside are younger, mostly. They squeeze back through the crack and descend the mountain and do whatever needs to be done in the world out there—a world you're now starting to think of as the *other* place, the *less real* place, which tells you something about what the quarter does to your sense of proportion. They go out and they come back, and when they come back they are briefly, subtly wrong—too loud, too expansive in their movements, their skin dried by wind and sun, their eyes narrowed against remembered brightness. It takes a day, maybe two, for them to re-acclimate, for their bodies to soften back into the quarter's humid, close, unhurried rhythms. You watch a young woman return from outside and pause just inside the crack, breathing, letting the warm wet air resettle on her skin, letting her eyes open wide again to drink in the beautiful, the sufficient, the total dimness. She stands there for a long time. She's not resting. She's *re-entering*, the way a diver re-enters water—slowly, deliberately, letting the medium receive her.

The oldest inhabitants, you realize, haven't been outside in decades. Maybe ever. The old man with the channel-picks, the old woman with her weaving—they are so perfectly adapted to this space that the outside world is not merely foreign to them but *physically impossible*. Their eyes couldn't take the light. Their feet couldn't handle the uncarved roughness of natural terrain. Their lungs, accustomed to warm, mineral-laden, motionless air, would struggle with the cold dry wind of the highlands. They are as much creatures of this specific environment as the blind thing you may or may not have seen in the reservoir. They could no more survive outside than it could.

And they don't want to. That's the thing you struggle with, because you carry the assumption that living in a pocket of stone inside a mountain must be a kind of deprivation, a last resort, a compromise that no one would choose. But watching them—the sureness of their movements, the calm of their faces, the deep, almost geological contentment of their daily rhythms—you begin to understand that *want* is the wrong framework entirely. They don't want to be here the way you want to be somewhere. They are here the way the water is here. The question of wanting doesn't arise. The question of *leaving* doesn't arise. The quarter is not a place they live. It's a place they *are*.

---

At night—at what they treat as night—you lie in a borrowed sleeping niche, your body folded into its shallow bowl, the stiff curtain drawn, the stone beneath you warm from the mountain's heat and slick with the thinnest possible film of moisture. The niche is too short for you and too narrow, and the ceiling is close enough to touch, and the rock is covered with those shallow scalloped tool-marks that press their pattern into your shoulder blade and hip. You are inside the mountain. You are inside the mountain the way a fossil is inside the mountain—pressed, embedded, held. The dripping continues. The hum continues. Through the curtain, the last fat-wicks throw their halos of orange onto the wet walls, and the moving shadows of people going to their own niches pass like slow fish across the stone.

You sleep, eventually. The stone holds you. The water touches you everywhere—on your skin, in your lungs, on your lips when you lick them. You dream of the ceiling marks, the accumulated record of every hand, and in the dream they move, slowly, the way the mountain moves, rearranging themselves into patterns that almost mean something and then dissolving back into randomness. You dream of the hum, and in the dream the hum has words, but the words are in a language that is older than language, a language made of mineral concentrations and seep rates and the slow chemical bonding of calcium to stone. You dream that you have always been here. You dream that *here* is all there has ever been.

In the morning—in what they treat as morning—you wake to the sound of a tool on stone. Somewhere in the quarter, someone is carving. The sound is soft, rhythmic, unhurried: *tch, tch, tch*. A shallow crescent with each stroke. A scale on the body of the fish. The work continues. The water continues. The people of the cistern quarter are doing what they have always done, which is the same thing the mountain has always done, which is the slow, patient, implacable work of becoming what they already are.
