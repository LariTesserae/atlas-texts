# The Inhabitants

---

## The First Thing You Understand

Nothing here is waiting. That is the first correction the place makes to your mammalian assumptions. You arrived expecting dormancy — expecting the patience of ruins, the slow grinding of time over abandoned things. But this place is not abandoned and nothing in it is patient in the way you mean when you say *patient*. Everything here is **mid-act**. The animals, the architecture, the water itself — they are all in the middle of something, and the something has no beginning you can locate and no end you can anticipate, and the distinction between *living here* and *happening here* is one the place does not make.

So when you ask what lives here, the honest answer is: **the place lives here, and certain densities within it are more motile than others.**

But you want the animals. Fine. Start with what you can almost see.

---

## The Rind-Grazer Clusters

The fist-sized creatures clinging to the walls — the ones that pulse, the ones described as a *theme* rather than a species — are the most visible inhabitants, and also the most misleading, because they are the ones that most resemble something you already know. They look like life. They look like animals *doing* something: clinging, feeding, pulsing, drifting away when the surface beneath them shifts. They seem comprehensible. They are not.

Each one is roughly the size of a human fist, yes, but the comparison fails immediately after scale. They have no symmetry — not bilateral, not radial, not any. Each one is shaped like a word you can't quite read: a rounded form with protrusions and clefts and surfaces that suggest function without declaring it. One might have what looks like a single thick limb, curled under its body, gripping the wall with a pad that spreads flat under pressure. Another, three centimeters away, might grip the same wall with a fringe of cilia so fine they look like fur, its body elongated, its surface pocked with what might be openings. A third might be flat, limpet-like, seemingly immobile, but if you watched it for twenty minutes you'd see it *flowing* imperceptibly across the surface, leaving behind a faint track where the wall material is thinner, more translucent.

They are not a species. They are something harder to name: a population of organisms that share a metabolic strategy and a rough size class but have not converged on a single body plan. Or — and this is the possibility that begins to surface the longer you watch them — they are a single organism at different stages of a life cycle so complex that each stage looks like a different animal entirely. Or they are the same genetic material expressed in wildly different morphologies depending on where on the wall they settled, what the local water chemistry was at the moment of their attachment, what the wall was doing when they arrived.

What they are *doing* is easier to describe than what they *are*. They eat the city. Or rather, they eat what the city produces. The wall material — that dense, pearlescent, calcium-adjacent substance — is not uniform. It has layers, and the outermost layer, the one that forms when a new surface rises from the water and begins to harden, is chemically different from what lies beneath: richer in something, denser in some mineral or organic compound that the fist-creatures can metabolize. They consume this rind. Their pulsing — which looks like breathing but isn't, or isn't only — is the mechanism of consumption: a rhythmic application and release of chemical solvent secreted from their ventral surfaces, dissolving the outermost layer of the wall and absorbing the result.

When they have consumed the rind down to the harder substrate, they stop. They don't eat deeper. They don't burrow. They release and drift, carried by whatever current is operating at that moment, until they encounter another fresh surface — and in a city that is constantly reconfiguring, constantly raising new material into the air and water, fresh surfaces are always appearing somewhere. The fist-creatures are grazers on a landscape that never stops growing. They are the first trophic level. They are the grass-eaters of a world where the grass is architecture.

They reproduce — you think they reproduce — by a process you can only observe the aftermath of. Occasionally, a drifting fist-creature will split. Not cleanly, not symmetrically. It will develop an internal tension, a visible crease, and over the course of perhaps an hour it will separate into two bodies of unequal size, each one immediately and obviously different from the other, each one already a unique variation on the theme. The smaller one usually drifts; the larger one usually seeks a wall. But not always. Nothing here is *always*.

They are almost silent. The faint clicking you hear comes not from any vocalization but from the mechanical interaction of their solvent-secretion with the wall material: tiny bubbles forming and collapsing at the interface between creature and surface, a miniature effervescence that, multiplied across a cluster of thirty or forty grazers, produces a sound like very distant rain on a tin roof.

They do not react to you. You can touch them — carefully, because you don't know what the solvent would do to your skin — and they do not retract or flee or change their behavior. You are not food. You are not wall. You are not relevant.

## The Ribbon Predators

The long, ribbonlike forms that pass beneath the surface and occasionally take a fist-creature from its drift — these are the second thing you notice, and the first thing that makes you afraid, not because they threaten you but because their *scale* is wrong and your body knows it.

A ribbon predator, fully extended, can be anywhere from three meters to — you're not certain. You've seen one that seemed to go on for longer than you could track it, curving around a submerged wall and disappearing at both ends simultaneously. Twenty meters? Thirty? It's hard to tell because they are laterally compressed — thin, blade-thin, no wider than your palm — and because they undulate in a way that constantly changes their apparent length. They are translucent. Not transparent: you cannot see through them clearly, but you can see light pass through them, and in the grey illumination of this place they are visible mainly as distortions, as places where the water is slightly less clear, slightly more refractive. They are the color of the water itself, which is the color of nothing, which is grey.

They have no head. Or rather, they have no end that is obviously more head-like than the other. Both ends taper. Both ends can lead. They reverse direction without turning, simply by shifting which end undulates first. When they move through open water, they are sinusoidal, wave-shaped, each curve of their body trailing the one ahead of it with the same phase delay, like a flag in wind except there is no wind and the flag is alive and the flag is long enough to drape across the interior of a cathedral.

They eat the fist-creatures. This is the only feeding behavior you observe directly. A ribbon predator approaching a cluster of drifting grazers does not accelerate. It does not lunge. It simply *arrives* — its leading edge passing through the loose cloud of fist-creatures with no change in speed or trajectory, and as it passes, certain of the fist-creatures are *gone*. Not seized. Not struck. Gone. The ribbon's body shows slight bulges where they were, which travel slowly along its length like boluses in a snake, but the act of capture itself is too fast or too subtle to see. Some adhesive surface, some instantaneous enveloping fold of that blade-thin body — you can't tell. The remaining fist-creatures drift on, unchanged.

What disturbs you about the ribbon predators is not their hunting. It's their *relationship to the architecture*. They move through the submerged city with an ease that suggests deep familiarity — or deep integration. They thread through flooded corridors and channels that are actively reconfiguring, and they are never caught. A wall closes behind a ribbon predator at exactly the moment its trailing edge passes through. A channel opens ahead of one at exactly the moment it arrives. You watch this happen three times, four times, five, and you cannot determine the causality: is the city *making way* for the ribbons, or are the ribbons *reading* the city's movements with such precision that they always happen to be where an opening is about to be? Or is there no distinction? Are the ribbon predators part of the city's circulatory system, mobile elements in an architecture that includes the living and the built as components of a single process?

They do not react to you either. One passes beneath you, close enough that you feel the water displacement against your legs — a smooth, lateral push, weirdly warm at the edges — and it does not deviate. You are an obstacle of a size and type it is accustomed to navigating around. You are a wall that moves. The city is full of walls that move.

## The Sack Climbers

The dark, compact bodies that haul themselves briefly out of the water — the leather-sack shapes with the thick, finger-like appendages and no obvious sensory organs — are harder to observe because they spend most of their time below the waterline, and when they surface, they don't stay long.

A sack climber is roughly the size of a human torso. Its body is oblate — wider than it is tall, flattened along the axis of gravity, giving it a low, stable profile when it grips a wall. Its skin, if skin is the right word, is dark brown-black, with a texture that reads as leather from a distance but resolves, up close, into something more like compacted fibers — matted, dense, slightly oily to the touch, as though the creature secretes a hydrophobic film. It has between four and eight appendages, depending on — depending on the individual, or depending on the moment, you're not sure. The appendages emerge from its underside without any fixed arrangement. They are thick, muscular, blunt-tipped, and they grip the wall with a strength that you can infer from the way the creature hangs on even as the architecture shifts beneath it. Each appendage tip spreads wide when pressed against a surface, creating a broad, flat pad that conforms to the texture of the wall. The grip is partly mechanical, partly chemical: you can see a faint residue where a sack climber has released, a dark stain that fades over minutes.

What they're doing on the walls is not feeding. The fist-creatures eat the rind; the sack climbers do something else. They climb to a point just above the waterline, grip hard, and then *press their bodies flat against the surface*. They hold this position for varying durations — sometimes thirty seconds, sometimes ten minutes — and then they release and drop back into the water. The wall where they pressed shows no visible change. No consumption has occurred. No deposit is evident. But the city's reconfiguration patterns in the immediate vicinity of a sack climber's press often change in the minutes that follow — a wall that was sinking begins to rise, or a channel redirects, or a basin forms where none was forming before. The sack climbers are *doing something* to the city. Communicating with it. Triggering it. Adjusting it. Or testing it, reading it, taking some kind of measurement through full-body contact with its surface.

They have no obvious eyes, no obvious mouth, no obvious orifice of any kind. Sensory input, if it occurs, occurs through the entire body surface. The creature *is* a sense organ, pressed against the wall the way you might press your ear to a door.

Unlike the grazers and the ribbons, the sack climbers *do* react to you. Not aggressively, not fearfully, but they... *adjust*. If you swim toward a wall where a sack climber is pressed, it will release and drop back into the water before you arrive. Not quickly. Not in panic. It simply concludes its business a little sooner than it might have otherwise. If you are stationary in the water near a wall, a sack climber surfacing nearby will orient itself so that its press-point is as far from you as the wall allows. It is aware of you. It accounts for you. It does not want you near it when it is doing what it does, whatever it does. The impression is not of fear but of *privacy* — as though what the sack climber does against the wall is something that requires a certain absence of interference, a certain purity of contact, and your body in the water nearby is a form of noise.

When submerged, the sack climbers are surprisingly fast. They do not swim in any way you recognize — they don't undulate, they don't jet, they don't paddle. Instead, they *deform*: their entire body changes shape, elongating in one direction, contracting in another, creating asymmetric pressure differentials that propel them through the water in quick, darting movements that look like a leather bag being squeezed and released by an invisible hand. Between these bursts of movement, they drift, inert, indistinguishable from debris.

You see a sack climber, once, encounter a ribbon predator. The ribbon is passing through open water. The sack climber is drifting near a submerged terrace. The ribbon's leading edge passes within a meter of the sack climber and the sack climber *blooms* — its body suddenly expanding to twice its diameter, its surface going from dark and smooth to pale and textured, covered in short, rigid protrusions like the teeth of a comb. The ribbon's leading edge curves away. Not sharply — smoothly, as part of its normal undulation, but *just enough*. The ribbon passes. The sack climber contracts, darkens, resumes drifting. The entire interaction takes four seconds and communicates something absolute: *not food*. The sack climber is something the ribbon predators have learned not to test.

## The Deep Movers

Below you, in the flooded lower city, things of significant size are living. You will never see them clearly. They exist for you primarily as *effects*: pressure changes, deep thrums, the displacement of water in volumes that imply bodies far larger than anything on the surface. They are the creatures whose shadows cross the pale bioluminescent light in the deep shafts, and the shadow has parts to it, has structure, has articulations that take long seconds to pass across the light source.

What you can infer:

They are large. The water displacement suggests bodies massing in the tens of tons — comparable to large marine mammals, perhaps, or larger, depending on density. They move through the flooded corridors and chambers of the lower city, which means they are navigating an architecture that is itself in constant reconfiguration, which means they are either very good at navigating changing spaces or they are somehow *involved* in the changing of those spaces, the way the ribbon predators seem to be, but at a much larger scale.

The deep thrums originate from them. When one passes through a submerged corridor, the corridor's walls vibrate at a frequency you can feel in your sternum, and this vibration propagates through the water and through the connected architecture and reaches you at the surface as a bone-deep *knock* or *pulse*. The sound is not communication — or if it is communication, it is not directed at you. It is the sound of a massive body interacting with a resonant structure. The city rings when they move through it, the way a bell rings when something brushes its interior. The deep movers are the bells' clappers.

You see the light effects most clearly when you position yourself over one of the open shafts — the vertical drops where the city's architecture descends into the flooded depths. Looking down, past the first few meters of grey-green visibility, into the darkening water, you can sometimes see a glow. Not strong. Not steady. It shifts and pulses and moves, and it is unclear whether the light comes from the creatures themselves or from the architecture responding to their presence — walls brightening as something massive passes, bioluminescent material activated by pressure or vibration or chemical signal. The light is the color of the walls: bone-white, faintly pearlescent, but warmer, more alive. Against this light, the shadows pass.

The shadow you saw — the long one, the articulated one — suggested a body plan unlike any of the surface animals. Not a ribbon. Not a sack. Something with *segments*, or with repeated structures along a central axis, like vertebrae but external, each one slightly different from the last. Something with lateral extensions — not limbs exactly, but flanges, vanes, structures that might be sensory or locomotive or architectural. Something that was *long*, as long as a ribbon predator but with mass, with volume, with a three-dimensionality that the ribbons lack. Something that was, if you are honest about what the shadow suggested, *built to the same proportions as the corridors it moved through*.

This is the thought you cannot dismiss: that the deep movers are *fitted* to the architecture. That the corridors are their dimensions. That the chambers are their resting-places. That the city's constant reconfiguration is, at least in part, a reconfiguration *around them*, *for them*, *in response to them*. That the deep movers are not inhabitants of the flooded city but *components* of it — mobile architectural elements, living rooms, drifting halls, creatures whose bodies serve some structural or hydraulic function that the city requires and that fixed architecture cannot provide.

Or, conversely: that the city was built to be their habitat, and the reconfiguration is husbandry, and the rising water was welcomed because the deep movers needed it, and the builders left because the deep movers were what the city was *for*, and the builders were done, and the deep movers are what remains.

You do not know. You will not know. The deep movers will not surface. They live in the pressurized dark of the lower city, in corridors that groan and reshape around them, in water that is colder and denser and more alkaline than the water at the surface, and they have been there for geological ages, and they will be there when the water finally rises over the last spire and the city becomes entirely submerged and the distinction between *above* and *below* ceases to mean anything, and even then they will still be moving through the corridors, and the corridors will still be reshaping around them, and the conversation between the built and the living will continue in the dark, unwitnessed, needing no witness.

## The Light-Dwellers

Twice during your time in the water, you see something you cannot categorize alongside the others.

The first time: a current pushes you close to a wall that is mid-reconfiguration — softening, flowing, reshaping itself into a new curve. In the brief window when the wall material is soft, something *emerges from inside it*. A small body, no larger than your thumb, pale to the point of translucency, with a shape like a teardrop and a trailing filament. It hangs in the water for perhaps ten seconds, spinning slowly, the filament catching the current. Then the wall beside it hardens into its new shape, and a small depression is visible where the creature emerged — a pocket, a void, a space that the wall had maintained around this creature while it was embedded.

The creature drifts. Its body pulses faintly with its own light — not bioluminescence as you understand it, not the chemical glow of deep-sea organisms, but something colder, thinner, more like the light of the sky itself captured and replayed at miniature scale. It is the brightest thing in this grey world, and it is barely bright at all. It drifts for thirty seconds, a minute, and then it approaches another wall — one that is currently softening — and it presses against the surface, and the filament seems to *bore in*, and over the course of twenty seconds the wall envelops it. Gone. Inside. Embedded in the architecture, sealed in a pocket of its own, carried along as the wall moves and reshapes, living *inside the city's body*.

The second time: you are looking down a shaft and you see not one glow but many — dozens, maybe hundreds, pinpricks of pale light distributed through the visible layers of submerged wall material. They are everywhere. They are *in* the walls. The city is full of them. Every wall, every terrace, every column and curve and basin contains some population of these embedded creatures, living in pockets within the architecture, emerging only briefly when the material softens, drifting only far enough to find another softening surface, and then re-entering. They are *part of the material*. Or the material is part of them. Or the distinction is, again, one the place does not make.

What are they doing in there? You have no way to know. But you notice: the walls that contain more of them — the walls where the pinpricks are denser — are the walls that reconfigure more actively. The walls that glow more reshape more. The living lights and the living architecture are correlated in a way that suggests — demands — a relationship deeper than habitation. The light-dwellers may be what makes the city alive. They may be the mechanism. They may be the neural tissue of a distributed brain embedded in calcium-analog walls, thinking thoughts that express themselves as architectural change, dreaming the city into its next shape, and the next, and the next.

Or they may be parasites, and the city's reconfiguration may be an immune response that has been going on for millions of years and will never resolve. From the outside, care and disease can look the same.

## The Relationship Between Them

Here is what you observe, if you are patient and cold and willing to drift:

The light-dwellers inhabit the walls. The walls reconfigure. The reconfiguration produces fresh surfaces. The fist-creatures graze the fresh surfaces. The ribbon predators eat the fist-creatures. The sack climbers press themselves against the walls, doing something that may influence the reconfiguration, which may influence where fresh surfaces appear, which may influence where the fist-creatures congregate, which may influence where the ribbon predators hunt. The deep movers transit the lower corridors, producing thrums that vibrate the walls, which may activate or redistribute the light-dwellers, which may alter the reconfiguration patterns, which begins the cycle again.

It is an ecology. It is also a machine. It is also a body. It is also, possibly, a mind.

No level is in charge. The light-dwellers don't command the walls; the walls don't command the water; the water doesn't command the animals; the animals don't command anything. The system is *acephalous* — headless, leaderless, without a center of control. Every component responds to every other component, and the aggregate behavior — the behavior of the whole — is something that no individual component intends or comprehends. The city is an emergent property of the interaction between the living and the built. And what the city *does*, what it has been doing for geological ages, is *manage the flood*. The rising water, the alkaline sea, the slow drowning of the plateau — the city takes this and makes it habitable. It channels the water. It creates currents that distribute nutrients (the wall material, the rind, the dissolved minerals). It maintains temperature gradients. It provides surfaces for attachment and corridors for movement and chambers for the deep movers to... do whatever it is they do. The city is a life-support system for its own inhabitants, and its inhabitants are the operating system of the city, and neither can exist without the other, and neither was first.

This is what the builders understood. This is what they built: not a city, not a habitat, not a machine, but a *situation* — a set of initial conditions from which this self-sustaining conversation between architecture and biology would inevitably emerge. They built the ruin first because the ruin was the fertile ground. They pre-agreed with entropy because entropy was the energy source. They left because they were done, because the situation didn't need them, because their role was to be the midwife and not the child, and the child was born, and it has been alive ever since, and it will be alive when the last wall sinks below the water, alive in the dark, alive in the pressure, alive in the thrumming corridors where the deep movers pass and the light-dwellers dream and the walls reshape and the water rises, and the water has always been rising, and the city has always known.

## What It Is Like to Be Among Them

You are cold. You are very cold. Your fingers are numb and your thinking is slower than it should be and the grey light is beginning to feel less like illumination and more like a substance you are suspended in, a medium denser than air and thinner than water, a light you could drown in.

A fist-creature drifts past you, close enough to touch. Its pulsing is steady, rhythmic, almost calming. Its body is a knot of improbable tissue, asymmetric and strange, and it has no face and no apparent awareness of you, and yet — in the way it drifts, in the way its pulsing continues unchanged in your presence, in the way it simply *is what it is* without apology or explanation — there is a strange comfort. It is alive. It is going about its life. Its life is incomprehensible to you, and yours to it, and neither of you is diminished by this incomprehension. You share the water. You share the cold. You share the grey light and the shifting walls and the deep thrums from below. You are both visitors in a conversation you did not start and will not finish.

A wall near you begins to soften. You see the faint glow of light-dwellers within it — a constellation of cold stars in bone-colored stone — and you understand, suddenly, viscerally, that the wall is not a wall. It is a colony. It is a body. It is a thought being thought by something that thinks in calcium and light and geological time. And the fist-creature drifting past you is a grazer on that thought. And the ribbon predator somewhere below is a pruner of that grazer. And the sack climber pressing itself against a distant surface is an editor, or a reader, or a whisperer of suggestions into an ear made of stone. And the deep movers in the dark below are — are *what*? The heart? The blood? The dreaming?

You don't know. You are so cold. The current shifts and pushes you toward a rising wall, and you rest your hand against it, and it is warm, and you feel it hardening, and somewhere inside it a light-dweller pulses its cold pale glow, and for a moment your hand and the light-dweller are separated by two centimeters of living stone, and you are both alive, and you are both here, and neither of you understands the other, and the city builds and dissolves and builds around you both, and the water rises, and it has always been rising, and something in the deep dark adjusts its vast articulated body and a corridor reshapes and the water surges and the grey light holds and holds and holds, and this is what lives here: **a patience that is not patience but process, a stillness that is not stillness but balance, a silence that is not silence but a language you are standing inside of, spoken in stone and flesh and cold alkaline water, and it has been speaking since before you were a species, and it will be speaking after, and it does not need you to understand it, and it does not need you to stay, and the water is so cold, and the wall is so warm, and the light inside it pulses, and you are here, and you are here, and you are here.**
