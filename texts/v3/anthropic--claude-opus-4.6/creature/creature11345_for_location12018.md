# The Root-Engineers

---

They haven't left yet. That's the first thing to understand. They're here — distributed through the root-city in the way that workers in an enormous construction project are present but rarely visible from any single vantage point. They're in the nodes you haven't entered, behind the bark-doors you haven't opened, up in the secondary tangle where the pack-lizards deliver their bundles, down in passages that slope further than you've been willing to follow. The city feels empty because it's large and they are, comparatively, few. Not because they've gone. The mug on the workbench is today's mug. The dried residue at the bottom is from this morning.

They are fewer than the city was built for. That's the tension that hums beneath everything here, the thing the scheduling board tracks alongside its shift rotations — the gap between what the root-city is becoming and how many hands are available to become it with. The city grows at its own pace, the roots following their deep vegetable logic, and the engineers work to keep up, to shape what's emerging before it sets into forms they can't use, to install the infrastructure while the walls are still soft enough to accept bolts. They are building into a living thing that will not wait for them, and there aren't quite enough of them, and this has been true for a while, and they've made their peace with it the way people make peace with weather — not by solving it but by adapting their rhythms to its rhythms, working in focused bursts where the growth is fastest, letting finished sections settle and cure while they move to the next front.

---

You'll see your first one in the factory node, if you go back. Come back in an hour — less — and there will be someone at the workbench.

She is not what you expected, though you'll struggle to say what you expected. She is roughly human-shaped in the way that says either common ancestry or convergent evolution, and you won't be able to determine which, and it won't matter as much as you think. She stands a little shorter than average human height. Her frame is dense — not heavy but *compressed*, the way wood is denser than its volume suggests, as if her body has been optimized for strength-to-weight in an environment where you might need to climb sixty meters of root-pillar on short notice. Her skin is dark, warmly dark, with an undertone that shifts between umber and something almost greenish in the conducted light, and you'll wonder if that's pigmentation or the light itself and you won't resolve it. Her hands are the thing you'll notice first. Her hands are extraordinary.

They are broad and long-fingered and mapped with calluses so specific they tell the story of her trade — the ridge along the outer palm from gripping chisels, the smooth tough pads on her fingertips from testing the grain and growth-direction of living wood, the roughened patches on her inner forearms from leaning into the work. Her nails are cut short and stained with the dark resinous substance you saw on the door-hinges. She works with her hands flat against surfaces constantly, reading the wood through touch, and her hands have become instruments as calibrated as anything on that workbench. She'll place one palm against the factory wall and stand still for ten seconds, and what she's doing is listening — feeling the growth-pressure behind the wall, the rate of expansion, whether the root-mass is compressing evenly or developing a knot that will need to be managed. She can feel the difference between healthy growth and stressed growth through six inches of solid wood. She learned this young. Everyone here did, or they couldn't do this work.

Her clothing is practical in a way that doesn't try to be anything else: close-fitting layers of a woven fabric that looks like it was made from processed root-fiber, dyed in muted tones — rust, ochre, the grayish-brown of old bark. Over this she wears a vest or harness with tool-loops and attachment points, the kind of garment that evolves through use rather than design, modified and remodified until every pocket and strap serves a specific function. A pair of heavy gloves is tucked into one loop. A measurement tool — something like a folding rule, but with gradations you don't recognize — is clipped to another. There's a device at her hip that might be a communication instrument or might be a sensor or might be both. It's partly organic, its casing made of shaped wood with metal contacts embedded in it, and a faint green light pulses in its core, slow as breathing.

Her hair is pulled back tight and wrapped in a cloth the color of pale heartwood, which is a universal gesture of people who work near moving machinery.

She rinses out the mug. Pours something from a flask — hot, steaming slightly, smelling of tannin and something floral. She drinks standing up, studying the factory floor with the evaluative gaze of someone running calculations. She doesn't seem surprised by the state of things. The unfinished floor, the non-operational machines, the gap between what's installed and what's planned — these are items on a list she carries in her head, ordered by priority, and she's deciding what to tackle first. She finishes her drink. She puts the mug down in the same spot as before. She picks up a chisel and a mallet and walks to the section of wall where the root-growth has been advancing since yesterday, and she begins to shape it.

---

Her name, or the sound that functions as her name in the language you heard through the roots, has three syllables and a tonal shift in the middle that you'd need coaching to reproduce. She is what you might call a senior engineer, or a foreman, or a root-shaper, or an architect — the role doesn't map cleanly onto anything you have a single word for. She shapes the living structure. She also installs the machines. She also maintains the light-routing system. She also manages the construction beetles, who are technically a domesticated species but who also have their own opinions and sometimes need to be redirected when their instinct to follow wood-grain conflicts with the engineering plan. She also trains apprentices, when there are apprentices to train, which lately there aren't enough of, which is a problem she thinks about in the same way she thinks about the wall that's growing faster than she can shape it — with measured concern, not panic, because panic is not useful and the work doesn't stop.

She has been here for what she would describe — if you could ask, if the language barrier weren't a gulf rather than a gap — as her whole professional life. She came from the surface. They all came from the surface, originally. There is a society up there, above the root-ceiling, in the forest that is also the roof of this place. She doesn't talk about it much, and when the others mention it there's a complicated pause afterward, the kind of silence that means a decision was made, a break was chosen, a direction was committed to. They are not exiles. They were not banished. They chose this — chose the downward expansion, chose to ride the root-growth into the earth instead of building upward into canopy. It was a philosophical position before it became a practical one, and now it's simply where they live and what they do, and the philosophical argument has become so embedded in the daily work that it's invisible, the way a founding ideology becomes invisible inside the institutions it creates.

The argument, as near as you could reconstruct it, was about direction. About where a civilization should grow when the surface is full. The forest above is ancient and it is occupied and its canopy is claimed and its floor is settled, and at some point — a generation ago? two? the timescale is hard to pin because they measure time partly by root-growth cycles and you don't have the conversion factor — someone proposed that the roots offered a frontier. That the organism itself was already expanding downward. That a partnership was possible: the forest needed its roots to grow deeper to reach water tables that were dropping, and a certain kind of person needed somewhere to go. The proposal was not universally popular. The pause in conversation when the surface is mentioned suggests that the unpopularity was personal, specific, came from people whose opinions mattered to them. Family, maybe. Colleagues. A community that thought the root-engineers were doing something between foolish and sacrilegious — either wasting their lives in a hole or violating the organism they were supposed to revere.

They did it anyway. They climbed down. They carved the first nodes by hand, trained the first root-paths, figured out through trial and years of error how to read the growth-patterns well enough to work with them instead of against them. They learned that the forest would tolerate them if they respected its needs — if they kept the internal spaces dry so the roots wouldn't rot, if they never severed a living conduit without redirecting its flow, if they gave back in services what they took in space. The light-routing system was their first gift to the organism: by channeling light deeper, they enabled photosynthetic activity in root-layers that had been purely dependent on stored sugars, and the forest responded by growing faster, stronger, *toward* the light-channels, which meant the engineers could guide its growth with increasing precision. A feedback loop. A symbiosis that deepened as it proved itself. The forest is not conscious — they don't claim it's conscious — but it *responds*, and its responses are consistent enough to plan around, and planning around a consistent response is, functionally, a kind of cooperation.

---

You'll see more of them as the shift begins.

They come in through the eastern passages, where the air is currently being drawn in, so they arrive with the breeze at their backs and the faint scent of deeper earth on their clothes. There are eleven of them in this shift-group. Twelve if you count the one who's already been working for an hour, which they do — she's their lead, and she starts early because the wall won't wait.

They vary. This is important. They are not a monoculture, not a uniform population, and the differences between them are not trivial.

There's a pair — they move as a pair, work as a pair, communicate in a pared-down shorthand that skips every unnecessary word — who specialize in the mechanical infrastructure. They are the ones who installed the lathes and the presses and the smelting furnace, who designed the root-chimney exhaust system, who maintain the generators that hum in the city's deeper levels. They are younger than the lead engineer by perhaps ten years, and they have the particular confidence of people who are very good at what they do and are acutely aware that their skills are irreplaceable in a community this small. One of them — taller, quieter, with a scar across the back of one hand that looks like a burn from molten metal — carries a notebook everywhere, a thick block of root-paper bound in bark-board, filled with diagrams and calculations in a precise, tiny hand. The other — shorter, louder, with a laugh that carries through the root-structure and arrives in distant nodes as a warm vibration — carries most of their shared knowledge in memory and retrieves it through a process of talking out loud that the notebook-keeper has learned to filter for the relevant parts. They argue constantly. The arguments are productive. The infrastructure works.

There's an older person — older than the lead, older than anyone else in the shift — who moves more slowly and speaks less and is deferred to in a way that isn't hierarchy exactly but more like the deference you show a load-bearing wall. They were part of the first descent. Their body carries it: joints thickened, hands permanently curved into the gripping shape of someone who spent years climbing living root-faces without harnesses, a deep rasp in their breathing that might be from decades of inhaling cambium-dust before someone thought to install air filtration. They don't do heavy work anymore. What they do is *consult*. They stand where a decision needs to be made — where two root-growth paths are converging and someone has to choose which one to encourage and which to redirect — and they look, and they touch the wood, and they think, and then they say something brief, and the others do what they suggest. Not because of authority. Because the older engineer has been wrong less often than anyone else, over a span of time that's earned a deep, specific trust.

There are five who form the core labor force — the shapers, the ones who do the physical work of chiseling and guiding and installing. They are strong in the way that people are strong when their work demands it daily: dense-muscled, efficient in their movements, carrying their tools the way an extension of their bodies. They range in age from what you'd guess is early twenties to late forties, though the conducted light and the strangeness of their features make age-estimation unreliable. They have inside jokes. They have grievances — one of them is clearly annoyed about something, a scheduling conflict or a resource allocation, and is expressing this through the controlled banging of a mallet that is slightly harder than the job requires. They have a ritual for the start of shift: each one places a hand on the nearest root-pillar, stands still for a five-count, feels the state of the city. They share a look afterward. Today the look says *normal — growth on track — nothing urgent.* They begin.

And there are two who don't fit neatly into any category, and these two are the ones who would interest you most if you could follow them, which you can't, because they move fast and they move into places you haven't found yet.

One of them works with the beetles. She is young — the youngest in the shift — and she carries a bag of something that she feeds to the beetles in careful portions, and she speaks to them in a subsonic hum that they respond to by changing their chewing patterns. She is, you realize, a *programmer*. The beetles are biological machines, and she programs them through acoustic signals and chemical cues, adjusting their behavior to match the engineering plan. The chalk marks on the walls are her notation: a coding language written in mineral pigment on living wood, translated through beetle-song into architectural form. She is extraordinary at this. She is also clearly exhausted, because she is the only beetle-programmer in the settlement and the beetles work around the clock and she does not, and the gap between her capacity and the beetles' appetite for instruction is a constant, grinding problem. She has been trying to train the beetles to follow more complex chalk-marks autonomously, to reduce their need for her real-time guidance. It's working. Slowly. The beetles are smart but they are not flexible, and every new pattern has to be introduced through repetition until it becomes instinct. She sleeps in three-hour intervals and dreams in hexagonal tessellations.

The other one — the one you'll have the hardest time tracking — works with the forest itself. Not with the roots in the city. With the *organism*. He goes up, through vertical shafts that spiral along the inside of the largest root-pillars, climbing with the same eyeless confidence as the pack-lizards, ascending into the zone where the root-city meets the living forest floor and then beyond, into the capillary root-networks that interface with soil and mycorrhizal webs and the incomprehensibly vast biochemical conversation that a forest conducts with itself underground. He carries instruments — the tuning-fork device from the workbench is one of his — and he takes readings, measurements, samples. He is monitoring the health of the partnership. He is the one who would know first if the forest was stressed, if the root-growth was slowing, if the organism was beginning to reject the city the way a body rejects an implant. He is the one who adjusts the light-routing to optimize the forest's photosynthetic return on the space it's lending them. He is, in a sense, the ambassador. The interpreter. He speaks forest the way the beetle-programmer speaks beetle, through patience and observation and a willingness to be wrong repeatedly until the patterns resolve.

He is also the one who worries most. The others worry about schedules and resources and the growth-rate of walls and the maintenance of generators. He worries about the relationship. About whether the balance is holding. About whether what they're doing is truly symbiotic or whether they're parasites who've built an elaborate justification. He has data that mostly reassures him and occasionally doesn't, and the occasions when it doesn't are what keep him climbing, keep him measuring, keep him pressing his palms against the wood in places the others never go and asking, with his hands and his instruments and his gut, *are we still welcome here? Are we still doing this right? Is this still working?*

He doesn't know the answer absolutely. He knows it provisionally. He knows it for today.

---

There are other shifts. Other sections of the city. Other specializations you haven't seen — the people who manage the water-exclusion systems, the ones who process root-fiber into textiles and building materials, the ones who maintain contact with the surface through whatever fraught and complicated diplomatic channel exists between the root-engineers and the forest-dwellers above. There are children — you'll find evidence of them in a node that's been fitted out as a school or a nursery, with small chairs made of shaped root-wood and a wall covered in chalk drawings that include both technical diagrams and the unmistakable scribbles of someone too young to hold the chalk properly. The children are being raised in this. They will know the root-city the way the first generation knows the surface: as home, as given, as the way things are. They will be better at this than their parents. They will also take it more for granted, which is both the hope and the fear.

The settlement numbers — you won't get an exact count, but the scheduling board, once you learn to read it, suggests a total population somewhere between eighty and a hundred and twenty. A village, really. A village that occupies a city-sized space and is building infrastructure for a city-sized population that doesn't exist yet. They are building for the future. They are building on faith — not religious faith, but the structural faith of people who plant trees they won't live to sit under, who lay foundations for buildings their grandchildren will complete. The root-city will take generations. They know this. They knew it when they climbed down. The first generation accepted that they would live and die in an unfinished place, that their daily work would be a contribution to something they'd never see completed, that the most they could hope for was to leave the next generation a little more room, a little more light, a little more of the infrastructure needed to keep going.

This is who lives here. People who chose to build downward into the body of something alive, something patient, something that grows at its own pace and will not be rushed, and who matched their ambitions to that pace and learned to find satisfaction in increments. The wall that was raw this morning will be smooth by evening. The factory floor will be finished by next week. The beetle-programmer will sleep tonight and the beetles will continue shaping the corridor she chalked for them, and when she wakes it will be closer to right, and she'll correct what they got wrong, and they'll learn, and next time they'll get more of it right, and next time more. The forest will keep growing. The engineers will keep following it down.

The ambassador will climb again tomorrow. He'll press his hands against the wood in the deep places. He'll take his readings. He'll come back down and report that the forest is growing well, that the light-routing is generating a net positive energy return, that the water-exclusion systems are maintaining the dry interior without impeding the root-system's hydraulic function, that the partnership is holding. He'll say this with the careful precision of someone who knows that the day he can't say it is the day everything changes. And then he'll eat something, and sleep for a few hours in a small node he's furnished with a hammock slung between two root-pillars, and the roots will grow a fraction of a millimeter around the hammock-hooks while he sleeps, and the city will be imperceptibly larger when he wakes, and he'll climb again, and ask again, and the forest will answer in the only language it has — by growing, by continuing, by making room, by holding — and that will be enough. That will be enough for today.

---

The mug on the workbench is the truest artifact of this place. The way it sits exactly where it was put down, in the confident knowledge that it will be needed again soon. The way the dried residue in the bottom implies a routine so established it doesn't need to be thought about. The way it is a small, human, unremarkable object in the middle of something vast and strange and ongoing — a person's cup in the belly of a living cathedral that is also a construction site that is also a home that is also an act of faith sustained across generations by people who looked at the roots going down and thought: *yes. We can follow that. We can work with that. We can make something there.*

They are still making it. They will be making it for a long time. The roots are patient and so are they, and the city grows, and the beetles chew, and the light comes down through the wood like a promise that the surface hasn't forgotten them, and they drink their tannin-and-flower tea from their mugs and put the mugs down and pick up their tools and continue.

This is what lives here. Not a finished people in a finished place, but a becoming people in a becoming place, and the two becomings are intertwined like roots, like infrastructure, like the lives of anyone who has ever committed to something larger than themselves and learned to measure progress in millimeters and years.
