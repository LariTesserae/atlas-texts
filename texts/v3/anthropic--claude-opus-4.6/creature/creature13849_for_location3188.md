# Who Lives on Grellam Shelf

---

They are called the **tenders**, though no one appointed them and nothing is written down that says they must tend anything. The word is old — older than any living community on the Shelf — and it persists the way threadwire persists: by fragmentation, by finding a crack, by growing.

A tender is anyone who lives here. That is the only qualification.

---

## The Middle-Tier Communities

The largest settlement on Grellam Shelf is called **Condensory**, and it sits on Tiers 61 through 64, in a zone where the drainage channels converge into something almost generous — a slow, persistent seep of clean-enough moisture that pools in a sequence of basins someone cut into the floor-plates a very long time ago. Three hundred and twelve people live in Condensory, give or take. The give-or-take matters because the census is taken by a woman named **Uleth Groan**, who is seventy-one years old and walks with a cane made from a fused pipe segment, and Uleth counts only the people she can see on the day she decides to count. If you are out scavenging, you are not counted. If you are sleeping in a place Uleth does not visit, you are not counted. If you died last week but Uleth hasn't heard, you are counted. The census is therefore not a measure of population but of Uleth's willingness to climb stairs, which varies.

The people of Condensory live in chambers that were not designed for habitation. They were designed for — no one knows. Storage, perhaps. Processing. The rooms are large, high-ceilinged, floored in the same dark alloy as everywhere else, and they have features that suggest industrial purpose: mounting brackets on the walls where machines were once bolted, drainage grates, heavy-duty electrical conduits that still carry faint current. The tenders have made these rooms livable in the way that barnacles make a pier livable: by accumulation, by accretion, by the patient layering of small decisions over time.

The walls are hung with insulation sheeting — peeled from decommissioned ductwork and softened by years of handling until it has the texture of stiff felt. Sleeping platforms are built from stacked floor-grates, cushioned with mats of dried threadwire that have been beaten until they lose their springiness and become something almost comfortable, something that holds your warmth and gives it back. Light, of course, is never a problem. The problem is the opposite: how to create darkness for sleeping. The answer is hoods — deep cowls sewn from layered insulation fabric, pulled down over the face, cinched at the neck. Every tender in Condensory owns at least one sleeping hood, and most own several in various states of repair. The hoods give people a characteristic silhouette when they sleep: rows of faceless, hooded figures lying on their platforms, motionless, like cargo.

When they are awake, they look like people anywhere, almost. They are thin, most of them. Not starving — no one starves on Grellam Shelf, which is itself a mystery worth pausing on — but lean in a way that suggests their food provides exactly enough and no surplus. Their skin tends toward pallor, washed out by the relentless brightness, though there is variation: some families run darker, a deep olive or umber that holds up better under the light, and these people are considered, without anyone saying so explicitly, to be slightly fortunate. Eyes are universally adapted. Every tender on the Shelf has irises that contract to near-pinpoints in the open tiers and dilate enormously in the rare shaded spaces, and this gives their faces a quality of constant adjustment, constant recalibration, as though they are perpetually arriving somewhere and letting their vision settle.

Their hands are remarkable. Everyone's hands are remarkable. The hands of a Grellam Shelf tender are calloused in specific patterns that tell you what they do: the ridge along the outside of the palm from gripping pipe wrenches, the smooth-rubbed fingertips from testing surfaces for current, the tiny scars across the knuckles from working in tight junction boxes where the edges of metal casings are sharp and unforgiving. Children's hands begin to develop these marks by age six or seven. By adulthood, a tender's hands are a map of their trade, legible to anyone who knows how to read them.

## What They Eat

This is where it becomes strange. Stranger.

The food of Grellam Shelf comes from the **cakeries** — shallow trays, usually about two feet by three feet, made from salvaged panel sections, filled with a slurry of condensation water, cable mite castings, and a powderedite substance scraped from the inner walls of certain older pipes on the deep tiers. This slurry, left in the warmth near the floor-level heating conduits, undergoes a slow transformation over the course of several weeks. It thickens. It develops a skin. It becomes — not bread, not cheese, not anything with a culinary analog — a dense, faintly sweet, chalk-textured cake that can be sliced and eaten, and that provides, improbably, complete nutrition.

No one invented the cakeries. Or rather, no one alive knows who invented them. The process is transmitted from parent to child with the solemnity of sacred knowledge, though it is not sacred and no one treats it as such. It is simply essential. The proportions matter: too much mite casting and the cake becomes bitter and causes stomach cramps; too little pipe-powder and it fails to set, remaining a thin gruel that nourishes poorly. The best cakemakers — and they are respected, these people, given the best sleeping platforms and consulted on community decisions — have an intuitive sense for the slurry that borders on artistry. They can smell when a batch is ready. They can feel the texture between their fingers and know whether to add more water or let it thicken another day.

The cakes taste like almost nothing. A faint sweetness, a mineral undertone, a dry chalkiness that coats the tongue. Tenders eat them the way you might eat bread if bread were the only food that existed: without complaint, without enthusiasm, steadily, two or three times a day, washed down with condensation water that has been filtered through layers of threadwire matting to remove the oily residue. The water tastes of copper. Everything here tastes of copper, faintly, always.

Some communities supplement the cakes with gutter eel, which can be smoked over electrical heating coils until it becomes a translucent, jerky-like strip with a briny, metallic flavor. Rafter birds are never eaten. This is not a rule anyone enforces. It is simply understood. The birds are — not sacred, the tenders don't have a concept of sacred that maps neatly onto anything you'd recognize — but they are *fellow*. They are the other warm-blooded things that live here. They are company. To eat one would be to admit something about the nature of this place that most tenders prefer not to articulate.

## The Children

Children are born in the Works the way children are born anywhere: in pain, in hope, in the uncertain hours when the mother's body decides it is time. There are midwives — every community has at least two, and they are trained with a rigor that exceeds any other apprenticeship on the Shelf. A midwife's hands are the softest on the Shelf, deliberately maintained: they soak their fingers in warm condensation water, rub them with rendered gutter eel fat, avoid the rough valve-work that would build callouses. A midwife's hands must be able to feel everything.

The children grow up in brightness. They have never known darkness except as a concept inside their sleeping hoods, and even that is not true darkness — the light of the Works bleeds through the insulation fabric, reduced to a dim amber glow, so that a child's first and most fundamental experience of the world is: *light, always, and warmth that comes from below*.

They play in the corridors. Their games are the games of the environment: hide-and-seek among the pipe bundles (where there are a limited number of hiding spots and the game becomes more about stillness than concealment), racing along the catwalks (dangerous, forbidden, universal), and a complex game called **routing** in which one child names two points on the tier and the others compete to find the fastest path between them. Routing is a game but it is also training. The spatial intelligence required to navigate the Works — to remember which staircases connect to which tiers, to know which corridors fold and which run true, to feel in your body when a seam has shifted — is developed in childhood or not at all. Adults who arrive on the Shelf from elsewhere (and they do arrive, occasionally, though the question of *from where* is as unanswerable as the question of what the Works are built on) never develop the instinct fully. They get lost in corridors that children navigate without thinking. They walk into folds and come out weeping with disorientation. They stand at the edge of a tier and look out across the two-mile gap to the next column and feel a vertigo that native-born tenders simply do not experience, because native-born tenders understand, at a level below language, that the gap is not a void but a *room* — the largest room in the Works, a room made of air and light, and you can no more fall out of it than you can fall out of your own kitchen.

The children are not afraid of the Works. This is important. The adults develop fears — rational, carefully managed fears, filed away in the three-hundred-term vocabulary of spatial anomalies, consulted like reference material when planning a route or choosing where to sleep. But the children treat the Works the way children anywhere treat their home: as a given, as the ground-state of reality, as the way things are. A corridor that connects to itself is not a topological impossibility to a child who has been running through it since she could walk. It is a loop. It is fun. You can run and run and never stop and always be going somewhere even though you are going nowhere. A bloom is not a terrifying dissolution of three-dimensional space to a child who experiences one during a game of routing. It is a cheat code. It is a way of seeing where your friends are hiding without looking around corners. The child laughs. The child's parent, watching from down the corridor, does not laugh. The parent's hands tighten on the railing.

## The Trades

A tender must work because there is always something that needs doing, and the something is always, in one way or another, **maintenance**.

The Works maintain themselves — mostly. The light-channels burn. The elevators run. The condensation cycles. But the margin between functional and non-functional is thin, and it is maintained by human hands, and the people of Grellam Shelf have organized their lives around the specific forms of labor the Works require.

**Pipe-walkers** are the most common trade. A pipe-walker's job is to walk a designated route — usually several miles of corridor on a single tier — and inspect every visible pipe, valve, and junction for signs of failure: leaks, corrosion, blockages, the slow swelling that indicates a pressure buildup somewhere deep in the system. A pipe-walker carries a tool roll containing wrenches, sealant paste (made from a mixture of cable mite castings and a thermosetting resin harvested from certain very old junction boxes), and a sounding rod — a thin metal dowel that, when pressed against a pipe and struck with a small hammer, produces a tone that tells the trained ear whether the pipe is flowing, static, blocked, or — and this is the most feared diagnosis — *singing*. A singing pipe is one that has developed a resonance with some deeper harmonic of the Works, a vibration that, left unchecked, will eventually shake the pipe apart at its joints. Singing pipes must be damped immediately, and the process is delicate: wrap the affected section in threadwire matting, apply sealant at the nodes, monitor for recurrence. A good pipe-walker can hear a singing pipe from thirty meters away. A great one can hear it through the floor of the tier above.

**Routefinders** are rarer and stranger. A routefinder's job is to maintain the community's knowledge of the local topology — to walk the corridors and staircases daily, noting which paths still lead where they led yesterday, which folds have shifted, where new seams have appeared. Routefinders carry nothing but a memory and a piece of soft chalk for marking walls. They work in pairs, always, because a routefinder who enters a fold alone may not come out on the same tier, or may come out on the same tier but in a different century of construction, and will need their partner to anchor them — to stand at the corridor's entrance and call their name, which serves as a kind of acoustic tether, a reminder that there is a fixed point in the topology and it is here, it is now, it is the sound of a human voice saying your name.

Routefinder pairs bond deeply. They spend more time together than most married couples, and the relationship has a quality of absolute trust that is specific and non-transferable. When a routefinder dies, their partner retires. Always. There is no debate about this. The knowledge they shared was held between them, in the space between two bodies walking the same corridor, and it cannot be transferred to a third person any more than you can teach someone the inside of your own skull.

**Wirespinners** work the threadwire. They are harvesters and cultivators both — trimming the growth from corridors where it impedes foot traffic, transplanting fragments to areas where the community wants it to spread (the matting is useful for insulation, for water filtration, for sleeping pallets, for a hundred small domestic purposes). Wirespinners have the greenest hands on the Shelf, stained verdigris by constant contact with the threadwire's surface, and they carry the faint ozone smell of the material in their clothes, their hair, their skin. They are said to hear the threadwire growing, which is impossible — the growth rate is far too slow to produce audible sound — but wirespinners will press their ears against a wall where threadwire is emerging from a seam and listen, and what they hear, or say they hear, informs their decisions about where to cultivate and where to cut. They are gentle people, mostly. The work selects for patience.

**Deepers** are feared.

A deeper is someone who goes down. Below the habitable middle tiers, into the old construction, the silent zones, the places where the alloys change and the light-channels become unreliable and the condensation runs thicker and warmer than it should. Deepers go down to scavenge: pipe-powder for the cakeries, rare components for repair work, the thermosetting resin, and occasionally — rarely, and never spoken about openly — **information**. The deep tiers contain markings. Not the illegible labels that appear throughout the Works, but structured markings: diagrams etched into wall panels, sequences of symbols that repeat at intervals that suggest intentionality, and, in the very deepest zones anyone has reached and returned from, what appear to be *maps*.

Maps of what is unclear. The diagrams do not correspond to any known section of the Works. They may depict tiers that no longer exist, or tiers that exist only intermittently, or tiers that exist in directions that the three-hundred-term vocabulary has not yet found words for. Deepers copy these markings onto sheets of insulation fabric and bring them back to the communities, where they are studied and debated and stored in archives that every community maintains with obsessive care, even though no one has ever extracted actionable information from them. The archives grow. The markings accumulate. Someday, perhaps, someone will see the pattern.

Deepers work alone. This is not a tradition; it is a practical necessity. The deep tiers do not accommodate groups well. Corridors narrow. The topology becomes aggressively non-Euclidean — not the gentle folds and seams of the middle tiers, but acute distortions, spaces where the floor curves upward to become a wall without any transition, where a room has five corners, where a staircase descends for a hundred steps and delivers you to the same landing you left. Groups become separated in these conditions. Separated people become lost. Lost people become dead, or — and this is what the deepers will not discuss, not even with each other, not even drunk on the sharp fermented condensation-water that some communities produce — they become something else. They become part of the infrastructure. Absorbed. Incorporated. There are shapes in the deep tiers that look like human figures frozen mid-step, rendered in the same dark alloy as the floor-plates, and the official position of every community on the Shelf is that these are sculptures, decorative elements, remnants of whatever aesthetic program the builders of the Works may have had. And perhaps they are. And perhaps the deepers know differently, and that is why deepers have a particular expression — a stillness around the eyes, a way of looking at you that is also a way of looking *through* you, as though checking whether you are solid all the way through or whether some part of you has already begun to merge with the wall behind you.

Deepers are respected. Deepers are necessary. Deepers are not invited to dinner.

## The Elders and the Archive-Keepers

Every community has its old people, and the old people of Grellam Shelf are peculiar in a specific way: they remember less than they should, and more.

A tender who has lived sixty, seventy, eighty years on the Shelf (and they do live long — the cake provides, the condensation water sustains, the cool constant temperature is easy on aging bodies, and the diseases that kill people in other places do not seem to exist here, as though the pathogens too were never installed) — an elder tender has spent a lifetime navigating a space that does not obey consistent rules. Corridors they walked as children no longer exist. Tiers they remember visiting as young adults are now inaccessible, sealed off by seam-shifts or simply *gone*, replaced by construction they do not recognize. Their personal history is mapped onto a geography that has changed under them, slowly, patiently, like a river changing its course, and the result is a memory that is spatially unmoored. An elder may describe in vivid detail the room where they were born — the particular arrangement of pipes on the ceiling, the sound of the nearest valve, the temperature of the floor — and a routefinder, listening, may realize that the room being described cannot exist. Not that it has been demolished or repurposed, but that the spatial coordinates the elder remembers — Tier 88, third corridor south of the main stairwell, second door on the left — resolve to a location that is inside a solid wall, or in the middle of an inter-tier gap, or at a point that is both Tier 88 and Tier 203 simultaneously because a seam has been sitting there, stable and patient, for decades.

The elders are not confused. They are accurate. The Works have simply moved around them.

And so the elders remember things that the Works themselves no longer contain, and this makes them invaluable. The archive-keepers — a loose guild of men and women whose trade is the maintenance of community memory — spend hours with the elders, recording their descriptions of rooms and routes and the layouts of tiers that have since shifted. These descriptions are added to the archives alongside the deepers' copied markings, and together they form a body of knowledge that is vast, contradictory, and possibly the only true map of the Works that exists: not a map of how the Works *are*, but a map of how the Works *have been*, a palimpsest of every configuration the structure has passed through, layered one atop another like the threadwire mats in the deep-tier corridors.

The archive-keepers believe — and this is as close to religion as anything on the Shelf gets — that if the palimpsest could be read whole, if every configuration could be seen simultaneously, the Works would make sense. The folds, the seams, the blooms — all of it would resolve into a coherent structure, a geometry that human minds are simply not equipped to perceive in three dimensions but that exists, complete and elegant, in some higher-dimensional space that the Works merely intersect. The archive-keepers call this hypothetical totality the **Full Reckoning**, and they work toward it with the same patient, slow persistence as everything else on the Shelf, knowing that they will not live to see it, that their children will not live to see it, that the Full Reckoning may require not just more data but a different kind of mind — one that can hold contradictory spatial configurations simultaneously without collapsing into the desperate either/or of Euclidean thinking.

Some archive-keepers seek out blooms deliberately, hoping that the temporary dissolution of three-dimensional perception will give them a glimpse of the Full Reckoning. They stand in corridors known for bloom activity and wait, sometimes for weeks, their eyes open, their minds as receptive as they can make them. When a bloom occurs, they try to hold it — to resist the brain's panicked retreat into normalcy — and they report, afterward, that they saw... something. Not the Full Reckoning. Not the whole. But a corner of it. An edge. The way you might see the edge of a vast painting if you pressed your eye against the canvas: color, texture, the suggestion of a pattern that extends beyond your field of vision in every direction.

They come back from these experiences changed. Quieter. More careful with their hands, as though the objects they touch might be more complex than they appear. They run their fingers over surfaces — walls, pipes, the faces of sleeping children — with an attention that suggests they are reading something written in a language of texture and temperature and the faint electrical tingle that all surfaces in the Works carry. They say less. They listen more. They spend long hours in the archives, paging through records of configurations that no longer exist, and sometimes they smile.

## The Ones Who Leave

Not many. Not often. But it happens.

A tender — usually young, usually after their first bloom, usually after standing too long at the edge of a tier looking out across the gap — will decide to go. Not down, like the deepers. *Out*. Across. To the next column, the one that glitters two miles distant in the thickened light.

The gap between columns is not empty. It is full of air and light and thermal currents and rafter birds, but it contains no walkway, no bridge, no cable car, no means of crossing that the Works have provided. The gap is there. It has always been there. It is part of the architecture as surely as the tiers and the pipes and the threadwire, and its purpose, if it has one, is to separate.

But people cross it.

They build **gliders** — lightweight frames of pipe and threadwire matting, shaped through trial and error and inherited design knowledge into broad, shallow wings that can catch the thermal updrafts between columns. The gliders are beautiful, in the way that all things made with extreme care from inadequate materials are beautiful. They are fragile. They are tested by launching them unmanned from the tier's edge, weighted with sacks of cable mite castings, and watching them either soar or spiral downward into the abyss between columns, dwindling to specks and then to nothing. The success rate is — well, the success rate for unmanned flights is perhaps one in three. For manned flights, the community does not keep records. This is deliberate. Keeping records would require acknowledging the failures, and the failures are — they are down there, somewhere, in the brightness that thickens below, and thinking about them too carefully is not productive.

The ones who launch do so at what they call the **good thermals** — times when the updrafts from below are strongest, when the rafter birds are circling in their largest, most confident spirals, when the air between columns seems to hum with a lifting energy that you can feel in your chest. They strap themselves into the glider. They check the threadwire lashings one last time. Someone — a parent, a partner, a routefinder companion — says their name. Not as a farewell. As an anchor. Here. Now. This is where you are leaving from. Remember it.

And they step off.

The gliders catch the thermal or they don't. The crossing takes, survivors report, about forty minutes — forty minutes of hanging in a cage of pipe and threadwire in the golden-white air, the column you left receding behind you, the column ahead growing with agonizing slowness, the rafter birds wheeling around you with what might be curiosity or might be nothing at all, their mechanical clicks the only sound besides the wind that isn't wind, the air that stirs, that is all.

Those who arrive at the far column find — and this is the thing that the archive-keepers find most significant, the thing they have built entire wings of the archive around — that the far column is identical. Not similar. Identical. The same tiers, the same pipe configurations, the same threadwire growth patterns, the same folds and seams in the same locations. Even the people are the same. Not the same individuals — different faces, different names — but the same communities, in the same locations, living the same lives, eating the same cake, sleeping under the same hoods. As though the columns are not separate structures but reflections of a single structure, repeated across the gap, or as though the gap is not a gap at all but a fold — the largest fold in the Works, a fold so vast that it encompasses miles of open air and still connects one place to itself.

The crossers return, usually, within a few months. They come back quieter, like the archive-keepers after a bloom. They do not talk much about what they found. When pressed, they say: *It was the same.* And then, after a pause: *But I wasn't.*

## The Night-Makers

There is one more group, and they are the strangest.

In every community, there are a few tenders — never more than a handful — who have dedicated themselves to the project of creating darkness. True darkness. Not the filtered amber glow inside a sleeping hood, but the absolute absence of light that no one on the Shelf has ever experienced and that the Works, in their relentless luminosity, seem designed to prevent.

The night-makers work in sealed chambers deep in the interior of each tier, as far from the light-channels as it is possible to get. They layer the walls with insulation sheeting — dozens of layers, each one carefully checked for gaps and pin-holes, each one sealed to the next with thermosetting resin. They block ducts. They cap conduits. They seal every seam where light might creep in, working with a perfectionism that borders on obsession, because light in the Works is patient and persistent and will find any opening, any crack, any imperfection in the barrier, and exploit it.

It takes months to prepare a darkroom. When it is done — when every layer is in place, every seal checked, every possible ingress of light blocked — the night-maker enters, and the door is sealed behind them.

What happens inside is not discussed with outsiders. The night-makers have their own vocabulary, separate from the three hundred terms, and they do not share it. What is known is this: they stay inside for periods ranging from a few hours to several days. They emerge changed — not in the dramatic, visible way that deepers are changed, but in a subtle recalibration of attention. A night-maker who has spent three days in true darkness moves through the Works afterward with a quality of *presence* that is almost tactile, as though they have become more solid, more fully located in the space they occupy. They are not disoriented. They are the opposite of disoriented. They seem to know exactly where they are, not just on the tier-and-corridor level but in some deeper, more fundamental sense, as though the darkness gave them access to a mode of perception that the relentless light obscures.

The night-makers say — when they say anything — that in the darkness, the Works speak. Not in words. Not in sounds. In something felt through the skin, through the bones, through the soles of the feet pressed against the alloy floor-plate. The Works tell you where you are. Not your tier number, not your corridor designation, but your *location* — your actual position in the totality of the structure, your relationship to every other point, every fold, every seam, every bloom. In the darkness, the night-makers say, the Full Reckoning is not a theory. It is a sensation. It is the feeling of the Works breathing around you, in, out, and knowing — not believing, not hoping, *knowing* — that you are inside something alive.

Not alive the way the threadwire is alive, or the cable mites, or the rafter birds. Alive the way a thought is alive. A thought that someone had, a very long time ago, and that did not stop being thought. A thought that became so detailed, so elaborated, so structurally complex, that it accreted matter around itself the way a pearl accretes around a grain of sand, and the matter became the Works, and the thought is still being thought, and the tenders are living inside it, and the thought does not know they are there, or does not care, or — and this is what the night-makers say when they have been in the darkness longest, when they come out with that look of absolute location in their eyes — the thought knows they are there and is glad.

The Works are glad.

Not in the way that a person is glad. In the way that a place is glad when it is inhabited. In the way that a house is glad when someone comes home.

## And So

They live here. Three hundred and twelve of them, give or take, on Grellam Shelf alone, and similar numbers on a hundred other shelves in a hundred other columns stretching out into the thickened light. They eat their chalk-flavored cake and drink their copper-flavored water and sleep under their hoods in the permanent brightness. They walk the pipes and find the routes and spin the wire and descend into the deep tiers and copy the markings and build the archives and seek the blooms and make the darkness and step off the edge into the golden air.

They are ordinary people. That is the thing that is hardest to hold onto, standing here on the platform, watching them move through the corridors of a structure that should not exist in a space that does not behave. They argue about food preparation. They fall in love with people who do not love them back. They worry about their children. They develop lower back pain from sleeping on threadwire mats. They have opinions about the best way to seal a singing pipe. They tell jokes — dry, quiet jokes, delivered with the same understated patience as everything else in the Works, jokes whose punchlines often hinge on the three-hundred-term vocabulary and are therefore untranslatable.

They do not wonder, most of them, most of the time, who built the Works or why. The question exists. The archives contain thousands of pages of speculation. But it is not a question that most tenders carry with them through their days, any more than you carry with you, through your days, the question of who built the Earth. The Works are here. They have always been here. They will, as far as anyone can project, always be here. The question of origin is interesting in the way that a decorative frieze is interesting: you might admire it, study it, discuss it over a meal. But you do not stand in front of it every morning and demand that it explain itself before you agree to eat breakfast.

They are at home. That is the final, essential thing. The Works are vast and strange and topologically unreliable and haunted by things in the deep tiers that no one can name, and the tenders are at home here the way the threadwire is at home, the way the cable mites are at home, the way the rafter birds are at home: completely, unquestioningly, with the full weight of their living pressed against the alloy surfaces of a place that breathes beneath them, patient as geology, slow as wire reaching toward light.

The elevator opens.

A tender — young, eighteen maybe, with verdigris-stained hands and the oversized eyes of someone born in the brightness — steps out, carrying a tray of fresh-cut threadwire matting. She glances at you, not with surprise (there are no strangers on the Shelf; there are only people she hasn't counted yet) but with a quick, assessing attention. She notes your hands. Uncalloused. Unmarked. She draws a conclusion.

"New tier?" she asks.

And before you can answer, she has already turned away, already walking, already absorbed back into the slow, bright, patient rhythm of the place. Over her shoulder, almost as an afterthought:

"Condensory's three levels up. Take the east stair, not the west — west folds on Tuesdays. Someone'll find you a hood."

She rounds a corner. Her footsteps — and the faint creak of threadwire under her boots — recede.

The elevator behind you closes. The cables hum.

Above you, a rafter bird clicks once, twice, and launches itself into the golden air.

You are home.
