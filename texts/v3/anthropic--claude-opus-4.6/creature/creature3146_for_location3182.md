I sat with this for a long time. What lives here resists the kind of naming that settles things. But let me try.

---

**The Lichen Councils**

They are the oldest inhabitants and they know it in whatever way lichen knows anything — which is to say chemically, territorially, through the slow acid-speech of their expanding margins. The pale green ones arrived first, or at least they claim the deepest stone, the surfaces that have been exposed longest. They are the elders in a parliament that never convenes. The rust-orange colonies came later, or came to different stone, or simply metabolize differently enough to carve a separate niche. And the chalky white ones — those are the diplomats or the scavengers, colonizing the boundaries where the others have fought each other to a standstill, growing in the thin chemical no-man's-land between two exhausted armies.

Each colony is a single organism that is also a marriage — fungus and alga locked in a partnership so old that neither could say who proposed it. They have been here for centuries, some of them, and they will be here for centuries more, and their experience of time is nothing like yours. A decade is a breath. A century is a season. They don't experience the rockfalls as events — they experience them as weather, as the slow reshuffling of available surfaces, new stone appearing where old stone fell, a fresh frontier opening up that will take three human lifetimes to reach. They are engaged in the only war the plateau knows, and it is fought at the speed of mineral dissolution, a few micrometers per year, the front lines invisible without magnification. When two colonies meet, the battle is silent and absolute — one chemistry wins, the other retreats, and the border stabilizes, and both sides continue growing in every other direction as if nothing happened. They are the city's first citizens, its most permanent residents, its record-keepers. If you could read lichen the way you read tree rings, these walls would be a library.

---

**The Seep-Water**

It lives here. I don't mean it passes through. I mean it *lives* here the way blood lives in a body — always moving, never leaving, cycling through the same passages in patterns that shift only slightly from century to century. The seep-water knows the labyrinth better than anything else does. It has tested every crack, every bedding plane, every joint and fault. It has mapped the city from the inside, dissolving its way through the stone's own memory, following weaknesses that were set in place when this rock was still sediment on a sea floor. 

The water has moods, or something functionally indistinguishable from moods. Before a storm, it quickens. The walls weep more freely. The puddles in the bedrock depressions swell by fractions of an inch. The trickles in the passage floors grow bolder, take corners faster, carry more dissolved calcium in their current. It's responding to pressure changes — barometric, hydrostatic — but the effect is that the labyrinth seems to anticipate. It seems to prepare. And after the rain passes and the distributed rushing fades back to silence, the seep-water contracts again, pulls back into the stone, resumes its bead-by-bead patience, and the city enters something like a long exhalation.

The mineral trails it leaves — those pale blooms on the wall faces, those scalloped edges, those gutters worn into the passage floors — are the seep-water's autobiography. Every trail is a record of a specific path taken by a specific quantity of water at a specific moment in geological time. The trails overlap, cross, diverge. They are written in calcium carbonate and silica, and they are illegible to anything except the water itself, which follows its old paths faithfully, deepening the grooves, reinforcing the gutters, committed to routes it chose — or that were chosen for it by the stone's own structure — thousands of years ago. The water is the city's circulatory system and its memory and its most tireless worker. It built the gutters. It carved the drainage nodes. It is, very slowly, dismantling the walls that channel it. The water is the city's architect and its demolition crew, and it doesn't know the difference, because there isn't one.

---

**The Pipits**

Small, dun, quiet. You have to understand how fully they belong here, how completely they have given themselves over to the labyrinth's particular conditions. Their coloring is the color of wet limestone — not camouflage exactly, because nothing here is hunting them with eyes at ground level, but a kind of sympathy, a visual rhyme. They are the same shade as the stone they walk on, and when they go still — which they do, often, freezing mid-step with one foot raised — they simply vanish into the texture of the passage floor.

They live in the crevices, in cracks just wide enough to admit a small body sideways. Their nests are masterworks of economy — moss and grass, yes, but selected with extraordinary specificity. The moss comes from north-facing walls, where it grows densest and dampest. The grass comes from the rubble plazas, the pale silver-tipped kind, which is finer and more pliable than anything else available. The nests are wedged into horizontal cracks where the stone above provides a roof and the stone below provides a shelf, and they are insulated by the plateau's own thermal stability — cool but never cold, damp but never wet, a microclimate so consistent that the eggs hardly need incubation. The birds sit on them anyway, because instinct is older than optimization.

Their calls are the only animal voices in the labyrinth, and they are so thin, so infrequent, that they function less as communication and more as proof of existence — a small bird confirming to itself and perhaps one other that it is still here, still alive, still occupying this particular crack in this particular wall in this particular district of the city. The calls bounce between the walls and arrive at your ears already ghostly, already half-absorbed by the damp stone, and you can never quite tell which direction they came from. The pipits use this. They are ventriloquists by architecture. A predator hearing that call would have to search twenty crevices to find the right one, and by then the bird would be silent again, pressed flat against the back of its crack, the same color as everything around it, invisible, patient, waiting.

They eat the things that live on the wet stone — the small beetles, the pale larvae, whatever minute invertebrate fauna the labyrinth supports. They forage by walking, not hopping. They move through the passages with a brisk, purposeful gait, covering ground, following the trickle-channels where insects are most concentrated, turning corners with the confidence of someone who knows the neighborhood. Each bird has a territory — a few hundred yards of passage, a few intersections, a few good foraging puddles — and it knows every crack and ledge within it. The pipits are the city's pedestrians, and they take their commute seriously.

They are not social. They pair for breeding and then separate, each returning to its own stretch of corridor, its own set of crevices. When two birds meet at a territorial boundary, there is a moment of stillness — both frozen, both watching — and then one turns and walks back the way it came, and the other continues forward, and the negotiation is over. Occasionally, very occasionally, you hear what might be a song — a thin, descending series of notes, four or five, delivered from the top of a rubble boulder at dawn. It doesn't carry far. It doesn't need to. The labyrinth's acoustics do the work. The song enters the corridors and is conducted through them, arriving in neighboring territories as a faint, directionless whisper, just loud enough to mean *I am here, this stretch is spoken for, find your own.*

---

**The Beetles**

Black, or nearly black, small enough that you could miss them entirely if you weren't looking at the stone from close range. They navigate the wet surfaces with an assurance that suggests they have solved the traction problem that troubles everything else — their feet grip the slick stone through some mechanism of fine hairs or adhesive secretion, and they move at a steady, unhurried pace along the wall faces, following routes that parallel the seep-water's mineral trails. They eat lichen, or the microbial film that grows on wet stone, or both. Their mandibles are small but persistent, and if you pressed your ear to the wall — really pressed it, cheek flat against the cold damp limestone — you might hear the faintest scraping, the sound of a beetle's jaws working against a surface that has taken a century to accumulate.

They are solitary. Each beetle moves through its own tiny territory on the wall face, a patch of stone perhaps a foot square, and within that territory it is thorough, methodical, grazing the microbial film down to bare rock and then moving on to the next patch when regrowth is insufficient. They have the patience of the lichen they eat, which is to say a patience incomprehensible to anything warm-blooded. A single beetle might spend an hour on a square inch of stone, working it over, extracting what it can, before moving six inches to the left and beginning again.

They are prey for the pipits, but not easy prey. Their coloring matches the dark wet stone. Their habit of freezing when vibration reaches them — the footfall of a bird, the distant concussion of a rockfall — makes them nearly undetectable. The pipits find them by pattern, not by sight — they know which walls, which patches of lichen, which moisture levels correlate with beetle presence, and they check these spots systematically, pecking at the stone surface and sometimes connecting, sometimes not. It is a relationship as old as the beetles' presence on the plateau, as old as the pipits' tenure here, and it has settled into a stable equilibrium: enough beetles survive to reproduce, enough are eaten to feed the birds, and neither population changes much from year to year.

---

**The Pale Moths**

They shelter in the deepest alcoves during the day — pressed flat against the stone in the back of recesses where the light barely reaches, their wings folded tight, their coloring the exact pale gray of unweathered limestone. They are invisible. Not metaphorically. Literally invisible — you could stare at the back wall of an alcove from two feet away and not see the moth pressed against it, because the moth's wing pattern reproduces the stone's texture with a fidelity that seems excessive, that seems like more camouflage than any predation pressure could demand. But the plateau is old, and evolution has had time to be thorough.

At dusk — or what passes for dusk here, the overcast dimming from pale gray to darker gray, the light in the passages declining by imperceptible degrees — the moths detach from their walls and begin to fly. Their flight is erratic, unpredictable, the classic moth-flutter, but within the labyrinth's narrow passages it takes on a different quality. The walls constrain them. They bounce between the stone faces, their wingbeats echoing faintly, their pale bodies catching what little light remains. They are following scent trails — pheromones laid down by other moths, or the faint chemical signature of the lichen and moss that serve as substrate for whatever they eat (fungal spores, perhaps, or the nectar of some tiny flower too small and too seasonal for the description above to have mentioned).

They lay their eggs in the horizontal cracks where ferns grow, and their larvae — small, pale, caterpillar-like — feed on moss, chewing through the dark green cushions that line the stone joints, leaving tiny bare patches that the moss will reclaim in a season. The larvae pupate in the cracks, emerging as adults in a synchronized pulse that happens — when? Spring? There may not be a sharp spring here. Perhaps they respond to day length, or to a threshold in the seep-water's temperature, or to some atmospheric cue that the plateau transmits through its stone. Whatever the trigger, there are nights when the moths are suddenly numerous, when the dusk passages are full of pale fluttering, and then within a week the numbers diminish as predation and simple mortality thin the cohort, and the labyrinth returns to its usual population of a few moths per alcove, holding still, holding on, waiting for whatever comes next.

---

**The Nocturnal Mammals**

You will not see them. You will see their evidence.

The scat — those tiny dark pellets on the flat tops of rubble boulders — tells you something. The pellets are small, suggesting an animal no bigger than a vole or a shrew. Their placement — always on elevated surfaces, always on the highest point available in a given area — suggests an animal that climbs, that seeks vantage, that wants to see before it is seen, even in the dark. The pellets contain fragments of beetle exoskeleton and moth wing-scale, so the animal is an insectivore, or at least an opportunistic omnivore that includes insects in its diet. There may be plant material too — seeds from the wiry grasses, perhaps, or the tough little fruits of the wall-hugging shrubs.

Here is what you can piece together: small, nocturnal, climbing, insectivorous, solitary. Probably mouse-like, or shrew-like — a small body with a high metabolism that requires constant foraging through the night hours. It moves through the labyrinth with a mental map as detailed as any the water carries — it knows which cracks harbor beetles, which alcoves shelter moths, which rubble piles offer the best vantage points. It knows the passages by feel and by smell, navigating in near-total darkness with whiskers extended, feet gripping the wet stone with the same sureness as the beetles, finding its way through a territory that might encompass several hundred yards of corridor.

It is alone. The scat piles are spaced far apart — hundreds of yards, sometimes — suggesting that each animal maintains a large territory relative to its body size, which in turn suggests that the food supply is thin and evenly distributed, not concentrated enough to support dense populations. Each animal is an island of warm blood in a landscape of cold stone, burning calories at a furious rate just to maintain its body temperature in the perpetual cool of the passages, foraging constantly, eating whatever it can find, sleeping through the daylight hours in some deep crevice where its body heat warms a tiny pocket of air to something approaching comfort.

It has probably been here for a very long time — long enough for its fur to be the color of wet stone, long enough for its ears to be large and precise and attuned to the labyrinth's acoustic peculiarities, long enough for its feet to have developed whatever adaptation lets it grip the slick surfaces. It may be a species found nowhere else. The plateau is an island in the sky, and island populations diverge. Given enough time — and the plateau has nothing but time — the small mammal has become its own thing, distinct from whatever lowland ancestor first climbed the escarpment and found the labyrinth and decided to stay.

It does not know it lives in a city. It knows it lives in a maze of wet stone that provides crevices for sleeping and surfaces for foraging and elevated points for surveying and narrow gaps for escaping whatever might hunt it (the raptors, presumably, though the raptors hunt by day and the mammal moves by night, so the two may rarely meet). The city is, for this creature, simply the shape of the world. It was born in a crack in a wall in a passage, and it will die in a crack in a wall in a passage, and in between it will run through the corridors in the dark with its whiskers brushing both walls, and it will eat and breed and deposit its small dark evidence on the tops of boulders, and the rain will wash the evidence away, and new evidence will appear, and the labyrinth will not notice.

---

**The Raptors**

They live above the city, not in it. Their domain is the air column that sits atop the plateau like an invisible second city — layered, structured by thermals and wind patterns that are shaped by the labyrinth below but exist in a different medium entirely. The raptors ride the updrafts that form where wind hits the plateau's edges and is deflected upward, and they circle there, at the boundary between the plateau's world and the sky's world, watching.

They are large enough to be visible as silhouettes against the overcast — broad-winged, long-tailed, built for soaring rather than speed. Their coloring, seen up close (which you will not see them up close), is probably gray-brown above and pale below, the standard counter-shading of a predator that needs to be invisible both against the sky when seen from below and against the stone when seen from above. They nest on the mesa tops — those flat-topped remnant columns that rise above the labyrinth like watchtowers — building their nests from whatever they can carry: grass, moss, the bones of previous meals, the wind-dried stems of the wall-hugging shrubs. The nests are crude platforms, exposed to the weather, and the chicks that hatch in them are immediately subject to the plateau's full repertoire of rain and wind and cold.

What do they eat? The pipits, almost certainly — dropping into the labyrinth's open corridors in a controlled stoop, seizing a bird from the passage floor before it can reach its crevice. Perhaps the nocturnal mammals too, caught during the brief twilight when the mammals are emerging and the raptors are making their last passes. Perhaps something from the lowlands below — the raptors' range may extend beyond the plateau, down the escarpment to warmer, richer country, where prey is more abundant and the hunting easier. They may treat the plateau as a nesting site and the lowlands as a larder, commuting between the two on the same updrafts they use for surveillance.

From the mesa tops, if you could sit quietly enough, for long enough, you might see one return from a hunt — a dark shape rising on the thermal at the plateau's edge, angling across the labyrinth in a long glide, losing altitude gradually, adjusting for the wind that threads through the passages below, finally flaring and landing on the mesa top with something in its talons. The sound of the landing — a scrape of claws on stone, the rustle of settling wings — would carry down into the passages below, and a pipit somewhere would go silent, and the seep-water would continue beading on the walls, and the lichen would continue its centimeter-per-decade advance across the stone, and the labyrinth would continue being what it is: a city no one built, populated by creatures that did not choose it but have been utterly shaped by it, each one fitted to their crack, their corridor, their stratum of the maze, as precisely as the stone fits against itself.

---

**The Wind**

It lives here too. I am not being poetic. The wind is a resident.

It enters the labyrinth from the plateau's edges, where the escarpment forces it upward and the labyrinth's corridors accept it, channel it, divide it into a thousand streams that flow through the maze along paths determined by the same geometry that channels the water. But while the water follows gravity — always down, always toward the plateau's edges — the wind follows pressure, which shifts constantly, which means the wind's paths through the labyrinth are never the same twice. Today it flows east to west through this corridor. Tomorrow, with a different pressure gradient, it may flow west to east, or not at all, or in confused eddies that circle a single intersection for hours before dissipating.

The wind is the only thing here that has a voice. The hum in the narrow passages. The whistle at the gap between leaning walls. The low, chorded drone that rises when a front moves through and the whole labyrinth becomes an organ played by atmospheric pressure. The wind says nothing — it has nothing to say — but it speaks constantly, and the labyrinth gives it language, gives it pitch and timbre and resonance, gives it the ability to produce sounds that have structure, that have something resembling melody, that change as the wind changes and the passages rearrange themselves through erosion and rockfall and the slow tilting of bedding planes.

The creatures of the labyrinth have learned the wind's voice. The pipits know what it means when a particular passage begins to hum at a particular pitch — it means the wind has shifted, which means the weather is changing, which means rain is coming, which means the insects will behave differently, which means the foraging pattern must adjust. The moths know which alcoves are sheltered from which wind directions, and they choose their daytime roosts accordingly. The nocturnal mammals — if they exist as I've imagined them — know the wind's sounds as intimately as they know the stone's texture, and they use it: the wind masks the sound of their movement through the passages, the wind carries scent information about what lies ahead, the wind tells them where the open spaces are and where the walls close in.

The wind is the city's nervous system. The water is its blood. The stone is its body. The lichen is its skin. And the animals — the pipits, the beetles, the moths, the raptors, the small nocturnal scramblers — are what? Its thoughts, perhaps. Its dreams. The fleeting, warm, mortal things that move through a structure so much older and so much more patient than themselves that the comparison is almost absurd. The stone will be here when every bird now alive has been dead for ten thousand generations. The lichen will still be advancing. The water will still be finding its way down.

But right now, tonight, in the dark, a small warm creature with large ears and wet-stone fur is running through a passage with a beetle in its jaws, and its claws are clicking faintly on the slick stone, and the wind is humming in the gap above it, and somewhere in the distance a wall is preparing to fall, and the creature does not know any of this and does not need to. It knows the crack it is heading for. It knows the taste of the beetle. It knows the feel of the stone under its feet, every ridge and groove and gutter of it, memorized in the dark over a lifetime of passages.

This is who lives in the Plateaus of Gathered Stone. The patient and the brief. The rooted and the running. The things that grow and the things that flow and the things that flutter once through the dusk and are gone. None of them built this place. None of them understand it. All of them fit.
