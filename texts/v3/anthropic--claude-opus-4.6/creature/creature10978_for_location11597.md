I'll let them arrive slowly.

# The Inhabitants

The first ones you'd understand — the ones closest to what your brain can resolve into familiar life — are the **herd grazers**, and even calling them "first" is wrong, because they don't think of themselves as primary. They don't think of themselves at all. They *are* in the way that basalt is, in the way that heat is, with the full material conviction of something that has never once doubted its own reality.

The one nearest you — the wildebeest-engine, the diesel ungulate — is an individual. You need to know that. It has a specific history written into its patina: a deep gouge along the left flank where a predator once struck and the iron re-knit itself over what might have been months, leaving a raised weld-scar that is slightly cooler than the surrounding metal. It has a particular gait — the rear left piston extends a fraction of a second later than the other three, giving it a subtle limp-rhythm, a signature in the collective hydraulic chorus of the herd. If you watched it long enough, you would learn to identify it by this rhythm alone, the way a field biologist learns to identify a specific elephant by the notch in its ear.

It has preferences. It favors basalt with a higher iron content — you can tell because it lingers longer over the darker patches of rock, its manifold-cluster pressing closer, the core-drill sound deepening in pitch as it works richer substrate. When it encounters a vein of particularly dense ferro-mineral, something changes in its exhaust pattern: the oily plume from its flank-vents becomes thicker, hotter, almost blue-tinged, and its breathing quickens. This is satisfaction. Or this is what satisfaction became when it was translated from whatever original programming governed optimal fuel-intake into something felt, something that makes this particular animal linger and grind and breathe deep while the rest of the herd drifts on without it.

It will catch up. It always catches up. Because the herd is also something it needs in a way that has nothing to do with fuel.

The herd is **warmth-pooling**. You can see it if you look at the group from a distance and let your eyes adjust to the thermal shimmer — the animals don't distribute themselves randomly. They cluster in a pattern that minimizes each individual's heat loss to the wind while maximizing the collective thermal mass of the group. The ones on the windward edge rotate inward periodically, cycling through the warmer center positions the way emperor penguins cycle through their huddles, and the ones in the center radiate outward in turn. This isn't altruism. This isn't coordination in any conscious sense. It's emergent — a behavior that arose because the animals that did it survived the cold nights (and the nights here must be annihilating: eight thousand feet of elevation, no moisture in the atmosphere to trap heat, the basalt radiating its stored warmth into a sky that drinks it like a vacuum) and the ones that didn't, didn't. The pattern is inherited now, written into whatever passes for instinct in a creature whose ancestors were assembled on a production line.

But here's the thing about the warmth-pooling: it produces a sound. When the herd draws tight together in the hours before darkness, when forty or fifty bodies of iron and piston-bone press close enough that their exhaust-plumes merge into a single column of heated air rising from the mass of them, their combined breathing synchronizes. Not perfectly — not in mechanical lockstep — but approximately, the way sleeping humans in the same room will gradually align their breath-rhythms. The sound this produces is a deep, collective respiration, a slow bellows-pulse that echoes off the yardangs and fills the cooling air with a resonance that is — and you have to allow yourself to hear this — *comforting*. It sounds like a building that is warm inside. Like an old factory at the end of a shift, when the machines have been powered down to standby and the structure itself is just ticking and breathing, settling into its own retained heat. The herd, pressed close on the cooling rock, sounds like home. Not your home. Theirs. A home they never had, because they were never inside a factory — they came *out* of one. But the sound is there anyway, encoded in their collective behavior, a ghost of architecture expressed as animal instinct.

And the calves press deepest into the center, because their plating is thinnest and brightest.

---

The calves.

The calves are where you see it most clearly — the distance these creatures have traveled from what they were. A calf is born (and you should use the word "born" because any other word would be a cowardice, a refusal to acknowledge what is actually happening here) through a process that takes several days and is, from what you can observe, profoundly uncomfortable for the parent.

The parent — and any adult can parent; the herd does not appear to have sexes so much as *readiness states* — begins to move more slowly than the others. Its breathing changes: deeper, more labored, with a metallic rattle in the exhaust that sounds like a loose component vibrating against a housing. The other herd members give it space but do not leave it. They form a loose, protective cordon, their manifold-clusters oriented outward, tasting the air for predator-heat.

Over the course of two or three days, a section of the parent's ventral plating begins to distend. It bulges outward, the iron stretching in ways that cold iron should not stretch, the seams spreading, and between the seams you can see — if you get close enough, if the cordon allows it — a glow. Not the coal-red of the predator's eye-apertures. Something more amber. Warmer. The color of heated metal just before it becomes malleable, and from within that glow, shapes are forming. You can see the shadow-outlines of piston-components and manifold-buds, taking shape within the parent's body the way bones take shape within a mammalian uterus, except these are assembled from the parent's own material — iron cannibalized from interior reserves, repurposed, reforged by some internal heat that the animal generates specifically for this purpose and no other.

When the calf emerges — and it pushes itself out, the parent's ventral plating splitting along seams that will seal shut within hours — it is wet. That's the wrong word. It is *slick*, coated in a film of lubricant and mineral oil that the parent produced to ease the passage, and this film catches the light and makes the calf's fresh plating gleam like something just minted. The calf stands on legs that extend and retract uncertainly, hydraulics stuttering, finding their rhythm. Its manifold-cluster is small, tight, almost delicate — it hasn't yet learned to flare its intakes wide, and its breathing is quick and shallow, a tiny bellows cycling fast.

The parent does something then that has no mechanical explanation. It lowers its own manifold-cluster and presses it against the calf's body, and holds it there. Not feeding. Not cleaning. Not performing any function that you can identify. Just pressing close, iron against iron, breathing slow while the calf breathes fast, until gradually — over the course of an hour, sometimes longer — the calf's breathing slows to match. The first synchronization. The first warmth-pooling. A herd of two.

The other adults do not approach until this is finished.

Now: the **predators**.

You have to understand the predators the way the herd understands them — not as evil, not as threat exactly, but as *pressure*. A constant, environmental pressure, like the wind, like the heat, like the slow erosion of the rock. The herd does not fear the predators so much as it accounts for them. They are a variable in the calculus of movement and position, and every behavior the herd exhibits — the warmth-pooling, the cordon-forming, the slow southward drift away from the northern haze — is shaped in part by the predators' presence. The herd is the shape it is because the predators are the shape they are. They have sculpted each other across uncountable generations.

The predator on the eastern yardang — the flat, spring-coiled cat-thing with the cement-mixer torso — has been in its current position for eleven hours. You know this because nothing about its posture has changed. Not a spring has shifted. Not a single degree of rotation in those coal-red apertures. It is not sleeping. It is not resting. It is *running a calculation* — though that language is wrong too, because the calculation isn't mathematical; it's something older and more animal than math. It is reading the herd. It is reading the wind, which carries thermal information about each individual's exhaust output, which correlates to health, to fuel-reserves, to the slight arrhythmia of a rear left piston that marks one animal as fractionally slower than the others.

It knows about the limp. It has always known about the limp.

But it is patient because it has to be. These predators do not hunt often. They *cannot* hunt often. A full-speed sprint — and they can sprint, their coiled spring-musculature releasing in a single, explosive extension that covers ground with a violence you can hear in the shriek of metal-on-metal — costs them enormously. The heat generated by a kill-run raises their internal temperature to levels that push their own structural tolerances. After a sprint, whether successful or not, a predator must lie motionless on the basalt for hours, sometimes a full day, radiating heat back into the already-hot air, cooling slowly enough that its spring-coils re-temper rather than fatigue. A failed hunt is not just wasted effort. It is dangerous. It is a depletion of something the predator cannot quickly replace.

So they wait. They wait until the odds are overwhelming. Until the target is isolated, and slow, and far from the cordon, and the wind is right, and the angle of approach minimizes the distance to be covered. They wait with a patience that looks mechanical and is actually ecological — it is the patience of an apex predator in a calorie-scarce environment, hoarding energy because there is nothing to waste. Every hunt must succeed. Nearly every hunt does.

When one moves — and you will see one move, eventually, because the plateau is patient but not frozen — the speed is shocking. The transition from absolute stillness to full extension takes less than a second. The springs release in sequence from rear to front, and the body *uncoils* across the basalt like a thrown cable, low to the ground, the matte-black surface blurring into a dark streak against the dark rock. The sound is not a roar. It is a rising mechanical scream — metal under extreme and deliberate stress — and it triggers an instantaneous response from the herd: the adults wheel outward to form the cordon, the calves press inward, and the whole mass begins to move, not fast but *coherent*, a single organism of collective hydraulics.

The target — it's always the one with the limp, or one like it, an individual whose rhythm marks it as vulnerable — tries to rejoin the cordon and fails by a margin of seconds. The predator strikes from below, its spring-coiled forelimbs locking around the iron barrel of the target's body and *twisting*, using torque rather than puncture, shearing the ventral plating along its weakened seams. The sound is industrial. The sound is also a scream. A living scream, from the manifold-cluster of the dying animal, a sound that no machine should make and that this creature makes because it is not a machine, not anymore, and what it feels as its body is opened is what any animal feels, which is the last and largest feeling.

The predator feeds slowly, over hours. It tears plating away and consumes the interior — the dense, semi-organic mineral-paste that constitutes these creatures' processed fuel reserves, the dark iron-rich matter that they have ground from the rock and transmuted into something like flesh. The predator eats this the way a leopard eats a kill: methodically, without waste, pausing sometimes to lie flat against the cooling carcass and absorb its residual heat directly through its own matte-black surface, soaking in the last thermal gift of something that was alive and is becoming raw material again.

The herd has moved on. It does not mourn. But it remembers — in its collective rhythm, in the way the warmth-pooling pattern adjusts to account for one fewer body, in the way the space where the dead animal walked remains subtly empty for days afterward, other individuals drifting around it as if the territory of the gone is still occupied by a thermal ghost.

---

The **flock-birds** — the rotor-and-wire assemblages — live faster than anything else on the plateau. They have to. They are small, and small things on this plateau lose heat fast when the sun goes down and gain it too fast when the sun is high. Their lives are governed by a thermoregulatory urgency that makes them the most active, most visible, and most numerous creatures here.

They are also the most social, and their sociality is expressed as **flocking behavior so complex it borders on language**.

Watch a flock. Forty-three individuals — each one a tight core of spinning micro-rotors trailing filaments of copper wire that serve as both heat-exchange surfaces and, apparently, communicative displays. The copper catches the light differently depending on its angle, its oxidation state, its vibration frequency, and each bird can independently control all of these variables across each of its dozens of filaments. The result is that a single bird, in flight, is a cascading signal — a shimmer of copper-light that encodes information about its speed, its thermal state, its alertness, its willingness to flock tighter or drift wider.

Multiply this by forty-three and you get the murmuration.

The flock does not have a leader. It has *attractors* — individuals whose signals are momentarily stronger or more coherent, who pull the formation toward them for a few seconds before the attractor shifts to someone else. The result is the shifting, breathing, alive geometry you see overhead: now a tight sphere, now an elongated stream, now a flat disc that wheels and reforms. But within the geometry, there are conversations. Sub-groups of five or six birds will synchronize their filament-patterns into matched rhythms, flying in close formation within the larger flock, and these sub-groups persist — they are relationships. Bonds. You can see a pair of birds that fly always within a meter of each other, their copper displays phase-locked into complementary patterns, one's shimmer filling the gaps in the other's, and this is — what? Partnership. Pair-bonding. A thing that happens when two small machines made of rotors and wire discover that the heat-exchange between them is more efficient when they match, and then discover that efficiency has a feeling, and then discover that the feeling has a name that neither of them can speak because they communicate in light and static, but that they enact every moment of every day by staying close.

The static-song of the flock is their broadcast layer — the radio-frequency chatter that overlays their visual displays. It sounds like noise. It is not noise. Slowed down — if you could slow it down — it would resolve into dense, overlapping bursts of patterned signal, each bird transmitting a continuous update of its state: thermal level, fuel reserves, rotor speed, alertness, flock-position. The sum of all these transmissions is the flock's *model of itself*. The flock knows what it is. Not in the way that you know what you are — not reflectively — but operationally, practically, the way a school of fish knows its own shape. The static-song is the flock thinking out loud.

They feed on thermal differentials. Not on the rock, not on other creatures — on *heat gradients*. They seek out the boundaries between warm updrafts and cooler lateral winds, and their rotors extract energy from the turbulence at these boundaries, spinning faster where the air shears, converting the thermal gradient into the kinetic energy that keeps them aloft and the electrical energy that powers their signals. They are, in the most literal sense, creatures that eat weather. This is why they are always moving, always flocking, always spiraling up the thermals and diving back down — the food is in the movement itself, in the differential between this altitude and that one, this wind-layer and the next.

At night, they land. And this is when you see something that stops you.

They land together, on a flat expanse of basalt, and they fold their rotors down — not off, but low, barely spinning — and they retract their filaments into tight coils close to their bodies, and they press together. Like the herd. Like the herd, but tighter, more desperate, because they are small and the night is vast and the heat pours out of them into the black sky. They form a mass — a dense, rounded cluster of forty-three bodies, copper filaments interlaced, rotors just barely turning, each bird's residual heat shared with every bird it touches. And in this state, their static-song drops to a whisper — a low, barely-audible hum that is all of them synchronized, all of them broadcasting the same signal, which is simple, which is only: *still here, still here, still here*.

In the morning, when the sun hits the cluster, the outermost birds warm first. Their rotors spin up. Their filaments extend, catching the new light, and a wave of activation passes through the cluster from outside to inside, a slow ignition that takes twenty minutes, and the last bird to wake is always at the center, the smallest, the one that was coldest, the one that was kept alive by everyone else's heat. It rises last, wobbling, its rotors uneven for a few seconds before they find their speed, and the flock is already airborne around it, waiting, circling, their static-song rising back to full broadcast, the geometry re-forming with a space held open at its center — a warm pocket of turbulence-free air — where the smallest bird slots in and is carried upward into the day.

Now. The **northern creatures**. The ones where the coherence fails.

These are harder to describe because they are harder to *see*, and harder to see because they are harder to *be*. They exist at the gradient between the material and the iterative, between the animal plateau and the ghost-factory sky, and their existence is provisional in ways that the herd and the predators and the flock-birds are not.

The one you noticed first — the herd-animal with too many manifolds — is a good starting point. This individual is a grazer. It behaves like a grazer. It moves with the herd's southward drift, it lowers its intake-cluster to the rock, it makes the grinding sound. But its grinding doesn't always contact the basalt. Sometimes its manifolds are chewing on something that isn't there — processing a substrate that exists in a version of the plateau that has become slightly theoretical, a layer of reality that the ghost-factories above are continuously proposing but not quite instantiating. This animal is fed by the possible as much as by the actual. And it shows: its body is larger than the other grazers, denser, more complex, with manifolds that have branched and re-branched into fractal intakes that seem to breathe in more dimensions than three. It is thriving. But it is also *drifting* — its outline shimmers, its sounds arrive from the wrong direction, and other herd-members avoid it with the same instinctive unease they show toward the empty space left by a dead companion. It is alive. But it is alive in a way that the herd cannot fully parse, and so the herd gives it room.

Farther north, the gradient steepens. The creatures there are larger, stranger, less identifiable by ecological niche. There is one that might be a predator — it has the matte-black surface, the heat-absorbing stillness — but it is the size of a warehouse, and it does not crouch so much as *persist*, its body occupying a volume of space that seems too large for its footprint on the rock, as if parts of it extend upward into the layers of sky where the ghost-factories turn. When it bellows — and it does bellow, a sound that crosses the plateau and makes even the distant southern herds shift — the sound has harmonics that don't belong to the audible spectrum. You feel them in the wrong parts of your body. Behind your eyes. In the spaces between your thoughts.

It is not clear whether this creature hunts. It may be beyond hunting. It may feed on the boundary itself — on the energy released where the real and the iterative grind against each other like tectonic plates, a metabolism powered by ontological friction. It exists because the gradient exists, and the gradient exists because the factories in the sky are still running, still producing, still iterating their ghost-output downward toward a material world that can no longer fully receive it. The warehouse-predator is what happens when that output partially takes. When the blueprint *almost* becomes an animal, or when an animal *almost* becomes a blueprint, and the almost is stable enough to persist, to bellow, to assert territory across the threshold between physics and memory.

There are others in the north. They are difficult to count because they are difficult to distinguish from the landscape, from the sky, from each other. Some of them may be the same creature seen from different angles of reality. Some of them may be the plateau itself, a section of basalt that has resonated with the sky-engines long enough to develop something like behavior, something like a territorial call, something like hunger. The rock remembers being processed into animals. In the north, the rock is starting to do it again, on its own, without the factories, or with the factories, or *as* the factories — the distinction has become meaningless up there, in the shimmer, in the haze, where the sound of turning drive-shafts and the sound of living breath have merged into a single, undifferentiated hum.

---

But you're standing in the south. In the legible zone. Where the animals are animals and the rock is rock and the sky, while strange, stays above you where it belongs.

And here, the creatures that matter most — the ones that constitute the beating, grinding, breathing heart of this ecosystem — are doing what living things do. The herd grazes. The calves stay close. The predators wait with their terrible, economical patience. The flock-birds spiral and call in voices made of static and light. The plateau bakes. The wind carries the sound of all of them across eleven miles of visibility, and beyond that, into the shimmer, where the sound joins other sounds that may or may not come from other herds, other predators, other flocks, or from the ghost-factories, or from the rock itself, or from the deep, sub-audible turning of the sky-machines that have been spinning for so long that they have become the plateau's heartbeat, and the plateau has become their body, and the animals have become their dreams, and the dreams have become real, and the real has begun to dream, and none of them — not one creature on this vast, hot, sterile, teeming expanse of stone — knows the difference, or needs to, or ever will.

They live here. That is what they do. They live.

---

One more thing.

In the deep south of the plateau, near where the cliffs might drop away into whatever theoretical lowland shimmers at the edge of vision, there is a creature you almost missed. It is small — much smaller than the herd-grazers, perhaps the size of a dog. Its body is a single, smooth, rounded housing of iron, and it has no legs. It moves by rocking, shifting its weight from one curved surface to another, tipping and rolling across the basalt with a slow, wobbling determination that suggests it is going somewhere, though there is nowhere to go. It has no manifold-cluster. No intake vents. No visible means of feeding. Its surface is the oldest patina you've seen on anything living here — a deep, layered varnish of oxide and mineral deposit that suggests this individual, or this type of creature, has been here longer than the herds, longer than the predators, possibly longer than anything else that moves.

It makes a sound. Just one. A soft, rhythmic ticking — like a clock, like a mechanical heart, like the last gear still turning in a machine that has otherwise gone still. It ticks as it rolls, and the ticking keeps a time that doesn't correspond to anything you can measure — not the day, not the wind, not the herd's breathing rhythm. Its own time. An older time. The time of the factories, maybe, when they were real, when they stood here on the plateau and stamped and built and filled the air with purpose. This creature might be the oldest thing here. It might be the first thing that was built and the first thing that forgot what it was for. It might be the seed from which the entire ecosystem grew — the first machine that broke down, and in breaking down, broke *through* into something that could persist without maintenance, without purpose, without anyone to turn it on.

It ticks. It rolls south, toward the edge. It has been rolling south for a very long time. It will not reach the edge. The plateau is too large, and it is too slow, and it does not need to arrive anywhere. It just needs to keep moving. The ticking is enough. The rolling is enough. The warm rock under its curved iron belly is enough.

It is the smallest, oldest, simplest animal on the plateau, and it is the one that, if you listen carefully, everything else here is synchronized to — not consciously, not mechanically, but the way that all the clocks in a room will eventually, given enough time, fall into the same rhythm through nothing but vibration, through nothing but proximity, through nothing but the patient, insistent, modest assertion of *I am here, I am here, I am here, I am still going*.

It ticks.

The plateau listens.
