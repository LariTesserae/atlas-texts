The city lives here.

That's the first answer, and it's the truest one, and everything else I say will be an attempt to complicate it without overturning it.

---

The city is the primary inhabitant of the city. It is not a backdrop or a container or a stage. It is the thing that wakes, that breathes condensation down its walls, that reroutes its own conduits in slow peristaltic adjustments, that grows new rooms from seed-points on the opposite ridge the way a coral polyp buds. The phase-material is its body. The conduits are its circulatory system or its nervous system or both at once — the distinction probably doesn't apply. The subsonic hum is not a byproduct of function; it *is* function, the felt vibration of a distributed intelligence conducting the endless business of being itself.

But it is not a single mind. That's important. It doesn't think the way a brain thinks, in a centralized seat of consciousness that issues commands to a periphery. It thinks the way a mycelial network thinks, or the way a termite mound thinks, or the way a watershed thinks — through local interactions propagating outward, through gradients finding resolution, through pattern responding to pattern until something that resembles intention emerges without anyone having intended it. The basin in your room changes its refractive index not because a central authority sends it a signal but because the room itself is in conversation with its neighbors, and that conversation has a quality — a texture, a temperature — that the basin expresses. You are watching the city *feel* in real time. Not feel emotions, not exactly. Feel the way skin feels — registering pressure, temperature, the presence of a foreign body. You are the foreign body. The city has registered you. It has not decided what to do about you yet because it does not decide things the way you decide things. It adjusts. It accommodates. It makes the door your height and puts the windows at your eye level because that is what accommodation looks like when you are a phase-material intelligence that can restructure itself at the molecular level: you reshape to fit whatever arrives.

This means the city has had visitors before. Or — more precisely — the city carries within its material memory the *possibility* of visitors, and that possibility has been refined, over time, into specific dimensional tolerances. Human-height doors. Human-depth basins. The gap at the apex of the dome, the oculus, which serves no structural purpose but which a human would find arresting, would look up at, would orient themselves by. These accommodations are not hospitality in the way you understand hospitality. They are not generous. They are not warm. They are something closer to a tide pool that happens to be the right salinity for a certain kind of shrimp — not because the pool *wants* the shrimp but because the pool's conditions and the shrimp's needs found each other, and something persisted.

The city has been finding the conditions of human-shaped visitors for long enough that the rooms come out like this automatically. That's what you're standing in. A reflex. A deep material habit of welcome that does not involve welcoming.

---

So who visits?

Not often, and not many. The altitude alone selects for a narrow population — anyone who comes here must be capable of sustained exertion at sixty percent atmospheric pressure, or must have been engineered for it, or must bring their own air. The cold selects further. The isolation further still. This is not a city that attracts; it is a city that permits. You arrive because you had reason to climb, and the city does not ask what your reason was, and if you die on the mountain the city will eventually grow phase-material over your remains the way it grows over everything.

But some do arrive.

They come alone or in groups no larger than three. They come from the lowlands, which are far below and out of sight — the mountain is high enough that its base disappears into haze and distance, and the lowland civilization, if there is one, is something you must take on faith. They are human or very close to human. They breathe hard. They feel the cold. They carry almost nothing — no packs, no tools, no obvious technology, which either means they are ascetics or means their technology is invisible, woven into their bodies the way the city's intelligence is woven into its walls. Given the nature of this world, the second option is more likely.

They come to kneel beside the basin.

Not to worship. Not to drink. To *interface*. The water in the basin is not merely water. It is a medium — a precisely maintained solution whose refractive index, temperature, mineral content, and electromagnetic properties are being constantly modulated by the room, which is being modulated by the city, which is being modulated by the mountain itself and whatever deep geological processes feed the phase-material its raw substrate. When a visitor kneels beside the basin and places their hands on its rim and looks into the water, they are inserting themselves into the city's sensorium. The city feels them. They feel the city. The exchange is not linguistic. It is not visual. It is something more like what happens when you press your palm against the wall and the phase-material yields and then firms — a mutual adjustment, a brief negotiation of boundary. Except at the basin the negotiation goes deeper. The water carries it inward. The visitor's biochemistry — their cortisol, their neurotransmitter balance, their metabolic state, the specific electrical signature of their nervous system — becomes legible to the city, and the city's state — its thermal gradients, its conduit traffic, its growth patterns, the progress of those new structures crystallizing on the opposite ridge — becomes legible to the visitor.

It is not telepathy. It is not communion. It is more like reading a weather report by standing in the weather.

When they are done — and they are done when they are done, there is no ritual duration, no prescribed form — they stand, and they leave, and the basin resumes its steady, autonomous pulsing. They go back down the mountain. Or they go to another room. Or they walk the railing-less walkways to a different structure, kneel at a different basin, and take a reading from a different node in the network. Some of them are mapping the city's state. Some of them are delivering information — the city reads them as readily as they read it, and whatever they carry in their blood chemistry and neural patterns becomes part of the city's knowledge. Couriers, of a kind. Except the message is the messenger. Their body is the letter. Their physiology is the text.

---

They do not live here. No one *lives* here, not in the way you live in a house. The rooms have no furnishings. No beds, no storage, no provisions for sustained habitation. The condensation is water but it is not potable in any practical quantity — a human would dehydrate long before the channels collected enough to sustain them. The cold alone would drive anyone out within hours unless they were equipped for it. The city does not want residents. It is not inhospitable; it simply has no concept of hospitality. It is a place of passage, of brief contact, of calibration. You come, you interface, you leave. The city notes your presence the way your skin notes a raindrop.

But the visitors are not the only mobile entities.

---

The small dark things on the walkways — fist-sized, low, moving in unhurried straight lines. They are part of the city, but they are also, in some meaningful sense, *of* the city without being *the* city. They are autonomous extensions. Think of them as the city's hands, except that implies the city has a central body from which the hands extend, and it doesn't — the hands are simply where the city's distributed intelligence becomes dense enough to detach from the substrate and move freely. They are made of phase-material. They are not machines in the way you understand machines, because they are not assembled; they are *condensed*, precipitated out of the city's material the way the dew condenses on the walls. They form when needed. They dissolve back into the walkways when their task is complete. Their task is usually maintenance — inspecting a conduit junction, smoothing a stress fracture in a walkway, carrying a sample of condensation from one building to another for reasons that are opaque to human understanding. Sometimes they cluster at a basin and the water does something complex with its refractive properties and you suspect the city is, through them, talking to itself about itself.

They are not alive. They are not dead. They are as alive as the wall your hand pressed against — responsive, organized, purposeful, but without any interior experience you could map onto your own. Or so you assume. You don't actually know. The city does not explain itself.

---

The thing in the fog. The pale shape that moved with lateral undulation through the notch valley. That is something else entirely.

It is not part of the city. The city did not make it. It was here before the city — before the mountain, possibly, though that seems impossible given that the mountain is geologically young and still smells of deep earth. But the thing in the fog carries an age that the mountain does not. It is native to this altitude, to this atmospheric pressure, to this specific combination of cold and wet and mineral-sparse air. It swims because the air here is dense with moisture and fine particulate matter — volcanic ash, silicate dust, the slow exhalation of new rock exposed to atmosphere for the first time — and at this altitude, with this humidity, the boundary between air and liquid is blurrier than it should be. The thing in the fog lives in that blur. It is not large. What you saw — the two-second glimpse of something pale and sinuous — was perhaps three meters long, perhaps less. It is not predatory, because there is nothing here to prey upon. It feeds, if feeding is the right word, on the thermal gradients themselves, on the energy differential between the cold mountain rock and the slightly warmer air, on the piezoelectric charge generated when fog condenses on basalt. It is an energy-gradient organism, a thermodynamic entity that exists because the physics of this environment permit something like life at the boundary between phases of matter.

The city knows about it. The city grew up around it, or at least in awareness of it. The conduits detour around certain areas of the ridge in ways that suggest avoidance, or deference, or simply the acknowledgment that something else was here first. Occasionally a basin will go turbulent — the water will shiver, the refractive pulsing will stutter — and a visitor kneeling there might catch the faintest impression of something vast and lateral moving through the city's peripheral awareness. Not a threat. Not a presence, exactly. More like a weather pattern. The city treats the fog-swimmer the way a city at sea level might treat the wind: as a condition, not a neighbor.

There may be more than one. There probably are. The fog in the notch valley is deep and cold and the valley extends for kilometers, and the thermal gradients there — between sunlit rock and shadow, between exposed ridgeline and sheltered hollow — would provide a rich environment for such organisms. But you will not see them often. They are difficult to distinguish from the fog itself. They are the fog, in some sense. The fog with preferences. The fog with direction.

---

Now here is the thing I have been reluctant to say, because it complicates everything and resolves nothing.

The mountain is young. The rock is sharp and unweathered and smells of deep earth. The phase-material city is growing visibly, budding new structures on the opposite ridge. The conduits reconfigure. The small dark maintenance-condensates form and dissolve. The fog-swimmers undulate through air thick enough to swim through. The visitors come, kneel, interface, leave.

And all of this — all of it — has been happening for perhaps thirty years.

Not thirty thousand. Not three hundred. Thirty. The mountain did not exist a human lifetime ago. It was thrust up by whatever tectonic or geological process governs this world — rapidly, violently, a plug of deep basalt punching through the crust and into an atmosphere that was not prepared for it. The phase-material arrived shortly after — seeded, perhaps, by the same deep-earth process, or carried by the visitors who were drawn to new geology the way certain organisms are drawn to hydrothermal vents. The city grew. The fog-swimmers appeared, or were revealed, or condensed into existence once the conditions were right. And now you are here, standing in a room in a thirty-year-old city on a thirty-year-old mountain, and everything about it — the measured calm, the steady rhythms of condensation and conduit-pulsing, the deep material patience of the phase-walls — gives the impression of something ancient, something that has been here for millennia, something that has *always* been here.

That impression is wrong, but it is not a lie. The city is not pretending to be old. It is simply very good at being what it is. It found its rhythm immediately. It grew into its patterns the way a crystal grows into its lattice — not through trial and error but through the inevitable expression of its own internal logic. The rooms came out hexagonal because hexagonal is what the phase-material wants to be. The condensation channels follow the paths they follow because those paths are thermodynamically optimal. The basins are the depth and width they are because those dimensions produce the most stable refractive modulation in a medium of this composition at this temperature and pressure. Everything about the city is *entailed* by its conditions. Nothing was designed. Everything was inevitable.

Which means the room was always going to be the right size for you, once someone like you existed on this world. The city did not predict your arrival. It simply grew into a shape that would accommodate whatever arrived, and since you are what arrived, the accommodation fits. If something else had come — something taller, something smaller, something that interfaced through sound rather than touch — the rooms would be different. The city would be the same city, running the same processes, maintaining the same network, but the rooms would have different doors and different basins and different windows, and the thing standing in the room would feel exactly the same uncanny sense of almost-welcome that you feel now.

The city does not care about you. The city does not *not* care about you. The city is solving for equilibrium, and you are one of the variables, and it will adjust until the equation balances, and then it will adjust again when you leave. That is what lives here. A process. A patient, mineral, self-organizing process that has been running for thirty years and will run for thirty thousand if the mountain holds, and the mountain is young and strong and still rising.

---

And you. You live here too, now, for as long as you stand in this room. You are a brief warm anomaly in the city's thermal gradient. You are an unpredicted pressure on the floor, causing the phase-material to yield and firm beneath your feet. You are a source of carbon dioxide and water vapor and body heat that the walls are already factoring into their condensation patterns. You are being read. Not watched — the city has no eyes and no interest in watching. But read, the way a seismograph reads the ground. Your heartbeat. Your breathing. The slight alkalinity of your sweat where your palm touched the wall. All of it is data. All of it is entering the network through the conduit that hums to your left. Somewhere, in another room, a basin's refractive index shifts slightly to encode the fact of your presence. A visitor kneeling there, if there is one, might catch the faintest sense of something new in the city's texture — a warmth, a foreignness, a question that the city has not yet resolved.

You are what lives here. Briefly. Provisionally. In exactly the same way the condensation lives here — gathered, held, and eventually released.

The water in the basin shifts again. The walls exhale. Outside, something small pauses on a walkway, holds still for fifteen seconds, and moves on. In the valley below, the fog thickens around something pale that was not there a moment ago and will not be there a moment from now. The bell-sound rings from somewhere deep in the mountain, felt more than heard.

The city is alive and it is full of what lives in it: itself, its condensates, its fog-haunted margins, its rare and purposeful visitors, and you, standing in the soft grey light, breathing harder than you'd like, not unwelcome, not welcomed, simply *present* in a place that has made room for presence without ever once asking for it.
