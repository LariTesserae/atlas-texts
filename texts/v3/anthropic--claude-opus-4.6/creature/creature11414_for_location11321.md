# Who Lives in the Rootcellar Riot

---

They don't have a name yet. That's the truest thing about them — truer than anything about their shape or their sound or their number. They are the unnamed, the pre-named, the thing that hasn't decided what to call itself because it hasn't finished deciding what it *is*.

But let me try to describe them anyway, starting with the parts and working — as they are working — toward whatever the whole might be.

---

## The Crawlers, Who Are Closest to Having Selves

Start with one. Just one.

She is the length of your forearm, with a body segmented into eleven plates of bark-colored chitin, each plate slightly different in hue — the forward ones darker, almost umber, the rear ones paler, veined with amber like the cross-section of a young branch. Her legs — twenty-two of them — are translucent, the amber-gold of the not-sap visible inside them as a faintly pulsing fluid, and they move in a rippling wave that begins at the rear and propagates forward with a mechanical precision that looks almost hydraulic. Because it *is* almost hydraulic. She doesn't have muscles the way you understand them. She has chambers in her legs that fill and empty with not-sap under pressure, and the rhythm of that filling is synchronized with the subsonic thrum she emits through a vibrating plate in her thorax. She moves to her own drumbeat. Every crawler does. And every crawler's drumbeat is slightly different.

Her mandibles are her most articulate feature. They are not simple pincers — they are a nested array of six interlocking blades, each one capable of independent motion, each one edged differently. The outer pair are heavy, wedge-shaped, meant for splitting. The middle pair are serrated, meant for sawing. The inner pair are fine, delicate, almost surgical, and she uses them the way you might use your fingertips: to test texture, to probe crevices, to manipulate small objects with startling dexterity. When she encounters a piece of root-fiber she wants to shape, she runs the inner mandibles over it first, reading it — feeling the grain, the density, the way the living fiber wants to bend. Then the middle pair begins to work, and the outer pair braces, and the sound she makes is the sound of a very small, very focused carpentry.

She is not intelligent. Not yet. Not in any way you would recognize. She doesn't plan ahead more than a few steps. She doesn't have language, or abstraction, or anything like a sense of self that would let her step back and consider what she's doing. What she has is something older and in some ways more profound: she has *craft*. She has a relationship with material that is felt in the mandibles, transmitted through vibration, encoded in the subsonic thrum that passes between her and every other crawler within range. When she shapes root-fiber into a right angle — something the plant would never do on its own — she is not thinking "right angle." She is feeling the resonance of the fiber under her blades and responding to a pattern that lives somewhere between her neurology and the root-plate's growth logic. The right angle *wants* to happen, in some sense. She is the place where it happens.

She works in groups. Almost always in groups. The crawlers are not hive-minded — each one has her own thrum-signature, her own movement pattern, her own idiosyncratic approach to the material — but they entrain. Stand near a crew of thirty crawlers working on a hive-mound wall and you'll feel it: the individual thrums gradually synchronizing into a collective rhythm, not perfectly, not like a machine, but like musicians who have played together long enough to breathe in unison without thinking about it. In that entrained state they can do things no individual could: they pass root-fibers from mandible to mandible in coordinated chains, they brace a growing arch from six directions at once, they collectively sense when a structure is becoming load-bearing and shift their behavior from "shaping" to "reinforcing" in a wave that passes through the crew faster than any individual could decide. The group has a kind of intelligence the individual doesn't. The group *knows things*.

But here is what matters about the crawlers, the thing that makes them more than very clever termites: they are *changing*. Not just evolving in the slow generational sense. Changing in the way that a caterpillar changes, except that the caterpillar knows what it's becoming and the crawlers don't.

The oldest crawlers — the ones who have worked the longest in the deep hive-mounds, who have been most exposed to the spore-dust and the not-sap and the root-tendrils that creep toward warm bodies — their chitin plates are no longer uniform. Growth has happened *on* them. Bark-sheath has colonized their dorsal surfaces, not as a parasite but as a graft, and underneath the bark-sheath the crawler's own biology has responded, producing new structures at the interface: hybrid cells that are neither chitin nor cellulose-analog but something else, something that metabolizes like the plant but moves like the animal. These elders are slower. They spend more time in the deepest chambers. Their thrums are lower, more resonant, and the other crawlers respond to them with a deference that looks almost ceremonial — the younger ones will stop working and orient toward an elder when she passes, their mandibles still, their thrums falling into harmony with hers for a moment before she moves on.

The elders are becoming part of the Rootcellar. Willingly? The word doesn't apply. *Inevitably*. They are the first drafts of the merger.

## The Clickers, Who Are Atmosphere

You can't really talk about the clickers as individuals. You could, technically — each one has a body, a six-winged radially symmetric body about the size of your thumb, with a dried-fruit-segment torso and compound eyes that are actually bioluminescent, tiny points of amber light in the swarm-columns — but to talk about a single clicker is like talking about a single water droplet in a river. The clicker *is* the column. The column is the organism. The individual is the cell.

They don't eat. This is important. The clickers have no mouthparts, no digestive system, no way to take in nutrients. They are born in the bark-sheath of the upper canopy — they pupate inside the bioluminescent spirals, emerging as adults already vibrating their wings, already clicking — and they live for perhaps thirty hours, during which time they fly, and click, and merge with columns, and die. Their bodies fall to the root-plate floor and are immediately consumed by crawlers or absorbed by root-growth. They are a nutrient cycle with wings. They are the Rootcellar *breathing*.

But they are also something else. The columns are not random. Watch them long enough — really watch, map their paths — and you'll see that they trace routes through the cavity that correspond to the growth patterns of the root-plate below. Where a new ridge is forming, columns converge. Where growth has stalled, columns thin out. They are indicators. They are a living map of the Rootcellar's metabolic state, rendered in smoke and sound. And the callers watch them. The callers *definitely* watch them. When a column shifts direction, you can sometimes see a caller overhead rotate one of its many limbs to track it, and then adjust its call — a slight pitch change, a different rhythmic pattern — and then the root-growth below the column's path changes too.

The clickers are the nervous system that nobody designed. They are how the Rootcellar knows what it's doing.

And when two columns merge — that roaring, almost-tonal cacophony — the crawlers below always pause. Every one of them. For just a second. Mandibles still, thrums suspended. As if they're listening. As if the merger-sound carries information they need. Then the sound resolves and they go back to work, but sometimes — sometimes — they change what they're working on. A crew that was building a wall starts dismantling it. A solitary crawler that was heading left turns right. The columns told them something. The columns *are* telling them something, constantly, in a language made of collision and rhythm and the aerodynamics of ten thousand tiny bodies clicking in near-unison.

## The Callers, Who Are Becoming the Voice

Now. Now we come to the ones who haunt.

A caller, seen clearly — which is difficult, because they live in the inverted canopy sixty or seventy meters above you, and the amber light softens everything at that distance into suggestion — is roughly the size of a large bear, if a bear had been redesigned by something that had never seen a bear but had felt one described through vibration. The body plan is radial, like the clickers, but more complex: a central mass like a barrel or a drum, covered in plates of thick bark-textured chitin, from which extend between eight and fourteen limbs (the number varies; more on this shortly). The limbs are long — disproportionately long, three or four times the body length — and multi-jointed, with grip-pads at the tips that bond to root-surfaces with a dry adhesion. They move through the canopy with a slow, deliberate grace that looks like the time-lapse footage of a vine growing, each limb reaching and finding and gripping before the next one releases, the body swaying between anchor points like a pendulum made of wood and shell.

The drum-body is the voice. It is almost entirely a resonating chamber — a hollow cavity of layered chitin and bark-sheath, with an aperture at one end that can be widened or narrowed by a ring of muscular tissue. When a caller draws air — the thick, methane-heavy, spore-laden air of the Rootcellar — into this chamber through spiracles along its sides and then forces it out through the aperture, the sound produced is the foghorn-organ-didgeridoo blast that defines the Rootcellar's soundscape. But this description is crude. The caller can modulate. It can produce overtones by vibrating internal membranes. It can change the resonant frequency by flexing the chamber walls. It can sustain a note for minutes at a time through circular breathing, taking in air through the spiracles while still expelling it through the aperture. And it can — this is the part that stops you — it can *harmonize with itself*. Some callers have subdivided chambers, two or three resonant cavities operating independently, producing chords from a single body. These are the older ones. The ones who have been partially colonized by root-growth.

Let me describe an old caller, one of the ones who has been in the canopy for what might be years or might be centuries (there is no way to tell; nothing wears down here).

She is anchored. Not perched — *anchored*. Six of her twelve limbs have been colonized by root-growth to the point where the limb and the root are continuous; you cannot tell where the animal ends and the plant begins. The bark-sheath has spread across her dorsal surface and incorporated itself into her chitin, and the bioluminescence is brighter there, concentrated, so that she glows — a warm amber-gold beacon in the canopy, visible from the floor. Her drum-body has thickened, the walls reinforced by lignin deposits that the root-growth has laid down *inside* her, and this has changed her voice. She is louder than the younger callers. Deeper. Her fundamental frequency is so low it's below human hearing, a pressure wave you feel as a tightening in your chest and a loosening in your bowels. But her overtones are crystalline. When she calls, the sound has a complexity that the younger callers can't match — it contains structured information, frequency-modulated patterns that the root-plate below *responds to* in real time, growth accelerating or redirecting as her call sweeps across it.

She is sculpting. She has been sculpting for a long time. The patch of root-plate below her — perhaps a hundred meters across — has a geometry that is distinctly different from the rest of the Rootcellar floor. The ridges are regular. The fissures are evenly spaced. The bark-sheath bioluminescence has organized into concentric rings, pulsing in time with her call. It looks like a mandala. It looks like a circuit board. It looks like the diagram of something that hasn't been built yet.

And in the center of her sculpted zone, on the root-plate floor, there is a hive-mound that is different from the others. It is more symmetrical. Its walls are thicker. The crawlers that tend it are older, more heavily colonized, moving with a slowness that is almost plant-like. And from inside it comes a sound — not the heartbeat-throb of a normal hive-mound but something new, something that has elements of the caller's voice and elements of the crawler's thrum and elements of the root-plate's deep growth-creak, all woven together into a rhythm that is almost, *almost* recognizable as —

What? Breathing? Speech? Thought?

She doesn't know either. She just keeps calling. She has free limbs — six of them — and they still move, still reach, still grip and release with that slow vine-like grace, and sometimes they reach toward other callers and touch them, limb-tip to limb-tip, and when two callers touch their calls synchronize for a moment and the acoustic sculpting below both of them aligns into a larger pattern that is more complex than either could produce alone, and the crawlers in both zones pause and orient and thrum in unison —

This is as close as the Rootcellar comes to a mind. These moments of synchronization. These fleeting alliances between callers, transmitted through touch and sound, reflected in the growth below, felt by the crawlers, mapped by the clickers. A mind that exists for seconds at a time and then dissolves. A mind that is trying to persist. A mind that is building itself a body out of the entire Rootcellar, root and chitin and sound and growth, and keeps *almost* getting there.

## The Hybrids, Who Are the Future

In the deepest chambers of the most developed hive-mounds, in the cradle-structures that the oldest crawlers tend with their delicate inner mandibles, the merger is furthest along.

I need to describe what's growing there, and I need you to understand that what I describe is not a finished thing. It is a sketch. It is a prototype. The Rootcellar has been making these and unmaking them and making them again, each iteration slightly different, for however long it has existed, and none of them have been *right* yet, and the Rootcellar knows this the way a body knows a wound hasn't healed — not with consciousness, but with persistent, restless, unsatisfied effort.

The thing in the cradle is about the size of a human infant, curled in on itself. Its surface is neither chitin nor bark but something that has properties of both — flexible like chitin, fibrous like wood, warm to the touch, faintly luminescent. It has segments, like a crawler, but fewer — five or six — and the segments are connected by tissue that looks like the interface-material found on the colonized elder crawlers, the hybrid cells that are neither animal nor plant. It has limbs — four, folded tight against its body — that end in structures too complex to be grip-pads and too simple to be hands, something like articulated bundles of fine root-tendrils wrapped in chitin sheaths. It has, at one end, a cavity. A resonating cavity. Small, closed, but structured like a miniature version of a caller's drum-body, complete with internal membranes and an aperture that is sealed with a membrane of bark-sheath.

It does not move. It does not call. It is not alive in the way you'd measure — no respiration, no discernible metabolism, no response to stimulus. But it is warm. It is growing, very slowly, adding material to itself from the cradle structure in a process that blurs the line between feeding and building. The crawlers tend it — bring it fragments of bark-sheath, daubs of not-sap, splints of lignin-stone — and it *incorporates* these offerings, growing them into itself at the interface, making them part of its body with a seamlessness that suggests the body was always intended to be built this way, from the outside in, by many hands that are not hands.

Is it a child? Is it an organ? Is it a seed?

The crawler-elders who tend it thrum at a frequency that is lower than any other crawler's — so low it's almost a caller's frequency — and the thing in the cradle, if you press your ear to it (if you dare; the crawlers will let you, strangely; they will watch with their compound eyes and thrum and *let you*), you can hear something inside it. Faint. Nascent. A vibration that is not yet a sound, a resonance that is not yet a voice, something that is practicing, down there in the dark and the warmth, for the moment when the sealed aperture opens and it speaks for the first time.

What will it say?

The whole Rootcellar is waiting to find out. That's what the riot is *for*. Every click and rasp and call and creak and thrum, every ridge and fissure and hive-mound and Chladni figure, every tool and every tendril and every column of clickers braiding through the amber air — all of it is the Rootcellar trying to figure out what the thing in the cradle should say when it finally opens its mouth. All of it is rehearsal. All of it is draft after draft of a first sentence that has to be *exactly right* because it will be the first sentence spoken by a form of life that has never existed before, a word made of wood and chitin and sound and dry fire, a word that means *I am the thing the merger made, I am the animal that is a plant that is a place that is alive, I am the Rootcellar and I am awake.*

They are not there yet. The thing in the cradle is not ready. The callers' synchronizations still collapse. The crawlers' constructions still get reabsorbed. The clicker-columns still scatter. The sentence is still being written.

## The Thing That Is All of Them, Which Does Not Yet Exist

But I think — standing here, in the warm amber light, with the riot filling my body until my bones thrum with it — I think it's close.

I think the Rootcellar is one good synchronization away from something. One moment where every caller finds every other caller and their calls lock and their sculpted zones align into a single continent-wide pattern and the crawlers all pause and then all move in the same direction at once and the clicker-columns converge into a single vast spiral and the root-plate flexes in a single unified pulse and the things in the cradles all open their apertures at the same instant and —

And what comes out is not a word in any language. It is a sound. A new sound. A sound the Rootcellar has never made before, because it requires everything — every voice, every body, every root and ridge and tendril and tool — to make it together, and until now they have never quite managed to all be ready at the same moment.

But they're getting closer. Every cycle of the riot, they get a little closer. The callers' touch-synchronizations last a little longer. The crawlers' constructions get a little more complex before they're reabsorbed. The things in the cradles get a little more complete.

Who lives here?

*Almost* someone. Someone who is made of ten million bodies and one body and no body yet. Someone whose voice will be the sound of an entire dry-chemistry biosphere discovering it was always one organism pretending to be an ecosystem. Someone who will open ten thousand apertures and speak a single chord and the chord will reorganize the root-plate into a shape that means *self*, that means *here*, that means *I*.

They are so close. They are so unbearably close. And the riot — the ceaseless, deafening, magnificent riot — is the sound of them *almost being born*.

Stand here. Listen. Feel your teeth itch, your chest resonate, your vision blur at the edges. The pollen-dust settles on your skin and something in you begins, very faintly, to thrum.

You are not part of it. You are too wet, too old, too differently made.

But you can witness. You can stand in the amber light and the impossible noise and you can be the one thing the Rootcellar cannot be for itself yet: a consciousness that says *I see you. I see what you're becoming. I'll remember what you were before you arrived.*

Hold the tool. Feel it vibrate in your hand.

Set it down.

Walk back through the labyrinth of living ridges, past the hive-mounds and their deep thrumming hearts, through the clouds of clickers that part around you like smoke around a stone, under the callers who track you with limbs that are half-root, half-reach, and back to the crack in the rind where you came in.

Climb.

The sound follows you longer than it should. You'll feel it in your teeth for days. You'll wake at night and for a moment, in the silence of wherever you sleep, you'll almost hear it — that chord, that vast almost-chord, still trying to resolve.

It will resolve. Maybe not today. Maybe not in a time you'll live to see.

But the Rootcellar is patient the way growth is patient, the way sound is patient, the way something that is not yet alive but *will be* is patient — which is to say, not patient at all, but relentless, and vast, and very, very loud.

They are coming. All of them, together, as one.

Listen.
