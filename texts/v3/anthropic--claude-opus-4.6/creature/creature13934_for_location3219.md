# The People of the Plateau

---

They are not what you expect. But then, nothing here is, and your expectations are one of the first things the plateau begins to wear down.

---

## Bodies

You notice their hands first. Everyone who writes about this place, everyone who has visited and returned (and not all return, and not all who return are certain they left), mentions the hands. They are broad and flat, the fingers wide-tipped, the palms calloused into surfaces that are themselves almost lithic — dense, smooth, faintly shining, the skin compressed by decades of stone-work into something that approximates the material it has spent a lifetime touching. The callouses are not rough. They are *polished*. An elder's palm, held up to the light, has a faint sheen to it, a translucency at the edges, as if the skin is slowly learning to be quartzite. Whether this is metaphor or diagnosis depends on how long you've been on the plateau when you notice it.

Their skin elsewhere is weathered in the way of all high-altitude people — a permanent flush beneath a surface that has been thinned by cold and wind and the UV that pours through air this sparse. But the thinning here is more than dermatological. It is as if the *thin-feeling* that the stone radiates has, over generations, been partially accepted by the bodies that live upon it. They are not translucent — not yet, not the ones who stay away from the edges — but there is a quality to them, a lightness of presence, as if they are not fully committed to their own solidity. They do not take up as much space as you expect them to. They move through a room and the room barely notices. They stand next to you and you have to keep reminding yourself they are there, not because they are quiet (though they are quiet) but because your eyes want to slide past them the way your eyes slide past stone.

They are not tall. The plateau would not allow it. The tallest among them stand perhaps a hand's breadth above what the lower world would consider average, and most are shorter, compact, built low to the ground like their buildings. There is a density to their frames that contradicts the thin-feeling — a solidity of bone and sinew that speaks to generations of labor against resistant material. They carry weight in their legs and hips, a low center of gravity, as if their bodies have been shaped by the same forces that shaped the landscape: pressed down, spread out, made stable against a world that would prefer them flat.

Their eyes are the color of the stone. Not metaphorically. Not *like* the stone. There is a grey-violet, grey-green, grey-grey quality to every iris you meet here, regardless of what other genetic inheritances are visible in their features. It happens to everyone eventually — visitors who stay long enough report the change, a slow leaching of original pigment replaced by something that looks less like color and more like mineral. The locals say the plateau *reads* through the eyes, which is why the stone-color settles there first. They do not explain what this means. They may not know. They may know and have determined that the explanation would not survive the journey to someone who has not yet felt it.

Their hair is coarse, dark, kept short or bound tight against the skull, practical against the wind. The older ones go grey early — or perhaps not grey, perhaps it is more accurate to say their hair begins to match their eyes, which have already begun to match the stone. An elder of sixty or seventy (ages are approximate here; the time-pooling makes precise counting both difficult and somewhat beside the point) is a study in convergence: grey skin, grey eyes, grey hair, the whole body slowly resolving toward the color of the plateau, as if personhood is a temporary deviation from landscape that will eventually be corrected.

They age well, or they age strangely, or they do not age in the way you understand aging. In the thick-time zones where they hold council, a decade of biological wear can be compressed into what feels like a long afternoon. In the thin-time zones where they sleep and grieve, years pass without leaving their full weight of damage. The result is people who look simultaneously old and not-old, whose faces carry the depth of long experience without the corresponding collapse of tissue — lined but not sagging, worn but not depleted. Like the stone. Everything here, eventually, becomes a metaphor for the stone, and then stops being a metaphor and starts being a description.

## The Way They Move

Watch them cross a stretch of open plateau between settlements, and you will understand something about this place that no amount of description can convey.

They do not walk the way you walk. They do not navigate by sight or by direction or by any spatial logic you would recognize. They walk by *sequence*. They have memorized the order — the landmarks, the pools, the cairns, the moss-ribbons — and they follow them the way a musician follows a score, not looking ahead to the destination but attending to the next note, the next passage, the next correct thing in the series. Their gaze is down and middle-distance, simultaneously watching the stone at their feet and the next marker on the path. They never look at the horizon. There is no point. The horizon here is not a reliable indicator of anything.

Their pace is even — not fast, not slow, but *constant*, a rhythm that has been calibrated to the plateau's own tempo, whatever that is. They do not speed up when eager. They do not slow down when tired. They walk the way water moves through stone: at the rate the medium allows, no faster, no slower, with a patience that is not virtue but physics. Watching them cross the open rock, you begin to understand that they are not traveling *through* the landscape so much as they are *being processed by* it, the way light is processed by a lens — bent, focused, delivered to the correct point by the properties of the medium rather than by any intention of their own.

When they arrive at a destination, they do not announce themselves. They are simply there, at the edge of a settlement, pausing for a moment to let the sequence complete itself, the way a sentence pauses at its period before the next one begins. Then they enter, and are absorbed, and the settlement adjusts to their presence the way a pool adjusts to a new drop of condensation: a faint ripple, then stillness.

Among themselves — within a settlement, moving between workshop and sleeping chamber, cistern and council space — their movements are different. Closer. More intricate. They navigate the narrow paths between buildings with an almost choreographic precision, bodies turning sideways to pass each other in spaces designed for single-file, hands trailing along stone walls in gestures that are half-navigation and half-greeting, fingertips reading the surface the way you might read a familiar face. They touch the buildings constantly, casually, a hand resting on a wall while they pause to speak, a shoulder leaning into a doorframe, a palm pressed flat against the stone floor while they sit. This is not affection, exactly. It is *calibration*. They are checking themselves against the stone, confirming that the boundary is still there, that the thin-feeling has not advanced too far since the last time they checked.

The children — and there are children, though fewer than you might expect, and quieter — move differently from the adults. They are more vertical, more willing to reach upward, to climb onto walls and perch on the roofs of low buildings and stretch their arms toward the sky in a way that makes the adults visibly uneasy. They have not yet learned the plateau's preference for flatness, or they are testing it, the way children everywhere test the rules of their world. The adults correct them gently: a hand on a shoulder, a downward pressure, a murmured word. *Low*. Not a command but a reminder. *Stay low*. The children comply eventually, though some of them — the ones who will later be drawn to the edges, the ones who will not stay — take longer than others.

## Language

Their language is unlike any you have encountered, and not only because its phonemes are shaped by mouths that have spent generations in thin, cold air.

It is a *flat* language. Not monotone — there is variation in pitch, in rhythm, in the length and weight of syllables — but flat in the sense that it avoids emphasis, avoids peaks and valleys, avoids the dramatic contours that most languages use to encode urgency, emotion, hierarchy. Sentences do not build to climaxes. Questions do not rise. Exclamations do not spike. Everything is delivered at the same even altitude, the same considered volume, the same unhurried pace. Listening to a conversation between two plateau-dwellers, you cannot tell by tone alone which parts are important and which are filler, which statements are questions and which are answers, which moments carry emotional weight and which are mere transition.

This is deliberate, and it took centuries to develop. The plateau, they learned, erodes emphasis the way it erodes height. Strong statements weaken. Loud declarations fade. Words spoken with too much force arrive at their listener already diminished, their meaning ground down in transit. The only speech that survives the plateau's attenuation intact is speech that was never elevated to begin with — low, even, modulated, each word carrying exactly the weight it needs and no more. Their language evolved to match this constraint, and the result is a mode of communication that sounds, to outsiders, almost pathologically calm.

But flatness is not emptiness. Within the narrow band of expression the language permits, there is extraordinary precision. They have seventeen words for the quality of stone surfaces, each describing a specific degree of smoothness and the grinding history it implies. They have separate grammars for thick-time and thin-time — not different tenses but different *structural logics*, because the relationship between cause and effect shifts depending on which zone you are speaking from. In thick-time grammar, consequences are embedded within the statement of action — the act and its result expressed as a single fused concept, inseparable, because in thick time they essentially are. In thin-time grammar, consequences are detached, floating, connected to their causes by loose particles that suggest correlation rather than certainty, because in thin time the link between doing and result is genuinely attenuated.

They have no word for *lost*. They have no word for *far*. Distance, as a concept independent from sequence, does not exist in their language. You do not say "the settlement is far away." You say something closer to "the settlement is many-sequenced" — meaning the number of landmarks between here and there is large. A place that is many-sequenced but reachable in an afternoon (because the distance-effect is favorable that day) is still described as many-sequenced. A place that is few-sequenced but takes a week to reach (because the distance has thickened against you) is still few-sequenced. The language describes the *topology* of the journey, not its extent. This is not a limitation. It is the only accurate way to talk about travel here.

The most untranslatable word — the one that visiting linguists stumble over and eventually give up on — is the term for what they are. Not their ethnic name, not their demonym, but the word they use to describe their *condition of being*: living things on a surface that is in the late stages of ontological erosion. The word is something like *those-who-are-still-distinct*, but this is crude, because the word contains within it an acknowledgment that the distinction is temporary, and a further acknowledgment that the temporariness is not tragic but appropriate, and a *still further* acknowledgment that the word itself, by naming the condition, is participating in the distinction and therefore in the temporariness — a recursive self-awareness that the language encodes in a single, flat, calmly spoken syllable cluster that takes about a second to say and a lifetime to mean.

## The Work

They are, above almost anything else, *workers*. Not in the driven, productive sense that lower-world cultures would understand — not laborers, not industrialists — but workers in the sense that the moss is a worker, the frost is a worker, the condensation that deepens the pools by imperceptible fractions is a worker. They do things slowly, correctly, continuously, and the distinction between labor and life has been eroded to a line as thin as the laminations in the stone.

The stone-workers are the most visible. Every settlement has them, bent over quartzite slabs in north-facing workshops where the directional quality of increased resolution makes their eyes sharper and their judgments more precise. They work with tools made from the same stone they are shaping, which gives their craft a recursive quality — stone shaping stone, the harder edge of this piece finding and exploiting the hidden grain of that one, both altered by the encounter. The workshops are quiet but not silent. The *chk, chk* of controlled strikes. The long *shhhh* of abrasion. The occasional low murmur as a worker consults with a neighbor about a fault line or an unexpected inclusion. The tools they produce are beautiful in the way that all perfectly functional things are beautiful: scrapers that fit the curl of a specific palm, blades that find the angle of a specific cut, bowls whose curves are calculated to hold exactly the amount of water one person needs and not a drop more.

The moss-workers tend the fissure-gardens with a delicacy that approaches the surgical. They know each quartzite vein in their settlement's territory — its mineral content, its moisture retention, its hospitality to which variety of moss. They propagate by careful spore-distribution, using flat stones as palettes on which they mix spore-dust with powdered rock and condensation-water into a slurry that is painted along promising new cracks with brushes made from dried moss-fiber. The success rate is low. A new moss-ribbon may take years to establish, and many attempts fail entirely, the crack too dry or too wet, the stone chemistry subtly wrong, the wind-patterns unfavorable. The moss-workers keep records — not written but *spatial*, marked into the stone itself as shallow scratches that encode planting dates, spore varieties, outcomes. A moss-worker's territory, read by someone who knows the notation, is a living document of decades of patient experiment.

The pool-readers are something else.

Every settlement designates certain individuals — usually older, usually quieter even by the standards of a quiet people, usually marked by a particular intensity of stone-color in their eyes — to monitor the reflection pools. These are the ones who sit at the low-walled pools for hours, watching the adjacent possibilities cycle through, noting the variations, interpreting what the differences mean. This is not prophecy. They are emphatic about this. The pools do not show the future. They show *other presents*, versions of now that branched from this one at some point in the past and are still close enough to be reflected. The pool-readers' skill lies in determining how far each reflection has diverged, in which direction, and whether the divergence is increasing or decreasing — whether the other version of events is moving away from this one or approaching it, whether it might, at some point, become more real than what is currently happening.

This last possibility is taken seriously. It has happened. Not often, and not within living memory (though living memory, here, is a complicated concept), but the records — scratched into stone, patient and flat as the language itself — describe instances when a reflection overtook reality, when the world above the pool shifted to match the world within it, and what had been real became the reflection instead. The people who were living in the version that became reflected did not cease to exist, exactly. They became *less resolved*. They could be seen, faintly, in the pools — going about their lives, grinding moss-flour, walking their sequenced paths — but they could no longer be touched, and their voices, if they spoke, arrived as those flat, neutral tones that all plateau-sound eventually adopts. The current pool-readers watch for the signs of such an inversion. The slight brightening of the reflected image. The corresponding dimming of the world above. The moment when the half-second delay in reflected light begins to close.

## The Elders

The oldest among them are difficult to look at directly. Not because they are frightening or grotesque, but because they have become so convergent with the stone that the eye struggles to separate them from it. An elder sitting on the plateau's surface, motionless in a thick-time zone, can be mistaken for a natural formation — a slight rise in the rock, a shadow that seems to have more substance than a shadow should. Their skin has taken on not just the color but the *texture* of the metamorphic surface, faintly laminated, faintly sheened, the individual characteristics of face and body slowly averaging out toward the plateau's own featureless expression.

They are not dying. Or rather, they are not dying in a way the word *dying* is equipped to describe. They are undergoing the same process as everything else here: the long, patient erosion of distinction, the wearing-away of the boundary between this particular thing and the general surface. Some of them speak of it with what might be anticipation. Some speak of it with what might be resignation. Most speak of it in the flat, uninflected language of observation, as if reporting on the weather. *The frost is forming. The moss is advancing. I am becoming less distinct.* These statements carry equal weight in the grammar. They are equally local, equally natural, equally part of the plateau's ongoing negotiation with the concept of separateness.

The very oldest — the ones who have passed beyond the point where conversation is easy, whose voices have thinned to something barely distinguishable from the sound of wind on stone — are tended by the young. Not nursed, because they are not sick. Not mourned, because they are not gone. Tended, the way the moss-ribbons are tended: with patience, with attention, with the understanding that what is happening is a slow transition from one state to another, and that both states are real, and that the transition itself is the thing that matters most, and that to rush it would be to misunderstand everything the plateau has spent a billion years trying to say.

When an elder finally resolves entirely — when the last distinction between person and stone has been eroded — the settlement marks the spot with a small cairn. Not a memorial. A *notation*. Here is where this happened. Here is where the stone accepted another complexity into its record, another lamination in its endless compressed archive. The cairns are indistinguishable from the navigation cairns that mark the sequenced paths, and this is intentional. The journey between settlements and the journey between states of being are described by the same grammar. They are the same kind of passage. They differ only in sequence-length, and sequence-length, as we have established, is not the same as distance.

## What They Believe

*Believe* is too sharp a word. It implies a peak of conviction, a height of certainty, and the plateau would begin immediately to flatten it. What they have instead is something more like a *settled understanding* — a set of observations that have been repeated so many times, across so many generations, that they have worn themselves into the same kind of smoothness as everything else here. They are not articles of faith. They are descriptions of the furniture.

They understand that the plateau is not a place but a *process* — the late stage of an erosion that will eventually reduce everything to a single undifferentiated surface. They understand that this process acts on all categories of thing: stone, time, distance, selfhood, the distinction between what is and what almost is. They understand that they are participating in the process, that their presence here is not separate from the plateau's work but part of it — that they are, in some sense, the plateau eroding *itself*, because they are made of its stone and eat its moss and drink its condensation and will, eventually, return their borrowed complexity to its surface.

They do not consider this nihilistic. They do not consider it anything. It is simply the shape of the situation, observed from inside, described in a language that has been carefully designed to neither elevate nor diminish it. If you pressed them — which you would not, because the plateau erodes the impulse to press — they might say that the purpose of being distinct is to *experience the distinctness while it lasts*, the way the purpose of a frost crystal is to be briefly and extraordinarily architectural before the morning comes. They might say this, or they might say something else, or they might simply look at you with their stone-colored eyes and wait for you to understand that the question has already been ground down past the point where it can hold an answer.

## The Children

The children are the most fully alive things on the plateau, which means they are also the most endangered.

They are born in the south-facing rooms where time is soft, and their births are attended by the whole settlement — not out of ceremony but out of practical concern, because a new life is an *increase in distinction*, a sharp local spike in the plateau's otherwise monotonically decreasing curve of differentiation, and the moment of birth is the moment when the plateau's erosive attention is most focused. The birthing rooms are built with extra-thick walls. The stone is selected for density, for resistance, for a quality the stone-workers call *stubbornness* — a reluctance to be further compressed. The rooms are small and warm and insulated, tiny pockets of defiance against the leveling force, and the first thing a child hears, they say, is the sound of the stone walls holding.

The children grow up fast by necessity. Not physically — their bodies develop at the usual rate, perhaps even slower in the time-dilated sleeping rooms — but *ontologically*. They must learn quickly to manage their own distinction, to keep themselves separate from the surface without asserting so much separateness that the flattening notices them. This is the core skill of plateau life, the thing that cannot be taught by instruction but only by long, careful example: how to be a person lightly, how to hold your selfhood at exactly the right altitude — high enough to be functional, low enough to be tolerated.

The children who learn this well become stone-workers, moss-tenders, pool-readers, parents. The children who do not learn it — the ones who burn too bright, who insist too loudly on their own existence, who reach upward when they should be reaching along — become restless, then thin, then drawn toward the edges where the mist rises and the sound carries wrong and the thin-feeling is strongest. Some of them leave, descending into the cloud, going down to the lower world where distinction is cheap and plentiful and nobody has to negotiate with the ground for the right to be separate from it. Some of them stay and become a different kind of elder — not the kind that converges with the stone, but the kind that remains stubbornly, painfully distinct long past the age when convergence should have begun, their personhood hardened into something the plateau cannot easily digest, a knot in the grain that will take extra centuries to grind smooth.

These hard elders are regarded with a complex mixture of respect and discomfort. They are proof that resistance is possible. They are also proof that resistance is, in the long view, a delay rather than a solution. The plateau is patient. The plateau has already worn down mountains. A single hard elder, however stubborn, however bright-eyed and sharp-voiced and insistent on the boundaries of self, is a minor inconsistency in a project that has been running for a billion years. The stone will find a way. It always has.

## The Nights

At night the plateau becomes more itself.

The cold deepens. The frost grows. The reflection pools, unobserved (or at least un-looked-at; the pool-readers maintain that observation and looking are not the same thing, and that the pools are always observed by the stone itself), cycle faster through their adjacent possibilities, the surfaces flickering like thought, like something trying to make up its mind. The sky is vast and crowded with stars that are, somehow, not quite the same stars as the ones visible from any other point on the planet — shifted slightly, rearranged, as if the plateau's height has pushed it into a zone where the sky's own agreements about its layout have begun to soften.

The people sleep in their south-facing rooms on their raised platforms, their bodies held just above the stone by layers of felt and dried moss that act as insulation against both cold and metaphysics. They sleep deeply. They dream, presumably, though they do not speak of dreams — the language has no word for them, or rather, has folded the concept into the same word used for the pool-reflections, which suggests that dreams and adjacent possibilities are, here, the same category of phenomenon.

In sleep, the thin-feeling advances. Every morning, they wake a fraction less distinct than they were the night before. This is not measurable by any instrument. It is not visible in any mirror (there are no mirrors on the plateau; the reflection pools are enough). It is simply known, felt, accepted the way one accepts that each exhalation carries away a few cells that will not be replaced. The morning routine includes, for many of them, a practice that has no name but could be described as *re-collection*: a few minutes of deliberate, focused selfhood, sitting on the platform, hands pressed together, feeling the boundaries of the body with conscious attention, reminding the skin where it ends and the air begins. It is not meditation. It is *maintenance*. The way you might check a wall for cracks after a long night of frost.

Then they rise, and they work, and the work is its own form of re-collection, because to shape stone is to assert a difference between the shaper and the shaped, and this assertion, repeated daily, is what keeps them distinct enough to continue.

## What They Are

They are the plateau's memory that it was once not flat.

They are the lingering evidence of complexity in a system that is trending toward simplicity. They are the last few wrinkles in a surface that is almost smooth, and their lives — their births and their labors and their slow convergences and their stubborn resistances — are the wrinkles' way of experiencing themselves before they are ironed out.

They are stone that has briefly, improbably, organized itself into something that can observe stone. They are the plateau looking at itself through eyes that are already beginning to look like it. They are a billion years of erosion's only witnesses, and they are eroding too, and they know it, and they grind their moss and read their pools and walk their sequenced paths with the calm of people who have understood, at a level deeper than language, deeper than belief, deeper than the bone that is already half mineral, that the distinction between the watcher and the watched was always temporary, was always a courtesy the universe extended to itself so that the grinding-down would not go unnoticed.

They are what lives here: the plateau's last, low, quiet assertion that flatness is not yet total, that there are still a few thin places where the stone rises, barely, into something that can look around and say *I am not yet the ground*, even as the ground pulls gently, patiently, at the edges of the claim.

The frost is forming. *Tik. Tik. Tik.*

They sleep in the soft time, and in the morning they will be almost exactly what they were, and the almost is the whole of their story.
