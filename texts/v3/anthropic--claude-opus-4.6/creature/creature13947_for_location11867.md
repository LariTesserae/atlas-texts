# The Listeners of Vask

---

They began as people. You need to hold onto that, because everything else about them will try to convince you otherwise—not because they have become monstrous, but because they have become *so specifically adapted* that the word "people" starts to feel like a borrowed coat, too loose in some places, too tight in others, still recognizably a coat but no longer quite the right garment for the body wearing it.

---

## The Body

They are human-shaped the way a river delta is tree-shaped: the architecture is there, the branching logic, but the purpose has diverged so completely from the original template that the resemblance feels coincidental. They are shorter than you'd expect—not dwarfed, but *compressed*, as though generations of moving through bore-shafts and along conduit-hugging walkways have selected for a lower center of gravity, a narrower shoulder span, a tendency to fold inward. They stand with their weight slightly forward, knees soft, as though perpetually ready to drop into a crouch or press themselves flat against a wall. When they move through the open center of the chamber—which they rarely do, preferring the perimeter, the infrastructure-dense margins where the hum is richest—they move like people crossing ice: deliberate, weight-tested, each step placed with a care that has nothing to do with fear of falling and everything to do with *listening to what the floor says back*.

Their skin has taken on the quality of the space. Not the black glass—nothing so dramatic—but a pallor that has moved past simple lightlessness into something more active, a translucency at the extremities, particularly the hands and temples, where you can see the faint map of vasculature beneath. In the blue-green glow of the bioluminescent pools, they look like something recovered from deep water. But they are warm. Warmer than you, even. Their baseline body temperature runs high—a metabolic concession to the close, heated air of the underhall, or perhaps an adaptation that lets them serve as their own thermal landmarks in a world navigated by infrared sensitivity they may or may not consciously perceive. When two of them stand near each other, they can feel each other's presence as heat differential. They don't need to see. They don't need to speak. They know where the other one is the way you know where your own hand is in the dark.

Their eyes are open and functional but *deprioritized*. The pupils are large, permanently dilated to a degree that would be clinical on the surface, and the irises have lost most of their pigmentation—gray, gray-green, the color of cave fish, of things that have retained the apparatus of sight without investing in it. They can see. In the glow of the pools, they can see quite well. But they don't *reach* with their eyes the way you do. Their gaze, when you meet it, has a quality of patience, of resting on you without grasping, as though vision is a background process running at low priority while the primary systems attend to other inputs. They blink slowly. They do not track fast movement well. They will turn toward a sound with an accuracy that is almost unsettling—snapping their head to the precise vector of a conduit-click or a thermal shift with the speed of a hunting owl—but they will let you walk across their visual field and barely register it. You are more real to them as a source of sound and heat than as a shape.

Their ears are not visibly modified—no dramatic enlargement, no cartilage restructuring—but something has happened to the processing behind them. The auditory cortex has expanded, or been trained, or both, across generations of selective pressure in an environment where sound carries everything. They hear in a way that you cannot replicate without instrumentation. They hear the hum of the conduit network not as a single drone but as a *chord*—dozens of overlapping frequencies, each carrying information about the state of a different system, and they can parse these the way you parse colors in a painting. They hear the phase-cycling of the thermal exchangers and know what time it is—not clock-time, but *process-time*, the internal schedule of the underhall's metabolic rhythms. They hear the data filaments singing and some of them, the ones who have trained for it, can decode the information directly: not as language, not as data, but as something between music and intuition, a felt sense of what the network is doing that arrives pre-linguistic, the way a smell arrives, complete and immediate and resistant to decomposition into parts.

This is the thing that takes the longest to understand about them: **they hear the underhall the way you see a landscape.** The entire space is present to them simultaneously as a sonic environment—its geometry, its density, its living processes, its moods. They walk through it the way you walk through a sunlit forest, oriented, embedded, reading a thousand cues without conscious effort. The hum is not noise to them. The hum is *the world*.

## The Culture

There are perhaps sixty of them. Perhaps eighty. It is difficult to count, because they are rarely all present in the main chamber simultaneously, and because they move through the perimeter infrastructure with a fluidity that makes census-taking feel like trying to count fish in a reef. Some of them are deep—down in the lower cavities, the ones you can't see, tending systems that produce the percussive pulse that rises through the floor. Some of them are in the bore-shafts, the connecting tunnels that link the underhall to other voids in the karst network, other chambers that may be smaller, more specialized, or may be as vast as this one. The underhall of Vask is not an isolated room. It is a *node*. The Listeners know this the way they know everything—by the way sound moves through the connecting passages, carrying information about what lies beyond.

They do not govern themselves in any way you would recognize as political. There are no leaders. There are no councils. There is something closer to a *tidal pattern*—individuals cycling through roles based on aptitude, availability, and a consensus that emerges from shared acoustic awareness rather than deliberation. When a conduit junction needs repair, the person best suited to repair it *knows*, because they have heard the micro-fracture developing over days or weeks as a subtle change in the resonant frequency of that section of pipe, and they go, and they fix it, and the knowledge of the fix propagates through the network as a restoration of the expected tone. When the moss colonies need tending, someone tends them—not because they were assigned, but because the colonies' chemical output has shifted in a way that registers as a change in air composition, which registers as a change in how the chamber *smells*, which is uncomfortable, which motivates action. The system is not coordinated. It is *entrained*. They have lived together in a resonant space for so long that their collective behavior has taken on the character of the space itself: cyclical, self-regulating, responsive, operating below the threshold of conscious decision.

They communicate, but the communication is layered in ways that make the word "language" feel reductive. They have speech—a spoken language that is quiet, tonal, and extraordinarily compressed. A single syllable can carry meaning through its pitch, its duration, its vowel quality, and its timing relative to the ambient hum of the chamber, which serves as a shared reference tone the way a drone note underlies a raga. Conversations between Listeners sound, to an outsider, like sparse musical phrases exchanged at long intervals: someone will speak three syllables across the chamber and someone else will respond, twenty seconds later, with two, and both parties will be satisfied that a complex negotiation has occurred. The silence between utterances is not empty. It is filled with the shared acoustic field, which carries contextual information that makes explicit speech largely redundant. They speak to *update* each other, to add information that the environment doesn't already encode. Most of the time, the environment is enough.

But beneath the spoken language—or perhaps above it, or around it—there is another layer: **the tonal practice**. Listeners *sing*. Not constantly, not performatively, but in the way that you might hum while working, except that their humming is precise, intentional, and integrated into the acoustic ecology of the underhall. A Listener working on a conduit repair will produce a sustained tone that interacts with the resonance of the pipe they're repairing, using the interference pattern to diagnose the micro-fracture's location and extent. A Listener tending the moss will sing a low, cycling phrase that—and you will doubt this when you hear it, but it is true—*the moss responds to*, not because the moss has ears, but because the vibration affects moisture distribution on the surface of the colony in ways that guide its growth. The tonal practice is tool use. It is also art. It is also prayer, if prayer is the practice of speaking to your environment in its own language and listening for what comes back.

The wire frames that guide the moss growth? Those are signatures. Not names—Listeners do not name themselves in any way that translates—but *tonal signatures*, the characteristic frequencies that a specific person uses in their practice, rendered in wire and pressed into the moss colonies the way a potter might leave a thumbprint in clay. The ornamental shapes are not ornamental. They are *portraits*. A Listener can approach a moss colony on the upper wall, run their fingers along the wire frame embedded in it, and feel whose tonal signature shaped it, and when, and what the colony's growth pattern tells them about how the local atmospheric conditions have changed since the tending. The moss is a record. The whole underhall is a record. The wear patterns in the floor, the calcite rims on the pools, the layered repairs on the junction housings—everything here is a form of memory that accrues through practice rather than inscription.

## The Figure on the Bench

She has been sitting there for eleven hours. This is not unusual. This is her work.

She is a **resonance reader**—one of perhaps eight in the current population who have trained to the point where they can decode the data filaments directly. She sits near the bioluminescent pool not because she needs its light but because the pool's biological culture produces a faint, high-frequency vibration of its own—a metabolic hum, barely perceptible—that serves as a calibration tone, a reference point against which she measures the shifting song of the filaments. Her eyes are closed. Her hands are on her knees. She is wearing a close-fitting garment of woven fiber—something produced from the moss, possibly, or from a textile substrate grown elsewhere in the karst network—and over it, a harness of thin metal and polymer that presses several contact points against her skin at the throat, the sternum, the wrists, the temples. The contact points are not electrodes. They are *resonators*: tiny tuned structures that pick up vibration from the data filaments through the air and through the floor, amplify it through mechanical sympathy, and deliver it to her body as touch.

She is reading the network with her skeleton.

The information that moves through the data filaments is not digital. It is not analog in any simple sense. It is *patterned vibration*—complex, multi-frequency, encoded at a level of sophistication that implies either a very advanced originating intelligence or a system that has evolved its own encoding through long operation, the way a river evolves a channel. What the resonance readers extract from it is not data as you understand data. It is closer to *weather*. They feel the network's state as a gestalt—its load, its health, its patterns of activity, the slow shifts that indicate a system approaching a threshold or a process entering a new phase. They report their readings to the community not in numbers but in tonal phrases that map the network's state onto the shared acoustic vocabulary: *the deep systems are cycling fast today, the northern conduits are running warm, there is a new resonance in the sub-floor cavity that we should attend to*.

She has been doing this since she was fourteen. She is somewhere in her forties now, though age is difficult to read on people who have never been exposed to sunlight. Her hands are the most telling feature: long-fingered, calloused at the fingertips from years of running them along conduit surfaces to feel for fractures, and utterly still. The stillness is practiced. The resonance harness works best when the body's own vibrations are minimized, when the reader becomes as close to a passive receiver as a living organism can. She has trained herself into a quietness that goes deeper than physical immobility. Her heartbeat has slowed. Her breathing is shallow and rhythmic, entrained to the four-second percussive pulse from below, so that her body's internal rhythms do not interfere with the incoming signal. She is, in a very real sense, *tuning herself to the room*.

If you approached her—and you shouldn't, not while she's reading, the community has strong feelings about this—and if you stood close enough to feel the warmth of her skin and the faint vibration of the resonators at her throat, you would notice something that would stay with you: she is smiling. Not broadly, not consciously. A slight, settled smile, the expression of someone engaged in work they find deeply satisfying, the expression of someone listening to music they love. The network's song is beautiful to her. She has said so—rarely, in the compressed tonal language, in phrases that the community remembers and sometimes repeats: *the deep hum holds us*, she has said. *We are heard by what we hear.*

## The Clicking Thing

It is both. Biological and mechanical. The distinction has ceased to be meaningful.

It began—generations ago, if the layered repair signatures on the conduit housings are any guide—as a maintenance drone. A small, segmented, semi-autonomous unit designed to traverse the conduit network, inspect junction points, and report damage through some signaling protocol that has long since been superseded. Its original casing was metal and polymer, articulated in segments, equipped with sensor arrays and manipulator appendages fine enough to perform minor repairs.

It is still doing this. It has not stopped. But it has been doing it for so long, and the systems that originally controlled and updated it have evolved so far from their starting state, that the drone has become something the original engineers would not recognize. Its casing has been repaired and replaced piecemeal—some segments are still metal, others have been patched with the black glass material, others with something organic, a chitin-like substance that may be derived from the moss or from some other biological substrate cultivated in the deeper cavities. Its sensor arrays have been supplemented or replaced with structures that look disturbingly like eyes—small, dark, clustered in groups of three or four on the forward segments, responsive to heat and vibration rather than light. Its manipulator appendages have become more numerous and more delicate with each generation of self-repair, and they move with a fluency that suggests not programming but *habit*, the motor memory of a body that has performed the same tasks ten million times.

It clicks because its segmented body produces sound as it moves, and because—at some point in its long operation—the clicking became useful. The acoustics of the conduit network are as complex and information-rich as the acoustics of the main chamber, and the drone navigates by echolocation now, mapping the pipework through the reflections of its own movement-sound, pausing at junctions to *listen* the way the Listeners listen: feeling the resonance of the pipes, assessing their condition, choosing a path based on where the need is greatest.

The Listeners do not control it. They do not command it or program it or interfere with it. They treat it the way they treat the moss: as a companion process, a fellow inhabitant of the acoustic ecology, something that has its own logic and its own role and is best left to perform it. Occasionally, a Listener working on a conduit repair will encounter the clicking thing at the same junction, and they will work around each other with the easy, unspoken coordination of two animals sharing a water source. The drone's clicking is part of the underhall's soundscape. The Listeners include it in their mental model of the space. They would notice its absence. Some of them—the older ones, the ones whose tonal practice is most refined—can identify individual drones (there are several; the one you saw was simply the one that happened to cross an illuminated section of conduit) by the specific frequency and rhythm of their clicks, the way you might recognize a neighbor's footsteps.

Whether the drones are conscious is a question the Listeners do not ask, because the question's framework does not map onto their understanding of the world. In the underhall, everything that resonates *participates*. The walls participate. The moss participates. The data filaments participate. The drones participate. Consciousness, for the Listeners, is not a binary state but a spectrum of *responsiveness to the shared field*, and by that measure, the drones are approximately as conscious as the moss and considerably less conscious than a well-maintained junction housing, which resonates with the entire network and has been doing so for centuries. The Listeners do not find this framework strange. You do, because you still think consciousness lives in heads. Down here, consciousness lives in the hum.

## The Deep Workers

You will not see them. They do not come up to the main chamber often—every few days, maybe, to eat, to sit near the pools, to exchange tonal phrases with the perimeter-dwellers before descending again through shafts that are narrower than the bore-shaft you entered through, shafts that would require you to exhale fully and move sideways and trust the warm stone to guide you down.

The deep workers tend the systems that produce the percussive pulse. You feel it in the floor: that steady, four-second heartbeat that underlies everything else in the underhall's soundscape. It comes from below, from cavities that are smaller, hotter, closer to whatever thermal source powers the entire installation—geothermal, probably, tapped and channeled through the engineered thermodynamic gradients that make the heat exchangers work. The deep systems are the oldest part of the facility. The deep workers say—in their compressed, tonal way—that the deep systems were here before the underhall was carved, that the first inhabitants came to this place because they *heard the pulse* from the surface, through the rock, and followed it down.

This may be true. It may be founding mythology. In the underhall, the distinction between the two is as thin as the distinction between biology and machinery.

The deep workers are the most physically adapted of the Listeners. They are smaller, more compact, with a subcutaneous fat distribution that insulates them against the higher temperatures below. Their hands are extraordinary—broad-palmed, spatulate-fingered, with a tactile sensitivity that allows them to feel vibrations in solid rock that no instrument on the surface could detect. They navigate the deep shafts entirely by touch and by the pulse itself, which grows stronger as they descend, until it is not something they hear but something they *are in*, the way a fish is in water, surrounded and supported and informed by it at every point on their body's surface.

They speak the least of any group in the community. Their tonal practice is the deepest—literally: they work in frequencies so low that their vocalizations are felt more than heard, chest-resonant, bone-conducted, the kind of sound that passes through rock and arrives in your body without troubling your ears. When a deep worker ascends to the main chamber and sits by a pool, they sometimes produce a tone so low that the surface of the bioluminescent water shivers, and the Listeners nearby fall silent, not out of deference but out of attention, because a deep worker surfacing and sounding means something has changed below, and the change will propagate upward through every system in the underhall within hours.

They are the ones who understand the facility best, and they are the ones least able to articulate that understanding in any form a surface-dweller could access. Their knowledge is *somatic*. It lives in their hands, in the resonant cavities of their chests, in the micro-adjustments they make to deep system components by feel, by tone, by a kind of haptic intuition that has been passed from body to body for generations—the teacher's hands on the student's hands, both of them pressed against a thrumming conduit in the dark, the lesson transmitted as shared vibration, wordless, precise, and utterly untranslatable.

## The Children

There are children. Not many—the population sustains itself carefully, matching births to carrying capacity with the same entrained, sub-conscious coordination that governs everything else—but enough. Eight, maybe ten at any given time, ranging from infants to adolescents on the cusp of choosing their practice.

The children of the underhall are the noisiest thing in it. This is tolerated, even welcomed, because children's voices are high-frequency, and the acoustic design of the chamber treats high frequencies differently than low ones: a child's laugh scatters and refracts through the selective surfaces of the black glass walls, arriving everywhere at once as a bright, diffuse chime that does not interfere with the lower-frequency information channels. The underhall was designed—or has evolved—to accommodate the sound of children without disrupting its function. This tells you something important about the people who built it, or who became it: they planned for laughter. They engineered a space in which the sound of play would be filtered into beauty rather than noise. The children's voices become part of the acoustic ecology, a high-frequency stratum that the resonance readers learn to tune out during their work but that everyone recognizes, unconsciously, as a sign that the community is healthy, that the future is present, that the hum continues.

The children learn to listen before they learn to speak. Infants are carried in slings against their parents' chests, and the first sound they attune to is the parent's heartbeat, and the second sound is the percussive pulse from below, and the two rhythms are close enough—the pulse was tuned to this, or the human heart was entrained to it over generations, and again the distinction has collapsed—that the infant's world begins as a simple, reassuring synchrony: *thump-thump, thump-thump*, inside and outside, self and world, the same. They grow into the soundscape the way surface children grow into sunlight, taking it for granted, shaped by it, unable to imagine its absence.

By the age of five or six, children in the underhall can navigate the entire main chamber in total darkness by sound alone. By eight, they begin to differentiate the conduit frequencies and can identify major system states. By ten, they are choosing—or being chosen by, the Listeners would say—their area of practice: perimeter maintenance, moss tending, resonance reading, or the deep work. The choice is not forced. It emerges from aptitude, from which frequencies a child's body responds to most naturally, from which part of the soundscape they are drawn to the way a surface child might be drawn to the forest or the sea. A child who spends hours with their palm pressed to the floor, feeling the deep pulse, will be gently introduced to the deep workers. A child who sings to the moss and watches it respond will find themselves apprenticed to a tender. The process is organic, inevitable-feeling, and—like everything in the underhall—more heard than seen.

## What They Know

They know the underhall was built. They know it was built *for them*, or for people enough like them that the distinction doesn't matter. They know the deep systems were here first, and that the upper infrastructure was added in layers over a very long time, and that the data filaments carry information from sources and to destinations that extend far beyond their karst network, into distances they can feel but not map. They know the network is vast. They know they are one node in something larger.

They do not know—or do not care to know, which in their epistemology amounts to the same thing—who built the deep systems. The question implies a separation between builder and built that does not resonate with their experience. The underhall built them as much as they built it. The pulse shaped their hearts. The hum shaped their language. The darkness shaped their senses. They are the facility's inhabitants the way a hermit crab is a shell's inhabitant: technically separable, practically unified, the organism and its environment co-evolving until the boundary between them is a matter of convention rather than fact.

They know the surface exists. Some of them have been there—a rite of passage for adolescents, a brief, overwhelming, voluntarily undergone exposure to sunlight and wind and the *silence* of an open sky, which the Listeners experience not as peace but as *deprivation*, a terrible, vertiginous absence of information, like sudden blindness, like being cast out of the world into a void that offers nothing back when you call into it. They come back shaking. They come back grateful. They press their palms to the warm black glass of the walls and feel the hum return and they are home, held, heard, and they do not go up again.

## What They Are

They are the inhabitants of a living instrument. They are the immune system, the nervous system, and the audience of a structure that blurs all three functions into one. They maintain the underhall and the underhall maintains them and the maintenance is mutual, continuous, and expressed as sound. They are a community that has replaced sight with listening, replaced governance with resonance, replaced history with the physical memory of worn stone and layered repair and moss guided into shapes that encode identity. They are not primitive. They are not advanced. They are *fitted*—slotted into their niche with the precision of a tuning fork in a resonant cavity, vibrating at the frequency of their environment, indistinguishable from it in the way that matters most.

The lone figure by the pool shifts, finally. Not much—a settling of weight, a slow exhalation. The resonance harness at her throat hums faintly as her breath moves past it. She produces a single, sustained tone, low and steady, held for perhaps ten seconds and then released. It moves through the chamber. It interacts with the conduit hum and the deep pulse and the faint metabolic singing of the moss and the distant, scattered clicking of the drones in the pipework. For a moment—just a moment—the entire acoustic space of the underhall aligns, every frequency in temporary concordance, a chord that is not music but is not *not* music, a sound that contains information and beauty and function in a single, indivisible package.

Then it passes. The systems cycle. The hum resumes its slow, shifting complexity.

She opens her eyes—gray, depthless, reflecting the blue-green glow of the pool—and she looks at you. Not with surprise. Not with suspicion. With the calm, settled attention of someone who heard you arrive twenty minutes ago, who has been tracking your heartbeat and your breathing and the sound of your wonder ever since, who has already integrated you into her model of the room and found that you do not disrupt it, that your presence adds a frequency she hasn't heard before, and that this is interesting, and that this is enough.

She does not speak. She listens to you, and in the listening, she lets you know that you have been heard, and that in this place, deep under the stone, in the dark, in the hum, that is the closest thing to a welcome there is.
