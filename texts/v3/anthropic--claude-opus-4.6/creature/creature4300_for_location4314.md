# Who Lives in the Pocket Under the Hull

---

Start with the nematodes, because they were here first, or claim to have been, or exist in such numbers that the distinction between "first" and "always" has been negotiated away. They are not individuals. That's the wrong frame. Each one is a transparent thread — a gut visible through a body that is mostly just the means of moving that gut from one point to another — and alone, any single nematode is nothing more than hunger given the simplest possible architecture. But they are not alone. They are never alone. They exist in densities that turn the sand itself into a living textile, a woven thing, their bodies threading between grains in patterns that shift continuously but never tangle. They have achieved something down there in the interstitial dark that is not intelligence and not cooperation but something older than both: a mutual *fitting*. Each one moves in the space left by the others' moving. They are a liquid that happens to be made of animals.

They eat the bacteria. This is their entire purpose, if purpose is a word you can use for something this reflexive. The bacteria coat the sand grains in biofilms — slick, invisible skins of metabolism — and the nematodes graze these films with mouthparts that are little more than muscular rings, clenching and unclenching, pulling sustenance from surfaces. The bacteria eat the sulfur that seeps from above. The nematodes eat the bacteria. Nothing eats the nematodes, not exactly, not here — when they die, they simply become part of the substrate, their transparent bodies collapsing into the organic slurry that feeds the bacteria that feed the next generation. The cycle is so tight, so closed, that it resembles less an ecosystem than a single organism breathing.

When the cracks come — those moments of tearing, of revoked *hereness* — the nematodes do not freeze the way the others do. They cannot. Freezing requires a nervous system complex enough to register alarm, and theirs is not. Instead, the crack passes through them, and for the duration of its passage, each nematode briefly exists in two states: present and absent, threaded through sand and threaded through nothing. They don't experience this. They resume. But afterward, for a few seconds, their movements are subtly different — slightly more synchronized, slightly more aligned with the engine-throb from above, as if the crack has tuned them, reset some oscillation in their bodies that was drifting out of phase. They are the pocket's most fundamental residents, and they are also its most calibrated instruments, though they measure nothing, record nothing, report to no one.

---

The **polychaete worms** are the architects, and they know it. Know is too strong. They *behave as if* they know it, which in a space this small, this ontologically uncertain, may be the same thing.

There are perhaps forty of them — perhaps sixty — the count depends on whether you include the juveniles, the fragments, the tubes that have been abandoned but still stand like monuments in a city whose residents have moved one block over. Each worm lives inside a tube of its own construction: mucus secreted from glands along the body, hardened by contact with seawater, studded with whatever material was close at hand during construction. Sand grains, mostly. Plankton shells. And increasingly, flakes of rust — tiny, brittle scales of oxidized iron that have drifted down from the hull and been incorporated into the tube walls with what looks like deliberate placement.

The rust-armored tubes are the ones that catch the eye, if there were an eye here to catch. They glow faintly orange in the sourceless, tea-colored light. They are stronger than the plain tubes, more rigid, and the worms that inhabit them are — there is no other word for it — *bolder*. Their feeding tentacles extend farther into the water column. They retract more slowly when disturbed. One of them, a large individual whose tube rises nearly to the hull itself, has begun to incorporate not just rust but small flakes of intact metal — shavings, perhaps, or fragments loosened by the thermal cycling of the iron above. This worm's tube has a faint magnetic quality. Iron filings in the water drift toward it, accumulate around its opening, giving it a dark halo. The worm inside is pale, segmented, eyeless, and utterly unaware that it is building something that looks, from certain angles, like a small exhaust port. Like a miniature echo of the hull's own architecture.

This is not mimicry. The worm has no concept of the hull. It is simply building with available materials, and the available materials carry the hull's identity in their chemistry, their texture, their magnetic signatures. The tube becomes hull-like because it is *made of hull*. And in this way — through the blind industry of a creature no longer than a grain of rice — the boundary between the living and the mechanical blurs. The worm is building itself into the machine. The machine is shedding itself into the worm. Neither notices.

The polychaetes breathe by waving feathery gills inside their tubes, drawing water through in slow, steady currents. These currents are tiny — microliters per minute — but they are *structured*. They pull water from specific strata: the warmer water near the hull, or the cooler water rising through the sand, depending on the worm's position and, it seems, some kind of preference. Some worms prefer warm water. Some prefer cold. There is no physiological reason for this. They simply do. And their collective breathing — forty, sixty small currents pulling and pushing in a space the width of two palms — creates a secondary circulation pattern in the pocket, a network of micro-currents that overlays the thermal convection and the engine-driven vibrations and the drift of particulate. The worms are not just living in the pocket. They are *plumbing* it. They are, without intention or awareness, maintaining the conditions that allow the pocket to sustain what it sustains.

When the cracks come, the polychaetes retract. Every one of them, instantly, tentacles snapping back into tubes with a speed that suggests the retraction is not a decision but a reflex triggered by something deeper than fear — by the sudden absence of water pressure along the line of the crack, perhaps, or by a vibration too low for anything but a worm's body to detect. They stay retracted for seconds afterward, sometimes longer, their tubes sealed with opercula of hardened mucus. And inside those sealed tubes, in the dark, in the stillness, each worm pulses to the rhythm of the engine-throb and waits for the world to re-commit to existing.

---

The **copepods** are the ones who seem most like *inhabitants* in the way you'd normally mean the word — creatures with trajectories, with behaviors that suggest something like decision-making, with enough complexity that watching one for a few minutes gives you the impression of watching a small, frenetic life being lived.

They are crustaceans, barely visible, their bodies a millimeter long — translucent, jointed, with a single dark eye-spot that may or may not function as an eye. They move by flicking their antennae, and each flick propels them forward in a burst so brief and sudden that they seem to teleport: here, then *there*, with no visible intermediate state. The hiss that fills the pocket — that constant static, that radio-between-stations sound — is the collective acoustic signature of hundreds of these flicks happening every second. They are the pocket's loudest residents. They are also its most numerous mobile ones. They fill the water column in swarms that condense and disperse according to rules that seem to shift hourly: sometimes clumping near the hull, sometimes descending toward the sand, sometimes forming vertical columns that rotate slowly, like living drill bits boring through the murk.

Each copepod eats plankton — the smaller plankton, the ones that exist at the very edge of visibility, the diatoms and dinoflagellates that drift in the greenish and amber strata. The copepods graze these strata like cattle on a hillside, moving through the living layers with their feeding appendages creating tiny vortices that funnel food toward their mouths. The vortices are exquisite. Each one is a small hydraulic masterpiece, a cone of rotating water precisely calibrated to capture particles of a specific size range. The copepods are, in their feeding, fluid dynamicists of the highest order, though they don't know fluid dynamics. They know only the shape of hunger and the motion that satisfies it.

But here is the thing about the copepods that sets them apart from the nematodes and the worms: they *respond* to the engine-throb. Not just passively, not just by having their bodies vibrated in time with it — they respond *behaviorally*. When the throb intensifies, they swarm upward, toward the hull. When it diminishes, they scatter. When it skips a beat — as it does when the cracks come — they go absolutely still, every one of them, hanging motionless in the water with their antennae extended, as if listening. As if *waiting* for the beat to resume. And when it does, there is a half-second delay before they move again, and in that half-second they all orient in the same direction — toward the nearest exhaust port — before breaking formation and returning to their chaotic, individual trajectories.

This looks like worship. It is not worship. It is probably phototaxis, or chemotaxis, or some other -taxis that describes the automatic movement of a simple organism toward or away from a stimulus. The exhaust ports release sulfurous gas, warm water, trace metals. The copepods are drawn to these things because these things correlate, in their tiny sensory world, with food, with favorable conditions, with whatever copepod well-being consists of. The engine-throb modulates the exhaust. The copepods respond to the exhaust. It *looks like* they are responding to the throb, and in a sense they are, but through a chain of intermediaries so long that the word "response" has lost most of its meaning by the time it reaches them.

And yet. When the crack comes and the beat skips and the copepods freeze and then orient — all of them, every one, with military precision — toward the exhaust port... it is hard not to see something more than chemistry. It is hard not to see a congregation. A flinch. A collective turning-toward-the-source in a moment of disruption, the way a crowd in a dark room turns toward the last candle.

They don't know what they're doing. Nothing here knows what it's doing. But the copepods come closest to the appearance of knowing, and in a place where appearance and reality have a troubled, unresolved relationship, that might be enough.

---

The **limpets** — those sand-grain-sized grazers on the underside of the hull — are the pocket's most solitary residents. Each one occupies a territory of perhaps two square millimeters of metal surface, and within that territory it moves in slow, deliberate circuits, scraping algae with a radula so small that its existence can only be deduced from effect: the tiny clearcuts, the wandering trails of bare metal left behind in the green film. A limpet eats. A limpet moves to where more algae has grown. A limpet eats again. This is the entire biography.

But the trails they leave. Oh, the trails.

Seen from above — if "above" meant anything here, if you could somehow gain altitude in a space the height of a thimble — the limpet trails form a map. Not a map of the hull, though they are on the hull. A map of time. Each trail records a journey: outward from a home scar (a tiny pit in the iron where the limpet returns after each grazing circuit, the same pit, always, with a fidelity that borders on the ritualistic), looping through the algae, and back. The loops are not random. They are constrained by the growth rate of the algae, by the presence of neighboring limpets' territories (they do not cross into each other's clearcuts; there is no contact, no confrontation, just an absolute, invisible boundary honored by all parties), and by something else — by the pattern of the algae itself, which grows in those circles, those crescents, those branching lines that look deliberate. The limpets graze the algae. The algae regrows in the spaces between the grazed trails. The new growth patterns are shaped by the old trails. The old trails were shaped by the old growth patterns. Limpet and algae are writing on each other, a palimpsest of mutual inscription that has been going on since — since when? Since the hull arrived? Since the algae colonized? Since the limpets appeared? The question has no answer. The trails and the patterns seem to have always been in dialogue.

And the trails are beautiful. Not "beautiful" the way someone standing above a coral reef might say "beautiful," at a safe aesthetic distance. Beautiful the way a proof is beautiful — tight, inevitable, every line following from the one before it with a logic that is not mathematical but biological, metabolic, the logic of a body that must eat and move and eat and move and return home and eat again, a logic so constrained by necessity that it produces, through sheer repetition and mutual adjustment, something that looks like art. The limpets are engraving the hull. They have been engraving it since they arrived. The hull bears their record the way a tree bears rings — except the rings are spirals, and the spirals are illegible, and the tree was never planted, and the limpets don't know they're writing.

When the cracks come, the limpets clamp down. Each one seals itself to the metal with a muscular foot that creates a vacuum seal so tight that the limpet becomes, for the duration of the crack, part of the hull. Metal and animal, indistinguishable. The crack passes over them or through them or around them — it is hard to tell — and they endure it by ceasing to be separate from the thing they cling to. They survive the revocation of *hereness* by refusing to be *here* in any sense distinct from the hull itself. They become rivets. They become metal. The crack cannot unplace what was never separately placed.

Then it passes, and they unclamp, and they resume their slow, gorgeous, pointless engraving.

---

The **filament** — that single vertical strand descending from hull to sand — is not alive. Or it is alive in a way that doesn't correspond to any category of life present elsewhere in the pocket. It does not eat. It does not grow. It does not reproduce. It pulses. Fluid or charge or intent moves through it in rhythms that are almost but not quite synchronized with the engine-throb — offset by a fraction of a beat, like a echo arriving from a slightly different distance than expected. The filament is thin. Impossibly thin at its center, widening slightly where it anchors to the hull above and where it buries itself in the sand below. It is the color of old bone. It is the color of nothing. It changes.

Around its base, the sand spiral tightens and loosens in response to the pulsing. The nematodes avoid it — not consciously, not by decision, but by the same fluid-fitting instinct that keeps them from tangling with each other. There is a zone of exclusion around the filament, a tiny circle of sand with no worm-threads in it, no bacterial film, no life at all except the filament itself and the spiral it has made or that has made it. The polychaetes nearest to the filament build their tubes with the openings facing away from it. The copepods, in their post-crack moment of orientation, sometimes orient not toward the exhaust ports but toward the filament — and these individuals, the ones that face the filament, hold their orientation a beat longer than the others before breaking away.

What is the filament? It is the pocket's only mystery that the pocket's residents seem to register as a mystery. Everything else — the hull, the sourceless light, the falling bubbles, the cracks — is simply *context*, the unquestioned furniture of a world too small and too busy for existential inquiry. But the filament provokes something. Not thought. Not even awareness. Something more like a gravitational effect on behavior — a warping of trajectories, a bending of patterns, a subtle reorientation of the pocket's ecology around a single vertical line.

If you could read the spiral in the sand — if it were a language, which it might be, which it probably isn't, which you cannot rule out in a place where a hull exists without being built — it might say something about what lives on the other side of the hull. About what the engine is. About why the cracks are coming more often. About whether the pocket is a pocket at all or a pore, a point of contact between two incompatible orders of reality pressed together like palms in prayer, with the pressure increasing, with the water between them thinning, with the lives inside them — the nematodes, the worms, the copepods, the limpets, the bacteria, the algae, the plankton, the filament itself — serving as the only buffer, the only mediation, the only thing preventing the two surfaces from touching.

But the spiral is unreadable. The filament pulses. The pocket holds.

---

The **bacteria** deserve mention, though they resist it. They are everywhere. They are the pocket's atmosphere, its substrate, its weather. They coat every surface — every sand grain, every tube wall, every millimeter of hull metal, every strand of algae, every copepod carapace. They form communities so dense that they constitute a second geography overlaid on the first: biofilms with their own architecture, their own gradients, their own micro-zones of oxygen and sulfide and pH. They metabolize the sulfur from the exhaust ports. They fix carbon from the dissolved CO2. They reduce iron from the rust. They do everything, chemically speaking. They are the pocket's engine — not the engine behind the hull, the unknowable one, but the engine *of* the pocket, the metabolic machinery that converts the raw, unlikely inputs of this space (sulfur, iron, trace light, thermal gradients) into the currency of life.

They are also the pocket's memory. Each biofilm is a record — of conditions, of chemistry, of the history of the surface it coats. The biofilms on the hull are different from the biofilms on the sand. The biofilms near the exhaust ports are different from the biofilms in the thermal dead zones. And the biofilms that form in the wake of the cracks — in the zones where reality briefly revoked itself and then returned — these are different from all the others. They are thinner. More uniform. Less diverse. As if the crack sterilized the surface it passed through and the recolonizing bacteria, arriving in the aftermath, arrived as a simpler community, a reduced alphabet, a language missing several of its consonants. Over time, diversity returns. Complexity rebuilds. But each crack leaves a mark in the microbial record — a thin layer of simplicity sandwiched between layers of richness — and these layers are accumulating. The biofilm is a stratigraphy of disruption. If you could read it (you cannot; no one can; the bacteria themselves cannot; they simply live it) you would see that the cracks are coming more often, and that each one resets the biological clock a little further back, and that the recovery between cracks is a little less complete each time, and that the trajectory of this — the long, slow, statistical trajectory — points toward a crack that does not heal. A simplification that does not reverse. A silence in the bacterial chatter that extends, and extends, and does not end.

But that hasn't happened yet. The bacteria are still here, still metabolizing, still coating every surface with their invisible, essential, unnoticed industry. They are the pocket's most numerous residents by many orders of magnitude, and they are its least visible, and they are the ones on whom everything else depends, and they do not know this, and it would not matter to them if they did, because bacteria do not have a relationship to mattering.

---

And then there is the question of **what lives on the other side of the hull.**

Not what *is* on the other side — that question, like so many questions about the hull, doesn't resolve. But what *lives* there. Because the engine-throb is not mechanical. Not entirely. It has the rhythm of a machine but the *texture* of something organic — a thud that is too wet, too variable, too responsive to the conditions in the pocket below. When the copepods swarm toward an exhaust port, the throb quickens fractionally. When the polychaetes all retract at once, it slows. When the filament pulses, the throb shifts pitch. These are not coincidences. They are too consistent to be coincidences. The engine on the other side of the hull is listening to the pocket, or feeling it, or digesting it — processing information from below and modulating its output in response.

This means the pocket is not just a habitat. It is a *relationship*. The creatures below feed on what the hull provides — sulfur, iron, warmth, the rain of rust. The hull, or whatever drives it, feeds on what the creatures provide — and what they provide is unclear. Waste heat? Chemical signals? The vibrations of copepod legs? The slow engravings of limpet radulae? Or something less material — pattern, complexity, the sheer *fact* of life persisting in an impossible space, the biological stubbornness that turns a glitch into a garden?

There is no way to know. The hull is opaque. The other side is inaccessible. Whatever lives there — and something *does* live there, something large enough or distributed enough to produce that throb, that wet industrial heartbeat — it is as unknowable to the pocket's residents as the pocket's residents are unknown to themselves. The relationship is mutual, asymmetric, and entirely unconscious on at least one side. Probably on both.

---

So. Who lives here?

**Everything that can.** Everything that can fit into a space this pressurized, this paradoxical, this thick with contradiction. Nematodes that have never encountered an edge to their world. Polychaetes slowly becoming the machine they live beneath. Copepods practicing an accidental liturgy. Limpets writing an unreadable scripture on a surface that was never made. Bacteria holding the whole thing together with chemistry and ignorance. A filament that is either a nerve or a root or a wire or all three, pulsing with something that might be information and might be longing and might just be fluid dynamics.

They live here the way anything lives anywhere: by fitting themselves to conditions they did not choose and cannot comprehend. By eating what's available. By building shelter from whatever's at hand. By reproducing faster than they die, or trying to. By ignoring the cracks.

The cracks are coming more often. The biofilm record says so, for anyone who could read it, which no one can. The filament pulses a little faster. The spiral in the sand is a little tighter. The copepods hold their post-crack orientation a beat longer each time, as if the source they're turning toward is getting closer, or louder, or more urgent. The hull throbs. The sand breathes. The water, packed with its impossible cargo of life, slides between the two temperatures that refuse to mix, carrying iron filings and plankton and the ghosts of bubbles that fell instead of rising, and the whole thing — the whole compressed, saturated, trembling, magnificent, doomed or undoomed ecosystem — continues.

Not because it must. Not because anything guarantees it. But because the nematodes don't know how to stop threading, and the worms don't know how to stop building, and the copepods don't know how to stop flicking, and the limpets don't know how to stop grazing, and the bacteria don't know how to stop metabolizing, and the filament doesn't know how to stop pulsing, and so the pocket holds, and holds, and holds, in the space between a machine that was never built and a seabed that has barely begun, in a fist-sized ocean that contains, for now, for this moment, for as long as the cracks keep healing, everything it needs to be a world.
