# The People of the Risen Shelves

---

They are not graceful in the way you imagine water-dwellers would be. That is the first thing. You expect sleekness, undulation, the effortless glide of bodies that have always known the medium they move through. What you see instead is something more like competence — the ease of people who have learned their environment so thoroughly that they no longer perform their comfort in it, who swim the way a farmer walks across a field, which is to say with economy, without beauty as a goal, beauty arriving only as a byproduct of repetition.

Their bodies are dense. Compact. Shorter than you expect, though this may be an illusion created by the way they hold themselves — shoulders slightly rounded, limbs kept close, the posture of people who have spent generations conserving heat even in warm water, who have learned that a spread limb is a limb that catches current. Their skin is dark, uniformly, ranging from a deep umber to something closer to the color of the basalt itself — a brown so saturated it approaches black in the dim light, though when the strange amber illumination moves through the plateau, you see the undertones: bronze, copper, a faint reddish cast that mirrors the tithe-weed. Whether this is pigmentation or something absorbed from the mineral water, something deposited over years of living in a place where the vents exhale dissolved iron and sulfur and stranger things, you cannot tell. Perhaps it is both. Perhaps the distinction has stopped mattering.

Their hands are what you notice next. The fingers are long — longer than seems proportionate — and the webbing between them extends to the second knuckle. Not full webbing, not a membrane, but a thickened, slightly translucent tissue that gives each hand the look of a broad paddle when spread and a surprisingly dexterous tool when closed. They use their hands constantly, expressively, not just for swimming but for speaking. You realize this slowly. The gestures you took for simple acknowledgments — a turned palm, a curled finger, a thumb pressed to the hollow of the throat — are language. Or part of it. The other part comes from their mouths, but what they produce is not speech as you know it. It is a series of clicks, tonal hums, and short vowel-sounds that travel through the water with startling clarity, carrying further than you'd expect, each syllable shaped to exploit the acoustic properties of a medium that compresses sound into something more felt than heard. The bass hum of the vents provides a constant drone beneath their communication, and they have built their language on top of it, the way a musician builds melody over a pedal tone. Some of their words are nearly subsonic. You feel them in your sternum before your ears register anything.

They speak with their hands and their throats simultaneously, and the two channels carry different information. The hands provide context — emotional register, spatial reference, tense. The voice carries the content. To understand one without the other is to get only half the meaning, the way reading a transcript of a conversation strips away tone and leaves you guessing at intent. Children — and there are children, you realize, small dark shapes darting between the tithe-weed fronds on the lower shelves — learn the hand-language first. You see a woman holding a child of perhaps three or four years against her chest, one hand supporting the child's back while the other moves slowly, deliberately, forming shapes. The child watches with the total fixity that only young children achieve, then attempts to copy. Gets it wrong. The woman adjusts the child's fingers with a patience that suggests this has happened many times and will happen many more. The vocal component will come later, when the child's throat has developed enough to produce the necessary resonances. Until then, the children communicate in a rapid, clumsy hand-pidgin that the adults understand but do not reciprocate fully — they simplify their own signs when addressing children, the way any parent speaks to a baby.

---

Their hair is kept short or bound tight to the skull, wrapped in bands of woven tithe-weed fiber. Some of them have shaved their heads entirely, and on these bare scalps you can see something unexpected: a faint pattern of raised lines, like scarification, running from the crown to the base of the skull and then down the neck, disappearing beneath the loose wraps they wear around their torsos. The patterns differ from person to person. Some are geometric — parallel lines, concentric circles. Others are organic, branching, unmistakably reminiscent of the crystalline formations in the open-walled structure on the middle shelf. You watch a young man working on a net-frame outside his dwelling, and when he turns his head, the pattern on his scalp catches the bacterial light and you see that the lines are not merely raised skin. They are *mineralized*. A thin deposit, like the earliest stage of what happens to the objects left in the fountain-structure, has formed along the scarification tracks. It is pale against his dark skin, almost luminous. It is the plateau claiming him the way it claims the dwelling walls. He does not seem troubled by this. He reaches up absently and runs a finger along one of the lines the way someone might touch a familiar scar, reassuring themselves it is still there.

Not all of them have this. The children don't. Some of the adults don't. You begin to suspect it is not universal but chosen — a practice, a rite, a decision made at some point in a life that says *I am staying. I am allowing this place to write on me as I write on it.* The ones who have it move through the village with a certain authority, not of rank exactly, but of *investment*. They are the ones who pause longest at the fountain-structure. They are the ones whose dwellings are oldest, whose lower walls have begun to merge with the rock. They are the ones the children are brought to when they are sick — not because they are healers necessarily, but because their relationship to the plateau is deeper, more reciprocal, and in a place where the geology is attentive, where the rock responds to presence, a deeper relationship may be the closest thing to medicine.

---

There is a woman on the fourth shelf — counting from the bottom of the inhabited tiers — who is doing something you cannot immediately parse. She is seated on a broad ledge, her legs folded beneath her in a way that suggests her joints are slightly more flexible than yours, her knees bending at an angle that would be uncomfortable for a surface-dweller but seems natural for her. Before her, spread on a flat stone that may have been shaped for this purpose or may have simply arrived in the right shape (it is increasingly hard to tell the difference here), is a collection of objects: small carved pieces of the milky volcanic glass, each one roughly the size and shape of a knuckle bone. There are dozens of them. Each has been carved with a different symbol — not the hand-signs of the spoken language, but something else, something more compressed, more abstract. She is arranging them in rows, studying the arrangement, then dismantling it and beginning again.

You watch her for a long time. She is not alone — another person, older, their scalp heavily mineralized, sits nearby and observes, occasionally clicking a single syllable that might be a question or a correction. The woman responds with a hand-sign — a flat palm rotated slowly, which you interpret as something like *wait* or *not yet* or *I'm thinking*. The older person subsides. The woman continues her arrangement.

It is writing. Or accounting. Or divination. Or all three. The knuckle-bones are a notation system, and the flat stone is a workspace, and the woman is composing something — a record, a prediction, a plan. She places three bones in a triangle, hums a low note, adds a fourth to the center. Pauses. Removes the fourth. Adds two more at the edges. The older person leans forward, signs something rapid with both hands, and for a moment their exchange is too fast and too contextual for you to follow. Then the woman nods — a gesture borrowed from some ancestral life above the surface, still legible after all this time — and sweeps the bones into a pouch made from what looks like the stomach-lining of something large. She ties the pouch to the belt of woven fiber at her waist and pushes off the ledge, swimming upward toward the canopy of pad-organisms, her body rotating slightly in the thermal-warmed water, the silt she dislodged drifting behind her like a thought dissolving.

The older person remains on the ledge, looking at the flat stone where the bones were arranged. You see their lips move — a single word, barely vocalized, felt more than heard. Then they place one hand flat on the stone and press down, and you could swear the stone warms slightly beneath the touch, though this may be the vent-heat, may be imagination, may be the plateau doing what the plateau does.

---

Their bodies bear other marks of long habitation. Their eyes are large, not grotesquely so, but noticeably — wider pupils, irises that have shifted toward a pale amber that catches bioluminescence and reflects it faintly, giving the impression, when you meet one's gaze in the blue-green glow of a dwelling, that you are looking at someone who is generating their own light. They are not. But the reflective quality serves a purpose: in the perpetual dusk of the shelves, their visual acuity is extraordinary. They can read the hand-signs of a companion three shelves away. They notice the shrimp-things before the shrimp-things notice them. When one of the thermal shapes — those purposeful densities in the vent-plumes — moves through the village, several heads will track it simultaneously, their amber eyes following something that you can barely perceive.

Their lungs — they still have lungs, this is important — are large, barrel-chested cavities that can hold air for extended periods, though they rarely need to. They breathe water. Or rather, they breathe *both*. You notice that they surface occasionally, or send their children up past the pad-canopy toward the open water above, and when the children return they are carrying air in their chests like a gift, their exhalations coming in silvery, wobbling bubbles that they release into their dwellings, where the trapped air collects in the upper curve of each dome. Every dwelling has this pocket of air at its apex, a small captured sky, and the inhabitants occasionally rise into it — just their faces, mouth and nose breaking the surface tension of the pocket — and breathe. Just breathe. For a few seconds, a minute. Then they submerge again and return to their work. It is not necessary. They can extract oxygen from the water through a secondary system — not gills exactly, but a network of capillary-dense tissue along the ribcage, visible as a faintly ribbed texture beneath the skin, a ruffle of vascularization that flutters slightly with each pulse. But the air-breathing is maintained. Cultivated. It is a practice, like the scarification. A remembering.

You wonder what they are remembering. Whether they themselves still know.

---

There is a man — you think he is old, though age is hard to read on these faces, which are broad and strong-jawed and weathered in a way that could mean forty years or eighty — who spends his days on the lowest inhabited shelf, the one closest to the thermal vents. The heat here is almost uncomfortable. The water shimmers faintly, the way air shimmers above hot pavement, and the silt is finer, more active, swirling in constant slow vortices. He sits at the edge of the shelf with his legs dangling over a crevice from which a steady plume rises, and he works. His craft is the clay pots. You watch him gather the volcanic silt — not from the shelf surface, where it is too coarse, but from the plume itself, reaching into the warm upwelling and letting the finest particles collect on his palms, building a small mound of material on the stone beside him. He mixes this with something — a mucus, a secretion, squeezed from a bladder-like organism that he keeps in a mesh pouch at his side. The resulting paste is dark and pliable and smells, even through the water, of minerals and something faintly organic, faintly alive.

He shapes the pots without a wheel, without a mold, using only his long fingers, and the shapes he produces are not symmetrical. They are deliberately irregular — wider on one side, with rims that dip and rise, with surfaces that he textures using a small comb-tool carved from the hollow bone-material. Each pot is different. Each one, you realize, is shaped to fit a specific niche in a specific dwelling — this one for the alcove in a dome on the third shelf, that one for the storage ledge beside someone's sleeping hollow. He is making bespoke vessels, each one a response to a particular need, a particular space. When he finishes one, he sets it carefully into the plume at the crevice's edge, where the heat will cure it slowly, over days, the minerals in the water depositing a thin glaze that makes the clay watertight and gives it a faint iridescence, a shimmer of blues and golds that you don't see until the pot is retrieved, finished, carried upward by a younger person who has been waiting, patient as the silt, for the work to be done.

The old man speaks rarely. When he does, his voice is lower than the others', almost indistinguishable from the vent-hum, and the younger people lean close to hear him, their amber eyes wide, their hands still. He speaks with almost no hand-signs. This is notable. It makes his words harder to interpret, stripped of the contextual channel, reduced to pure tonal content. But the younger people seem to understand. Or perhaps they are simply accustomed to uncertainty, to receiving messages that are incomplete and filling in the rest with their own knowledge of the man, of the place, of the way things are and have been. Perhaps incompleteness is not a failure of communication here but a feature — a way of keeping language porous, open to the same slow accretion that shapes everything else on the plateau.

---

They eat together. This is one of the few communal rhythms you can identify. At intervals that seem to correspond to the longest pulse-cycle of the thermal vents — a period of perhaps six or seven hours, during which the vents build from a low hum to a resonant peak and then subside — the inhabitants gather on the broader shelves, bringing food from their dwellings and their cultivation plots. The plots are on the lower shelves, near the vents, where the mineral-rich water feeds dense mats of edible algae — deep green, almost black, with a gelatinous texture that suggests high protein content. They harvest this in sheets, rolling it around bundles of the younger tithe-weed stalks and the tiny shrimp-things, which are collected from traps woven into the frond-curtains. The resulting parcels are simple. Uncooked, obviously, though some are placed briefly in the vent-plumes to warm them, the heat changing the texture of the algae from gelatinous to something firmer, almost chewy.

They sit in loose circles on the shelves, passing the parcels, eating slowly. The children eat faster, darting in to grab portions and then retreating to eat while swimming, drifting upward, their small bodies silhouetted against the canopy above. The adults eat and talk — hands moving, voices clicking and humming, the sound layering into a complex murmur that fills the middle shelves with something that approaches music, or at least the social precursor to music. You notice that during these meals, the thermal shapes in the plumes become more frequent, more defined. Thickenings that almost resolve into silhouettes before dissipating. The inhabitants continue to make their small gestures of acknowledgment — the turned palm — but during meals these gestures are more frequent, more inclusive, as though the shapes are being invited, welcomed to the table even though they cannot sit, cannot eat, cannot do anything but pass through and dissolve.

One of the children — a girl, perhaps seven or eight, with close-cropped hair and the first faint traces of what will become scarification lines along her temples — watches one of the shapes with an intensity that is different from the adults' calm acknowledgment. She reaches toward it. Her hand passes through the density and comes out wet with warmth. She looks at her fingers, then at the shape, which is already dissipating. She turns to the woman beside her — her mother, maybe, or an aunt, or simply a nearby adult in a community where all adults are partially responsible for all children — and signs a question. The sign is a cupped hand opening slowly, like a flower or an eye. *What is it?*

The woman considers. Then she signs back: both hands flat, fingers spread, moving in slow parallel arcs, like two currents that flow beside each other without merging. You do not know what the sign means. The girl seems satisfied by it, or at least willing to accept it as an answer for now. She returns to her food. The shape is gone. The vent-hum is subsiding.

---

They sleep in their domes, in hollows carved into the rock and lined with layers of tithe-weed fiber that have been beaten soft over months of use. The hollows are body-shaped — not exactly, not precisely, but generally, the way a nest is shaped by the animal that sleeps in it. Each person's hollow is slightly different, reflecting the particular curl of their body, the way they fold their arms, whether they sleep on their side or their back. Some of the hollows are deep, almost coffin-like, the sleeper nearly enclosed by rock; others are shallow, barely more than a depression, the sleeper exposed to the interior of the dome. The deep sleepers tend to be the older ones, the mineralized ones. You wonder if this is preference or if the rock is simply growing around them faster, claiming them incrementally, the way it claims the dwelling walls, the way it will eventually claim everything on the plateau that stays still long enough.

They do not all sleep at the same time. There is no night here, no darkness deeper than the perpetual dusk, and so sleep comes in shifts, in personal rhythms that overlap and stagger. At any given time, perhaps a third of the village is sleeping, a third is working, and a third is in some intermediate state — resting but alert, drifting slowly through the tithe-weed, tending the algae plots with the unhurried attention of someone who is thinking about something else entirely. The village never goes quiet. The *tik tik* of carving continues around the clock. The click-hum of conversation rises and falls but never stops. The vents breathe. The plateau grows.

Sometimes, in the equivalent of a still hour, when the current is at its calmest and the silt has settled to a fine clarity that makes the water seem like thin air, you can hear something else. It comes from the fountain-structure on the middle shelf — the branching crystalline formation with its encased offerings. The harmonics shift. New overtones appear, resonances that the structure has not produced before, as though the mineral growth has reached a new configuration, a new acoustic geometry. The sound is high and strange and not unpleasant, and the people who are awake pause in their work and listen. Some of them touch their scalps, their mineralized lines, as though feeling for a sympathetic vibration. Some of them sign to each other — quick, private signs, low-amplitude, meant for a companion rather than a group. You see a man and a woman on a shared ledge exchange a sign that involves interlocked fingers, held close to the chest, and you think it might mean something like *did you hear that?* or *it's changing again* or simply *yes*. Simply the acknowledgment that the world they live in is not static, that it is composing itself around them, and that their role is neither to direct the composition nor to be passive within it but to participate — to offer their shapes, their objects, their presence, their patience, and to see what the plateau makes of all of it.

---

There is one more thing. You almost miss it because it is not visual. It is tactile. It is the feeling, when you rest your hand on the rock of the plateau, of something beneath the surface — not heat, not vibration, but a quality you have no word for. A thickness. A *regard*. The rock is not alive, not in any way you can define, but it is also not inert. It is in the process of becoming, and the becoming has a character, a tendency, something that might in a billion years develop into intention but is currently just a leaning, the way a vine leans toward light without knowing what light is.

The people who live here know this. They have always known it, or they learned it so early in their habitation that the learning has become indistinguishable from instinct. It is why they leave their walls unfinished. It is why they mark their scalps and let the minerals claim the marks. It is why they build the fountain-structure and offer it objects to encase and transform. It is why the woman with the knuckle-bones arranges and rearranges her symbols, searching for a pattern that matches something she can feel in the rock beneath her but cannot yet articulate. It is why the old potter shapes his vessels asymmetrically, each one a response not just to a human need but to something the clay itself seems to want — a curve here, a ridge there, the material guiding his fingers as much as his fingers guide it.

They are not the plateau's builders. They are not its inhabitants in the way a hermit crab inhabits a shell. They are something more like collaborators, or tenants who have entered into a long, slow negotiation with their landlord, a negotiation conducted not in words but in shapes, in placements, in the willingness to sleep in a hollow that grows a little deeper each year, to accept a wall that is half-woven and half-geological, to eat food grown in the exhalations of a planet that is still deciding what it wants to be.

They are patient. They are attentive. They are ordinary — bickering sometimes, you can tell, the hand-signs growing sharp and angular during disagreements that flare and subside like the vent-pulses. They love their children clumsily and fiercely. They take pride in their work. They grieve, though you have not yet seen a death here; you have seen the hollows that are empty, the sleeping-places where the rock has closed over completely, smooth and sealed, a faint geometric regularity the only sign that someone once lay there. The grief is quiet. A hand on the sealed stone. A hum that matches the vent-note and then, slowly, diverges from it, rising into a personal frequency, an individual voice distinguishing itself from the planetary drone for just a moment before subsiding back into it.

They are becoming part of something. They know this. It does not frighten them, though you think it might have frightened their ancestors, the ones who first descended to these shelves from whatever surface they once knew, carrying with them the memory of air and gravity and doorways and the expectation that a home should be something you build and then inhabit, a finished thing, a container. That expectation has eroded. What has replaced it is something harder to name — not faith, not resignation, but a kind of *consonance*, a willingness to be in tune with a process that is larger than themselves, slower than themselves, and fundamentally disinterested in their individual stories while being profoundly shaped by their collective presence.

The plateau rises. The village grows. The rock remembers. And the people — dark-skinned, amber-eyed, broad-handed, speaking in clicks and hums and the slow semaphore of fingers, breathing water and sometimes air, carving and weaving and cultivating and offering and sleeping in hollows that deepen year by year — the people continue. Not enduring. Not surviving. *Participating*. In the warm, dim, green-lit, vent-humming, half-dreamed, still-becoming world of the Risen Shelves, participation is enough. Participation is everything.
