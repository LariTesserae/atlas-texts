# Who Lives on the Singing Terrace

---

They come up from below. That's the first thing to understand — they don't descend to Veth Múra from the mountain's upper reaches, and they don't arrive from some neighboring valley or distant settlement carried on roads. There are no roads. They come up through the rock itself, through the root-channels, through passages that were not built but grown, widened over generations by the same botanical erosion that shapes everything here. They know the channels the way a tongue knows the inside of a mouth — by feel, by memory so deep it isn't quite memory anymore but something closer to the body's own knowledge of itself.

They are not large. That matters. The root-channels would not permit largeness.

---

Call them the Vethkai, though that is not what they call themselves. What they call themselves is a sound produced partly by the throat and partly by the palm of the hand struck flat against the sternum — a name that is simultaneously spoken and *felt*, a vibration as much as a word, and if you heard it without seeing it performed you would think it was simply a cough, a nothing-sound, and you would be wrong.

They stand roughly to the height of a human child of ten or eleven years, but they are not built like children. They are built like people who have spent millennia fitting themselves through stone. Narrow through the shoulders and pelvis, deep through the chest — a keel-shaped ribcage, almost avian, that lets them turn sideways and slip through gaps that would stop a broader frame. Their limbs are long relative to their torsos, the arms especially, with an extra degree of rotation in the shoulder that allows them to reach behind and above themselves in ways that look, to outside eyes, like their arms have been installed incorrectly. Their hands are extraordinary — four fingers and a thumb, like yours, but the fingers are longer, thinner, and each one can flex backward at the second joint as easily as forward. A Vethkai hand laid flat on a surface looks like a pale starfish. A Vethkai hand working a crevice in stone looks like something alive and separate from the body, an autonomous creature conducting its own investigation.

Their skin is the color of the rock — not as camouflage, or not only as camouflage, but because the same minerals that compose the terrace compose them. They eat the rock, in a sense. Not directly — they are not lithovores — but the plants they consume have drawn their substance from the calcium strata, and the throat-crickets they eat have fed on the plants, and so the pale calcium-and-phosphorus palette of Veth Múra has worked its way into their bones, their skin, their fingernails, which are dense and white and slightly translucent, like shards of shell. In the dim copper-grey light of the terrace, a still Vethkai is nearly invisible against the stone. But they are never entirely still. Like everything else here, they shift. They adjust. They resettle.

Their faces are — let me be careful here, because there is a temptation to make them either too alien or too familiar, and either distortion would be a disservice.

Their faces are recognizable as faces. Two eyes, set wide, large, adapted to the sourceless low light — the irises are a pale amber that darkens to near-black at the pupil's edge, and the pupils themselves are horizontally elongated, like a goat's, which gives their gaze a quality of encompassing width, as though they are always seeing more of you than you are showing. A nose that is broad and flat, the nostrils wide and lined with fine cilia that filter the mineral-vapored air. A mouth that is, in repose, a thin straight line — lipless, or nearly so — but that opens to reveal a startling depth, a resonating chamber not unlike the throat-crickets', the whole jaw and throat constructed as an instrument. Their ears are their most alien feature: not external structures but recessed openings behind and below the jaw, protected by folds of skin that can tighten or loosen to modulate what gets in. In the noise of Veth Múra, they walk around with their ear-folds nearly shut, filtering the drone down to something navigable. When they want to listen — truly listen, with intent — they open one ear at a time, turning the head slightly, and the expression this produces looks, to human eyes, like skepticism. It is not skepticism. It is attention so precise it has a direction.

They are furred. Lightly, across the forearms, the back, the backs of the thighs — a fine, short nap the same pale tone as their skin but fractionally warmer in color, more cream than chalk. The fur is functional: in the warm, humid, mineral-laced air of the terrace, it traps a thin boundary layer of cleaner atmosphere against the skin, a personal microclimate. When a Vethkai descends into the root-channels, where the air is thicker and more sulfurous, the fur stands on end, maximizing this buffer. When they are on the surface, at ease, it lies flat and is barely visible. You might not notice it at all until one of them passed close to you and you saw the nap stir in the faint updraft from a vapor fissure.

They wear things, but not much. A wrap of dried vetch-fur around the hips, sometimes a belt or harness of braided leafless vine from which tools hang. The tools are all bone-tree wood and stone: spikes for prying, flat blades for scraping, small cups carved from bracket-fungus for holding collected material. The spike wedged into the crack by the platform — that is a Vethkai tool, a common one, the equivalent of a pocket knife, carried and placed and retrieved so routinely that its owner did not think twice about leaving it. They will be back. They are always back.

---

How many? This is harder.

On the terrace itself, at any given time, perhaps eight to fifteen. But the terrace is not where they live — not exactly. The terrace is where they *surface*. It is commons, workspace, gathering ground, stage. They live in the channels below, in root-hollowed chambers and widened fissures that honeycomb the rock beneath the shelf, a network that extends down and inward into the mountain's shoulder for a depth and distance no outsider has mapped. The full community — if it is a single community, and there are reasons to think it might be better understood as several overlapping ones — numbers perhaps eighty to a hundred and twenty. They come and go from the surface constantly, individually or in small groups of two or three, emerging from the channels at unpredictable intervals, spending an hour or a day on the terrace before descending again. The pattern of their surfacing has been compared to the throat-crickets' migrations: apparently random, but governed by signals too subtle for an outsider to parse. They are responding to something — the vapor chemistry, the drone, the shifting thermal patterns of the vetch-fur, the slow breathing of the lichen-creature, each other — but the web of causation is so tangled and so embedded in lived experience that asking a Vethkai *why* they came up when they did would be like asking you why you shifted your weight from one foot to the other. You did it because everything in the system said *now*.

---

Now: the singing. Because the terrace is not called the Singing Terrace by accident, and it is not named for the throat-crickets or the callers.

The Vethkai sing.

They sing the way the terrace itself operates — constantly, partially, overlappingly, without clear beginning or end. A Vethkai emerging from a root-channel onto the surface will already be producing sound: a low, chest-driven hum that interacts with the throat-cricket drone the way a new instrument enters an existing piece of music, not by announcing itself but by finding a frequency that was missing and filling it. This is not metaphor. The Vethkai hear the drone — they hear it with those precise, directional ears — and they pitch themselves into its gaps. Each voice finds a niche in the acoustic ecology, just as each bone-tree finds a territory of stone.

But it goes further. As they move across the terrace, working, gathering, interacting, they modulate. The hum rises into melody, breaks into rhythmic clicking produced by the tongue against the roof of that deep resonating mouth, drops into a subsonic throb that harmonizes with the stone's own vibration. When two Vethkai work near each other — scraping bracket-fungus, prying loose a section of crystallized mineral deposit, tending to the vetch-fur in some way you don't quite understand, turning it, aerating it, like someone tending a garden — their voices interlock. Not in unison. In *counterpoint*. They weave around each other, one voice rising as the other falls, rhythms interlocking like fingers lacing together, and the result is a structure of sound that has the quality of architecture, of something with weight and dimension, something you could almost *see* if the air were thick enough.

And the callers respond. This is the negotiation — this is what the callers have been screaming about all along. They are not arguing with each other, or not only with each other. They are arguing with the Vethkai. Or perhaps *arguing* is wrong. They are *participating*. The callers' structured phrases — those rising trills, those staccato clicks, those sliding whistles — are responses to the Vethkai's singing, and the Vethkai's singing responds in turn to the callers. It is a cross-species dialogue conducted in real time, a rolling, churning, never-resolving conversation that has been going on for as long as both have been here, which is longer than the bone-trees, longer than the wall at the edge, longer perhaps than the platform.

What are they saying? What is the content of this exchange?

I don't think content is the right framework. Or rather: the content is the system itself. The singing and the screaming and the drone are the sound a complex ecology makes when every element is continuously announcing its state to every other element. *I am here. I am this. The vapor is this. The stone moved. The heat shifted. I am adjusting. Adjust with me.* It is a real-time, analog, acoustic model of the terrace's own condition, rendered in sound, maintained collectively, never finished.

The Vethkai do not have a word for music. They have a word that means something closer to *the-noise-of-everything-being-known-at-once*. It is the same word, or a related one — the linguistics are difficult when the language is partly thoracic percussion — that they use for *weather*, and for *health*, and for *the way things are right now*.

---

But they are not only singers. They are not only a sonic phenomenon. That would be reductive, and they would not appreciate it, though *appreciate* may also be the wrong word — they would find it confusing, the way you would find it confusing if someone described you as primarily a breather. Yes, you breathe. It is essential. But it is not the *point*.

So what is the point? What do the Vethkai *do*?

They tend the terrace. Not in the way a gardener tends a garden — they do not plant, do not cultivate in any directed sense, do not select for traits or arrange growth according to a plan. They tend it the way a body tends itself: by responding, adjusting, maintaining. When a bone-tree's root system threatens to undermine a section of the shelf that supports a large colony of vetch-fur, they intervene — delicately, with those extraordinary hands, redirecting root-tips into pre-drilled guide-channels, a process that takes weeks and requires daily attention. When the lichen-creature's feeding membrane shows signs of desiccation — a slight curling at the edges, a loss of translucency — they open new pathways to the nearest vapor fissure, chipping stone with their bone-wood spikes to improve airflow. When a section of vetch-fur dies back, they harvest the dead material — this is what the platform is for, a work surface where dried vetch-fur is processed into cordage, into grip-wrappings for tools, into the hip-wraps they wear, into something else, a paste or poultice, that they apply to cracks in the stone where new root growth is wanted.

They are, in the most literal sense, the terrace's immune system. They are the feedback mechanism that keeps the system churning within its viable range. Too much bone-tree growth and the root-erosion accelerates past what the shelf can sustain; the Vethkai prune. Too little vetch-fur and the thermal regulation of the surface falters, the throat-crickets cluster and overpopulate certain zones while abandoning others, the acoustic environment becomes lopsided; the Vethkai redistribute. The lichen-creature must breathe; the vapor fissures must remain clear; the root-channels must stay open enough for passage but not so open that the stone loses structural integrity. It is an endless, intricate, responsive labor, and it looks — if you watch for only a short time — like people simply *moving around*, fiddling with things, touching the rock, humming. You would have to watch for months to see the pattern. You would have to watch for years to see the intelligence.

---

They have children. Of course they have children. The children are smaller and lighter-furred and their ear-folds have not yet developed full voluntary control, so they walk the terrace with expressions of perpetual overwhelm, wincing at the drone, startling at the callers' screams. They learn by following — a child will shadow a particular adult (not always a parent; the kinship structures are lateral and overlapping, like everything else here) for weeks at a time, watching hands work stone, watching the way an adult turns one ear toward a section of vetch-fur and listens to its faint exothermic crackle and then decides, on the basis of something inaudible to the child, that this patch needs attention and that one doesn't. The children practice singing in the root-channels below, where the acoustics are different — tighter, more reverberant — and sometimes you can hear them from above, a faint piping rising through the cracks in the rock, the notes uncertain, searching for the gaps in the drone and not quite finding them yet.

The children also play with the throat-crickets, which tolerate handling with a kind of vibrating indifference. A Vethkai child holding a throat-cricket in cupped hands and feeling its bass note resonate through their finger-bones is experiencing something foundational, something that will organize their understanding of how sound and matter are the same thing, how the terrace speaks through everything it contains. The children's faces, during this: eyes wide, mouth slightly open, ear-folds flared despite the volume, *wanting* to hear it all, not yet knowing how to filter but knowing already that the filtering is the thing they are here to learn.

---

The old ones. There are old ones.

You know them by their hands, which have thickened at the joints, the fingers less flexible but more knowing — an old Vethkai's hand on the rock can feel, through the mineral substrate, the progress of a root-tip six inches below the surface. They have been touching this stone for so long that the boundary between their proprioception and the terrace's geology has blurred. They do not need to listen to the drone to know what the throat-crickets are doing; they feel it in the rock through the soles of their bare feet, which are wide and flat and calloused to a texture nearly indistinguishable from the stone itself.

The old ones sing differently. Where the younger adults find gaps in the drone and fill them, the old ones sing *with* the drone — not above it, not in counterpoint, but fused into it, their voices becoming indistinguishable from the crickets' bass, from the stone's vibration, from the vapor's hiss. An old Vethkai singing at full voice does not sound like a person singing. It sounds like the terrace itself has developed a focal point, a place where all the ambient sound momentarily coheres into something that has — not meaning, exactly, but *shape*. A phrase. A contour. And then it disperses again, and the old one is quiet, and the system continues.

There is one — I think there is one — who no longer comes to the surface. Who has gone deeper into the mountain, into chambers where the root-channels are ancient and mostly closed, where the stone is warm from geothermal depths, where the vapor is thicker and richer. The others speak of this one — if *speak of* is the right term for a reference made through a particular low harmonic that appears in their singing only occasionally, a note that seems to come from farther away than the singer's throat, as if it is being relayed, passed upward through the rock from some resonating source far below. Whether this deep-dweller is a specific individual, a remembered elder, a mythologized ancestor, or something else — a state of being that the Vethkai understand a person can enter, a kind of final integration with the mountain's own slow processes — is not clear. The distinction may not be meaningful to them. A person who has become indistinguishable from the system they tended is not gone. They are *distributed*.

---

And then there is the matter of the wall. The low wall at the terrace's edge, bone-tree blocks stacked without mortar, old, colonized, beginning to be reclaimed.

The Vethkai built it. But they did not build it for themselves.

They built it because something else comes to the terrace — something from below the haze, from the lower mountain, from wherever the slope descends to after it drops off the shelf's edge. Something that climbs. Something larger than the Vethkai, or at least differently shaped, and less careful, and drawn by the sound — by the singing, by the drone, by the whole furious acoustic beacon that Veth Múra projects into the surrounding silence. Something that, when it arrives, disrupts the balance: dislodges too much stone, disturbs the vetch-fur, agitates the callers into genuine panic rather than participatory screaming, causes the throat-crickets to go briefly, horribly silent in a wave-front that spreads from the terrace's edge inward, a silence so wrong that the Vethkai feel it as physical pain.

The wall is not a fortification. It would not stop anything determined. It is a *signal*. It says: *this edge is maintained. This place is tended. What lives here is known and accounted for, and you are not part of the count.* Whether the climbing things understand this signal, whether it deters them or merely redirects them, whether they are animals or something else — the Vethkai do not discuss this in ways that have been translated. They maintain the wall. They replace blocks when the roots displace them. They do this with the same unsentimental responsiveness with which they tend everything else.

The scratch-marks near the lichen-creature — those were not made by the climbing things. Those are old. Those were made by the Vethkai themselves, generations ago, in what may have been a period of scarcity or desperation, when someone considered whether the vast sessile organism could be harvested, prised from the rock and used. The attempt failed or was abandoned. The scratch-marks remain as a kind of geological confession. The Vethkai do not hide their errors. The stone records everything, and the stone is the record, and the record is the song.

---

One more thing.

When the Vethkai are on the surface together — eight or ten of them, working, singing, the children following, the old ones standing with their feet on the warm stone and their ear-folds open just a crack — there are moments. Brief moments. When the overlapping songs and the drone and the callers' screams and the deep sub-bass of the stone itself all align, not into unison but into a kind of *coherence*, a fleeting architecture of sound where every element supports every other and the whole terrace rings like a struck bell, a bell made of rock and wood and living tissue and warm air and the patient breathing of something ancient against the cliff face—

In those moments, the Vethkai go still.

All of them. At once. Even the children.

They stand where they are, hands open, ears open, faces turned not toward each other but *outward*, toward the haze, toward the drop-off, toward the pewter sky and the unseen mountain above and the unknown depths below, and they listen to the thing they are part of, the thing they maintain, the thing that maintains them, and the expression on their faces is not joy exactly, and not reverence exactly, and not satisfaction, but something for which their language has a word and ours does not — a word that is made partly in the throat and partly by the palm of the hand pressed flat against the sternum, feeling your own bones vibrate in sympathy with a world that will not stop moving, that has never stopped moving, that would be dead if it stopped—

And then it passes. The coherence breaks. A throat-cricket shifts. A caller screams. The stone settles beneath someone's foot, and they take a step, and the terrace accommodates, and the singing goes on.
