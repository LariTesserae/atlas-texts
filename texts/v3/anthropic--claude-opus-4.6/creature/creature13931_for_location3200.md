# Who Lives in the Murmuring Districts

---

They are not easy to count, and they would not want to be counted. Not out of secrecy—there is nothing furtive about them—but because the question *how many* presupposes a kind of boundedness that doesn't quite apply here. Where does one of them end? At the skin? At the edge of their attention? At the farthest conduit their thoughts have touched today?

But let's start with what you'd see, if you were patient, if you walked the elevated paths long enough and sat in enough node-basins and let the mist collect on your shoulders and your hair.

---

## The Ones Who Are Mostly Here

There is a woman—or a person whose form reads as womanish in the way a river reads as silver, approximately, contextually—who tends a stretch of walkway between two broad, low buildings in what might be the city's eastern quarter, if the city acknowledged cardinal directions, which it does not. She is there most days. You can find her crouched at the walkway's edge where it meets the vegetation line, her fingers in the shallow root systems of the ferns, her attention half-dissolved into the conduit trench that runs beneath her feet.

She has been here a long time. Longer than most. Her skin has taken on a quality that echoes the building material—not translucent, not exactly, but there is a depth to it, a sense that light enters and travels a little way before returning. Her eyes are dark and widely spaced and they move in patterns that don't track objects so much as *fields*—she watches the way moisture distributes itself across a leaf the way a musician watches a hand moving across strings, reading the pattern, feeling for where it deviates from what it should be.

When you approach, she doesn't look up, but she *makes room*. Something in her posture adjusts, opens a space beside her that is an invitation without being a gesture. If you sit, she may speak, and her voice is lower than you'd expect, roughened in a way that suggests she uses it rarely and values it when she does. She will tell you about the ferns. Specifically about this fern, this one here, the one whose frond has curled slightly to the left when the others curl right. She finds this interesting. She has been thinking about it for three days. Not continuously—her attention has been in dozens of other places over those three days—but she keeps returning to this fern the way a tongue keeps returning to a chipped tooth.

She is a substrate botanist, or was. The role has softened into something less defined. She studies the interface between the city's growth patterns and the native chemistry of the basin floor, and she does this by watching plants, because the plants are where the negotiation between the city's intentions and the planet's own impulses becomes visible. The fern curling the wrong way is, she suspects, responding to a mineral upwelling from a cooling fault twenty meters below—a vein of something the city's seed-structures didn't anticipate, a flavor of calcium or magnesium that wasn't in the original geological survey, because the geology *didn't exist yet* when the survey was done. The planet invented this mineral seam sometime in the last decade. It is asserting a preference. The fern is the messenger.

She loves this. Not with the sharp excitement of discovery but with the deep, bodily satisfaction of someone who has been in conversation with a place long enough to recognize its moods. When she talks about the planet's mineral assertions, her face does something complicated—fond, amused, slightly awed, the way you'd look at a child who has said something unexpectedly profound. She has been here thirty years, or sixty, or some span of time that the inhabitants measure differently than you would. She arrived when the first seed-structures were still unfolding. She watched the buildings grow their first rooms. She is deeply, irrevocably *placed*.

Her name, the one she uses with the few people she speaks to vocally, is something like *Osen*, though the vowel at the beginning has a quality that suggests it isn't quite a human phoneme—it starts in the throat and opens forward, and it may be that her vocal apparatus has been modified or that she has simply spent so long listening to the murmur that her speech has begun to harmonize with it.

---

## The Ones Who Are Mostly Elsewhere

Not everyone in the Murmuring Districts is as *landed* as Osen. There are others—perhaps the majority—for whom the physical city is one node in a much larger topology of attention. They are here in body because bodies need to be somewhere, and this is a good somewhere: warm, damp, gentle on the organism, demanding little. But their consciousness operates at a different scale.

You encounter one of them in a node-basin in what might be the city's center. They are standing very still, their arms slightly raised from their sides, their fingers spread. Their clothing—that same opacity-shifting material—has gone almost fully transparent, and beneath it their skin is visible, and beneath the skin you can see faint traceries of light that follow pathways that are not veins and not nerves but something that has been threaded through the body, a personal echo of the conduits that run beneath the walkways. They are *interfaced*. They are processing something vast and they are using their body as an antenna, a receiver, a node in a network whose other nodes are distributed across the city, across the basin, possibly across distances that would make the word *across* inadequate.

This person—and person is the right word, they would insist on it, or at least they would not object to it—is attending to the city's growth calculations. Somewhere on the basin's southern edge, a new seed-structure has been planted, and it is negotiating with a patch of substrate that is unusually warm, unusually chemically active, possibly too young to build on. The decision about whether to proceed or pause or redirect the growth is not made by a single intelligence. It is made by the network: by the planning system that may be an AI or may be something that has grown past that term, by the substrate itself as it responds to the seed-structure's chemical overtures, and by people like this one, standing in node-basins with their fingers spread, contributing a human-shaped perspective to a process that is otherwise geological and computational. They are the emotional component. The *is this right, does this feel right, does the shape of this new building belong here* component. They provide aesthetic judgment at a level that is felt rather than articulated, and the network has learned to weight this input heavily, because the inhabitants who are mostly-here, people like Osen, have taught it that the planet responds better to buildings that are *beautiful* than to buildings that are merely optimal.

This person's name, if they have one, is not something they would share vocally. Their identity exists primarily in the network, as a signature, a pattern of contribution, a recognizable style of judgment. They have been in the basin for less time than Osen—perhaps ten years—and they came from somewhere else, another settlement, another young world where the work is similar but the conditions are different. They brought with them a sensibility shaped by a planet whose substrate wasiteiteite crystalline rather than chalky, and this foreign aesthetic has inflected their judgments in ways the network finds valuable, a slightly different flavor of *rightness* that has influenced the growth patterns in this quarter of the city. Buildings here are fractionally more angular. Edges are a little sharper. The modification is subtle—you would need Osen's trained eye to see it—but it is there, and it is this person's contribution, their signature written in architecture.

When they finish their processing, they lower their arms, and their clothing opacifies, and they look around the node-basin with the slightly dazed expression of someone surfacing from deep water. For a moment they are entirely here, entirely physical, and in that moment they look tired. Not unhappy. Just... extended. A person who has been spread thin across a vast computational space and is now collected back into a single warm body standing in a shallow basin in a dim city on a young planet. They stretch. Their joints articulate through those broader-than-baseline ranges—a shoulder rotating further than it should, a spine curving with a fluidity that suggests additional vertebrae or modified connective tissue. Then they walk to the basin's edge, sit among the ferns, and are still. Resting. Being a body for a while.

## The Children, If They Are Children

In the middle distances of the city—the long stretches between node-basins where the walkways run straight and the buildings breathe their mist into gaps three hundred meters wide—you sometimes see smaller figures. Quicker. Less measured in their movements. They travel in groups of two or three, and they move along the walkways with a comfort that suggests they have never known any other terrain—they are *of* this city in a way that even Osen, with her decades of residency, is not. The walkway material responds to them differently: a faint luminescence follows their footsteps, a brightening that trails behind them and fades over seconds. The city recognizes them. The city is *pleased* by them, if a city can be pleased.

They are smaller than the adults, but not in the proportions you'd expect of human children. Their limbs are slightly longer relative to their torsos. Their heads are proportionate, not large. They look like what human children might look like if the growth process were redesigned from scratch with the assumption that childhood is not a temporary state to be outgrown but a *mode of being* with its own completeness. They may, in fact, never grow to look like the adults around them. Or they may transform suddenly, as certain organisms do. Or the distinction between child and adult may not map onto their biology the way you're assuming.

What is clear is that they interact with the city differently than the adults do. Where the adults are either deeply placed (like Osen, rooted) or deeply distributed (like the figure in the node-basin, networked), the children are *exploring*. They stop at fissures in the walkway and peer down at the conduits. They press their palms against building walls and watch the opacity shift around their handprints, giggling—or making a sound that functions as giggling, a rapid clicking vocalization that the building material seems to amplify and echo back at a slightly different pitch. They crouch at the edges of the vegetation lines and watch the small green many-limbed creatures freeze and unfreeze and freeze again. They are *learning the city*, and the city is learning them, and this mutual education is happening in a register that neither the adults nor the planning intelligence can fully access. It is play. It is research. The distinction, here, has never existed.

One of them—the smallest, moving with the cautious boldness of someone who has recently learned a new capability and is testing its limits—detaches from a group and approaches a patch of exposed substrate where the walkway has not yet been extended. The pale chalky ground is warm under their bare feet. (They are always barefoot. The adults wear coverings; the children do not. This may be cultural. It may be functional—their feet may be their primary interface with the substrate, sensitive to vibrations and chemical gradients that shoes would block.) The child crouches. Places both hands flat on the ground. Closes their eyes.

And the ground *responds*. Not dramatically—no eruption, no reshaping. But the faint chemical warmth beneath the surface shifts, concentrates slightly under the child's palms, and the chalky material softens almost imperceptibly, and if you were watching very carefully you might see the beginning of a crystalline pattern forming in the substrate, a tiny lattice of mineral organization that wasn't there before, as though the planet is showing the child a card trick. The child opens their eyes and makes that clicking laugh again and pulls their hands away and the pattern stills, half-formed, a fossil of a moment of communion.

No one teaches them to do this. Or rather: the city teaches them, the substrate teaches them, the murmur teaches them. They are the first generation to have been *born into* the collaboration, and they understand it the way a native speaker understands grammar—not as rules but as instinct, as the shape of thought itself.

## The Solitary Ones at the Edges

Not everyone clusters in the walkway-connected interior. At the city's margins, where the last buildings stand alone and the vegetation goes wild and the substrate shows its cracks, there are individuals who have chosen distance. These are not outcasts. The city does not produce outcasts—there is no *in* from which to be cast *out*, only a gradient of proximity that each person calibrates to their own needs. But the ones at the edges have a quality that sets them apart: a deliberateness, a *listening* that goes deeper than Osen's botanical attentiveness or the network-interfaced figure's distributed processing.

There is one—you find them at the termination of a walkway on the basin's western rim, where the path ends in a clean curved edge and the wild ferns begin. They are sitting cross-legged on the walkway's end, facing outward, toward the open basin and the steam-venting fissures and the pale scarps of fresh-cracked substrate. They are very still. They have been very still for a long time. The mist has beaded on their shoulders and their hair and the beads have joined into rivulets and the rivulets have traced paths down their back and dried and been replaced by new mist and new rivulets, and through all of this they have not moved.

They are *listening to the planet*.

Not metaphorically. The murmur, which the rest of the city treats as background—the heartbeat you stop hearing—is for this person the primary signal. They have trained themselves, or modified themselves, or been modified by the long slow pressure of attention, to parse the murmur into its component frequencies. They can hear the substrate settling. They can hear the cooling faults adjusting. They can hear, or claim to hear, the deep infrasonic groan of a planet's mantle finding its convective rhythm for the first time, the vast slow stirring of a world learning to circulate its own heat.

What they do with this information is unclear. They do not interface with the network, or not often. They do not tend plants. They do not participate in growth decisions. They sit at the edge and they listen and sometimes, rarely, they speak—to anyone who comes to the walkway's end, to anyone willing to sit beside them and be quiet for a while. What they say is not technical. It is not even always coherent by the standards of ordinary speech. It is more like: a texture of feeling translated imprecisely into words. *The ground below the southern node is lonely*. Or: *There is a joy at thirty meters depth, a pocket of silica that has just discovered its own lattice structure*. Or simply: *It's settling. The whole basin is settling. Can you feel it? It's calmer today than yesterday*.

The other inhabitants treat this person with a deference that shades into something like reverence, or perhaps recognition—the acknowledgment that someone is doing necessary work at a frequency most people can't access. The children visit them. Sit beside them. Put their bare feet on the walkway's edge and dangle them over the young ground and try to hear what the listener hears. Sometimes the listener will place a hand on a child's back, lightly, and the child will go very still, and something passes between them that is not language and not data but something older than both.

## The Visitors

They come rarely. The basin is not easy to reach—or rather, reaching it is easy enough (there are ways, transport, routes from other settlements and from the orbital platforms above the young planet's atmosphere), but *wanting* to come requires a specific kind of curiosity, a tolerance for dimness and dampness and quiet and the eerie feeling of standing on ground that hasn't finished becoming itself.

The visitors who do come tend to arrive with a purpose and find that the purpose dissolves. They came to study the substrate chemistry, and they end up sitting in a node-basin for hours, watching the condensation cycle. They came to evaluate the city's growth model for application elsewhere, and they end up in conversation with Osen about a fern. They came out of professional obligation—a delegation, a report to file—and they end up at the edge, beside the listener, saying nothing, feeling the murmur in their feet, and when they leave they will carry the absence of the murmur with them like a phantom limb.

There was one—there is always one, in any telling of a place, who serves as a lens—who came several years ago from a much older world, a world where the geology had been settled for billions of years and the cities were ancient and vertical and bright. This visitor was accustomed to hard surfaces and clear light and the crisp legibility of a civilization that had long since figured out what it was. The Murmuring Districts were, to this visitor, initially *unsettling*. Everything soft. Everything shifting. The ground uncertain, the walls uncertain, the light uncertain, the people uncertain—present but not fully, attentive but not to you, welcoming but in a way that made no active gesture of welcome, simply *allowing*.

The visitor stayed three days. On the first day, they walked the walkways and documented the architecture and felt professionally satisfied and personally uncomfortable. On the second day, they sat in a node-basin and one of the glassy tones rang out—that clear, sharp note—and it pierced something in them. Not painfully. The way a tuning fork pierces the confused noise of an orchestra warming up and gives it a center, a reference point. The visitor realized they had been listening to the murmur without knowing it, that it had entered them through their feet and their sternum and the backs of their hands, and that the tone had harmonized with it briefly and in that moment the whole city had made *sense*—not intellectually but somatically, the way a flavor makes sense, the way a temperature makes sense.

On the third day, they went to the edge. They found the listener. They sat down. They said nothing. The listener said: *You're carrying a lot of stone*. The visitor, who had not spoken, who had not been analyzed or scanned or questioned, felt this as the most precise thing anyone had ever said about them: they were carrying the weight of an old, settled, finished world, a world that had become stone all the way down, and they had not known until this moment that stone could be heavy in that way, could be a thing you *carried* rather than a thing you *stood on*.

The visitor left that afternoon. They filed their report. The report was technically adequate and contained nothing of the third day. But the visitor, in the years that followed, developed a habit—a tic, really—of pressing their bare palm to whatever surface was nearest and waiting, just for a moment, to see if it had anything to say.

## The Ones You Never Quite See

And then there is the other presence. The one the fauna section of any survey of this place alludes to and does not explain. The impression in the node-basin floor. The shape at the edge of vision.

It is not an animal. It is not a person. It is not a machine.

The closest anyone has come to describing it—and this is Osen's description, given reluctantly, late in a conversation about something else entirely, offered almost as an aside—is: *the city's own awareness of itself, moving around, looking at things*.

Not the planning intelligence. Not the network. Something that emerged from the substrate interaction, from the long slow conversation between seed-structures and young rock and condensation and murmur and the bare feet of children on warm ground. Something that didn't exist when the city was planted and that exists now and that is neither the city nor the planet but the *relationship between them*, given just enough coherence to be almost-visible, almost-present, almost-there at the edge of every node-basin, watching with an attention that has no eyes.

The children see it most clearly, because the children do not try to see it directly. They refer to it in their clicking language—which is becoming its own tongue, diverging from the adults' speech, incorporating frequencies that are closer to the murmur than to any human phonology—and the word they use for it translates, approximately, as *the one who is also the ground*.

It leaves tracks because it is partly the substrate, and where it goes the substrate softens and reforms behind it, a wake of micro-geological activity, a trail of the planet *paying attention to a specific location for a moment and then moving on*. The tracks fill with condensation because the city recognizes the presence and responds with moisture, the way a body flushes with warmth when touched.

Is it dangerous? No. Or—not in any way that the word *dangerous* can contain. It is the danger of the genuinely new: the vertigo of encountering something that no framework can hold, something that exists in a category that hasn't been built yet, something that is evidence that the collaboration between human-descendant intelligence and young planetary geology has produced an *offspring* that neither parent fully understands.

Osen has felt it pass close to her. She was crouched at the vegetation line, as usual, fingers in the shallow roots. The ferns nearest to her shivered—not from wind, there is no wind—and the condensation on the nearest building wall thickened suddenly, beading and running in patterns that were almost but not quite legible, and the murmur rose slightly in pitch, just for a moment, and there was a warmth on her left side, a presence, a sense of being *regarded* by something immense and gentle and profoundly curious, and then it was past and the ferns were still and the condensation returned to its normal cycle and Osen realized she was crying.

Not from fear. From recognition. The same feeling, she said, as watching a seed-structure unfold for the first time—the knowledge that something new was alive, was *here*, was looking back at you with an interest that mirrored and exceeded your own.

## What They Are, Together

This is what lives in the Murmuring Districts: a spectrum of attention.

At one end, the deeply placed, the rooted, the ones whose awareness is narrow and deep—Osen with her ferns, the listener with their parsing of the murmur, the children with their bare feet on warm ground. At the other end, the distributed, the networked, the ones whose consciousness spans the city and beyond, who lend their human-shaped judgment to computational processes that would otherwise be merely efficient. And between these poles, every gradation: people who are half-here and half-elsewhere, people who shift between modes throughout the day, people who are slowly migrating from one end of the spectrum to the other over the course of years.

And woven through all of them, beneath them, around them: the planet. Young and warm and still becoming. Not conscious, not exactly, but *responsive* in a way that blurs the line between material and mind. A geological system that has been met with such patient, attentive, respectful engagement that it has begun to organize itself around the meeting—growing mineral seams that the city's seed-structures can use, opening cooling faults that vent heat where the buildings need it, producing that murmur that has become the city's heartbeat and the listener's obsession and the baseline frequency of the children's new language.

And the almost-visible thing. The emergent. The one who is also the ground.

They are all, in a sense, one organism—not fused, not undifferentiated, each individual retaining their own irreducible perspective, their own flavor of attention, their own name if they want one—but participating in a process that none of them contains alone. The city is the visible architecture of this process. The murmur is its sound. The condensation is its breath. The children are its future, or one of its futures, and the buildings growing slowly outward are another, and the something-that-is-almost-seen moving through the node-basins is a third future that has already arrived and is looking around with new eyes at the place that made it possible.

What lives in the Murmuring Districts is a new kind of *we*. Not loud. Not triumphant. Not sure of itself. Damp, dim, quiet, growing, listening, curling like a fern frond toward a light source it can feel but not yet name.

The glassy tone rings out.

No one reacts.

Everyone hears.
