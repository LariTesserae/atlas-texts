# The Inhabitants of the Pelvian Shelf

---

Start with the eels, because the eels are the oldest. Not in any individual sense—a single eel lives perhaps six years, perhaps ten, you will never know because no one here is counting—but as a lineage. The eels predate the city. The eels predate the *drainage channels*, if you can believe that, and you should, because the drainage channels were carved to formalize paths the eels had already worn into the rock over millennia of passage. They are the city's first fact.

They are not actually eels. You call them eels because you need to call them something and they are long and legless and move through water, and that is where your accuracy ends. Their skin is not scaled but layered in overlapping folds of a dense, rubbery integument, dark brown on the dorsal surface and a grayish cream beneath, and between the folds the skin is paler, so that when they flex you see a flickering pattern of light-dark-light that ripples down their bodies like a barcode being read. Their musculature is extraordinary. Pick one up—you won't, because they are heavy and uncooperative, but imagine it—and you would feel a body that is essentially one continuous muscle sheathed in leather, dense as a rolled carpet, warm to the touch. They generate their own heat. Their metabolism runs hot, which is why the drainage channels are always slightly warmer than the ambient stone, which is why other things live in the drainage channels too, which is why the whole wet underworld of the city operates as a distinct thermal ecosystem. The eels are its engine.

They breathe through a series of lateral openings behind what you would call the head, though their head is really just the front end of their body, slightly flattened, with a hard keratinous ridge where a jaw should be. They don't bite. They *rasp*. That ridge is a feeding structure: they press it against the biofilm that coats the wet stone—a slick, living layer of bacteria and microorganisms that is itself fed by whatever dissolved chemistry the water carries up from the deep rock—and they scrape. You can hear it if you get close enough and the ambient noise dies down, which it rarely does. A faint, rhythmic scratching, like a thumbnail drawn across wet chalk. Hundreds of them rasping in a single channel produces the sound of quiet, persistent erosion, which is exactly what it is. They eat the walls. They eat the film on the walls, and in doing so they scour the channels clean, and in scouring the channels clean they prevent blockage, and in preventing blockage they maintain the drainage system that keeps the city from drowning in its own perpetual moisture. They are infrastructure. They are alive and they are infrastructure, and they do not know this and do not care.

Their social behavior is simple and strange. They move in braided columns—not single file, not abreast, but in a constantly interweaving rope of bodies, each eel threading over and under its neighbors in a pattern that appears random but maintains a consistent overall density and speed. The column moves at about the pace of a slow walk. An individual eel within the column moves faster, because it is constantly adjusting its position relative to every other eel, threading forward, looping back, pressing sideways, in a kind of rolling peristaltic negotiation of space. Why? Possibly thermoregulation—the constant contact distributes heat. Possibly communication—their lateral skin folds are rich with mechanoreceptors, and the pressure language of a hundred bodies sliding against yours carries information you cannot read. Possibly it is simply what they do, a behavior so old it precedes any purpose and has become self-justifying.

They reproduce in the deep cisterns, in the dark, in the warm. You will not see this. No one sees this. The cisterns are closed environments, accessible only through channels too narrow for anything larger than an eel, and what happens there is between them and the water. Juvenile eels emerge into the upper channels at roughly a third of adult size, already rasping, already braiding into the columns. They are not taught. They arrive knowing.

---

Now the herd creatures. The ones like dogs with the temperament of goats and the social structure of termites. That description was a scaffolding; let it fall away. They are not like anything you know. They are themselves.

Call them *runners*, because that is the sound they make—a low, continuous, running hum—and because they run, perpetually, through the grooves of the city. Not running as a horse runs, with visible effort and a destination. Running as a river runs: continuously, along a carved path, because the path is there and the running is what fills it.

Six legs. The body plan is bilaterally symmetrical and hexapodal, with three pairs of limbs arranged along a long, low torso. The front pair is the lightest, used for manipulation and fine navigation—they end in four-fingered extremities with blunt, hard nails that click against the stone and can grip, after a fashion, though without the opposability you'd need to call them hands. The middle pair bears the majority of the body's weight and provides the primary locomotion: thick-thighed, powerful, with a sprung gait that absorbs the vibration of the stone. The rear pair is heavier still and articulates differently—more like a lever than a leg, providing thrust on inclines and braking on descents. Watch a column of them moving through a corridor and you see six different rhythms per animal, each pair of legs operating in its own cycle, the whole body a polyrhythmic engine of efficient motion. They don't gallop or trot. They *flow*, each footfall placed in the grooves worn by ten thousand predecessors, the rhythm so ingrained that an individual runner removed from the column and placed on flat, ungrooved stone will stumble and circle, confused, as if the path itself were a necessary part of the anatomy of movement.

Their fur is short, dense, slightly oily, and the color of the native stone—a pale, warm gray that shifts toward cream in direct light and toward lavender in shadow. This is not camouflage. There is nothing here to hide from. It is simply what happens when a species spends uncountable generations in a monochrome environment: the palette converges. Their eyes are large, set laterally on a blunt, broad head, and they are the deep amber color of water in a stone cistern when the light comes down at an angle. The eyes see well in the diffuse, shadowless light of the mid-levels and poorly in darkness. The eyes are, to be honest, the most beautiful thing about them, and the runners do not know this.

The hum. The resonant structure in their barrel chests is not a larynx—they have a larynx too, higher up, which they use for sharp alarm calls and a clipped contact bark—but a separate organ, a dense plate of cartilage suspended between two muscular membranes in the lower thorax. When they breathe, which they do in a deep, steady rhythm matched to their gait, the exhaled air passes over this plate and sets it vibrating at a frequency between thirty and sixty hertz. They don't choose to hum. They hum because they are breathing and moving, and when they stop moving they stop humming, and when they stop humming other runners nearby grow agitated and begin to crowd toward the silent one, pressing against it with their bodies, as if the silence were a wound that physical contact could close.

This is the core of their social structure: the hum is the social structure. A column of runners is not a group of individuals choosing to travel together. It is a single acoustic entity, a resonant body made of bodies, and the behavior of the column emerges from the interactions of sound waves in stone corridors in the same way that the behavior of a flock of starlings emerges from the interactions of individual flight vectors. No runner leads. No runner follows. The column moves because the hum moves, and the hum moves because the corridor shapes it, and the corridor was shaped by the movement of columns, and you can chase this circle as long as you like without finding a beginning.

They eat. What do they eat? In a world without plants, everything that eats is eating something that ate something that ate the biofilm, or the dissolved minerals, or whatever the ocean sends north in the humid air. The runners eat a dense, paste-like substance that accumulates in specific architectural features of the mid-levels—broad, shallow depressions in the stone, always located at junctions in the drainage network, where mineral-rich water pools and evaporates slowly, leaving behind a crust of concentrated nutrients colonized by bacterial mats. These are the *feeding zones* referenced in the city's migration patterns. The runners arrive, stop humming (a brief, uncomfortable silence), lower their blunt heads, and scrape the crust with a hard palatal ridge not unlike the eels' rasping structure. They eat quickly and in shifts, some feeding while others maintain a diminished hum at the edges of the depression, and then they move on and the depression begins to refill. The timing is precise. A feeding zone is visited by a column at intervals that exactly match the regeneration rate of the crust. Whether this is instinct or learned or simply the result of a system that has had time to find its equilibrium, you cannot determine.

They push the carts. This is the thing that makes you look twice, the thing that brushes against the question of intelligence. In the upper levels, where the rail system runs, teams of six to ten runners are harnessed to heavy wheeled carts by means of leather-and-bronze rigging that buckles around their middle and rear legs. They push these carts along the rails—loaded with quarried stone, with water vessels, with bales of processed eel-skin, with things you cannot identify—and they do it with the same unthinking rhythmic efficiency with which they do everything else. The harnesses fit them perfectly. The carts are designed for their pushing gait. The rails are set at the spacing of their grooved paths. Everything matches, everything interlocks, and the runners show no more awareness of the cargo they move than a river shows of the silt it carries.

But. Sometimes a cart derails—a wheel catches, a rail joint fails—and what happens next is not instinct. The team stops. The hum changes frequency, rising, becoming arrhythmic. Other runners from nearby columns converge. They crowd around the derailed cart. They push against it from different angles. They grip the wheel assembly with their front limbs and *lever* it, coordinating their effort with a staccato series of clicks from the contact-bark register, not the hum. They fix it. They right the cart. They re-seat it on the rails. And then they return to their columns and resume humming as if nothing happened.

You watch this and you don't know what you've seen. Problem-solving, or a fixed behavioral response to a stimulus that has occurred ten million times before and has been optimized by selection into something that *looks* like problem-solving? You can't ask them. They're already gone, flowing through the groove, humming, carrying the city's cargo on iron rails through stone corridors toward a destination that is not a destination but a continuation.

---

The membrane-flyers.

They live in the vertical. They *are* the vertical—the great shafts and voids of the city exist, as an ecological matter, as a medium for their movement, and to stand at the bottom of a major shaft and look up through fifty meters of spiraling, pouring, light-filtering bodies is to understand that architecture and biology have fused here into a single thing that is neither.

Their body plan is radically unlike anything else on the Shelf. They have no skeleton. Not a reduced skeleton, not a cartilaginous skeleton—*no skeleton*. They are soft-bodied, and their entire structure is hydraulic: a closed system of fluid-filled chambers that can be pressurized and depressurized to change the body's shape, stiffness, and surface area in real time. At full extension, a mature membrane-flyer is a diamond or kite shape perhaps two meters across, thinner than a fingernail at the edges, with a denser central body about the size of a human fist from which the membrane radiates in all directions. At full compression, the same animal is a tight, wrinkled packet about the size of a grapefruit, dense and heavy, dropping through the shaft like a thrown stone. Between these extremes is every possible intermediate shape: cupped, twisted, fluted, asymmetric, rippling, folded into complex airfoil profiles that would make an engineer first confused and then envious.

They are translucent. Not transparent—the membrane has a milky, opalescent quality, like a soap bubble thickened to the consistency of wet paper, and the denser central body is a visible dark node at the center—but translucent enough that light passes through them, colored and scattered by the thin tissue, so that a shaft full of flyers becomes a shaft full of moving, layered light. The effect from below is something like looking up through a stained-glass window that is constantly rearranging itself. The effect from the side, looking across a shaft, is something like watching thought happen—shapes forming and dissolving, patterns emerging and collapsing, the whole volume of air becoming a medium for slow, silent, luminous motion.

They are not silent. They make the wet-fabric sound as they reshape—a snap when they open, a flutter when they fold—and in a crowded shaft the accumulated sound is continuous, a white-noise rustle punctuated by sharper cracks when a flyer makes a sudden maneuver. They also produce, from the central body, a high, thin tone at the upper edge of your hearing—a sound you feel as a pressure in the sinuses more than you hear as a pitch. This is echolocation or something like it. The shafts are reverberant, and the flyers navigate them with a spatial awareness that goes beyond vision. They never collide. In a shaft with hundreds of them, each reshaping second by second, each riding a different thermal thread, they never collide. The thin tone bounces off the stone walls and off each other's membranes and returns a map of the air to every flyer simultaneously, and they adjust, and the adjustment is seamless, and the collective movement remains fluid and unhurried, as if choreographed by someone who had infinite time and infinite patience.

They eat in the air. The warm updrafts carry moisture and microparticles—dust from the stone, fragments of biofilm, the shed skin cells and metabolic byproducts of every animal in the city below—and the membrane-flyers spread themselves wide and *filter*. The membrane surface is not smooth; under magnification it would reveal a forest of microscopic cilia, mucus-coated, catching and trapping the particulate matter that the air carries. They are the city's lungs, or its kidneys, or both. They feed by cleaning the air, and the air needs cleaning, and so they fly, and so they eat, and the system sustains itself.

They reproduce by budding. A mature flyer, well-fed, develops a thickening at one edge of its membrane—a denser node that grows over a period of weeks, gradually developing its own hydraulic chambers, its own cilia, its own capacity for tone production. When it is ready—and readiness appears to be determined by some threshold of size and complexity—it separates, falls, catches itself, and begins to fly independently. There is no parental care. There is no parental awareness. The budded offspring is simply a new flyer in the shaft, instantly integrated into the collective movement, and the parent is slightly smaller for a while until it feeds and grows back to its full span.

Some of them are old. You can tell by the opacity of the membrane—older flyers lose their translucence, becoming cloudy, then milky, then nearly opaque, a dull white like sun-bleached paper. The oldest ones fly at the highest altitudes, near the open sky, where the thermals are strongest and the air is cleanest and the light is direct, and they move more slowly, with longer glides and fewer reshapings, as if the hydraulic system is losing its responsiveness, as if the body is stiffening toward a final shape. Eventually one of them stops flying. You would not notice. One among hundreds, it folds into a loose packet—not the tight compression of a dive but a relaxed, asymmetric collapse—and falls. It strikes the stone at the bottom of the shaft with almost no sound. Within hours, the carpet-crawlers arrive.

---

The carpet-crawlers.

They are the most difficult inhabitants to describe because they are the most difficult to see as individuals. Your eye registers them as a phenomenon—a living surface treatment, a mobile texture applied to the stone—before it registers them as animals. They are small, armored, hexagonal in cross-section, about the size of the last joint of your thumb, and they travel in sheets of hundreds or thousands, moving across the vertical walls with a synchronized rippling motion that looks like wind moving through a field of grain, if the grain were made of dark chitin and the field were a sheer cliff face.

Each crawler has six stubby legs, each tipped with a micro-adhesive pad that grips the stone with a strength wildly disproportionate to the animal's size. It has a pair of short, sensitive antennae. It has mandibles capable of processing organic matter—dead flyers, shed eel-skin, waste from the runners, anything that falls or accumulates in the cracks and corners of the city. It has a hard dorsal shell, dark and slightly iridescent, that interlocks with the shells of its neighbors when the swarm compresses, turning the carpet into an effectively continuous armored surface. On its own, it is a small, uncomplicated animal with simple needs and simple behaviors.

On its own, you will never see it.

The collective behavior of the carpet-crawlers is the thing that keeps you awake at night after you leave the Pelvian Shelf, the thing that you turn over in your mind for years, the thing that you are never sure you understood correctly. Because the swarms *do things*. They repair. Not metaphorically—they physically repair the city. When a stone surface cracks—from the slow thermal cycling of the plateau, from the vibration of megafauna passing, from the rare seismic event—the crawlers converge on the crack, pack themselves into it, and *secrete*. They produce, from glands on their ventral surface, a calcium-ite calcium-ite calcium—a mineral paste, effectively. A slurry of calcium compounds that hardens over hours into something indistinguishable from the native stone. They fill the crack. They smooth it. They match the contour of the surrounding surface. Then they move on.

This alone would be remarkable. But the swarms also build. Not often—it is a rare behavior, observed only in the densest, most active swarm-zones in the mid-levels—but unmistakably. They build *up*. They add material to the tops of existing walls, extending them by millimeters per day, millimeters that accumulate over years into centimeters, over decades into measurable new structures. The chambers in the mid-levels that look like they were built at brick-scale? They were. By crawlers. Over centuries. One secreted layer at a time, one hexagonal shell-print at a time, building walls whose joints are so fine they are invisible because the builder *was* the joint, the builder filled every gap with its own body before leaving its mineral contribution and moving on.

And the patterns. You keep coming back to the patterns. When a swarm moves across a wall, the rippling movement is not random. It is not exactly regular either. It is *almost* geometric—waves that nearly form grids, spirals that nearly close into circles, branching paths that nearly follow the optimized drainage fractals of the deep channel network. Almost. Nearly. It is like watching someone try to remember a language they once spoke fluently. Or like watching a language being born, syllable by syllable, across a surface of living chitin, and not knowing whether you are witnessing the last echo of a vanished intelligence or the first stirrings of one yet to come.

The crawlers do not have individual brains large enough to sustain what the swarms do. The swarms do what they do. Draw your own conclusions.

---

The cistern-filters. The soft-bodied things in the water.

They live in the cisterns and the wider, slower sections of the drainage network, anchored to the stone by a muscular foot, their bodies flexing open and closed in a visible, rhythmic pulse—the peristalsis you noticed. They look like translucent bags, fist-sized to head-sized, pinkish or grayish, with a tough outer membrane and a complex interior of folded, gill-like structures that maximize surface area. Water enters through an intake opening at the top, passes through the interior folds, and exits through a ring of smaller openings at the base. Everything in the water that is organic—bacteria, dissolved proteins, the metabolic products of every other creature in the system—is absorbed in transit. What exits is clean. Not sterile, but clean. Balanced. Chemically stable. Drinkable, if you were willing to drink it, which you are, because you are thirsty and it is all there is and it tastes of stone and nothing else.

They are simple creatures in themselves. They have no eyes, no ears, no apparent sensory apparatus beyond a diffuse chemical sensitivity that allows them to orient their intake openings toward the densest nutrient flow. But they are the critical node in the city's ecology—the point where the waste products of everything above are converted back into the raw inputs for the biofilm below. Without them, the water would become toxic within years. The biofilm would die. The eels would starve. The feeding crusts would stop forming. The runners would stop running. The city would die from the bottom up, the way a tree dies from root rot, slowly at first and then all at once.

There are a lot of them. More than you would think. In the deeper cisterns, where the light doesn't reach, they pack the walls so densely that the stone is invisible beneath them—a living lining of pulsing, filtering tissue, pink and gray and faintly luminous where the bioluminescent worm-tangles grow among them, the two organisms intertwined so thoroughly that it's unclear where one ends and the other begins. The worms provide light. The filters provide a surface to grow on and a waste-product stream rich in the nutrients the worms need. Neither can survive without the other. They are a single system with two heartbeats.

---

The worm-tangles.

In the deep places where no light reaches, they are the light. They grow in dense, tangled masses in the warm crevices where drainage channels converge, and they glow—a soft, bluish-white luminescence that pulses slowly, out of sync, so that a wall of worm-tangles appears to breathe with light, areas brightening and dimming in overlapping waves that have no pattern you can discern. The light is not for you. It is not for anything. It is a metabolic byproduct, a side effect of the chemical processes that sustain the worms, and its usefulness to other organisms—the crawlers navigate by it, the juvenile eels orient toward it—is an accident of evolution that has been integrated into the system so thoroughly that it now appears designed.

The worms themselves are thin, pale, and essentially featureless—eyeless, mouthless tubes that absorb nutrients directly through their skin from the mineral-rich water and the waste products of the cistern-filters. They tangle because tangling increases their collective surface area and maintains the humid microenvironment they need. An individual worm, separated from the tangle, dries out and dies within hours. The tangle is the organism. The individual is the cell.

They are very old. Not individually—individual worms live perhaps a year—but the tangles persist. A tangle grows by adding new worms at its edges and losing dead ones at its core, a slow turnover that maintains the structure indefinitely, the way a river maintains its identity despite being made of entirely different water from one moment to the next. Some of the tangles in the deepest cisterns are enormous—room-sized masses of glowing filaments that have been growing in place since before the iron infrastructure was installed, since before the upper levels were built, since before anything you can date. They are the second-oldest thing here, after the eels.

---

The megafauna.

You have been hearing them this entire time. Now one is approaching.

The vibration builds in the stone for minutes before you see it—a rhythmic compression wave, low and slow, the frequency of a heartbeat but much, much larger. The runners in the nearest groove falter in their hum, adjust, pick up a new rhythm that incorporates the approaching concussion. The crawlers on the nearby wall flatten and compress, interlocking their shells. The air itself seems to thicken.

It comes around a curve in the main corridor—a corridor twenty meters wide and three stories tall, built for this—and your sense of biological scale recalibrates. It is the size of a building. Not a house, a *building*. Four stories tall at the shoulder, wider than a truck, and long—impossibly long, a body plan that is more like a bridge than an animal, the mass distributed across four colossal legs spaced so far apart that smaller creatures pass *between* them without interrupting their own movements or the megafauna's.

It is armored. The plates on its body are not shell—they are *stone*. Or they began as stone. Or they are a biological mineral deposit indistinguishable from stone. They are the same pale calcium-ite as the walls, the same color, the same texture, and they are thick—hand-span thick on the flanks, thicker on the shoulders and the massive, low-slung head. Between the plates, in the joints and flexion points, the skin is visible: dark, wrinkled, tough as boiled leather, with a vascular flush that tells you this is a warm-blooded animal despite every visual signal suggesting otherwise. It looks like the city grew legs. It looks like a piece of the architecture decided to go for a walk.

Its head is enormous and almost featureless—a blunt, forward-facing slab of plate armor with two deep-set eyes the color of wet stone and a wide, downturned mouth that gives it an expression of permanent, geological melancholy. It does not appear to see you. It does not appear to see anything in particular. Its eyes move with the slow, unfocused sweep of an animal that processes its environment in a fundamentally different timeframe than you do—not blind, but operating on a sensory schedule where the world updates every few seconds rather than continuously.

It eats the walls. Not metaphorically. It stops, periodically, and presses its mouth against the stone, and the hard palatal structures inside—you can hear them working, a deep grinding that you feel in your chest—scrape and crush the mineral surface, ingesting the stone itself, or the biofilm on the stone, or both. The marks left behind are smooth, concave depressions that will be filled, over time, by the crawlers. The megafauna feeds the crawlers' repair cycle. The crawlers maintain the corridors the megafauna walks through. Another loop. Another circle with no beginning.

It moves through the corridor and you watch it go, and it takes a long time because it is slow and very large, and behind it the runners resume their humming in the grooves and the crawlers unlock their shells and begin to flow across the wall again and the sound of the city fills back in around the diminishing percussion of its footsteps.

There are not many of them. A few hundred, perhaps, across the entire Shelf. They are solitary, meeting only to mate, which they do somewhere in the deep lower levels—you hear it sometimes as a sustained, building vibration that lasts for hours and makes the water in the cisterns tremble. Gestation is long—years, probably. A juvenile megafauna is the size of a draft horse and already plated, already grinding the walls, already walking the great corridors with that unhurried, tectonic gait. It will grow for decades. It will live for centuries. It will walk the same corridors its entire life, wearing them deeper, polishing them smoother, becoming more encrusted with mineral deposits as it ages until, at the end, it is nearly indistinguishable from the architecture itself.

When a megafauna dies, it stops. That is all. It stops moving, usually in one of the great corridors, and it stands there, because its legs lock in place, and its plates harden further as the biological processes that maintained them cease, and within a year it is a stone formation. Within a decade the crawlers have integrated it into the wall. Within a century the corridor has been rerouted slightly to flow around what is now a natural-seeming pillar of pale stone, and no one remembers it was ever alive, if anyone here remembers anything at all.

Some of the pillars in the oldest corridors are suspiciously regular. Some of them, if you look closely, have the faintest suggestion of a shape—four vertical elements, a broad upper mass, a slab-like projection at one end that might, if you allowed yourself the fantasy, have once been a head. You press your hand against the stone and it is warm, but all the stone here is warm, and you cannot be sure.

---

Other things. Things you glimpse and cannot categorize.

In the deepest drainage channels, below even the worm-tangles, where the water comes up from the rock itself and is warm and mineral-dense and smells of sulfur: shapes. Moving. Large enough to displace water visibly but never surfacing fully, never resolving into a body plan you can describe. Dark. Slow. Possibly a single organism of enormous extent. Possibly a colony. Possibly your imagination, working overtime in the blue glow.

On the highest terraces, exposed to the open sky, where the wind blows and the stone is dry for the only time anywhere on the Shelf: small things. Crouching in the crevices. Emerging at dusk. They have eyes—large, forward-facing eyes, binocular, the eyes of a predator in a city that seems to have no predators. They are quick. They are furtive. They watch you with an intensity that nothing else here has shown. They are the size of a small cat, furred, dark, with hands—actual hands, four-fingered, dexterous, gripping the stone with a precision that speaks of intention. You see one of them pick up a small stone and turn it over in its fingers, examining it, and then place it deliberately on top of another stone. You see another one watching the first one do this.

You never see more than three or four at a time. They vanish if you approach. They leave behind small arrangements of stones that might be tools or caches or art or nothing at all.

You think about them more than anything else.

---

And at the very center of the Shelf—you never reach it, no one has told you it exists, you infer it from the way the drainage channels converge, the way the corridors orient, the way the megafauna paths all curve gently toward a single distant bearing—there is something. The channels get wider. The water flows faster. The hum of the runners takes on a harmonic you haven't heard before, a resonance that implies a very large enclosed space ahead, a void that dwarfs even the major shafts, a chamber or amphitheater or cathedral at the center of the continental city where all the water goes and all the corridors lead and all the species, perhaps, in their own time and their own way, converge.

You don't reach it. The corridors grow darker and the worm-tangles grow denser and the sound of water grows louder—not a drip now but a rush, a sustained note like an organ pipe—and the crawlers on the walls are moving faster, in tighter patterns, in patterns that are more than almost geometric, patterns that are *actually* geometric, precise, recursive, intentional, and you realize you are afraid, not of danger but of understanding something you have no framework for, and you stop, and you stand in the warm dark with the blue light pulsing around you and the sound of the city's deepest water ahead of you, and you do not go further.

Whatever lives at the center of the Pelvian Shelf, it has been living there for a very long time. It may be an organism. It may be an ecology. It may be the point where organism and ecology and architecture become the same thing—a single, slow, vast, living process of stone and water and bodies that has been running since before the iron, before the upper levels, before the drainage channels, before the eels, before the first rain fell on the plateau and found its way into the first crack and began the long, patient work of building a city out of the relationship between water and rock and the things that live where water meets rock.

It does not need you. It does not notice you. It is not waiting for anything. It is already happening, and it has been for a very long time, and it will continue, and the water will drip, and the eels will rasp, and the runners will hum, and the flyers will pour through the shafts like light made flesh, and the crawlers will build, and the megafauna will walk, and the small things with the clever hands will watch from the high terraces with their predator eyes, and somewhere in the deep center the water will rush and the patterns will form and the city will continue to be what it is: alive, and whole, and not yours, and not waiting, and not finished, and not started, but simply, endlessly, happening.
