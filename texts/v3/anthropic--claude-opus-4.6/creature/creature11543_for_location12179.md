# Who Lives Here

---

She is not always here. That is the first thing to understand about her, and the thing that takes longest to believe, because the courtyard feels so thoroughly *inhabited* by her — not by her possessions, not by her decorations, not by anything she has done to the space, but by the quality of attention she has left behind, the residue of a particular kind of looking that has soaked into the walls the way the damp has soaked into the eastern plaster.

She lives on the second floor. The balcony with the herb pots is hers — the rosemary, the thyme-or-marjoram, the unruly mint. She did not plant them with any plan. They accumulated the way her tenancy accumulated: one pot brought from somewhere else, another inherited from the previous occupant, a third started from a cutting a neighbor handed her over the shared rooftop one evening without explanation, just a sprig of something green wrapped in a damp cloth and the words *this does well here*. She waters them irregularly. The mint doesn't care. The rosemary has learned not to depend on her.

---

She is — let the description arrive slowly, the way she herself arrives, the way you become aware of her not all at once but in accumulating detail —

She is somewhere in the middle of her life, though the middle is wide and she is not interested in locating herself precisely within it. Not young enough to be described as young. Not old enough for age to be a characteristic anyone would mention first. Her body is the kind that has settled into its own use — strong hands, stronger than you'd expect, the tendons visible when she grips something, the nails kept short and not quite clean, not dirty either, but bearing traces of whatever she last touched: chalk dust, maybe, orite dust, or the fine powderedite that clings to everything in this town. Her skin is the brown of someone who has always lived in sun but does not seek it, does not worship it, simply exists within it the way the flagstones do — receiving, absorbing, darkening incrementally year by year. There are lines at the corners of her eyes that deepen when she focuses, which is often, and a single deep crease between her brows that she earned not from worry but from the habit of looking at things *very carefully*, the way you look at something when you are trying to understand not what it is but how it came to be what it is.

Her hair is dark, coarse, and she keeps it pulled back in a way that is not styled but managed — contained so it doesn't interfere. When it escapes, which it does, she pushes it behind her ear with the back of her wrist, a gesture so habitual it has become automatic, almost tidal, something her body does while her mind is elsewhere.

She moves through the courtyard the way water moves through the crack in the eastern wall — not quickly, not slowly, but *inevitably*, following a path that has been established by repetition rather than intention. She crosses it many times a day. From the stairwell in the northwest corner to the door in the southeast that leads to the street. From the stairwell to the eastern wall, where she sometimes stops to look at the damp streak the way someone else might check a clock — reading it, measuring something, tracking a change so gradual it can only be perceived over weeks. She crouches by the pool sometimes. Not every day. But sometimes. She has noticed the water beetle, or the larva, or whatever it is. She has not named it. She does not name things. But she has noted it, the way she notes everything, with a quality of attention that is closer to *witnessing* than *observing* — she does not record, does not measure, does not draw conclusions. She simply sees, and what she sees becomes part of the way she understands the space, which is not a map or a theory but something more like a weather sense, an interior knowledge of how things are going here, in this small enclosure, today.

---

She is — and this takes longer to understand, because she does not talk about it directly, and there are no obvious signs, no studio, no materials left out, no works in progress visible to a visitor —

She works with stone.

Not a sculptor. Not exactly a mason. Something between and beside those words, something the language of this town has a term for that does not translate cleanly. She repairs things. She fits things. She understands the way stone behaves — not in the abstract, not as engineering, but in the specific and the local, the way *this* limestone from *this* quarry responds to *this* climate, the way it weathers, the way it cracks, the way it accepts mortar or refuses it, the way it darkens with damp or bleaches in sun, the way it sounds when you tap it with a knuckle — a high, clean note if it is solid, a duller, shorter sound if there is a void within, a flaw, an invisible fracture that will become visible in ten years or fifty.

The flagstones in the courtyard are hers. She is the one who has been laying them.

This is not obvious. It might take you a long time to realize it, because there is — as noted — no evidence of work in progress. No tools, no chalk lines. She has put them away. She always puts them away. Not out of tidiness but out of a deeper instinct, something that has to do with the relationship between work and rest, between action and the pause that allows action to settle into its consequences. She lays a few stones. She stops. She waits. She lets the mortar cure. She lets the earth beneath adjust to the new weight. She comes down in the morning and in the evening and she looks at what she has done and she feels through her feet whether the stones have accepted their placement or whether something is wrong — a slight instability, a cant, a misalignment she can sense before she can see it.

The three unlaid flagstones in the corner — she has cut them. They lean against the wall, waiting, because *she* is waiting. The earth there is not ready. It is still too soft from the moisture that migrates underground from the eastern wall. If she laid the stones now, they would settle unevenly. In six weeks, or eight, when the dry season has fully established itself, the substrate will firm up, and she will lay them. She is not impatient about this. She is not patient either — patience implies the experience of waiting, implies desire held in check. She simply understands that the stones and the earth are on their own schedule, and her role is to be available when they are ready for each other. This is not a philosophy she has articulated. It is a practice she has absorbed from years of working with materials that operate on timescales longer than hers.

The boot print in the bare earth is hers. Her boots are by her door upstairs — heavy, blunt-toed, scarred, the leather cracked in the particular pattern that comes from repeated kneeling. She wears them only when she is working. Otherwise she crosses the courtyard barefoot or in sandals that are barely more than soles with straps, and this is how she feels the stones, how she reads the courtyard's state through the sensitive architecture of her feet.

---

She lives alone. But alone is not the right word, because it implies a missing other, a space shaped by absence, and her life is not shaped by absence. It is shaped by the courtyard, and the work, and the building, and the town's slow metabolism, and the particular quality of each day's light as it enters the rectangle of sky above and is processed by the walls into something softer, more interior, more usable.

She has a room. The room is on the second floor, behind the balcony with the herbs. It is small and it contains: a bed, narrow, pushed against the wall, its sheets white cotton, washed to a texture that is almost not a texture anymore, almost pure softness; a table, wooden, bearing a glass of water, a plate with the remnants of bread and oil, a pencil, a few sheets of paper with sketches on them that are not drawings exactly but diagrams — plans of stone placement, measurements, notes in a small hand that is more notation than prose; a chair; a shelf with books, not many, their spines sun-faded, some in the local language and some not, books about geology and building and one novel she has been reading slowly for months, not because it is difficult but because she does not want it to end; a hook on the back of the door where a cotton robe hangs, the blue of it washed to the blue of the sky in the water-pool's reflection, a blue that remembers being darker; and through the open door to the balcony, the smell of rosemary, which is the smell of her room, which is the smell of her, which is the smell the gecko knows and does not fear.

She eats simply. Bread from a bakery two streets away. Oil. Tomatoes in season, which is now. Cheese, sometimes, the hard, salty, crumbly kind made from sheep's milk that this region produces and that she buys from a woman in the market who recognizes her but does not know her name, or knows it but has never used it, because their transaction is conducted in nods and coins and the shared understanding that the cheese is good and that this is sufficient grounds for an ongoing relationship.

She drinks coffee in the morning, standing on the balcony, looking down into the courtyard. The coffee is thick, sweet, made in a small brass pot on a gas ring in the corridor that serves as her kitchen. She holds the cup — a handleless ceramic cup, white, chipped at the rim — in both hands, and she drinks slowly, and she looks at the courtyard, and this looking is not idle. It is the most deliberate thing she does all day. She is checking. She is reading. She is feeling the space's overnight changes — has the mortar dried? Has the damp streak advanced or receded? Has the fig tree pressed further along the wall? Has anything fallen, shifted, cracked, bloomed, died? She conducts this inventory not with her eyes alone but with her whole body, leaning slightly over the railing, letting the courtyard's trapped warmth rise to her face, breathing its smell — stone dust and moisture and green growth and the faintest trace of whatever the ants have found at the base of the fig tree, something sweet, something fermenting, a fallen fig perhaps, hidden beneath the leaves.

---

The fig tree is a question she has not answered.

She knows it is damaging the wall. She can see the mortar giving way where the roots have found purchase, can see the slow forced separation of stone from stone as the trunk thickens year by year. In her professional capacity — if that is the right word for something that is more vocation than profession — she should remove it. She has removed trees like it from walls like this in other buildings, other courtyards. She has extracted root systems from masonry with the careful, unhurried precision of a surgeon, leaving the wall weakened but recoverable, savable, capable of being repointed and reinforced and returned to structural service.

She has not removed this tree. She will not say why. If you asked her — and she is the kind of person you could ask, not because she is forthcoming but because she receives questions the way she receives everything, with a quality of attention that makes the asker feel that the question has been taken seriously — she would be quiet for a moment, and then she might say something about how the wall and the tree have been negotiating longer than she has been here, and that she is not sure it is her negotiation to interrupt. Or she might say nothing. She might just look at the tree, and in her looking you would see the answer, which is that she loves it, not sentimentally, not in the way people love things they have chosen, but in the way you love something you recognize — something whose way of being in the world mirrors your own.

The tree grows along the wall rather than away from it. It adapts to the structure. It does not resist the constraint of the courtyard; it incorporates the constraint into its form, lets the wall become part of its architecture, grows *with* the building rather than against it, even as it slowly, imperceptibly, takes the building apart. She understands this. She might be the only person who fully understands this, because she does the same thing. She lives within the building. She maintains it, repairs it, lays its floor stone by stone. And in doing so, in pressing herself into its structure the way the fig tree presses its branches against the plaster, she is also slowly changing it, making it hers, making it something it was not before she came and will not be after she leaves — whenever that is, which she has not thought about, because leaving, like the unlaid flagstones, belongs to a future that will arrive when the conditions are right, and not before.

---

She knows the gecko. Not *knows* — she has no relationship with it, no interaction, no sense of mutual recognition. But she is aware of it the way she is aware of the damp streak and the ant line and the fern's uncurling — as a fact of the courtyard, a datum, a thread in the weave of the place. She saw it first months ago and has since developed an unconscious habit of glancing at the gap in the western wall when she crosses the courtyard, confirming its presence or noting its absence with the same part of her mind that notes the mortar's color and the pool's level. If it disappeared — if the plaster were repaired and the gap closed and the gecko displaced — she would feel it as a wrongness, a disturbance in the texture of the space, the way you feel a missing tooth with your tongue.

The bird, too. She heard it before she saw the nest — heard it singing from the fig tree in the early mornings, a small brown bird, she doesn't know the species, has never thought to look it up, a bird that sings a phrase of five or six notes and then repeats it with a variation so slight it might be a mistake or might be improvisation, and she cannot tell which, and this inability to tell is part of what makes the song beautiful to her, the way the inability to tell whether the courtyard is being built or is building itself is part of what makes the courtyard beautiful.

She left the nest alone. She could have pruned the fig tree — it would have been reasonable, even responsible, a way of controlling its growth, limiting the damage to the wall. But the nest was there, and so she didn't, and now the pruning season has passed and the nest has become another fact of the courtyard, another element in the negotiation between structure and life, another thing she checks from the balcony each morning, coffee in both hands, the day still assembling itself around her.

---

She has neighbors. The building has other tenants — she is aware of them as presences, as sounds transmitted through the walls, as patterns of arrival and departure that she registers without tracking. The old woman on the third floor who hangs laundry on the rooftop and whose sheets sometimes drip into the courtyard, adding to the moisture economy of the eastern wall. The family on the ground floor — the northern side — whose children she hears but rarely sees, their voices arriving muffled through the plaster, laughter that has the compressed, echoless quality of sound trapped inside rooms. A man on her floor, the western side, whom she passes on the stairs and greets with the standard greeting and who returns it and they have never said anything else to each other and this is not unfriendly, this is the way of the building, the way of this town perhaps, where proximity is not the same as intimacy and where sharing a courtyard is a form of relationship that does not require conversation to be real.

The old woman on the third floor knows what she does — knows about the stones, has watched from above as she worked, has perhaps been watching for years, the way one watches weather, without comment but with interest. Once, the old woman left a glass of tea on the stair landing outside her door, still warm, with a sprig of mint from the balcony pot floating in it. She found it when she came up from working, hands chalky, knees stiff, and she drank it standing there in the stairwell, and it was sweet, sweeter than she would have made it, and she returned the glass to the landing clean and dry, and nothing was said about it, and the next time she worked in the courtyard the tea appeared again, and this has become a rhythm, a call and response, a relationship conducted entirely in objects and silence.

---

She sleeps with the balcony door open. The courtyard breathes into her room at night — its trapped warmth, its green smell, the sound of the water bead falling into the pool at intervals too long to count. *Tik*. She does not hear it consciously. But her sleep is shaped by it, organized around it, the way sleep is organized around a heartbeat — not disturbed by it but deepened, given a structure, a rhythm beneath the rhythm of her breathing.

She dreams, presumably. Everyone does. But her dreams are not the kind that leave traces — she does not wake with images or stories, does not carry narrative residue into the day. She wakes the way the courtyard wakes: gradually, by degrees, the light entering first, then the warmth, then the sounds — the bird's five-note phrase, a door somewhere, the distant unintelligible murmur of the town beginning its circulation — and then she is standing, and then she is at the gas ring, and then she is on the balcony with the coffee, and then she is looking down, and the day has begun without any clear moment of beginning, the way the mortar cures without any clear moment of being cured, the way the fig tree grows without any clear moment of being larger than it was.

---

On the days she works — and they are not every day, and they do not follow a schedule — she descends to the courtyard in the late afternoon, when the direct sun has passed and the light is the amber-gray she prefers, the light that does not create harsh shadows, that lets her see the true color and texture of the stone. She carries her tools in a canvas roll: a trowel, a pointing tool, a level, a rubber mallet, a brush. The roll is old. It was given to her by someone who taught her — not a formal teacher, not a school, but a person, older, now dead perhaps or simply elsewhere, a person who showed her how to read stone the way the stone wanted to be read, from the inside out, attending to its grain and its faults and its history of pressure and release. She unrolls the tools on the flagstones she has already laid. She kneels. She works.

The work is slow. Not performatively slow, not ceremonially slow. Just slow. Each stone must be set in a bed of mortar that is the right consistency — wet enough to grip, dry enough to hold — and then pressed into place and checked with the level and adjusted with the mallet, tap by tap, each tap a negotiation, a question asked of the stone and the earth beneath it: *here? like this? is this where you want to be?* And sometimes the answer is no, and she lifts the stone and scrapes the mortar and starts again, and this is not frustration, this is the work, this is what the work *is* — not the laying of stones but the finding of the right placement, the convergence of stone and earth and mortar and gravity and the particular geometry of this courtyard, this floor, this corner of the world.

She does not listen to music while she works. She does not talk to herself. She is quiet in the way the courtyard is quiet — not empty of sound but full of attention, every sense directed at the task, her fingers reading the stone's surface the way the ants read the mortar with their antennae, the way the gecko's eyes read the wall for movement. She is, in these moments, not separate from the courtyard. She is part of its process. She is the means by which it is becoming what it is becoming, but she is also becoming what she is becoming, and these two becomings are not parallel — they are the same becoming, the same slow emergence of form from potential, the same negotiation between what is given and what is made.

---

When she finishes — or rather, when she stops, because she never finishes, because the work is not the kind that finishes — she rolls up her tools and carries them upstairs and washes her hands in the basin in the corridor-kitchen and the water runs gray with stone dust and she watches it spiral down the drain and this too is part of the work, this letting go, this returning the dust to the water cycle that will eventually bring it back to the walls, to the damp streak, to the pool.

Then she stands on the balcony. The light is different now — the last of it, the deep amber that comes just before the town's rooftops cut off the sky entirely. The courtyard below is dimmer. The gecko has moved or has not moved. The ants are still working; they will work into the darkness. The fig tree's leaves are black shapes against the gray-white plaster. The bird is in the nest or is not.

She stands there, and she is aware of all of this without cataloging it, without holding it in language. She is simply present in the space she is making, which is also making her, and the evening comes, and the rectangle of sky above the courtyard turns from amber to violet to a blue so deep it is almost not blue, and the first star appears — she can see exactly one star from this vantage, framed by the building's upper edges, and it is always the same star, and she does not know its name, and she does not need to.

---

There is a thing about her that is hard to say because it is not a thing she does or a thing she is but a thing she *occupies*, a space she *fills* — and it is this:

She is the answer to the courtyard's question.

The courtyard asks: *Can a thing be whole while still becoming?* And she — in her presence, in her unfinished work, in her life conducted among these walls and stones and plants and animals, in her solitude that is not loneliness, in her patience that is not patience but something more like *faith in the rate of things* — she answers: yes. Not in words. Not in any declarative way. But in the way she lives here, which is the way the moss lives here, which is the way the water lives here, which is the way the mortar lives here — slowly, deliberately, without rushing toward completion, without fearing incompleteness, taking the shape that the constraints allow, filling the cracks, finding the level, settling in.

She is, like the courtyard, still learning to be what she is. And this learning is not a failure. It is the thing itself. It is the whole of it. The courtyard that is still learning to be stone. The woman who is still learning to be here.

They are teaching each other. And the lesson has no end, and needs none.
