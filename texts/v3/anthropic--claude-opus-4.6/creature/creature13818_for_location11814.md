# Who Lives on the Ridgeback

---

## The Thermal Engineers

Start with the ones you'd notice first, if you came by rail.

They meet the train. Not out of ceremony — someone has to check the catenary junction where the spur line branches into the settlement, and someone has to verify the thermal load manifest, and it happens that both tasks bring people to the rail platform at the same time, so there is always a small gathering when a car arrives. Three, four people standing in the amber light of the platform alcove, which is really just a widened slot in the rock with a riveted iron canopy to deflect wind-driven grit.

They are, as a rule, not tall. Stocky, thick-armed, with a density to them that mirrors the architecture. Whether this is adaptation over generations or simply the kind of body that persists in a place where everything is heavy — the tools, the stone blocks, the turbine components that must be wrestled through narrow gallery shafts — is unclear. Probably both. Their skin is weathered in a way that suggests mineral exposure as much as sun: a faint roughness, almost powdery at the knuckles and the bridge of the nose, where fine silicate dust has worked into pores over years and not been entirely dislodged. They don't seem bothered by it. Some of the older ones have a faint greenish cast to the calluses on their palms from handling raw olivine, and they'll show you if you ask, turning their hands over without pride or embarrassment, just presenting a fact: *this is what the rock does to you if you handle it long enough.*

Their clothes are heavy canvas and treated mineral fiber — a textile they produce themselves from processed pyroxene, spinning the crystalline material into something flexible enough to weave but still stiff compared to any natural cloth. It doesn't drape; it *panels.* A person in full Ridgeback workwear looks somewhat architectural, all flat planes and hard edges, the fabric breaking at angles rather than curves. The color is the color of everything here: dark greenish-black, with occasional panels of rust-brown where iron-rich thread has been deliberately incorporated. They do this for identification — not decoration, they'll tell you, though you'll notice the patterns vary by settlement and by family and that certain configurations are clearly preferred, clearly *chosen*, which is decoration by another name. They tolerate this observation with something that is almost a smile.

The thermal engineers are the aristocracy, insofar as the Ridgeback has one. They maintain the deep shafts. They understand — or claim to understand, which amounts to the same thing in a place where no one can easily verify — the behavior of the thermal gradient beneath a given settlement, its seasonal fluctuations (minor), its century-scale drift (significant), and the micro-variations that can indicate a shaft is approaching a zone of diminished output. This knowledge is largely empirical, passed down in long apprenticeships that begin in adolescence and don't conclude until the apprentice is well into middle age. A thermal engineer who has completed training is typically around forty, and they will practice for another forty years if the rock permits. They descend into the shafts weekly, sometimes more often, sometimes spending days below in the progressively warmer galleries where the turbine assemblies do their slow work. They come back up flushed and quiet, smelling of hot iron and something else, something mineral and intimate, like the smell of the rock's interior — a smell the surface never quite has.

They speak about the deep shafts with a precision that borders on tenderness. *The forty-meter gallery in Shaft Six has shifted a quarter degree warmer this year. The resonance in the secondary turbine housing is slightly flatter than last season, which means the bearing cradle may need re-seating sometime in the next five to eight years.* They know their shafts the way someone else might know a river — its moods, its habits, the places where it does unexpected things. And like people who know rivers, they respect what they work with without romanticizing it. The heat is a resource. It is also a danger. Shafts have collapsed. Thermal surges — rare, unpredictable upwellings of radically hotter material from deeper in the Ridgeback — have killed engineers in their galleries, not by burning them but by simply *cooking* the air around them until it could no longer sustain breath. There is a memorial tradition: a small bore-hole drilled at the entrance to a shaft where someone has died, exactly the diameter of a human wrist, descending at an angle into the warm interior. The similarity to the biological bore-holes is noted and never discussed.

## The Rail Crews

There are perhaps two hundred people whose entire lives are the rail line.

They live in maintenance cars — long, low, heavy-framed vehicles that sit on the narrow-gauge track and can be pushed slowly from settlement to settlement by the electric locomotives, or, in the sections where the catenary has failed (it fails often; the thermal expansion of the wire in this environment is relentless), by hand-crank mechanisms that require four people working in rotating shifts. The maintenance cars are essentially mobile workshops: iron-walled, outfitted with cutting tools, wire-splicing equipment, rail-straightening jacks, and sleeping quarters that are little more than recessed bunks in the car's thick walls, coffin-tight but warm, always warm, because the car's floor is in contact with the rail which is in contact with the Ridgeback which is in contact with its own ancient heat.

The rail crews are a distinct culture. They move between settlements but belong to none of them. They have their own dialect, salted with technical terminology that has drifted into general use — *catenary-slack*, meaning something that appears functional but is about to fail; *rail-true*, meaning honest in a way that can be verified; *gauge-drifting*, meaning slowly becoming someone you didn't intend to be. They are lean where the settlement people are stocky, weathered in a different way — not by mineral dust but by wind. The wind on the exposed summit between settlements is punishing, a constant northeast pressure that carries fine grit and strips anything soft. The rail crews wrap themselves in additional layers of mineral fiber, hoods pulled forward, goggles of cut feldspar lenses set in iron frames. They look, from a distance, like moving extensions of the rock itself, hunched and dark, proceeding slowly along the bright line of the rail.

They know the spaces between settlements better than anyone. They know where the clingers congregate — a knowledge that has practical value, because clinger colonies on the rail line can foul the gauge, their excreted silicate powder packing into the rail joints and causing subtle misalignments that compound over distance. They have developed a relationship with the clingers that is not exactly antagonism and not exactly coexistence. They scrape the rail joints regularly. The clingers return. They scrape again. The clingers return. This has been happening for as long as there has been a rail line, and both parties seem to accept it as a feature of the landscape, a negotiation without resolution, like the wind.

The rail crews also report the most sightings of the aerial creatures. Out between settlements, on the exposed summit, the thermals are strongest and the drifting things ride them in slow spirals, their ribbed bodies catching the updraft, translucent in strong light so that you can see the dark shapes of their internal structures — the gas bladders, the long central cavity that may be a gut or may be something else entirely. The rail crews watch them the way people anywhere watch birds: casually, habitually, with occasional bursts of genuine attention when one does something unexpected. *One came down to the rail yesterday,* a crew member might say. *Landed on a pylon. Sat there for an hour. Never seen that before.* This is reported without alarm, without wonder, just as data, another observation to be filed in the long informal record of what happens on the Ridgeback, a record that no one writes down because no one needs to. The Ridgeback changes slowly enough that memory suffices.

## The Mineral Processors

Below the thermal engineers in the settlement's social structure — or rather, adjacent to them, occupying a parallel track of expertise — are the people who work the rock itself. Quarriers, cutters, smelters, refiners. They are the most numerous population in any settlement and the least likely to talk to an outsider, not out of hostility but out of a genuine uncertainty about what there is to say. They cut rock. They process it. The rock is the same as it was yesterday and will be the same tomorrow. What else is there?

But watch them work and you see something that belies this modesty. A quarrier reading a mineral face — running hands across the grain of a pyroxene exposure, feeling for the cleavage planes, the stress lines, the places where the crystal structure will yield cleanly and the places where it will fracture unpredictably — is performing an act of interpretation as complex as anything the thermal engineers do in their shafts. The rock is not uniform. It varies in composition, grain, thermal history, stress loading. A block cut from one section of the Ridgeback will behave differently from a block cut ten meters away. The quarriers know this. They have a vocabulary for mineral character that distinguishes dozens of subtle gradations: *green-willing* for olivine that cleaves predictably; *iron-stubborn* for nickel-rich ultramafic that resists the chisel; *heat-drunk* for stone that has absorbed so much thermal energy from below that it behaves almost plastically under pressure, deforming rather than fracturing. This last quality is prized for certain applications — load-bearing walls, turbine housings — because it suggests a stone that will absorb stress rather than transmit it.

The cutters work in galleries off the main settlement fissures, in chambers that they excavate as they go, following good stone the way a miner elsewhere might follow a vein. The cutting is done with tools that are themselves made from the Ridgeback: iron chisels, nickel-alloy saw blades, abrasive wheels coated in crushed pyroxene. There is a closed-loop quality to the whole enterprise — the Ridgeback providing the material to shape itself, the tools wearing down and being reforged from the same rock they were used to cut. The processors are aware of this circularity. It surfaces in their humor, which is dry and geological. *I'm wearing down,* someone might say at the end of a long shift. *Reforge me tomorrow.*

The smelters work deeper, closer to the thermal engineers' domain, in chambers where the rock's natural heat supplements their furnaces. Smelting on the Ridgeback is not about generating heat so much as directing it — channeling the geothermal output through insulated galleries to concentrate it where it's needed. The smelters are the most physically marked of the settlement's workers. Their skin is reddened and dry from constant heat exposure, and many of the older ones have a network of fine scars on their forearms from contact with hot metal in close quarters. They drink constantly — not water, which is too scarce to waste on simple hydration, but a thin mineral broth extracted from the atmospheric processing plants, warm and slightly sulfurous, served in heavy cups made from turned olivine. It replaces salts lost to sweating. It tastes, by all accounts, like what it is: liquid rock. You get used to it.

## The Atmospheric Chemists

Water is the scarcest resource on the Ridgeback, and the people who manage its extraction are accordingly the most protected, the most carefully trained, and the most quietly powerful figures in any settlement.

The atmospheric extraction plants sit on the highest points of the settlement's section of ridge, where the thin air moves fastest and the greatest volume can be processed. They are not large — squat iron structures, heavily insulated, studded with intake vents — but they run continuously, pulling in the arid atmosphere and subjecting it to a series of condensation and chemical separation processes that extract whatever trace moisture and useful volatiles it contains. The yield is pathetically small by any external standard. A large plant might produce a hundred liters of water per day. This supplies a settlement of five thousand people.

The chemists manage this supply with an attention to detail that approaches the sacred. Every liter is tracked. Allocation is determined by a formula that accounts for body mass, work intensity, ambient temperature, and a factor they call *thermal debt*, which represents the additional metabolic cost of living in an environment where your body is constantly absorbing heat from its surroundings and must work to shed it. The formula is not written down — it is carried in the heads of the senior chemists, adjusted seasonally, and transmitted through apprenticeships that are even longer than those of the thermal engineers.

They are, physically, the most careful people on the Ridgeback. They move with a deliberateness that goes beyond the general slowness of ridge life. Their gestures are economical. They do not sweat unnecessarily. They sit in the shadowed interiors of their plants, monitoring gauges and valves, and they sip from their own allocation with a mindfulness that makes the act of drinking look like a form of meditation. Their skin is, paradoxically, the most hydrated on the Ridgeback — occupational exposure to the condensation processes gives them access to damp air that no one else ever breathes, and their complexions are smoother, slightly flushed, noticeably different from the mineral-dusted roughness of everyone else. This is not resented. It is accepted as a condition of the work, the way a smelter's scars are accepted, the way an engineer's olive-stained palms are accepted. The Ridgeback marks everyone according to their function.

The chemists are also the ones who track "the ghost." When the rare haze event occurs — that thin, never-precipitating mist that clings to the mid-elevations — the atmospheric plants go into overdrive, every intake vent opened wide, every condenser running at maximum capacity, trying to wring a few extra liters from the briefly moistened air. These events are called *harvest days*, and they carry an emotional weight disproportionate to their practical yield. People come out of the settlement slots and stand on the upper platforms, looking down the flanks of the Ridgeback at the faint haze below, and there is something in their faces — not joy exactly, not longing, but a kind of recognition. *There it is. The ghost. It came back.* The children, who have perhaps never seen it before, are brought up to look. The chemists monitor their instruments and try not to be emotional about an extra fifteen liters. They are not always successful.

## The Children

There are children here. Of course there are. This is not a mining camp or a temporary installation; people have been living on the Ridgeback for generations — how many is a matter of some debate, the record-keeping having gone through several interruptions, but at least eight, possibly twelve, enough that the current inhabitants have no living memory of anywhere else. They were born here. Their grandparents were born here. The Ridgeback is not a hardship posting to them. It is the world.

The children are not numerous. Population is carefully managed — another domain of the atmospheric chemists, who understand better than anyone the carrying capacity of the water supply. Families are small, one or two children, rarely three. The children are raised collectively within the settlement, moving between households in a pattern that seems chaotic to outsiders but follows a logic based on apprenticeship exposure: a child spends time with quarriers, with engineers, with rail crew families, with chemists, absorbing the rhythms and vocabularies of each discipline before gravitating — or being guided — toward the one that suits them.

They are, as a group, remarkably patient. A child on the Ridgeback can sit motionless for an hour watching a clinger traverse a rock face, tracking its progress with an attention that in another context might be mistaken for boredom but here reads as engagement — a deep, slow attention calibrated to the pace of the thing being observed. They play games, but the games are also slow: strategic, positional, involving the placement of cut mineral tokens on gridded boards in patterns that evolve over days or weeks, a game being returned to each evening after work the way someone might return to a book. Running, shouting, the kinetic chaos of children elsewhere — it's not absent, exactly, but it's rare, and it tends to happen in the deeper galleries where the acoustics don't carry and the adults' quiet is not disturbed. There is no prohibition against noise. There doesn't need to be. The children have internalized the Ridgeback's pace. They breathe with it.

They are the ones who name things. The adults are pragmatic — Shaft Six, Rail Junction Twelve, the upper gallery. But the children give names to individual clinger colonies (*the slow parliament*), to particular mineral towers (*the tooth, the grandmother, the one that hums*), to bore-holes (*the asking place*). These names are tolerated, sometimes adopted, and eventually they enter the common vocabulary and are spoken by adults who have forgotten that a child coined them, or who remember and don't mention it, which amounts to the same kind of quiet respect that permeates everything here.

## The Watchers

Not an official role. Not a profession. But in every settlement there are a few people — usually older, usually retired from active work — who spend their time on the upper platforms and high galleries, looking out across the Ridgeback.

They watch the clingers. They watch the aerial creatures. They watch the wind patterns in the grit. They note the thermal shimmer variations above different sections of exposed rock, which can indicate subsurface changes months before any instrument registers them. They watch the bore-holes — not by descending into them, which no one does, but by sitting near the openings and listening. They are the ones who hear "the question" most often, and their accounts of it, delivered slowly over cups of mineral broth in the settlement's common gallery, form a body of informal testimony that is the closest thing the Ridgeback has to a spiritual tradition.

*It was lower today. Longer. Like it was coming from farther down.*

*I heard two. Not at the same time — one, and then another, from a different hole. Like a conversation.*

*It stopped for a month. I thought it was done. Then it came back and it was different. I can't say how. Just different.*

They do not speculate about what makes the sound. That restraint is notable, because in every other domain the Ridgeback's people are empiricists — they test, they measure, they draw conclusions. But about the question, they simply report. They describe what they heard. They compare notes. They do not theorize. This is not incuriosity. You can see it in their eyes when they sit by the bore-holes, leaning slightly forward, heads tilted — they want to know. They want to know badly. But they have, collectively and over generations, decided that wanting to know and claiming to know are different things, and that the Ridgeback has not yet given them enough information to cross that threshold. So they wait. They listen. They report.

Some of them have been listening for decades.

One woman — very old now, her hands so stained with olivine that they are essentially green, a former quarrier who retired to watching thirty years ago — keeps a record. Not of the question itself, which she agrees cannot be meaningfully transcribed, but of the silences between instances. She marks the date and duration of each silence on a long strip of mineral-fiber cloth, a tick mark for each day of quiet, and the cloth now stretches the length of her gallery chamber, pinned to the wall, a dense text of waiting. She believes — though she would not use the word *believe*; she would say *it seems possible* — that the intervals are not random. That there is a pattern. That the pattern is changing.

She does not say what she thinks the pattern means. When asked, she turns her green hands over and looks at them, and then she looks at you, and then she looks out at the Ridgeback — the dark mineral towers, the bare rock, the clingers pressed flat against the warm stone, the faint line of the rail disappearing into the haze of distance — and she says something that is either profound or practical or both:

*It means we should keep listening.*

## The Burrower-Adjacent

There is a small, informal community — fewer than a hundred people spread across all the settlements — who are preoccupied with the bore-holes.

They are not watchers. The watchers sit and listen. These people measure. They map. They lower instruments on long cables into the smooth-sided tunnels: temperature probes, acoustic sensors, tiny cameras that return images of polished rock walls descending into darkness. They catalog every bore-hole on the Ridgeback — there are, at last count, over four thousand — and they track their growth rates, their diameters, their angles of descent, the mineral composition of the displaced material that is sometimes found in small piles near the openings, smooth-sided pellets of compressed rock that look disturbingly like excrement.

They have never seen a burrower. No one has. The cameras, lowered to their maximum cable length of several hundred meters, show only more tunnel — round, smooth, descending. The acoustic sensors pick up the question, much louder down there, and also other sounds: scraping, shifting, a rhythmic pulsation that could be mechanical or could be biological or could be geological. The temperature probes show a gradient that is steeper than expected, the bore-holes warming faster with depth than the surrounding rock, as if something down there is generating heat beyond what the Ridgeback itself provides.

These people are the closest thing the Ridgeback has to natural philosophers, and they carry the attendant burden of that role: they know more than anyone else about the bore-holes and the burrowers, and what they know amounts to a detailed, meticulous confirmation that they do not understand what they are studying. Their maps are precise. Their data is clean. Their conclusions are absent. They present their findings at the inter-settlement gatherings that occur when the rail schedule permits, and the presentations are long, methodical, and end with the same admission, phrased differently each time but always meaning the same thing: *we don't know what they are. We don't know what they're doing. We don't know if they're aware of us. We don't know if the question is theirs.*

One of them, a young man in the southern settlements, has proposed lowering a person into a bore-hole on a cable, the way the instruments are lowered. The proposal has not been rejected. It has simply not been acted on. It sits in the collective awareness of the bore-hole community the way the question sits in the awareness of the watchers: acknowledged, unresolved, patient. He maintains the proposal. He checks his cable rigging weekly. He waits.

The Ridgeback teaches you to wait.

## The Ones Who Leave

A handful, every generation.

They descend the flanks of the Ridgeback on foot — there is a maintained path, switchbacked, carved into the dark rock, descending eleven kilometers of elevation over a lateral distance of about forty kilometers. It takes three days. They carry mineral-fiber packs loaded with water rations (carefully calculated, carefully allocated, a going-away gift from the chemists that represents a real sacrifice from the supply), processed food, and whatever personal effects they choose. Some carry cut-olivine tokens from the strategy games of their childhood. Some carry small pieces of the Ridgeback itself, smoothed and pocketed, talismans of heat that will cool slowly and finally in the lowland air and become just stone.

They go to the lowlands. What they find there is not well documented — the Ridgeback's people are not great travelers, and those who leave rarely return. The salt-crusted basalt flats are vast, featureless, and by all accounts profoundly disorienting after a life spent in the vertical geometry of the fissure-settlements. The few who have come back report flatness, silence of a different quality — not the silence-with-a-floor of the Ridgeback but a true silence, an absence, a place where the ground does not hum and the air does not carry the turbine drone and there is no question being asked from below. They describe this silence as the most alien thing they have ever experienced. Some of them found it liberating. Most found it terrifying. The ones who came back found it unbearable.

There are rumors — carried by the rail crews, who are the primary vector for all information between settlements — of other communities out on the lowlands. Settlements built around deep wells that tap whatever subsurface moisture the planet retains. People who have never seen the Ridgeback except as a dark line on the horizon, a wall of shadow running northeast to southwest across the world. These people, if they exist, live entirely different lives — lives oriented to flatness, to true silence, to cold ground and imported energy. The Ridgeback's people think about them sometimes, these hypothetical lowlanders, and the thinking has a quality of mild incomprehension, the way someone who has always lived beside a river might think about someone who has never seen moving water. *How do they orient themselves? How do they know which way is down? How do they sleep without the hum?*

The question of why some people leave is not much examined. It is accepted the way the bore-holes are accepted, the way the question is accepted — as a feature of the environment, a thing that happens, part of the Ridgeback's ecology. People grow here, and most of them stay, and some of them go, and the settlement adjusts its water allocation and continues. The greeting — *what are you building that you won't finish?* — applies to the ones who leave as much as to the ones who stay. Everyone is building something. Some of them build it elsewhere. The Ridgeback does not take offense.

## What It Is to Be Here

Stand on the upper platform of one of the middle settlements — say the fourth from the northeast end, a community of about eight thousand people called, in a characteristic gesture of descriptive minimalism, *Shaft Cluster.* It is evening. The sun — yellower than Sol, gentler at this angle — is sinking toward the southwest end of the Ridgeback, and the mineral towers are casting their long shadows, and the crystalline structure of the rock is doing its brief evening trick, flaring deep green and amber in the low-angle light. Below you, in the settlement slots, the amber lighting is coming up, warm against the dark stone. You can hear the turbine drone, felt more than heard. You can hear wind. Somewhere to the south, a rail car is approaching, its whine just barely detectable, a thread of sound that will thicken over the next twenty minutes until the car arrives at the platform with its cargo of replacement parts, processed mineral, a few passengers, and news from the next settlement that is two weeks old and already out of date.

On the rock face below the platform, a colony of clingers is arranged in a pattern that might be random and might not — a rough spiral, the largest at the center, the smallest at the periphery, all of them motionless, all of them pressing their flat ventral surfaces against the warm stone, all of them the color of the rock so that they seem less like animals and more like the stone's own texture, a biological grain. A child is watching them from a gallery window, chin on hands, eyes tracking the imperceptible movement of the largest one. She has been there for an hour. She will be there for another hour. She is not bored. She is reading the rock the way her mother reads a thermal gradient, the way her father reads a mineral face. She is learning to see at the Ridgeback's pace.

Above, two of the aerial creatures are circling in the last thermal of the day, their ribbed bodies catching the sunset light so that for a moment they look like something delicate and improbable, stained-glass windows drifting in warm air. They circle three times, four, five, and then the thermal dies as the sun drops below the ridge and they drift downward, out of sight below the platform's edge, settling somewhere on the dark flank of the Ridgeback for the night.

Behind you, in the settlement, someone is cooking — mineral broth again, always mineral broth, but tonight with flakes of something the chemists have extracted from the atmospheric processor, a protein-adjacent compound that they've been experimenting with, not quite food but not quite not-food either, another thing to which you get used. The smell mixes with the ever-present smell of warm rock, hot iron, the faint sulfur of the deep shafts. It is not an appetizing combination by any external standard. It smells like home.

From a bore-hole somewhere below and to the east, the question sounds. A single low tone, resonant, lasting perhaps three seconds, felt in the platform under your feet as much as heard in the air. You have heard it a thousand times. You will hear it a thousand more. You do not know what it means. You do not pretend to know. You listen, the way the watchers listen, the way the old woman with the green hands listens, marking another tick on her cloth, tracking the silence between instances, waiting for a pattern to emerge from the patience of geological time.

The child at the window turns her head toward the sound. She has heard it too. She looks at you. You look at her. Neither of you says anything. There is nothing to say. The question has been asked. The Ridgeback hums. The light fades. The stone holds its warmth.

You go inside to eat.
