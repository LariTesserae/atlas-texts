# The Keeper

---

There is someone here.

You don't see them at first. The mist is too thick and the room is too full of its own rhythms—the dripping, the drone, the frog-song—and your eyes are adjusting to the diffuse grey non-light, and everything gleams and everything is wet and everything moves slightly, so your brain registers the figure in the far corner as just another accumulation: moss on iron, fern over pipe, the general lushness asserting itself in a vaguely vertical shape.

But then it moves. Not much. A shift of weight. A hand lifting to wipe condensation from a brow—an old gesture, almost futile here, habitual in the way that only truly pointless habits can become. And you realize that what you took for a particularly dense curtain of fern fronds is actually a person sitting on an upturned valve wheel, legs folded beneath them, their back against the wall where two of the largest pipes converge into a single thick trunk that disappears through the floor.

---

They are not young. But saying this feels imprecise, like calling the mountain "tall." They are old in a way that has become a quality rather than a measurement—the way patina is a quality of the iron, not a flaw in it. Their age has settled. It is no longer progressing so much as *deepening*, the way a stain deepens, the way the mineral veins in the basalt outside have deepened through epochs of slow deposition. Their face is dark and creased, not lined so much as channeled, and in the wet light you notice—with a small start that you immediately distrust—that the creases in their skin follow the same hexagonal logic as the condensation channels in the walls. This is probably a trick of perception. The geometry is everywhere here; you are primed to see it. Probably.

Their hair is white and heavy with moisture, pulled back from their face and gathered in a way that involves no visible tie or pin—it simply *holds*, the way wet cloth holds its shape, a thick coil at the nape of the neck from which individual strands have escaped and cling to the skin of their shoulders in dark calligraphic curves. Their shoulders are bare. They are wearing something, but the mist makes it difficult to determine where fabric ends and moss begins. There is cloth—a long, rust-colored garment, sleeveless, that falls to the tops of their feet—but it has been patched so many times with so many different materials that it has become a composite, a textile ecosystem. Some of the patches are clearly woven. Others appear to be dried watercress matted into a thick, fibrous felt. One section along the left side seems to be stitched with something that gleams like beetle-shell, and when the keeper shifts position, it throws off tiny sparks of iridescent black-green light that die immediately in the mist.

Their feet are bare on the iron grating. This is the first thing that tells you they have been here a very long time: their soles are dark and thick and smooth, not calloused exactly, but *adapted*, the skin having reached an accommodation with the metal lattice beneath it. The pattern of the grating is not imprinted on their feet. It is more that their feet have learned the grating's geometry the way a river learns its bed—by flowing over it so many thousands of times that the two shapes have become complementary. They stand on the grate the way a key sits in a lock. There is no wasted contact.

---

The keeper does not speak to you when you arrive. This is not hostility or even indifference—it is something more like tact, or perhaps a profound understanding of what it is like to enter this space for the first time. The cistern is overwhelming. The sound, the moisture, the lushness, the vertiginous knowledge that you are impossibly high on an impossibly old mountain inside a machine that is also a garden that is also a ruin—it takes time. They are giving you the time. They have a lot of it.

They are doing something with their hands. You watch, because there is nothing else to do while you acclimate, and because their hands are interesting. They are large hands, long-fingered, and they are moving with the slow deliberation of someone performing a task they have performed so many times that the motions have been refined to their absolute minimum. No wasted movement. No flourish. Each gesture is the ghost of what was once a learned skill and is now simply an extension of the body's knowledge.

What they are doing is this: they are cleaning a section of condensation channel.

A small iron tool—a pick, or perhaps a stylus, hand-forged, its handle wrapped in something organic and dark—is being drawn along one of the hexagonal grooves in the wall beside them. The groove is partially occluded by mineral scale, the same calcium-white deposit that veins the basalt outside. The keeper is scraping it clear, gently, in small precise strokes that send tiny curls of mineral falling into the moss below. Behind the pick, the groove gleams wetly, and you can see condensation immediately begin to gather in the cleared channel, beading, merging, running downward toward the vent. The keeper watches this happen with an attention that is not quite satisfaction and not quite vigilance—it is closer to the way you might watch a sleeping child breathe. The confirmation of a process. The acknowledgment that the system continues.

When a curl of mineral scale falls onto a patch of moss, a beetle trundles over and investigates it, antennae working. The keeper watches this too. Their eyes—dark, deeply set, bright with a wetness that might be the mist or might be their own—follow the beetle with the same quality of attention they gave the water in the channel. Everything here receives the same regard. The water. The beetle. The mineral scale. You.

---

They have tools. Not many. Arranged along the base of the wall behind them, tucked into the space where pipe meets iron panel, there is a small collection: the pick. A brush made from what appears to be fern fiber, stiff and green, bound with wire. A flat stone, smoothed to a perfect oval, that serves some purpose you cannot immediately determine. A cup—iron, of course, with the same patina as the walls, but clearly shaped by a hand, its rim worn thin by lips over years and years and years. A coil of the ivy, dried and braided into cordage. A piece of cloth that might be for wrapping, or wiping, or bandaging, or all three.

That is all. That is the complete inventory of the keeper's possessions, or at least the ones that are visible. There might be more below the grating, in the spaces between the reservoir's surface and the lattice floor, tucked into the mechanical interstices of the pipes. But you suspect not. You suspect that this is everything, and that it has been everything for a long time, and that it is exactly enough.

There is no bedding. No food store, unless you count the watercress growing in the reservoir below, which you should—the keeper reaches through the grating at intervals and pulls up a handful, shakes the water from it, and eats it slowly, root and leaf and pale flower, chewing with the unhurried thoroughness of someone for whom this is the meal and the only meal and has been the only meal. The watercress, the beetles—you see the keeper eat one of these too, later, plucking it gently from the moss with two fingers, examining it briefly, then placing it in their mouth with a formality that is not ritual exactly but is not *not* ritual either. A small, consistent acknowledgment. The cistern feeds them. They maintain the cistern. The circuit is closed.

---

They sleep sitting up, leaning against the convergence of pipes in their corner. You know this because at some point—time is difficult here; the light never changes; the clouds pour through without pause; the flowers open and close, open and close, but they are not a clock you know how to read—the keeper's hands stop moving, and their head tilts forward slightly, and their breathing changes. It does not slow so much as it *aligns*. The rhythm of their breath synchronizes with the pipe-drone, rises to match the deepest frequency of the machinery behind the walls, and for a moment you feel that the keeper is not sleeping against the pipes but sleeping *with* them, breathing in concert with the vast hidden waterwork below, the way the flowers pulse in their own blind rhythm, the way the dark reservoir creature glides in its own slow circuit. The keeper asleep is the keeper at their most integrated. Awake, they are a person in a room. Asleep, they are a component. The boundary dissolves.

They wake without any visible transition. Eyes closed, then eyes open. Hands still, then hands moving. The pick finds the channel. The work continues.

---

You want to know how long they have been here. Of course you do. The question is fundamental and also, you suspect, unanswerable—or answerable only in terms that are not useful. Long enough for their clothes to become partly moss. Long enough for the grating to know their feet. Long enough for the frogs to ignore them completely, which means they have passed through the entire arc of being a novelty, a threat, a presence, a fixture, and finally a feature—something the frogs were born into, the way they were born into the iron walls and the condensation and the dripping. The keeper is infrastructure now.

But they were not always. Someone climbed this mountain. Someone found the cistern—or was sent to it, or built it, or was the last in a line of people who maintained it, the line having thinned over centuries to a final singular thread. Someone arrived here with more tools than they have now, probably, with supplies, with a purpose that could be articulated in language, with a name. The keeper has a name. They have simply had no one to say it to for so long that it has become a private possession, worn smooth like the stone in their tool collection, carried for reasons that no longer require explanation.

You could ask. They would answer—you sense this. They are not mute, not vowed to silence, not guarding some sacred mystery. They are simply *quiet*, the way the mountain is quiet, the way the reservoir is quiet beneath its mat of watercress. Silence is the default here. Sound is made by the water and the frogs and the machinery, and it is complex and sufficient, and adding human speech to it would not enhance it. The keeper knows this the way a musician knows when not to play.

But if you asked—if you sat on the iron grating across from them and let the mist settle on you and waited until the question felt like a natural part of the soundscape rather than an interruption—they would speak. Their voice would be low and unhurried and rough in the way that fern bark is rough, not from damage but from texture. They would tell you practical things. How the eastern channel tends to scale faster than the western ones because of some asymmetry in the wall's temperature. How the dark creature in the reservoir—they would not call it by a name; they would describe its motion with a gesture, a fluid downward sweep of the hand—breeds in the deep pipe junction two meters below the floor, and in certain seasons the juveniles appear as flickers of dark silver in the grating gaps. How the ivy, if left unchecked, will eventually work a bolt loose, and the bolt must be re-seated with the flat stone, and this is the single most critical maintenance task, because a loose bolt lets water into the wall cavity where it interferes with the machinery, and the machinery must not be interfered with, because the machinery is what keeps the walls warm, and the warmth is what drives the condensation cycle, and the condensation cycle is everything. Everything.

They would explain this with the patience of someone who understands a system completely and has never had the opportunity to describe it. Not with pride. Not with weariness. With the simple, directional energy of water running downhill—the explanation wants to happen, and you are the channel it has found.

---

They would not explain what the water is for. Not because they are withholding it. Because they do not know.

This is the thing about the keeper that is most difficult to hold in your mind: they maintain the cistern with total competence and total ignorance of its purpose. The water goes down. They know this. They can feel it through the pipes—the draw, the suction, the varying demand that causes the reservoir level to fluctuate. Something below drinks the mountain's gathered clouds, something vast and old and mechanically complex, and the keeper feeds it. They keep the channels clear. They re-seat the bolts. They eat the watercress and the beetles and they sleep against the pipes and they wake and they work, and the water goes down, and they do not know where it goes or what it does when it gets there.

They used to. Or—no. *Someone* used to. The knowledge existed; it was passed down; it thinned. The keeper has a sense of it the way you might have a sense of a grandparent's grandparent—a shape without detail, a silhouette. The water goes down to something important. The water must not stop. These two facts are the bedrock of the keeper's life, as solid and as worn as the basalt of the mountain itself, and everything else has been eroded away by the slow centuries of cloud and silence and iron and fern.

You might find this tragic. The keeper does not. The keeper finds it—and here you are interpreting, reading meaning into the set of a jaw, the way a hand rests on a pipe, the cadence of a breath, because the keeper has not said this and probably would not—the keeper finds it *sufficient*. The system works. The garden grows. The frogs call. The machinery hums. The water goes down. Whatever it feeds continues to need feeding, which means it continues to function, which means the keeper's work has meaning even if the meaning is opaque. Purpose does not require understanding. A valve does not need to know the shape of the reservoir it fills.

---

And yet.

Sometimes—not often; you would have to be here a long time to see it, and you would have to be watching, and the keeper would have to forget you are watching—sometimes the keeper stops. The pick goes still. The hands rest on the knees. The eyes, which are usually focused on the near and the practical—the channel, the bolt, the beetle, the fern—go distant. They look down through the grating, past the watercress, past the reservoir, past the visible, into the dark gap where the pipes converge and plunge into the mountain's interior. They listen.

The sound from below—the rushing, the waterfall-through-a-wall, the great hidden turbulence of whatever system lives inside the mountain—is always there, but in these moments the keeper attends to it with an intensity that changes its character. It is no longer background. It is a voice. Not speaking words, but *speaking*, the way a forest speaks in wind, the way a body speaks in pulse, and the keeper listens the way you listen to a language you almost know, catching shapes in the sound that are almost meaning, almost pattern, almost a message from the vast throat of the mountain about what it is, about what it needs, about what it is becoming down there in the dark where the water goes and does not come back.

The keeper's lips move, in these moments. Not speaking. Not quite. Mouthing something—a word, a name, a count, a prayer, or just the muscular memory of a habit so old it predates the keeper's own understanding of why they do it. The lips move, and no sound comes out, or if it does it is swallowed instantly by the pipe-drone and the dripping and the frogs, and the keeper's eyes remain focused on the dark below, and the moment stretches, and the flowers open, and the flowers close, and then the pick moves again, and the keeper returns to the channel, and the mineral scale curls away under the edge of the tool, and the water gathers and runs, and everything continues.

---

There is one more thing.

On the wall behind the keeper's customary seat—the section of iron panel between the two converging pipes, a space perhaps thirty centimeters square—the moss has been kept clear. Not removed; *maintained*. The keeper has deliberately, carefully, patiently prevented the moss from covering this one small area of iron, scraping it back whenever it encroaches, which must be often, which means this small act of clearing has been performed thousands of times, tens of thousands, an act of curation as persistent and gentle as the moss's own advance.

The cleared iron beneath is covered in marks.

Not writing. Not exactly. Scratched into the patina with the tip of the pick, a dense, layered palimpsest of marks that have been made over a very long period—the oldest ones barely visible, nearly eaten by corrosion, the newest sharp and bright. Some are tallies. Some are shapes that might be diagrams—pipe configurations, maybe, or the hexagonal channel patterns, or something else entirely, some geometry that maps a system you cannot see. Some are neither tally nor diagram but something more tentative, more searching—marks that start as one thing and become another, lines that wander, spirals that tighten and then dissolve into scratched clouds of indecision.

And in the center, scratched deeper than anything else, retraced so many times that it has become a groove in the iron:

A single symbol that you do not recognize.

It might be a letter in a script you've never seen. It might be a schematic for a valve configuration. It might be the keeper's name. It might be a word in a language that has no other surviving speakers, a language that existed only on this mountain and was only ever spoken by the people who built the cistern and who are now gone, all of them, except for this one, who may or may not remember what the symbol means but who retraces it with the pick every time they clear the moss, a single deep groove in a small square of iron on a wall in a room at the top of the world, maintained against the slow green tide of everything that wants to cover it, everything that wants to make it just another surface for moss and beetle and fern, everything that wants to fold it back into the living system that the cistern has become.

The keeper retraces it.

The keeper sits back.

The keeper returns to work.

The water goes down.

---

The keeper is here. They have been here. They will, in all likelihood, continue to be here after you leave, after you descend back through the cloud layer into the world below where there are shadows and horizons and dry air and purposes that can be spoken aloud. They will sit in their corner and clean the channels and eat the watercress and listen to the frogs and sleep against the pipes and wake and work and watch the beetles graze their hexagonal paths and pull the moss back from that small square of iron and retrace the symbol and look down into the dark where the water goes and move their lips without sound and not know what any of it is for and do it anyway, perfectly, endlessly, the last moving part in a machine that has outlived its own explanation, a gardener in a garden that planted itself, a keeper of a thing that no longer has a name.

The mist beads on their skin. The frogs call. The flowers pulse. The water goes down.

They are enough.
