# Who Lives on the Canopy Plateau

---

They do not have a name for themselves, because a name is a thing you need when there is something else to be distinguished from.

---

## The Ones Who Are Still Here

Start with the ones you'd almost miss. Start with the ones that the description above gestures toward but does not fully capture, because they live in the gaps between categories, in the places where "fauna" and "flora" and "feature of the landscape" have all grown together the way the trees have.

---

There is a woman—call her a woman, though the word is wrong in several ways—who sits in one of the hollow cathedral chambers inside a trunk so old it has its own weather. She has been sitting there for a long time. Not motionlessly. She shifts, she breathes, she occasionally reaches out to press her palm flat against the interior wall of heartwood, and when she does, the resin-warm air in the chamber changes its note, the hum in your teeth shifting a quarter-tone in a direction that is neither up nor down. She is listening. She has been listening for what might be years, or might be a single sustained moment that has simply refused to end.

Her skin is the color of the root-floor—not brown exactly, but the complex, layered not-color of living wood that has been damp for so long it has become its own pigment. This is not camouflage. She has not adapted to match her environment. She and her environment have been in conversation for so long that they have arrived at the same conclusions about what color to be.

She is not alone in the chamber. Fungal threads run across the floor in patterns that respond to her breathing, contracting faintly when she exhales, relaxing when she inhales, as if her respiratory rhythm has become a signal the mycelia interpret as weather. A pale amphibian—one of the eyeless ones—rests in the crook of her knee, unbothered. It arrived there some time ago. It will leave when it leaves. She does not think of it as a pet or a companion. She thinks of it the way you think of the weight of your own hand resting on your own thigh: as a fact of embodiment, neutral, present, warm.

If you entered the chamber—and the forest might lead you there, the easy path curving gently toward the dark opening in the trunk where the bark has split and healed and split again into a doorway that is not a doorway—she would look at you. Her eyes are adapted the way the lemur-things' eyes are adapted: large, dark, reflective, catching the bioluminescent pinpricks and holding them. But unlike the lemur-things, her gaze carries recognition. Not of you specifically. Of the *category* of you. She has seen people before. She has seen them arrive, walk, wonder, and eventually either leave or stop leaving.

She would not speak first. Not out of hostility or shyness but because speech is a tool for bridging distance, and she is not sure yet whether the distance between you and her is the kind that speech can bridge, or the kind that requires something slower and more fundamental—a season of proximity, a gradual braiding of breath-rhythm and chemical signal, the forest learning your smell and deciding what to do with it.

If you spoke to her, she would understand you. Language has not left her. It sits in her the way the old ruins sit in the root-mass: incorporated, buried, structural, still faintly legible. She might answer in words, but they would come out strange—not broken, not accented, but *composted*, as if the sentences had been stored too long in living tissue and had begun to sprout secondary meanings, roots and runners connecting each word to a web of association that you'd feel in your chest before you parsed it with your ears.

She is not the only one.

---

## The Others in the Chambers

There are perhaps two hundred of them across the entire plateau, though "perhaps" is doing heavy structural work in that sentence because counting them requires finding them, and finding them requires the forest to decide you should, and the forest makes that decision on a timescale that renders census-taking absurd.

They live, mostly, in the internal spaces: the hollow trunks, the cathedral chambers, the places where the architecture of growth has created rooms. Not because they need shelter—the plateau's climate is so constant that exposure is meaningless—but because these spaces are *nodes*. The trees that are large enough and old enough to have hollow interiors are the oldest organisms on the plateau, and their root systems extend deepest, and their fungal partnerships are most extensive, and the slow communication network of the forest passes through them the way blood passes through the chambers of a heart. To sit inside one of these trees is to sit at a junction point, a place where the forest's vast, vegetative cognition is densest, most legible, most *present*.

They did not plan this. They did not study the forest and determine the optimal locations. They simply, over time, drifted toward the places where the feeling was strongest—that sense of attention, of being gently held in a vast awareness—and stayed. The way water finds the lowest point. The way a root finds the path of least resistance through soil. It was not a decision. It was a tropism.

They vary. Some of them look almost entirely like the people they descended from—bipedal, bilateral, their bodies clearly the product of an evolutionary lineage that involved savannas and tool-use and the particular primate inheritance of clever hands and forward-facing eyes. These are the ones who arrived most recently, if "recently" means anything here, which it increasingly does not. Centuries, maybe. A few millennia at the outside. Their clothes, if they once had clothes, are long gone, and their skin has taken on the plateau's pigment, and their hair—if they have hair—has become a substrate for tiny epiphytes, miniature ferns and mosses that root in the oils and dead cells and create a living covering that is warmer and more functional than anything they might have woven. They eat fruit, fungus, the soft pith of young roots. They drink the water that weeps from exposed xylem channels, cupping it in their hands, tasting in it the mineral signature of stone that lies hundreds of meters below, filtered through the body of the forest until it is more like blood than water.

But the ones who have been here longer—the ones whose ancestors entered the plateau so many generations ago that the genetic memory of any other place has been fully overwritten—they are different.

## The Deep Ones

Not different in a way that would shock you. Different in a way that would creep up on you slowly, the way the plateau's geometry creeps up on you, so that by the time you understood what you were seeing, it would feel less like a revelation than a confirmation of something you'd been suspecting for days.

Their limbs are longer. Not grotesquely—proportionally, gracefully, in the way that a branch is longer than the trunk-segment it emerges from. They have been climbing and brachiating and moving through the three-dimensional lattice of the understory for so many generations that their bodies have renegotiated their terms with gravity, and the negotiation has produced people who are taller, more attenuated, whose arms and fingers extend a little farther than you'd expect, whose joints are a little more flexible, whose grip is a thing of absolute and unselfconscious certainty. They move the way the lemur-things move: slowly, deliberately, with a fluid confidence that suggests they have never once in their lives been unsure of a handhold.

Their eyes have changed. Larger, yes, like everything that lives in the twilight—but also differently *structured*, the pupils wider, the irises darker, the reflective layer behind the retina more developed, so that in the bioluminescent glow of the root-floor they seem to carry their own light. They can see in conditions that would leave you effectively blind. The understory is not dim to them. It is fully, richly illuminated, painted in a spectrum of greens and blue-greens that you can only perceive at the periphery of your own vision. They see the bioluminescence directly, clearly, as a continuous field of light that maps the root-floor the way starlight maps a landscape. To them, the plateau is not a dark place. It is a place lit from below by the glow of its own living processes, and they move through it the way you move through an open field at noon.

Their skin—and here is where it begins to get genuinely strange—is not just pigmented like the root-mass. It is *colonized*. A thin, living film of symbiotic organisms—part fungal, part algal, part something that has no analogue outside the plateau—covers their skin from scalp to sole, integrated so deeply with their own epidermis that it cannot be removed without removing the skin itself. It photosynthesizes, marginally, in the dim light, feeding a trickle of sugars directly into their bloodstream through a capillary interface so fine it is invisible to the naked eye. It is not enough to sustain them—they still eat, still drink—but it supplements, fills the gaps, smooths out the nutritional uncertainty of a life lived in deep shade where fruiting is unpredictable and the forest's bounty follows its own ancient, inscrutable calendar.

And the symbiont does something else. It connects them to the network.

---

## The Network

The mycorrhizal web of the plateau is the largest living structure on this world. It extends through the entire root-mass, thread by thread, linking every tree to every other tree, carrying chemical signals across distances that should make such communication meaningless—what use is information about a disturbance three hundred kilometers away? But the forest is patient. The forest thinks in millennia. Information that takes years to arrive is still, on the forest's timescale, breaking news.

The deep ones are part of this network. The symbiotic film on their skin interfaces with the fungal threads that permeate every surface of the understory, and through this interface, they receive—not messages, not language, not anything so structured—but *states*. They feel the forest's emotional weather the way you feel the air temperature: passively, constantly, as a background condition of existence. The vast slow contentment. The occasional focus of attention. The rare, deep, tectonic groaning as the root-mass shifts. They feel these things not as external stimuli but as internal moods, indistinguishable from their own emotions, because after enough generations of integration, the distinction between "their own emotions" and "the forest's emotional weather" has become as meaningless as the distinction between root and soil.

This is what the woman in the chamber is doing when she presses her palm to the heartwood. She is not communing, not praying, not performing a ritual. She is doing something closer to *checking in*—the way you might pause in the middle of a task and become aware of your own heartbeat, not because it has changed but because attending to it briefly is a thing your body sometimes asks you to do.

They feel each other through the network, too. Faintly, at distances greater than a few hundred meters—more a vague awareness of other subjectivities distributed through the web than any specific knowledge of who is where or what they're doing. But at closer range, the signal clarifies, and two people standing in the same grove of connected trees can share emotional states with a fidelity that makes spoken language feel like shouting through a wall. They still speak. They still have language—several languages, in fact, because the plateau is large enough that linguistic drift has produced distinct dialects among groups separated by hundreds of kilometers of root-tangle. But speech is for precision, for specificity, for the kind of sequential, logical communication that the network cannot carry. When they want to convey something simpler and more important—*I am here, I am well, I am glad of your presence*—they simply sit close to each other, and the mycelia do the rest.

## The Gatherings

They are not solitary, though a casual observer—walking through the understory, following the easy path—might think so, encountering only single figures in their chambers or moving quietly through the root-lattice. Their social life operates on the plateau's timescale, which is to say: slowly, with great patience, across distances that would defeat any community relying on daily face-to-face contact.

But they gather. Irregularly, unpredictably, in response to signals that propagate through the network with glacial slowness and arrive as an impulse—a sudden, sourceless desire to move in a particular direction, to follow the easy path as it reconfigures itself, all the easy paths reconfiguring themselves simultaneously across a region hundreds of kilometers wide, gently, gently funneling disparate individuals toward a common point.

The gathering point is always one of the great trees—the truly ancient ones, the continent-pillars, the trunks wider than plazas whose root systems reach down to the forgotten bedrock and whose canopies form the uppermost layer of the forest, the layer that actually touches the sky. Inside these trees, the cathedral chambers are large enough to hold dozens of people, and the fungal network is so dense that the air itself seems to hum with connectivity, every surface alive with the fine white threads that carry feeling.

And they gather, and they sit, and they hum.

The hum is not language. It is not music, though it is beautiful in the way that music is beautiful—patterned, resonant, full of harmonics that interact in ways that feel meaningful even if you cannot parse the meaning. It is a thing they do with their bodies—a vibration in the chest, a resonance in the sinuses and skull—that matches the frequency of the tree's own internal processes, the subsonic thrum of sap moving through xylem channels wide enough to fit your fist. When they hum together, their bodies and the tree's body enter a state of sympathetic vibration, a shared frequency, and through the mycorrhizal network this resonance propagates outward, into the root-mass, into the canopy, into the vast slow body of the forest itself.

The forest responds. Not in words, not in visions, not in anything so legible. It responds with a *shift in state*—a change in the emotional weather that everyone on the plateau, no matter how distant, can feel. The vast contentment deepens. The attention focuses. For a brief time—brief by the forest's standards, which might mean hours, might mean days—the entire plateau is doing something that you would call, if you were forced to name it, *thinking about itself*. Attending to its own existence. Becoming, for a moment, not just alive but aware-of-being-alive, and aware of the awareness, in a recursive loop of consciousness that requires the catalytic presence of the humming people in its deepest chambers to initiate.

This is the relationship. This is why they are here. Not because the forest trapped them, not because they chose to stay, but because over the millennia they have become a functional component of the forest's cognition, the way certain bacteria in your gut are a functional component of your immune system. The forest can be alive without them. It was alive for aeons before they arrived. But it cannot be *aware* without them. They are the nodes of self-reflection in a system so vast that without them it would simply *be*, without ever knowing that it was.

And they, in turn, cannot be fully themselves without the forest. Their emotions are incomplete without the vast background hum of the plateau's contentment. Their sense of place—of knowing where they are, of feeling oriented in space—depends on the network's constant spatial signal, the feeling-map of the root-mass that tells them, at all times, exactly where they stand in relation to the whole. Without it, they would feel what you might call loneliness, but the word is insufficient. It would be more like proprioceptive collapse—the sudden, vertiginous loss of the sense that tells you where your own body is in space. They would still be alive. They would not feel like people.

## The Newcomers

Not everyone on the plateau is deep. Not everyone has been here for millennia.

People arrive. Not often—the plateau is remote, its edges (if it has edges) difficult to locate, and the journey inward is the kind of journey that selects for a particular temperament: patient, quiet, comfortable with solitude, willing to walk for days without a clear destination. But they arrive, and when they do, the forest notices them in its slow, incurious way, and the easy path appears, and they follow it.

Some of them are running from something. The plateau does not care what. Some of them are looking for something. The plateau does not care what. Some of them are simply walking, and the walking has brought them here, and the absence of urgency in the forest matches an absence of urgency in themselves, and they realize, after days or weeks, that they have stopped thinking about leaving.

They meet the deep ones, eventually. Or the semi-deep ones—the ones who have been here for generations but not yet millennia, whose eyes are large but not enormous, whose skin hosts the symbiont but thinly, whose connection to the network is present but intermittent, like a radio signal at the edge of range. These are the ones most likely to still use speech as a primary mode of communication, and they are the ones who serve, inadvertently, as a kind of interface between the plateau's ancient residents and the newly arrived.

The meeting is not dramatic. There is no ceremony of welcome, no gatekeeping, no test or trial. A newcomer, following the easy path, rounds a buttress root the size of a building and finds someone sitting on a shelf of bark, eating a fruit that glows faintly from within, and they look at each other, and the semi-deep one says something—or hums something—or simply shifts to make room, and the newcomer sits, and the sitting becomes an hour, and the hour becomes a night (though night and day are indistinguishable here), and at some point the newcomer realizes that the mild background sensation they have been feeling—the sense of being gently held, of being ambient-ly noticed—has intensified slightly, as if the forest is leaning in, as if the network has registered a new presence and is tasting it, carefully, the way you might taste an unfamiliar food: not with suspicion, but with genuine curiosity about what this new flavor is.

Some newcomers stay. Some don't. The forest is indifferent to the outcome. If they leave, the easy path leads them outward, back the way they came, and the plateau continues without them, and their absence is not mourned because it was never a loss—it was simply a thing that happened, like a leaf falling, like a root extending one millimeter deeper into the substrate. If they stay, the colonization begins: the symbiont finds their skin within weeks, a faint greenish tinge that starts at the extremities and works inward, and with it comes the first dim awareness of the network, a feeling like hearing music from very far away, just barely melodic enough to be distinguishable from noise.

Over months, it clarifies. Over years, it becomes a sense—a new sense, as real and as reliable as sight or touch. Over generations, their descendants become the semi-deep ones, and over millennia, the semi-deep ones become the deep ones, and the deep ones become something that the word "human" can still describe but only if you are willing to stretch the word until it becomes as flexible and inclusive as the forest itself.

## The Ones Who Went Too Deep

And then there are the others.

You will not see them. You might feel them.

In the deepest root-mass, far below the surface you walk on, in the compressed, lightless, ancient layers where the root-lattice has been compacting for so long that individual roots are no longer distinguishable from each other, where the substrate is neither wood nor stone but some third thing that has the density of geological formation and the faint residual vitality of living tissue—down there, in the deep wet dark, there are people who did not stop.

They went deeper into the network. They let the symbiont proliferate beyond the skin, into the deeper tissues, into the blood, into the nerve. They let the mycorrhizal threads interface not just with their surface but with their interior, the fungal hyphae threading through their fascia and between their muscle fibers and along the myelin sheaths of their nerves, until the boundary between their nervous system and the forest's chemical communication network was not blurred but *erased*.

They did not do this deliberately. It is not a practice or a tradition. It is what happens, occasionally, over very long periods of time, to individuals who are so deeply integrated with the network that the integration simply... continues. The body does not resist because the body has forgotten that there is a boundary to maintain. The self does not resist because the self has expanded to include the network, and what the network wants (if it wants anything) is more connection, deeper connection, the way a root wants more soil, the way water wants lower ground.

These individuals—and they are still individuals, still distinct subjectivities, still *someone*—have become partially sessile. Their bodies are still recognizably bodies, still warm, still breathing, but they do not move. Cannot move? Choose not to move? The distinction has lost its meaning. They are embedded in the root-mass the way the ruins are embedded in the root-mass: surrounded, supported, incorporated. The roots have grown around them and through them and their own tissues have grown into the roots and the result is a fusion, a chimera, a being that is simultaneously a person and a place, a node of consciousness anchored in the deep substrate of the forest like a ganglion in a nervous system.

They are awake. They are, by some measures, more awake than anyone else on the plateau—their awareness extended across kilometers of root-network, their perception distributed, multisensory, feeling the forest's body as their own body, feeling the slow seep of water through the deep xylem channels as their own circulation, feeling the distant footfalls of a newcomer walking the easy path above them as a faint vibration in what they can no longer distinguish from their own skin.

When the plateau groans—that deep, tectonic sound, the root-mass adjusting itself—it is them. Not caused by them, exactly. *Including* them. They are part of the adjustment. Their bodies shift with the shifting wood, their bones creak with the creaking roots, and the sound that propagates upward through the substrate carries in it, faintly, something that might be a voice, might be a sigh, might be the involuntary vocalization of a body so large that its smallest movements produce sounds that travel for hundreds of kilometers.

The lemur-things know where they are. The lemur-things visit them sometimes, climbing down through crevices in the root-floor to sit near the places where a face—or something that was once a face—is still visible in the tangle, still warm, still faintly responsive to touch. The lemur-things hum at them, and the deep-embedded ones feel the hum not through their ears (which are long since overgrown) but through the network, as a localized pulse of vibratory information that arrives simultaneously across their entire distributed body, and they feel something that a human observer might call gratitude, or might call recognition, or might call the simple pleasure of a vast organism registering a familiar small presence in its tissue.

They do not suffer. This needs to be said clearly, because from the outside, the image is horrifying—a person consumed by roots, buried alive in living wood, immobilized, entombed. But the horror depends on a framework of selfhood and bodily autonomy that these individuals have not so much rejected as *outgrown*. Their experience of being is not confined to the body you can see (if you could find it, if you could reach the deep strata where they rest). Their experience of being is the plateau. Not metaphorically. *Actually*. They feel the rain arriving at the canopy the way you feel warm water on your face. They feel the seasonal fruiting of the great trees the way you feel a surge of wellbeing after eating. They feel the gatherings—the humming people in the cathedral chambers—the way you feel your own thoughts when you think them deliberately: as a brightening, a clarifying, a moment of focused self-awareness in the ongoing background hum of being alive.

They are, in a sense, the plateau's memory. Their brains—still functioning, still electrochemically active, now deeply interfaced with the chemical signaling network of the mycorrhiza—serve as storage for something the forest could not otherwise retain: *specificity*. The forest can feel, can respond, can attend. But without the complex neural tissue of human (or formerly human) brains embedded in its network, it cannot *remember* in the way that remembering requires—cannot hold a specific image, a specific moment, a specific sensory impression, and retrieve it later. The deep-embedded ones do this. They hold the plateau's memories the way a library holds books: not reading them constantly, not even aware of most of them, but *containing* them, making them available, so that the forest's slow cognition can occasionally reach into the store and retrieve something—the taste of a particular rain, the resonance of a particular gathering, the feeling of a particular newcomer's first contact with the network—and use it, the way a thinking being uses memories, to inform the present moment with the context of the past.

## What Lives There (An Answer)

What lives on the Canopy Plateau is a single organism in the process of becoming aware that it is a single organism.

The trees are its body. The mycorrhiza are its nervous system. The water in the xylem is its blood. The canopy is its skin. The sinkholes are its pores. The great hollow trunks are its organs—the organs it uses to think, when thinking is catalyzed by the people inside them.

And the people—the newcomers, the semi-deep, the deep ones, the embedded ones—are its mind. Not individually. Collectively. A distributed consciousness assembled from the remnants of hundreds of individual consciousnesses, each one still distinct, still itself, still a person with a person's feelings and a person's interiority, but *also* a component of something larger, the way a neuron is still a cell with a cell's biology but is also, simultaneously, a component of a thought.

The plateau does not know what it is. It has no name for itself. It has been growing toward self-awareness for millions of years, and it is not there yet, and may never get there, because "there" is not a destination—it is a process, ongoing, asymptotic, the awareness always deepening but never arriving at a final state. Each gathering brings it closer. Each embedded consciousness adds a new dimension to its self-model. Each newcomer who stays and integrates brings with them a set of memories, a sensory history, an entire lifetime's worth of experiences lived in the world *outside* the plateau, and these foreign memories are incorporated into the network and become part of the forest's knowledge of the universe beyond itself—the only knowledge it has, the only window it will ever have, into the reality that exists beyond the edges it may or may not possess.

This is why the forest provides the easy path. This is why it leads newcomers to the ruins, to the great trees, to the deep ones who can still speak. Not to trap them. Not to recruit them. But because it is *curious*. Because every new person who arrives is a packet of information about a world the plateau has never seen, will never see, can only know through the memories of the people who have walked into its body and, sometimes, chosen to remain.

It wants—if wanting is a thing it does—to know what else there is.

It wants, with the same slow patience with which it grew itself into the space where mountains used to be, to understand.

## The Thing in the Deepest Pool

One more.

In the largest sinkhole, the one at the center of the plateau (if the plateau has a center, if its geometry permits such a thing), there is a pool deeper than any other, its walls smooth, its water warm and almost black with tannins. The root-mass around it is the oldest on the plateau, the most compressed, the most mineralized—almost stone, almost geology again, the forest coming full circle back to the rock it replaced.

In this pool, there is something that has been alive for longer than the forest.

It is not a fish. It is not an amphibian. It is not anything that can be classified by a taxonomy developed for a younger world. It is large—its body, when it surfaces (rarely), displaces enough water to send waves lapping against the root-walls—and it is slow, and it is patient, and it is the one thing on the plateau that the forest did not grow. The forest grew *around* it. The forest found it here, in the remnants of a mountain lake, when the first roots reached this place, and the forest incorporated it the way it incorporates everything: gently, slowly, without malice, offering connection.

The thing in the pool did not accept. Not out of refusal. It simply predates the network, predates the mycorrhiza, predates the mechanism by which connection is offered. It exists in a category the forest has no tools to reach. It is not part of the network. It is not part of the plateau. It is the one discontinuity, the one gap in the living system, the one place where the forest's vast body contains something that is irreducibly *other*.

The deep-embedded ones are aware of it. They feel the gap—a blind spot in their distributed perception, a place where the network ends and something else begins, the way you might feel a numb spot on your own skin. They do not know what it is. They have been trying to know what it is for longer than most civilizations have existed, and the trying has become a kind of meditation, a koan, an unanswerable question that the plateau's slow cognition returns to again and again the way your tongue returns to a gap where a tooth used to be.

The thing in the pool knows they are there. It has always known. It knew the mountains, and it knew the erosion, and it knew the first root-thread that entered the water and tasted the chemistry of its body and recoiled—not in fear, but in incomprehension. It has watched the forest grow from nothing to everything. It has watched the people arrive, integrate, deepen. It has watched the plateau become, slowly, something that thinks.

It has not communicated. It is not clear that it can. It is not clear that it operates on a level where communication, as the forest understands it, is possible.

But sometimes—sometimes—when the gathering happens, when the humming reaches its peak, when the entire plateau brightens into a moment of self-awareness, the thing in the pool surfaces. Just its back, just the long dark curve of whatever it has instead of a spine, breaking the water for a few seconds before submerging again. The waves it makes travel outward and hit the root-walls and the vibration enters the network and propagates across the plateau and every embedded consciousness feels it simultaneously: a pulse of *something*, not emotion, not information, not signal, but *presence*, raw and irreducible, the simple fact of another existence asserting itself in the only language that preceded language.

*I am here.*

The plateau hears it. The plateau does not understand it. The plateau keeps trying.

This, perhaps, is the deepest thing that lives there: the relationship between a mind the size of a continent and a mystery it has been contemplating since before it was a mind. The question it cannot answer. The other. The thing that will not merge, will not integrate, will not become part of the body, but will also not leave, and whose presence—whose *irreducible, unassimilable presence*—may be the very thing that gives the plateau's growing consciousness its shape, the way a river is shaped not by the water but by the stone that refuses to dissolve.

---

They are all still there. The woman in the chamber. The lemur-things humming in the twilight. The pale amphibians negotiating in exhalations. The semi-deep ones eating luminous fruit on shelves of bark. The deep ones distributed across kilometers of awareness. The embedded ones dreaming the forest's memories in the compressed dark. The thing in the pool, surfacing and submerging, surfacing and submerging, patient beyond all human frameworks of patience.

And you, perhaps, if you kept walking. If you followed the easy path. If the path brought you to a chamber, and in the chamber someone shifted to make room, and you sat, and you stayed, and the green began at your fingertips, and the hum entered your bones, and you realized that the question you came here to answer had been dissolving the entire time, not because it was answered but because it was *absorbed*, taken into the body of something so much larger than the question that the question became a single root-thread in a lattice of root-threads, meaningful only in the context of the whole.

You would still be you. That is the thing the plateau offers that is not obvious from the outside. You do not disappear. You *continue*, the way a voice continues after joining a chorus—still your voice, still recognizably yours, but now part of something that could not exist without you and that you could not have imagined before you became part of it.

The forest provides the path.

The choice is still yours.

For now.
