She is old. That is the first thing you would know if you saw her, though *old* is not quite the word — *seasoned* is closer, the way wood is seasoned, the way a tool handle darkens and contours itself to the grip that has held it for thirty years. She is not frail. She is reduced. Pared down to what is necessary, the way the hollow itself is pared down: no waste, no ornament, every element load-bearing.

---

Her hands would tell you the most, if you could see them. They are the hands of someone who has spent decades working with things that bite back — wire, shears, bark that splinters, engines that pinch. The skin is thickened across the palms and fingertips, not into calluses exactly but into a kind of leather, darkened and smooth, permanently stained in the creases with a green-black residue that is part plant sap, part machine oil, and part something else, something that has no name because it only exists here, in the place where those two substances meet and mingle. Her fingernails are short, not clipped but worn, and there is a dark line under each one that will never come clean. The knuckles are swollen — not cripplingly, not yet, but enough that her grip has changed over the years, enough that she has had to modify her tools to accommodate the slow protest of her joints. The wire cutters have had their rubber grips built up with wrappings of bark-cloth. The shears have been re-handled twice. She does not complain about this because she does not think of it as deterioration. She thinks of it the way she thinks of a branch that thickens where it bears weight: the body responding to its load.

Her right index finger is missing its last joint. She lost it to the diesel engine — not to the engine running, but to the engine being *reclaimed*. A root had grown through a gap in the laminated housing and wrapped around the flywheel, and when she reached in to cut it free, the root, under tension, snapped back and drove her finger into the gear teeth. That was eleven years ago. Maybe twelve. She does not mark time in years so much as in growth cycles — the finger was lost during the season when the eastern canopy finally closed its last gap, when the light in the hollow shifted from dappled to fully diffused, and she had to move the solar cable for the third time, chasing a patch of sky that was no longer there.

She wrapped the stump in a poultice of the dark-capped mushrooms and a paste she makes from the iridescent roots near the diesel's oil-drip, and it healed clean, without infection, faster than it should have. She noted this. She notes everything, though not always in the book. The book is for knots. For the relationship between tension and growth. For the shapes that fibers take when they are threaded through living wood and left to find their own equilibrium. The body's lessons she keeps in the body.

---

She is not tall. Was perhaps average height once, but the low canopy has done something to her posture over the decades — not stooped her, exactly, but *fitted* her. She moves through the hollow without ducking because she has learned, in her muscles and spine, the precise geography of every beam and hanging vine. Her movement has the quality of water through the root-mat: unhurried, finding the path of least resistance, arriving. She does not walk so much as navigate. Sixty paces at its widest, and she knows every inch of it — knows it the way you know your own kitchen in the dark, by feel and memory and the sound of your feet on the floor.

She is barefoot. Has been for years. Shoes rotted. She tried wrapping her feet in bark-cloth for a while, but the root-mat is not unkind to bare skin — it is warm, yielding, and the moisture keeps it from being abrasive. Her soles have thickened in response, and she sometimes thinks she can feel things through them that she shouldn't be able to feel: the slow pulse of water moving through the root system, the faint vibration when the largest loom is under tension, the almost subliminal tremor when a new shoot pushes up through the mat somewhere in the hollow. She does not claim this as mysticism. She thinks of it as calibration. You live in a place long enough, your senses adjust to its frequencies.

Her clothes — what remains of them — are the most visible evidence of the hollow's central project, the merging of the made and the grown. She wears a vest, or something that was once a vest, woven on the largest loom from a blend of bark-fiber and salvaged synthetic thread. It has been repaired so many times that the original weave is visible only in patches. The rest is new growth: sections where she has threaded living vine through the fabric and allowed it to root into the weave, so that parts of the garment are, technically, still alive, still photosynthesizing, still slowly growing. In the humid warmth of the hollow, she does not need much clothing. The vest is less for warmth than for *carrying* — it has pockets, loops, small sheaths for tools, all integrated into the weave. It is a garment and a workshop apron and a living organism, and it fits her so precisely because it has been growing on her body for years.

Below the vest, a wrap of heavier material around her hips, tied with a cord that is part copper wire and part vine. Her skin where it is exposed — arms, legs, the back of her neck — is brown, not from sun but from the hollow itself, from years of contact with sap and tannin and the humid air that deposits a faint film of organic residue on every surface. There are marks on her forearms that might be scars or might be stains or might be something else entirely — thin lines that follow the path of her veins, greenish-dark, as though something has gotten under her skin and traced her circulatory system with ink. She noticed the first of these lines seven growth-cycles ago. She watched it spread with the same patient attention she gives to a shoot threading itself through a wire frame. She is not alarmed. She is curious.

---

Her face. You would need to see her face to understand the rest of it.

It is a lean face, strong-boned, weathered in the way of someone who has spent a lifetime outdoors even though she has spent it under canopy — weathered by humidity and labor rather than by wind and sun. Deep lines bracket her mouth and radiate from the corners of her eyes, but they are not the lines of someone who frowns or squints. They are the lines of someone who *concentrates*. Who looks closely at small things for long periods. Who holds a fiber between thumb and forefinger and examines the way a rootlet has begun to grip it, and does this for an hour, and finds the hour short.

Her eyes are dark, quick, and set deep under brows that have gone wiry and grey. They have a quality you notice in people who work in dim or diffuse light for years — the pupils are wide, adapted, and in the green-gold illumination of the hollow they seem almost black, reflecting the stained-glass canopy in miniature. She does not look at you the way most people look at strangers. She looks at you the way she looks at a new shoot: assessing not what you are but what you might become, what role you might play in the system, where you might be trained to grow.

Her hair is grey, long, and usually bound up with a twist of copper wire into a knot at the back of her head — a knot she ties without thinking, the same knot every time, one of the dozens catalogued in the waterlogged book. When she undoes it at night, before climbing into the hammock, her hair falls past her shoulders and there are things in it — tiny fragments of bark, a curled leaf-tip, a thread of green fiber that might have blown there or might be growing there, she isn't sure anymore and doesn't find the ambiguity troubling.

---

She does not speak often. Not because she has no one to speak to — though she doesn't — but because speech is not the primary language here. The hollow communicates in tension, in the creaking of frames, in the slow tightening of threads, in the way the humidity rises after she runs the diesel and the canopy responds by opening its stomata wider, exhaling, creating a breeze that cools the engine block. She speaks to the hollow in the same language: she tightens a thread here, loosens one there, prunes a shoot that is growing against the structural logic of the nearest loom, grafts a young stem to an older one to strengthen a joint that has begun to flex too much. These are conversations. They take months to unfold. She is in the middle of hundreds of them at any given time.

When she does use words — muttering to herself as she works, or speaking aloud to the diesel engine when it won't start, which is more and more frequently these days — her voice is low, roughened, with a cadence that has picked up the rhythms of the hollow. She speaks in short phrases, pauses, waits, as though listening for a response. Sometimes she gets one: a creak, a shift, a leaf turning. She does not anthropomorphize. She does not think the plants are *listening*. She thinks the system is responsive, the way any complex system is responsive, and that her voice — its vibration, its frequency, the carbon dioxide she exhales when she speaks — is part of the input. She is not wrong about this.

---

She came here. That much is certain. She was not born in the hollow; the hollow is not old enough for that, or perhaps it is, but she is not. She came from *outside* — through the seam in the vegetation, the same one you found — carrying tools, carrying wire, carrying the book when it still had a cover and dry pages and text you could read. She came with a purpose. She was going to build something. A loom, a workshop, a place where she could weave in peace. She chose this hollow because it was sheltered, because the plants were already thick, because there was water.

That was the plan. The plan lasted perhaps three years before it began to change.

She does not remember the exact moment — the exact growth-cycle — when she realized that the plants were not merely tolerating her structures but *incorporating* them. When the first root grew through the first loom-frame and she had to decide whether to cut it away or let it stay. She let it stay. That was the decision that mattered, though she didn't know it at the time. She let it stay, and the root gripped the frame, and the frame became stronger, and the next season the root sent out lateral shoots that reached for the adjacent fibers, and she thought: *what if I threaded those shoots through the warp?*

And then she was no longer building a loom in a garden. She was building something else. Something she still does not have a word for, because the thing she is building is also building itself, and the word for it would need to contain both of those facts simultaneously, and language — human language, the language of subjects and objects, of makers and things made — does not do that well.

---

Her name. She had one. Kettner is not it — Kettner was the name on a map she found when she was looking for a place to build, a surveyor's name for a geographic feature, a hollow in the terrain that she made her own. Her actual name has gone unused for so long that it has lost its texture in her mind. She does not think of herself by name. She thinks of herself in terms of function: the one who tends, the one who threads, the one who runs the engines, the one who sleeps in the hammock, the one whose hands know the difference between a fiber under productive tension and one about to snap. These are not identities. They are roles, the way *root* and *trunk* and *leaf* are roles. She has a role in the system. The system has a role in her. The green lines on her forearms are, she suspects, the system extending its role deeper into her biology, and she does not object, because she has been extending her role deeper into the system's biology for decades, and turnabout — she thinks, working a copper thread through a living frame with her eight-and-a-half fingers — turnabout is fair.

She eats. This is worth noting because it is one of the few activities that still marks her as unambiguously animal. She eats the dark-capped mushrooms, cooked over a tiny fire she builds in a metal pan — the only fire she allows in the hollow, small, brief, fed with deadwood she collects from the canopy's natural pruning. She eats tubers that grow at the deeper levels of the root-mat, starchy, bland, sufficient. She eats a paste she makes from certain leaves, though she is careful about which ones, because some of the leaves in the hollow have begun producing compounds she doesn't recognize — new chemistry, emerging from the interaction between plant biology and the trace metals leaching from the engines and wires. Some of these compounds make her drowsy. One, from a leaf near the hand-cranked engine, sharpens her focus to a point so fine she can sit at the loom for six hours without blinking and produce weave-work of a tightness and regularity she cannot achieve in her normal state. She uses this leaf sparingly. Not because she fears it, but because the work she does under its influence is *too* regular. Too perfect. It lacks the slight variations that allow the plants to find purchase in the weave. Imperfection, she has learned, is the invitation. The crack the root grows through.

She drinks the water that collects in the cupped leaves. It tastes of chlorophyll and tannin and a faint mineral sharpness — copper, she thinks, from the wire. She has been drinking this water for decades. She does not know what it has done to her. She knows that she does not get sick, has not been sick since the early years, and she attributes this to the mushroom poultice and the general biology of the hollow, which seems to suppress bacterial infection in favor of fungal and plant colonization. She is not sure this is entirely benign. She is not sure it is not.

---

She sleeps in the hammock, behind the curtain that is half textile and half living leaf. When she lies still, the leaves closest to her body turn toward her, almost imperceptibly, the way leaves turn toward light. She radiates heat. She exhales carbon dioxide. She is, to the plants, a small warm engine, producing useful outputs. She has sometimes woken to find a tendril resting on her arm, or a new rootlet exploring the edge of the hammock where her sweat has soaked into the fabric. She removes these gently, because the hammock needs to remain functional as a hammock, but she does not remove them urgently. There is a negotiation happening. It has been happening for years. The terms are being drawn up in a language made of growth and tension and the slow chemistry of two kingdoms of life finding the edges of their compatibility.

She dreams. She has told no one this — there is no one to tell — but the dreams have changed over the years. In the early days, she dreamed of the world outside, of roads and faces and the clatter of human machinery. Now she dreams of the hollow. But not as she sees it in waking — in the dreams, she sees it from *inside*, from within the root-mat, looking up through layers of growth, feeling the pull of water through capillaries that are also her veins, feeling the tension of threads through frames that are also her bones. She wakes from these dreams not confused but *oriented*, as though the dream gave her information she couldn't access while conscious. She goes to a part of the hollow she hadn't planned to visit and finds a fiber that needs re-threading, a joint that is failing, a young shoot that has been tagged but forgotten. She tends it. She does not question how she knew.

---

There is a thing she is making. Or a thing that is making itself through her. It is on the largest loom, and it has been in progress for — she calculates, running her thumbnail along the growth rings visible where a supporting trunk was cut and healed — nine years. Possibly ten. It is a panel of woven material roughly four feet by six feet, and it is the densest, most complex thing in the hollow. The warp is copper wire alternating with living bark-fiber. The weft is synthetic thread, vine, steel filament, and something she cultivated herself: a hybrid fiber grown by training a plant shoot through a tube of thin copper mesh, so that the resulting strand is both vegetable and mineral, alive and conductive. The weave pattern is not regular. It follows a logic she works out in the margins of the book, a logic that is partly mathematical, partly botanical, and partly intuitive — she feels where the next thread should go the way a musician feels the next note, not by calculation but by the accumulated understanding of a pattern she has been inside for so long that it has become her primary language.

The panel, where it is complete, is extraordinary. It has the density of metal and the flexibility of cloth. It is warm to the touch — warmer than it should be — and when you press your ear to it, you can hear the hum of the hollow amplified, concentrated, as though the panel is an antenna tuned to the frequency of the place itself. The living fibers in the weave are still growing, still extending rootlets into the adjacent threads, still slowly incorporating the metal and synthetic elements into biological tissue. In places, the boundary between wire and root has become genuinely ambiguous. Under magnification — she has a hand lens, scratched but functional, in the ammunition crate — you can see cellular structures wrapping around copper strands, engulfing them, and the copper has taken on a greenish patina that is not oxidation alone, that has an organic architecture to it, as though the metal is being *digested* and rebuilt into something that serves both kingdoms.

She does not know what the panel is for. She knows it is important. She knows it in the same way she knows where the failing joint is before she sees it — the dream-knowledge, the body-knowledge, the knowledge that comes from being a node in a system rather than an observer of one. She thinks it might be a membrane. A threshold. A place where the translation between the mechanical and the biological becomes so dense and so complete that the distinction ceases to be meaningful. She thinks it might be the thing the whole hollow has been working toward — not just her, but the plants, the fungi, the engines, the water, the copper and steel and rubber slowly being subsumed into living tissue. She thinks she might be building a door. Or a skin. Or a new kind of organ for a new kind of body that is the hollow itself, that includes her, that has been growing around and through her for decades the way she has been growing around and through it.

She sits on the polished stool. She picks up the hybrid fiber — the one that is alive and conductive — and threads it through the warp. The loom creaks. A leaf adjusts overhead. A drop of water falls from a copper wire to a cupped leaf and begins its slow descent through the layers.

She is sixty-three years old, or sixty-seven, or somewhere in that range. She is healthy in ways that do not map onto medical charts. Her blood pressure is unknowable. Her resting heart rate, she suspects, has synchronized with something — with the slow pulse of water through the root-mat, or the rhythm of the largest loom's creaking, or some deeper cycle she hasn't identified yet. She will die here. She knows this without grief. She will die here and the root-mat will receive her and the fungi will do their work and the minerals of her body — the calcium, the iron, the copper she has been drinking in the leaf-water for decades — will enter the system, and the system will use them the way it uses everything, the way it is using her now: threading her through itself, weaving her in, tightening the pattern.

She bends to her work. The shears are within reach. The tin cup is half-full of leaf-water. The book is open to a page of knot-drawings that are also, if you look at them long enough, maps of the hollow, or diagrams of cellular structure, or instructions for something that has not been built yet but is building itself, slowly, in the green-gold light, in the sound that lives in your sternum, in the space between the made and the grown where she has made her home.

The loom weaves. The garden grows. She is both weaver and thread.
