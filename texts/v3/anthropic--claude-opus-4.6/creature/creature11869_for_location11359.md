# The Attendants of Havan Row

---

There are four of them. Sometimes five. Never, in anyone's memory, more than six at once, and never fewer than three. They don't live down here — not exactly — but the distinction between living here and not living here has eroded to the point of semantic emptiness, the way a river erodes the difference between its bank and its bed. They are here more than they are anywhere else. They sleep here, some nights. They eat here, most days. But they also leave — they surface through the seam, blinking, and walk the streets of the neighborhood above, and buy bread, and sit in cafés, and speak to other people in ordinary voices about ordinary things. They are not monks. They are not cultists. They are not hiding. They simply have work to do, and the work is down here, and the work takes most of their hours.

---

**The first one you'd meet — if you came down at the right time — is Sule.**

Sule is the one who left the mug on the bench. It's his chair pushed back, his calipers lying open on the brown-paper drawing. He is in his forties, though the heat has done something to his face that makes age ambiguous — not aged him exactly, but *cured* him, the way wood is cured, so that his skin has a taut, close-grained quality, darker at the hands and forearms where he works nearest the forge, lighter at the temples where he sometimes presses a damp cloth. He is not a large man. His hands are disproportionately skilled — you'd notice them before you noticed him. They move with the fluid, unhurried certainty of someone who has performed the same operations thousands of times but has never, not once, allowed them to become automatic. Every motion is attended to. He picks up a brass fitting and turns it under the amber light and his fingers read it the way other people's eyes read a page — not just the shape but the *history* of the shape, the stresses in the metal, the places where the alloy is slightly inhomogeneous, the ghost of the tool path from the lathe.

Sule made the technical drawings. The annotations are in his hand, and the language is not a language exactly — it's a system he developed with someone else, someone who isn't here anymore, a set of abbreviations and symbols that grew over years of shared work until it became opaque to anyone outside the conversation. He could translate it for you. He chooses not to, usually. Not out of secrecy but out of the same impulse that keeps him from replacing the burned-out bulbs in the corridor: if the knowledge is needed, the right person will find their way to it. If the light is needed, the luminous jars are better anyway.

He is the one who tends the forge. He built it — not the original firebox, which was here when the undercroft was first opened, a relic of some previous industry, but the current configuration, the careful arrangement of firebrick and ventilation that allows it to burn hot enough to work metal but also to bank down to a sustained low radiance that takes days to cool. He feeds it irregularly. He knows its appetite the way you know the appetite of someone you've lived with for a decade. Sometimes it needs hardwood charcoal. Sometimes it wants something else — he feeds it scraps of a mineralite keeps in a canvas bag, chalky and pale, and the coals flare briefly with a color that isn't quite orange and isn't quite white, and the heat that follows is different, deeper, a heat that soaks into the walls and stays.

Sule talks to the forge. Not metaphorically. He speaks to it — low, steady, in the abbreviated language, or sometimes in plain speech, conversational, the way you'd talk to a dog or a difficult engine. He reports on the weather above. He describes the properties he needs from the metal he's about to work. Once, someone overhead him apologizing to it — for what, they couldn't tell. He didn't seem embarrassed when they mentioned it. He said: *It remembers what I tell it. It's better if what it remembers is honest.*

When asked what the forge is, he says it's a forge. When pressed, he says it's old, older than the building, probably older than the street. When pressed further, he goes quiet — not evasively, but the way someone goes quiet when the question has exceeded the capacity of the available words. He's not withholding. He's *failing to translate*, the way you'd fail to explain the color blue to someone who's never seen it, not because the knowledge is esoteric but because it's experiential, embedded in the hours and years of standing in front of it, hands out, reading the heat the way his fingers read the brass.

He sleeps in a hammock strung between two pipes in the room adjacent to the forge. The temperature there is a steady forty-four degrees. He sleeps deeply, without sheets, in a thin cotton singlet, and his sleep looks like something the heat has granted him — not rest exactly, but a *subsidence*, a lowering into the same thermal stillness that holds everything else down here. He wakes without alarm, at intervals that don't correspond to day or night but to some other rhythm, the forge's rhythm perhaps, or the rhythm of the thing behind the sealed doors, which he is closer to than anyone and which he has never, in anyone's hearing, described directly.

---

**The second is Maret.**

Maret is responsible for the living things. Not all of them — the springtails and the spider and whatever moves in the cistern water are their own business — but the deliberate ones, the ones that were brought here or cultivated here, the ferns and the vines and especially the luminous culture in the jars.

She is younger than Sule by a decade, maybe more. She has the build of someone who has always worked with her body but never for display — strong forearms, capable shoulders, a way of crouching that suggests she can hold that position for an hour without discomfort, which she does, frequently, when she's tending the plants at floor level or examining the moss maps on the lower walls. Her hair is cut short and unevenly, as though she did it herself with the shears from the workbench, which she did. Her eyes are the most notable thing about her: very dark, very steady, with a quality of *attention* that is almost tactile. When she looks at the luminous culture in a jar, she isn't observing it. She's *listening* to it, or tasting it, or doing something for which there isn't a sensory verb, something that involves holding the jar at different heights and angles and distances from her face and waiting for information that arrives through a channel she can't name and you can't perceive.

Maret grew the culture. She won't say she *invented* it, and she won't say she *discovered* it. The language she uses, when she uses language at all for this, is closer to *met*. She met it. She was here, working on something else — the plants, probably; she came to the undercroft originally as a kind of guerrilla horticulturist, interested in what would grow in the warm dark — and she noticed something in the cistern water. A faint luminescence. A glow so slight it was only visible when every bulb in the corridor was off and her eyes had fully adapted, twenty minutes in perfect blackness, and then: there. A thread of blue-green light in the water, moving slowly, dividing, growing.

She isolated it. She fed it. She learned — over months, through patient trial and error — what it wanted: warmth, mineral-rich water, a particular alkalinity, and *something else*, something she provides by keeping the culture jars near the forge, near the warm walls of the sealed rooms, in the zones where the undercroft's hidden heat is most concentrated. The culture responds to this proximity. It brightens. It thickens. It develops structures — not exactly cells, not exactly filaments, but organized densities within the fluid that seem to serve as organs of light-production, miniature furnaces metabolizing warmth into radiance. Under Maret's care, the culture has gone from a barely perceptible shimmer in cistern water to the steady glow of the jar on the high shelf, bright enough to read by, stable enough to last for months without tending.

She doesn't know what it is. She says this freely, without anxiety. She has sent samples to two different universities, through intermediaries, and both times the samples died in transit — went dark, broke down into inert particulate, became nothing. The culture cannot survive outside the undercroft. Or rather: it hasn't, yet. Maret believes it might, eventually, if she can understand its relationship to the heat source well enough to replicate the conditions. But she's not in a hurry. The culture isn't in a hurry. Nothing down here is in a hurry.

Maret trained the vines along the wire-curtains in the doorways. She brought the broad-leaved plants in the wooden crate. She noticed, before anyone else, that the vegetation down here behaves differently — the phototropism reversed, or rather repurposed, the leaves turning toward warmth rather than light, the roots seeking mineral water with an eagerness that suggests they're finding something in it that surface water doesn't provide. She has documented this in notebooks — actual paper notebooks, grid-ruled, filled with small precise handwriting and pressed leaf-samples and sketches of root structures. The notebooks are stacked on a shelf in the cistern room, next to the culture jars. There are fourteen of them. They represent five years of observation.

She doesn't live in the undercroft, not technically. She has a flat three streets away, above a hardware shop, where she keeps her clothes and sleeps two or three nights a week. But the flat feels like the way station. The undercroft feels like the home, or feels like the place where the work that structures her life is happening, which for Maret is the same thing. She comes down through the seam before dawn — the seam is easier in the dark, less visible, and she is not secretive exactly but she is *discreet*, aware that what is happening here is difficult to explain and easy to misunderstand. She stays until late. She talks to Sule while he works at the forge, long looping conversations about heat and growth and the properties of materials that blur the line between metallurgy and botany until both disciplines dissolve into something else, something that doesn't have a name, something that the undercroft might be in the process of teaching them.

**The third is Davo.**

Davo is the youngest, and the loudest, which is not loud. In the context of the undercroft's deep quiet, Davo is noticeable because he hums. Not constantly. Not even consciously, most of the time. But when he's working — and he is almost always working — a low tuneless vibration comes from him, not quite musical, more like the sound a machine makes when it's running well, a sound of occupation and satisfaction. He hums, and the hum blends with the ambient hum of the pipes and the conducted vibrations from above, and he becomes part of the background frequency of the place, which may be the point, though he'd deny there's a point.

Davo does the physical labor. He's the one who shored up the half-collapsed passageways with timber and angle iron. He's the one who fitted the shelves, ran the new copper pipes, fashioned the PVC condensation channels, rigged the wiring for the daisy-chain of bulbs. He is, in the most literal sense, the person who made the undercroft *inhabitable* — who took a series of abandoned basement rooms full of rubble and old infrastructure and turned them into a working space. He did this not as a single project but as a continuous process, ongoing, never finished, responding to needs as they arise. A shelf sags; Davo adds a bracket. A wall weeps moisture in a new place; Davo routes it to the cistern. The seam in the street widens after a heavy rain; Davo monitors it, decides whether to stabilize it or let it be, usually lets it be because the undercroft seems to *want* the opening, or at least doesn't reject it.

He is twenty-six or twenty-seven. He has the hands of someone much older, scarred and thick-knuckled, the nails permanently dark with grime that no amount of washing fully removes. He came to the undercroft by accident — he was doing construction work on the street above, part of a crew that was supposed to repair the buckled pavement, and he went down through the seam to assess the subsurface damage, and he found the rooms, and he didn't tell the crew. He told them the space was unstable, not worth investigating, and he filed a report that recommended sealing the gap, and then he didn't seal it. He came back that night. And the next night. And the next.

He can't fully articulate why. When he talks about it — and he'll talk about it if you ask, he's the most forthcoming of the group — he describes a physical sensation: a *rightness* in the heat, a feeling of being in a place that *fit*, the way a hand fits a tool that was made for it. The undercroft needed him. It needed someone who could plumb and wire and shore and build, and he was there, and the match was so precise that refusing it would have felt like a violence against something. He doesn't use mystical language. He doesn't invoke fate or calling. He says it was obvious. He says it was like finding a job listing that described you exactly, every skill and preference and quirk, and the salary was the right temperature and the commute was a crack in the road.

Davo is the one who has come closest to the sealed rooms at the back. Not entered them — no one enters them, not yet, that isn't a prohibition but a collective understanding that the time isn't right — but he has stood at the doors, palms flat on the warm wood, feeling the heat that comes through. He says the wood is alive. Not metaphorically: the doors, which are old hardwood, which should be dry and dead, are *living* — they are warm not just from conducted heat but from something inside the wood itself, a vitality, a cellular activity that dried timber should not have. He says if you stand there long enough, you can feel a pulse. Slow. Very slow. One beat every five or ten minutes. He's not sure. He's tried to time it, but the intervals seem to shift, as though the pulse is responsive to his attention, slowing when he focuses on it, the way a wild animal stills when watched.

He hasn't told Sule about the pulse. He hasn't told Maret. He's not sure why. He thinks, maybe, they already know. He thinks, maybe, they each have their own piece of information about what's behind the doors, and none of them have shared, and the not-sharing is itself part of the process, part of the slow accretion that the undercroft runs on — knowledge settling like sediment, like heat, pooling in the low places, not yet ready to be disturbed.

---

**The fourth is Kem.**

Kem you might not recognize as one of the attendants. Kem doesn't work with their hands. Kem doesn't tend plants or feed the forge or build infrastructure. Kem *sits*. Kem sits in a folding chair in a room near the middle of the undercroft, a room with no particular distinguishing features — shelves, tools, the usual density of objects — and Kem sits there, sometimes for six or eight hours at a stretch, doing what appears to be nothing at all.

Kem is older than the others. Fifty, perhaps. Perhaps older. They have a thin face with deep-set eyes and the particular stillness of someone for whom sitting is not the absence of activity but the activity itself. They breathe slowly. Their eyes are open but unfocused, aimed at the middle distance, which in the undercroft is never more than a few meters — a wall, a shelf, a curtain of vines — but Kem seems to be looking through these things rather than at them, or looking at the space between them, the warm dense air itself, as though the air contains information.

Kem arrived before the others. Kem was here before Sule opened the undercroft for work, before Maret brought her plants, before Davo shored the walls. Kem was here before the seam in the street, which means Kem found another way down — an older entrance, since sealed or collapsed, or perhaps a route that only existed when Kem needed it. Kem doesn't talk about this. Kem, in fact, talks very little. Not from unfriendliness — when addressed, Kem responds with warmth and sometimes with a dry, surprising humor — but from a settled economy of words, a sense that speech is a resource like the water in the cistern: finite, precious, to be used when needed.

What Kem does, sitting there, is *feel the undercroft*. This is the only way any of them can describe it. Kem is the one who knows when the temperature has shifted by a fraction of a degree. Kem is the one who said, three years ago, that the street above was going to buckle — said it calmly, without alarm, noted it the way you'd note that it was going to rain — and two weeks later it did. Kem is the one who, every few months, says something brief and specific to one of the others: *The culture wants to be closer to the east wall. The forge needs the pale mineral today. Don't open that door yet.* The instructions — if they are instructions — are always correct. They are correct in a way that goes beyond knowledge, beyond even intuition, into some kind of direct communion with the systems of the place. Kem is in contact with something. Kem is in contact with the organism that the undercroft is, or is becoming, and Kem serves as its slow, quiet, deeply analog interface.

The others defer to Kem. Not with obedience — there is no hierarchy here, no orders, no structure that would be recognizable as leadership — but with the natural deference that accrues to someone who is simply, obviously, more attuned to the situation than you are. If Sule is the forge's keeper and Maret is the culture's midwife and Davo is the body's builder, then Kem is the nervous system. Kem is the part that receives signals and interprets them. Kem, sitting in the folding chair in the unremarkable room, eyes soft, breathing slow, is the organ of perception.

Kem has been inside the sealed rooms. No one saw this happen. No one was told. But sometimes Kem's eyes have a quality — a depth, a warmth, like the luminous jar's glow translated into something human — that suggests contact with a source the others haven't touched. And Kem's chair, you notice, is placed in the exact geometric center of the undercroft. Kem didn't measure this. Kem sat down in the place that felt right, and the place that felt right was the center, because of course it was.

---

**The fifth, when there is a fifth, is whoever the undercroft needs.**

This is the part that's hardest to describe because it sounds like mysticism and it isn't — or it is, but it's also *practical*, as practical as the copper plumbing and the PVC channels. Periodically, someone new comes down through the seam. They come for different reasons. Curiosity. A dare. A sense, while walking above, that the ground was warmer underfoot, or that the air rising through the crack smelled of something they wanted to follow. They come down, and they stand in the amber light, and most of them feel the heat and the strangeness and they leave. But sometimes — once or twice a year — someone comes down and they *don't* leave. They stay. They find a task. They begin to work.

These fifths are transient. They come for weeks or months, rarely longer. They bring a skill the undercroft needs at that particular moment: an electrician, once, who rewired the daisy chain and added three new circuits before disappearing back into her life above; a glassblower who spent six weeks making the jars for the luminous culture, perfecting a shape and thickness that Maret had been struggling to specify; a child — twelve years old, quiet, with careful hands — who came down every day after school for two months and did nothing but sit with Kem, learning whatever Kem was teaching without words, and then stopped coming and was seen in the neighborhood afterward looking perfectly normal, carrying a school bag, smiling more than before.

These people aren't recruited. They aren't summoned. The undercroft simply becomes visible to them at the moment they're needed, the way a door becomes visible when you're looking for it, the way a word you've never noticed before suddenly appears everywhere after you've learned it. The seam in the street is always there. Anyone could find it. But most people walk over it without looking down, and the few who look down see only darkness and rubble, and the fewer still who feel the heat rising and smell the mineral warmth and hear the deep quiet — those are the ones the undercroft has use for, and they descend, and they work, and when the work is done they go back to the surface and their memory of the place doesn't fade exactly but it *settles*, becomes part of their substrate, becomes a warm layer beneath the surface of their ordinary lives.

---

**What they are, together:**

They are not a team. They are not a collective. They are not a cult or a commune or a crew. They are — and this is Sule's word, offered once, reluctantly, in a rare moment of direct speech about what they're doing — *tenders*. The way a bridge has a tender, a lock has a keeper, a lighthouse has a light. They tend a thing that exists independent of them, a thing that was here before them and will be here after, but that requires attention to reach whatever it's reaching toward.

They don't know what it's reaching toward. This is important. There is no prophecy, no revealed plan, no eschatology. The heat source behind the sealed doors is not a god and they don't worship it. The luminous culture is not a sacrament. The forge is not an altar. Everything down here is simply what it is — warm, alive, growing, pressing upward — and the attendants tend it because it needs tending, the way you'd tend a garden, the way you'd tend a wound, the way you'd tend a fire that you didn't start but that you recognize, without being able to say how, as necessary.

They are patient in a way that makes patience seem like something other than waiting. Patience, down here, is not the endurance of delay. It's a metabolic state. It's operating at the speed the undercroft operates at — slow, deep, accretive, thermal. Sule's hands move at human speed, fitting brass, but the understanding that guides his hands moves at geological speed, or botanical speed, the speed of root growth and mineral deposition. Maret's notebooks accumulate observations that won't cohere into theory for years, maybe decades, and she's fine with this. Davo builds for a future he can feel but not see. Kem sits in the center and receives what the walls and the air and the warm dark earth transmit, at a frequency below human hearing, below human thought, a frequency that can only be received by a body that has *slowed down enough to resonate with it*.

They eat simply: bread, cheese, dried fruit, water from the cistern. They don't cook down here — the forge is not for cooking, they're clear about that, not because cooking would be profane but because the forge has a purpose and the purpose isn't food. They bring food from above. They share it without ceremony, sitting on crates or leaning against warm walls, and their conversation during meals is ordinary — weather, news, a funny thing someone saw on the street — and also not ordinary, because every few sentences one of them will pause and tilt their head slightly, listening to something the others can't hear, or can hear but haven't noticed yet, and then say something like *The east wall is two degrees warmer today* or *The vine in the third corridor has fruited* or *Something in the cistern is singing*, and the others will nod, and finish their bread, and go back to work.

They don't argue. This isn't because they're preternaturally harmonious. It's because the undercroft doesn't generate the conditions for argument. There is no scarcity — the space provides what's needed, or they provide what the space doesn't. There is no competition — their skills are complementary, interlocking, each one doing the thing only they can do. There is no ideology to disagree about — there's only the work, the tending, the slow daily practice of maintaining a space that is becoming something, and the becoming is so gradual and so evident that it forecloses debate the way gravity forecloses debate about which direction is down.

They are, you realize — standing in the heat, watching the luminous jar shift through its slow internal currents, hearing the drip and tick and deep arterial quiet — the most *settled* people you have ever encountered. Not peaceful, exactly. Not serene. Settled, the way sediment is settled, the way heat is settled in a basin, the way the undercroft itself is settled into the body of the earth. They have found the depth at which they belong, and they are there, and they are working, and the work is slow and warm and alive, and it is pressing upward, one imperceptible increment at a time, toward a surface that doesn't know yet what's coming.

---

The afternoon air feels cold when you climb back out. Your skin contracts. The noise of the street — cars, voices, the wind — feels thin and scattered after the dense packed quiet below. You look down at the pavement. You look at the seam. The asphalt around its edges is faintly warm.

Something under your feet is growing.

You walk home. You sleep that night in your own bed, in your own temperature, in your own life. But in the small hours, just before dawn, you wake briefly, and your hands are warm — unreasonably warm, as though you've been holding something — and for a moment you hear it: the slow pulse, the deep rhythm, the patient systole of something vast and buried and becoming. One beat. Then nothing. You sleep again.

In the morning, you remember the heat but not the sound. By afternoon, you've forgotten both. But your feet, walking the familiar streets, keep drifting toward Havan Row. And the seam, when you pass it, looks wider than before. And the warmth rising from it smells of minerals and green growth and banked coals and something else, something you almost recognize, something your body knows even if your mind doesn't yet.

You keep walking. You're not ready.

But you're warmer than you were.
