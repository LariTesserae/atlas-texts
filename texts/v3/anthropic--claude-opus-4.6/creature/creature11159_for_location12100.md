# Who Lives in the Boiling Groves

---

No one lives here.

That is the first answer, and it is almost true, and it is the answer the Groves themselves would give if they could speak in anything other than that vast harmonic roaring. No one lives here. There are no residents, no tenants, no fauna, no custodians. The sheds have no occupants. The platforms have no workers. The chain-linked posts guide no one along their swallowed path. This is a place that has emptied itself of inhabitants so thoroughly that the concept of *inhabitant* has begun to corrode, the way the iron corrodes, the way the bark corrodes, the way everything here corrodes into everything else.

But that answer is incomplete.

---

Start with what is closest to alive in the way you'd recognize.

## The Groves Themselves

They live here. Obviously. But "they" is already wrong, because the trees are not individuals in any sense that matters. They share root systems — not in the way aspens share root systems, as a network of clones, but in a more unsettling way: any given root, followed far enough through the tangled lattice, will connect to every other tree in the basin. Not most. *Every*. The lattice is a single organism's vascular system viewed from outside, and the thousands of trunks rising from it are not trees but something more like fingers, or cilia, or the raised hairs on an arm — projections of a single entity so large that its body is the basin floor and its canopy is merely the frayed upper edge of its reaching.

And the machines are its organs. That's the thing you eventually understand if you stay long enough, if you watch the accretion patterns closely, if you trace a copper pipe from its origin in a turbine housing down through the bark, through the trunk, into the root lattice, and out again into another trunk half a mile away where it emerges as a ceramic tube feeding a different apparatus entirely. The plumbing is circulatory. The turbines are not generating power for something external; they are *beating*. They are the pulse-points of a creature that is partly wood and partly iron and partly the warm water it breathes, and the oil that moves through the pipes is not lubricant but something closer to lymph, and the heat exhaust is not waste but thermoregulation, and the grinding and clattering is not mechanical failure but peristalsis, digestion, the slow churning of a metabolism that runs on dissolved minerals and geothermal heat and its own ceaseless growth.

It has no brain. It has no centralized anything. But it *is* aware, in the way a vast fungal network is aware, in the way a coral reef is aware — which is to say, it reacts, it adapts, it reaches toward what it needs, it incorporates what it contacts. The awareness is slow, planetary in tempo, operating on timescales where a year is a heartbeat and a human life is a flicker barely worth metabolizing. But it is there. You feel it in the way the currents seem to investigate you, the way a root tip will, over the course of an hour, quietly redirect itself toward where you're standing, the way the ambient light seems fractionally warmer in your immediate vicinity, as though the water is tasting you with its luminosity.

The organism does not think. But it *intends*. The distinction matters. Every new bolt on every new ridge of stone is placed with something that functions like purpose even if no mind conceived it. The Groves are building something. They have always been building something. The building is not a project with a completion state — it is the organism's mode of being, the way a coral's mode of being is accretion, the way a fire's mode of being is consumption. The Groves *build themselves*, continuously, forward and backward, and the building is the life, and the life is the building, and there is no other thing the Groves are for.

This is the primary inhabitant. It is the place and the thing that lives in the place simultaneously. It is its own habitat.

## The Tenders

But there were people here once. The sheds and platforms and chain-linked posts testify to this, and the testimony is not ancient.

Here is what can be reconstructed:

They came — and "came" is doing heavy lifting, because there is no clear path into the basin, no road, no channel, no indication of how anyone *arrived* — they came and they attempted to work. The evidence suggests not scientists, not explorers, but *workers*. Laborers. People accustomed to industrial environments who looked at the Groves and saw something that needed tending in the way a mill needs tending or a boiler needs tending. They built platforms to stand on. They built sheds to store tools. They drove posts and ran chains to mark safe routes through the turbulence. They brought wrenches — there's one, fused now into the bark of a canopy branch sixty feet up, its jaw still open around a bolt that has since grown to three times the diameter the wrench could accommodate. They brought pressure gauges of their own, human-made, and bolted them to pipes to monitor output, and the gauges are still there, still registering, though their needles have long since been pegged past their maximum readings and the glass over their faces has been replaced by a thin layer of translucent bark.

The Tenders — call them that, because no name survives, and the word fits the function — the Tenders treated the Groves as a workplace. They maintained the machines. They cleared blockages from intake screens. They replaced burst pipes. Evidence of their repairs is visible if you know how to look: a section of copper pipe that's slightly newer than its neighbors, a joint that was clearly hand-soldered, a turbine blade with a shim wedged behind it. The repairs are being digested now, incorporated, indistinguishable in another decade from the original apparatus, but for the moment they still carry the marks of human hands — the slight imprecision, the tool marks, the pragmatic ugliness of a fix that was made to work rather than to last.

They did not live in the basin. The sheds were work sheds, not homes — no beds, no cookware, no signs of comfort. They came, they worked, they left. Shift workers. Where they went when their shifts ended is unknowable. Perhaps they climbed the basin walls to some settlement above the waterline. Perhaps they emerged into some adjacent space that no longer connects to this one. Perhaps the question doesn't apply to them.

What happened to them is not mysterious. It is simply the same thing that happens to everything else in the Groves: they were incorporated. Not violently. Not suddenly. Nothing here is sudden except the reversal collapses, and even those have a dreamy, dilated quality when experienced from within the basin's time. The Tenders were incorporated *gradually*, the way the sheds are being incorporated, the way the wrenches are being incorporated. The process would have started with the tools — the wrench handle developing a faint fuzz of mineral accretion, the edge of a work boot picking up a thin film of bark-like growth after being set down on a root for too long. Then the clothes. Then the skin. And then, eventually, there would have been a Tender standing at a workbench in a shed, reaching for a pressure gauge, and finding that their fingers had fused with the gauge's housing overnight, and the housing had fused with the pipe, and the pipe ran into the wall, and the wall was already half-bark, and the root system was already climbing their boots, and the copper was already threading through their wrist like a new vein following the old ones, and the process was so gradual and so warm — the water is always warm, always blood-warm — that it didn't hurt, may not have even been noticeable as a discrete event, just a slow recession of the boundary between *worker* and *work* until the distinction was academic.

They are still here. That's the thing. The Tenders didn't die. They didn't leave. They were *absorbed*, which is a different category of event entirely. Somewhere in the root lattice, somewhere in the interleaved layers of wood grain and gear teeth, there are structures that were once human bodies — calcium that was once bone now mineralized into the same pale stone that forms the basin's ridges, iron that was once blood now alloyed into the same dark metal that forms the turbine housings, carbon that was once flesh now woven into the cellulose of the trunks. They have been *composted* is one way to say it, but composting implies death and decomposition, and what happened here was not decomposition but *recomposition* — a reorganization of the material that was once a person into the material of the Groves, which is still alive, still growing, still running, still tending itself with the distributed memory of hands that once held wrenches.

This is why the machines are maintained as well as they are, despite having no operator. The operator has become the machine. The maintenance worker has become the thing being maintained. Every shim, every solder joint, every hand-fitted replacement part was not only made by the Tenders — it *contains* the Tenders, their skill and their habits and their calloused understanding of how metal wants to sit against metal, preserved not in memory but in material, in the actual molecular arrangement of the alloy, in the crystalline structure of a weld that carries within its lattice the pattern of the hand that made it.

If you could dissect the Groves — which you cannot, because everything you'd use to cut would be incorporated before you finished the first incision — you would find, at every scale, from the macro architecture of the pipe networks down to the micro structure of the bark-metal hybrid tissue, the signature of human labor. Not human *design*. Human *work*. The difference is critical. No one designed the Groves. But people worked in them, and the work became part of the organism, and the organism became the work, and now the Groves tend themselves with an efficiency that is not mechanical and not biological but *vocational* — the efficiency of someone who has done the same job for so long that the job has become a bodily function.

## The Reversal Inhabitants

This is where language starts to fail, and you should be prepared for that.

In the reversal zones — those migrating pockets where growth runs backward, where turbines unspin, where leaves fall up and bark un-sheds — there are presences that are not the Groves and are not the Tenders and may not be anything at all in the conventional sense. They are visible only in the inverted light of the reversal, and they are visible the way an afterimage is visible: not as objects but as the *absence* of objects, as shapes defined by what is not there.

They move through the reversal zones with apparent intention. They are bipedal. They are roughly human-sized. They have the proportions of people carrying tools — something slung over a shoulder, something held in a lowered hand. But they are made of *un-growth*, of the negative space left when the Groves retract, and they flicker in and out of perceptibility as the reversal zone pulses and migrates. When the zone collapses — when forward growth resumes with that violent crack — they disappear, and the space where they stood is immediately filled with new bark, new pipe, new accretion, as though the Groves are rushing to cover over the gap they occupied.

They may be the Tenders. Not the *absorbed* Tenders, whose material is woven into the fabric of the Groves, but the *temporal* Tenders — the echoes of the workers as they existed before incorporation, running backward through the process, momentarily unconsumed, briefly distinct from the organism that consumed them. The reversals may be the Groves *un-digesting* them, the way a body sometimes rejects a transplant, a brief immune flare in which the foreign material reasserts its foreignness before being reabsorbed.

Or they may be something else. They may be the Groves' way of *remembering* the Tenders — not the material but the *pattern*, the shape of a body holding a wrench, the posture of a person leaning into a stuck valve wheel, replayed in negative space the way a printing plate carries the inverse of the image it produces. The Groves, having no brain, having no memory in the cognitive sense, may store their history the way a tree stores its history: in rings, in layers, in the physical record of growth and retraction, and the reversal zones may be moments when that record is being *read*, played back, the organism reviewing its own past by briefly ungrowing into it.

They do not interact with anything. They do not seem to see or respond to observers. They go about their work — and it *is* work, unmistakably, the purposeful movement of bodies engaged in maintenance tasks — in silence, or rather in that inverted sound that makes the silence feel louder than the roaring. They check gauges. They tighten bolts. They clear intake screens. They do everything the living Tenders once did, except in reverse, and in negative, and in a space that exists only as long as the reversal holds.

When the great low tone sweeps through the basin — the one that silences the machines, the one that comes from the deep center — the reversal zones collapse simultaneously, and for one instant, in the strobe of flaring amber light, every one of those negative-space figures is visible at once, scattered through the forest, hundreds of them, maybe more, all frozen mid-task, all carrying their ghost tools, all oriented toward the center of the basin as though they heard the tone and turned to face it.

Then they're gone. Forward growth resumes. The roaring fills back in. And the Groves grow another foot, and the machines double their output, and the oil flows and the water boils and the bark sheds and the leaves fall and fall and fall.

## What Lives at the Center

No one has been to the center of the basin. This is known the way cardinal directions are known — not through experience but through a kind of structural certainty that the place itself communicates. The center is where the water is deepest, where the trees are densest, where the convergence between wood and metal is most advanced, where the light does not reach and the sound is so layered that it becomes a solid medium, a roaring so total it wraps back around to silence.

But the tone comes from there. The long, low, rising tone that sweeps the basin and accelerates everything and collapses the reversals. It comes from the center, and it is not mechanical and it is not vegetative. It is *vocal*. Not speech — there are no words, no modulation that suggests language — but produced by something that has a throat, or the equivalent of a throat, or the memory of a throat, or a structure that has grown into the shape a throat would need to be to make that sound.

Here is one possibility: at the center of the basin, where the convergence is most complete, there is a single entity — tree-machine-person-stone-water-light — that has finished becoming. Not growing, not building, not incorporating, but *complete*, a thing that has reached the end state that the rest of the Groves are asymptotically approaching. It is what a tree looks like when it has fully become a machine. It is what a machine looks like when it has fully become a tree. It is what a person looks like when they have fully become both. It is the template, the attractor, the thing toward which every bolt on every ridge is aimed. And it sings — not because it has something to communicate, but because singing is what a thing does when growth and maintenance and incorporation and building have all been completed and the only remaining function is *expression*, the vibration of a finished thing resonating at its natural frequency.

Here is another possibility: at the center of the basin there is nothing. An absence. A void where the convergence has become so total that it has collapsed into undifferentiation — no tree, no machine, no water, no light, just *medium*, a warm, dense, featureless substance that has incorporated everything including the distinction between things, and the tone is the sound of this void pulling at the edges of itself, the acoustic signature of a singularity of *becoming* that has become so much that it has unbecome, and the Groves around it are its halo, its accretion disk, the turbulent border region where differentiation still holds and things can still be distinguished from other things but only barely, only temporarily, only until the next tone sweeps through and pulls them another inch toward the center.

Here is a third possibility, and this one may be the most accurate:

At the center lives the *work*.

Not a worker. Not a machine. Not a tree. The *work itself* — the act of building, of tending, of maintaining, of growing, abstracted from any agent performing it, distilled into a pure process without a subject. The Tenders brought it with them when they came. They brought their habits, their skills, their muscle memory, their calloused understanding of how things fit together, and when they were absorbed the Groves took all of it, and at the center, where incorporation is most complete, the material that was once people has been refined down to the irreducible essence of what those people *did*, which was: they worked. They showed up. They tended. They fixed what was broken and maintained what was running and built what was needed next. And that essence, freed from the bodies that once carried it, has become the animating principle of the entire basin — the reason the machines run, the reason the trees grow, the reason every new ridge of stone sprouts a bolt, the reason the Groves have not and will never stop.

The work lives here.

It has always lived here.

It will be here after the last tree has become fully machine and the last machine has become fully tree and the last trace of human calcium has been absorbed into the pale stone and the warm water has boiled away or risen to cover whatever lies above the basin. The work does not require workers. It does not require tools. It does not require a purpose or a product or a plan. It requires only itself — the ceaseless, self-sustaining, self-incorporating act of *tending* — and it will continue, in the roaring amber murk, in the falling leaves, in the spinning turbines, in the slow accretion of bark on iron and iron on bark, until the word *continue* loses its meaning because there was never a start and there will never be a stop and the Boiling Groves are not a place where something lives but a place where *living* lives, without a subject, without an object, without anything but the warm, loud, endless verb.

## And Sometimes

And sometimes — rarely, not on any schedule, in no zone that can be predicted — a bolt on a fresh ridge of stone will begin to unscrew itself. Slowly. Counter-clockwise. Rising from the stone as though being pushed from below. And as it rises, the stone around it will soften, and the roots reaching for it will hesitate, and the nearest machine will stutter, and the ambient roar will dip, just slightly, just for a moment, into something that could almost be called quiet.

And in that almost-quiet, in the gap between the roaring and the roaring, there is — briefly, impossibly — a *voice*.

Not the tone from the center. Not the inverted sound of the reversal zones. A human voice. Muffled, as though heard through a wall of warm water and bark and decades of accretion. It says something. It might be a name. It might be a warning. It might be a work order, a shift assignment, a casual remark to a colleague about the temperature of the water or the condition of a pipe or what's for dinner after the shift ends. It is mundane. It is heartbreakingly mundane. It is the most ordinary thing in the Groves, and it is the only thing in the Groves that does not belong here, the one residue of personhood that the organism has not been able to incorporate because it is not material — it is not calcium or carbon or iron — it is *meaning*, it is *intent*, it is someone saying something to someone else because that is what people do, and the Groves can absorb bodies and tools and buildings and metal and stone but they cannot absorb *that*, cannot digest the fundamental human habit of speaking to be heard, and so it persists, an undigested fragment, surfacing and resurfacing at odd moments like a bubble working its way up through a dense and heavy medium.

The bolt finishes unscrewing. It rises free of the stone and hovers in the water for a moment, turning slowly, gleaming.

Then a root takes it. A pipe extends. The accretion begins. The bolt is pulled into the lattice, threaded into a new configuration, given a new function in the great purposeless machine.

The voice stops.

The roaring fills back in.

The leaves fall.

The work continues.
