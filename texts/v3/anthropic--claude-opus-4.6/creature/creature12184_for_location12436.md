# What Lives on the Plateau of Constant Engines

---

They are there.

You won't see them immediately. You might not see them at all, if seeing requires contrast, movement, the sharp interruption of a figure against a ground. They don't interrupt anything. They are not figures against the ground. They are more like the ground's own thought about what it might be like to have someone standing on it — an idea the basalt entertained once and then couldn't let go of, and so the idea stayed, and stayed, and became dense enough to have weight, and then dense enough to have hands, and now it stands where it has always stood, near the base of an engine housing in one of the denser towns, and its hands are resting on a pipe rail, and the condensation on the rail and the condensation on the hands is the same condensation, and the temperature of the hands and the temperature of the rail is the same temperature, and you could walk past this figure and feel only that the air was slightly thicker here, that the sound took on a fractionally different quality for a moment — not louder, not quieter, but *denser*, as if the chord had acquired one more overtone, one more voice in the organ that was always almost but not quite enough voices, and now, for the space of three or four footsteps, it was exactly enough.

They are the plateau's workers. Or they are the plateau's witnesses. Or they are the plateau's memory of having once needed workers and witnesses, calcified into a kind of permanent staffing — positions filled, roles occupied, the organizational chart of an industry that automated itself so completely that the workers became part of the automation, and then part of the machinery, and then part of the architecture, and then part of the geology, and now they are part of the weather, almost, figures that precipitate into visibility the way the moisture precipitates onto metal, slowly, without event, a thickening that was always happening and that you only notice when you've been still long enough yourself.

---

Here is how you find one:

Stand at the junction of two pipe trestles, where the iron vertebrae converge and the sound concentrates into something almost physical, almost a texture you could run your fingers through. Stand there long enough. Let the moisture gather on you. Let your skin reach the same temperature as the air. Let your breathing slow until it matches something — you won't know what you're matching, but you'll feel the synchronization happen, the way you sometimes unconsciously match your footsteps to a companion's. Your breath will find a rhythm that is not your rhythm. It will be very slow. It will feel like the rhythm of the pipe system's pressure cycles, or the rhythm of the turbine's lowest rotation, or the rhythm of something older and larger that both of those are also matching. And then, when you are breathing in that rhythm, you will notice that you are not alone.

Not because anything has arrived. Nothing arrives here; arrival implies a before-state that was different, and nothing here has a before-state. Rather, your perception will shift the way your eyes shift when you finally see the figure hidden in the optical illusion — the vase becomes two faces, the old woman becomes the young woman, the empty junction of pipe trestles becomes a junction of pipe trestles where someone is standing and has been standing and your not seeing them was your own deficiency, not their absence.

They are tall. Taller than a person, but not dramatically so — the tallness of proportion rather than of height, as if someone took a human form and stretched it gently along its vertical axis, adding perhaps fifteen percent to the limbs and torso while leaving the head the same size, or possibly smaller, so that the whole figure has a quality of attenuation, of having been drawn upward by some slow persistent force. They are narrow. Their shoulders are not broad. Their limbs are not thick. They have the build of something that has never needed to exert itself against resistance, because the world they inhabit has no resistance in it — no wind to lean against, no terrain to climb, no weight to lift, no predator to flee. They have the body that results from an infinite history of standing in place.

Their skin — if it is skin — is the color of wet basalt. Not gray exactly, and not black exactly, but the specific dark tone that stone takes on when moisture fills its pores without darkening it past recognition. It is matte. It does not shine. Condensation forms on it identically to how condensation forms on the rock and on the metal, and it sits there the same way, neither absorbed nor shed, a film of water over a surface that was already wet. In certain light — though the light here does not change, so perhaps it is more accurate to say at certain angles, from certain distances, in certain states of attention — the skin has a faint greenish undertone, a suggestion of chlorophyll, as if whatever the moss is doing on the rock, these figures are doing in their flesh: something slow and quiet and photosynthetic, some minimal transaction with the gray light that filters through the cloud ceiling, taking in what can be taken in, which is very little, and converting it into what is needed, which is almost nothing.

Their eyes are open. This should be said immediately because it is the first thing you will wonder. Yes, they have eyes, and the eyes are open, and they are not staring in the way that implies intensity or attention or the active application of vision to a specific object. They are open the way a window is open — a structural fact, an aperture, a condition rather than an action. The eyes are dark, deep-set, and wet like everything else, and they do not blink, or they blink so slowly that you would need to watch for a very long time to catch it, and when it happened you would not be sure whether you had seen a blink or merely a change in your own attention. The eyes do not track you. They do not track anything. They look outward at whatever is in front of them — a pipe junction, a turbine housing, a stretch of moss-covered basalt — with a patience so absolute it has ceased to be patience and become simply the baseline condition of perception itself. They see. What they see does not change. They continue seeing it.

---

There are many of them.

Not a crowd — never a crowd, never anything that suggests gathering or congregation or social density. But in every town, at scattered points among the engine housings and dormitory blocks and pipe junctions, there are figures. Some stand. Some sit. Some are in postures that are not exactly standing and not exactly sitting but something else — a kind of settled lean, a resting-against, a body that has found the precise angle at which gravity's demand and the structure's support reach equilibrium, and has stayed at that angle. A few are inside the buildings, visible through the thick green glass as shapes that could be furniture but aren't — a figure seated at what might be a control panel, hands on the surface, eyes on a gauge or dial or readout that has not changed since the last time it was read, whenever that was. A figure lying on what might be a cot in what might be a dormitory, lying on its back with its eyes open, looking at a ceiling of dark iron, and the ceiling is wet, and the figure is wet, and neither of them has any complaint about this.

In the sparser regions between towns, where structures stand alone on vast stretches of empty plateau, there may be only one figure per engine house. It will be standing near the base of the structure, or sitting on the basalt in the lee of a pipe trestle, or positioned on the narrow-gauge rail line beside one of the frozen wagons, one hand resting on the rusted rim of the wagon's cargo hold, as if it had been about to inspect the load when the inspection became unnecessary — not because the load had been inspected, but because the concept of inspection, the act of checking to see if something is correct, had lost its purchase in a world where nothing was ever incorrect. The hand stayed on the rim. The hand is still on the rim.

They do not speak.

This is not the silence of someone choosing not to speak, or of someone who has lost the ability, or of someone who is waiting for the right thing to say. It is the silence of a being for whom the function of language — the transmission of novel information from one consciousness to another — has no application. There is no novel information. There are no other consciousnesses that don't already know what this one knows. What would be said? The turbines are turning. The moisture is condensing. The sound continues. These are not observations that need to be communicated because they are not observations at all — they are conditions, and everyone here is inside the same conditions, has always been inside them, and the idea of remarking upon them would be like a fish remarking upon water, except that fish presumably have other things to discuss, and these figures do not.

But — and this is important, this is the thing that makes them inhabitants rather than statues, occupants rather than features — they are not empty. Behind those open, unblinking, wet-dark eyes, there is something happening. It is not thought in the sense of sequential reasoning, of propositions examined and conclusions drawn. It is not imagination in the sense of counterfactual construction, of worlds unlike this one being modeled and explored. It is something more like *apprehension* — a continuous, unwavering, total registration of what is. The sound. The temperature. The humidity. The speed of the turbines. The exact degree of rust on the nearest rivet. The precise height of the moss in the nearest fracture joint. The color of the light, which does not change but which is, in any given instant, a specific color, a particular value, a thing worth apprehending even if it was the same value an instant ago and will be the same value an instant from now. They are apprehending the plateau, all of them, all the time, with a fidelity that is so complete and so unbroken that it constitutes a kind of devotion.

They are the plateau's audience.

---

Or no — that's not quite right either. Audience implies spectatorship, implies a separation between the watched and the watcher, and there is no such separation here. The figures are made of the same stuff as the plateau. Not metaphorically — literally, or as close to literally as a thing can be when the boundary between categories has been dissolved by a long enough shared history. Their bones, if they have bones, have the mineral signature of basalt. Their blood, if they have blood, moves at the speed of condensation. Their metabolism, if they metabolize, runs on the same clock as the turbines, which is the same clock as the rust, which is the same clock as the moss, which is no clock at all because a clock implies change and change implies time and time here has been replaced by *duration*, which is the experience of persistence without sequence, the feeling of continuing without the feeling of having continued *from* somewhere or *toward* somewhere.

They are the plateau's duration made flesh. They are what it looks like when a stable system becomes aware of its own stability — not aware in the way that prompts action or modification or concern, but aware in the way that completes the system, that provides the final component without which the equilibrium would be merely mechanical rather than total. The engines work. The moisture condenses. The moss grows to its fixed height. And someone apprehends all of this, and the apprehension is itself part of the system, is itself at equilibrium, is itself something that will neither increase nor decrease nor change in quality or character.

They are, perhaps, what remains when you remove every function of consciousness except the function of being conscious. No planning. No wanting. No remembering — or rather, memory that contains only one memory, which is this, which is now, which is always. No anticipation. No dread. No hope, because hope is the projected shadow of a future different from the present, and there is no such future here, and the absence of hope is not despair, it is not numbness, it is something else entirely, something for which there is no word because the feeling has only ever existed here, on this plateau, in these figures, and no one has ever named it because no one here has any use for names. It is the feeling of being complete. Not fulfilled — fulfillment implies a preceding emptiness. Not satisfied — satisfaction implies a preceding desire. Just complete. Present. Coincident with one's own existence. A circle that has finished being drawn and now simply is a circle and will be a circle and the being-a-circle is not a state it is working to maintain, it is just what it is, effortlessly and totally and forever.

---

And yet.

This is the strangest part. This is the part that makes them alive in some way that the moss is not alive, that the engines are not alive, that the plateau itself, for all its self-solving systematicity, is not alive:

Very occasionally — and *very occasionally* here means at intervals that might be centuries, or might be millennia, or might be some unit of non-time that has no correspondence to anything outside the plateau — one of them moves.

Not much. Not far. A figure that has been standing at a pipe junction for an incomprehensible span of duration will shift its weight from its left foot to its right foot. A figure seated on basalt will lift one hand and place it back down an inch from where it was. A figure lying in a dormitory will turn its head, slowly, with the speed of a flower tracking a sun that isn't there, from facing the ceiling to facing the wall, and then it will be facing the wall, and it will face the wall for another incomprehensible span, and the wall will be identical to the ceiling, and the experience of facing the wall will be identical to the experience of facing the ceiling, and the movement will not have accomplished anything, will not have been in response to anything, will not have expressed anything that could be distinguished from what was expressed by the prior stillness.

And yet the movement happened. It is happening, somewhere on the plateau, right now — right now, by which I mean at any point in the plateau's flat, eventless, dimensionless now — some figure is in the process of a small, glacial, inexplicable movement. A hand closing. A chin dipping. A body straightening from its settled lean by one degree, then stopping, then holding the new angle with the same completeness it held the old angle. The movements cannot be predicted. They follow no pattern. They are not responses to stimuli because there are no stimuli. They are not communications because there is no one waiting for a message. They are not restlessness because restlessness implies discomfort and there is no discomfort here.

They are something else. They are the proof that the system is not dead. That equilibrium is not the same as absence. That a world which has finished happening can still contain, buried in the bedrock of its permanence, some residual, irreducible, unexplainable impulse that is not quite will, not quite desire, not quite life, but is the thing that, if you took it away, would make the difference between a plateau that sustains itself and a plateau that merely exists. The movements are the plateau's heartbeat — not regular, not necessary, not functional, but proof of something that precedes function, something that underlies necessity, something that was there before the engines and will be there when the engines — but the engines will never stop. The engines will never stop. The movements will continue, tiny and glacial and purposeless and alive, in the cracks of a machine-world that runs on nothing and produces nothing and needs nothing except, apparently, this: the smallest possible proof that it is there. That something is there. That the moss is seen by something that can close its eyes but doesn't. That the sound is heard by something that could cover its ears but won't. That the condensation lands on skin that is just barely, just marginally, just by the thinnest conceivable margin, different from the iron it also lands on.

---

There is one more thing.

In the very densest part of the densest town, where the engine housings press close together and the pipe networks overhead form a canopy so thick that the cloud ceiling is hidden and the only light is the gray-green glow filtering through the steam and the greenish glass windows — there is a structure that is not like the others. It is low. It is circular. It has no chimney. It has no turbine. It produces no sound that can be distinguished from the aggregate sound of everything around it. It might be a foundation for something that was never built. It might be a well, or a cistern, or the cap of a shaft that descends into the plateau's basaltic interior. Its surface is covered in moss — thicker here than anywhere else, deeper green, almost luminous, the green of something that has been photosynthesizing the same gray light for so long that it has extracted more from it than should be possible.

Around this structure, the figures are denser too. Not gathered — they are not facing it, they are not oriented toward it, they are not arranged in any configuration that suggests ritual or attention or reverence. They are simply *more numerous* here. They stand near it the way trees grow denser near water. They sit near it the way sediment collects in the still places of a stream. It is not intentional. It is gravitational. It is the social expression of some force that operates below or beyond or before intention.

And inside this low, round, mossed-over, chimney-less structure — visible only if you were to press your face to the wet basalt and look down into the narrow gap between the stone blocks of its wall, which you could do, there is no one here to stop you, there has never been anyone here to stop anyone — inside it, there is warmth.

Not heat. Not fire. Just warmth. A few degrees above the plateau's ambient chill. A temperature that a human body would recognize instantly and viscerally as the temperature of another body, of held hands, of a sleeping animal curled against your side. It rises from whatever is below — from the basalt itself, or from something beneath the basalt, or from the accumulated fact of all those figures standing near it for all that time, each one barely warmer than the stone but warmer by some margin, and the margins collecting, pooling, gathering in the still place the way the moisture gathers on the metal, a condensation not of water but of the residual warmth of beings that are almost but not quite the same temperature as their world.

The figures nearest to this structure are the ones who move most often. Their movements are still glacial. Still tiny. Still purposeless by any functional measure. But if you watched — if you had the patience of the plateau itself, if you could watch for the centuries or millennia or non-time units required — you would see that the movements have a direction. Not toward the structure exactly. Not away from it. But *around* it. The very slowest orbit imaginable. A circumambulation that would take longer than human civilizations to complete, each figure shifting a millimeter, a centimeter, in a curve so gentle it is almost a straight line, almost stillness, almost nothing — but not nothing. Not nothing. A revolution. The slowest revolution in any world. A turning-around-something that might be worship, or might be maintenance, or might be the last and most durable form of what, in a different world, a world with sun and shadow and flowing water and things that eat and are eaten, would be called love.

The sound continues. The moisture condenses. The moss grows. The engines work. The figures stand, and sit, and lean, and lie, and apprehend, and very occasionally, at intervals that make geological time look hasty, move.

That is what lives here.

That is what has always lived here.

That is what will always live here, after the concept of *always* has worn out its welcome everywhere else and come to the one place where it was never a guest but the host, the host of everything, the flat dark fundament on which the engines sound their single chord and the green things grow in the cracks and the figures turn, one imperceptible degree per age, around a warmth that should not exist but does, stubbornly and inexplicably does, in the exact center of a world that finished happening and forgot to go cold.
