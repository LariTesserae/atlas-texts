# Who Lives Here

---

They arrived in ships that were already old.

Not ancient — not yet — but ships that had been built for a generation prior, refitted, patched, their hulls re-welded in dockyards that no longer exist on whatever world those dockyards once occupied. The ships were heavy-keeled and diesel-hearted, designed for ocean work on calmer seas, and when they first entered the channels between the peaks, the turbulence nearly killed them. Two vessels were lost in the first month. Not to storms — there are no storms here, not in the dramatic sense, no hurricanes or typhoons — but to the confused, chaotic wave action that occurs where currents collide between mountain islands. A standing wave caught one ship broadside in a narrow passage and held it, vibrating, until the hull seams opened. The other was driven onto a submerged ridge that its charts said should have been thirty meters deeper. The charts were old. The water was still rising.

The people who survived those first losses learned something that has defined every generation since: *the maps are always wrong by the time you need them.*

---

They are called, by themselves, something that translates roughly as **the Bolters**.

Not as in those who flee — though they did flee, once, from somewhere — but as in those who bolt things down. Those who drive steel into stone. It is an identity rooted in the fundamental act of their survival: the anchoring of impermanent things to a world that dissolves everything impermanent. To bolt is to insist. To bolt is to say *this will stay* in a place where nothing stays. The name carries a note of defiance that has long since curdled into something more complicated — pride and grief and stubbornness and the private, bone-deep awareness that every bolt they drive will eventually fail.

They number, across the entire archipelago, perhaps eleven thousand. Perhaps fewer. No one has conducted a census in living memory. The distances between inhabited peaks are too great, the crossings too dangerous, the communication too unreliable. Each settlement knows its own count and has rough estimates of its neighbors, and beyond that, there are rumors — of peaks farther out where small groups still cling to existence, of signal towers that occasionally flicker to life in patterns that might be language, of boats spotted in distant channels that no known settlement claims.

Eleven thousand, then. Give or take the ones nobody can confirm.

---

A settlement — a typical one, though no two are exactly alike — occupies the upper flanks of a summit dome, arranged in a rough vertical band between the high ridgeline where the bird colonies nest and the wave zone where the water chews at the rock. The habitable band is narrow. Maybe two hundred vertical meters on a good peak, less on an eroded one. Within this band, everything that sustains human life must be compressed: shelter, cultivation, storage, fabrication, docking.

The shelters are the most immediately visible structures. They are built from what is available: gneiss blocks cut or pried from the mountain itself, fitted together without mortar in the old style — dry-stone construction, the blocks shaped to interlock, the walls thick enough to bear the constant wind. The roofs are the critical engineering challenge. Wood does not exist here; there are no trees, have never been trees in any living or recorded memory. Metal is precious, finite, recycled from the old industrial structures and from whatever was salvaged from the original ships. So the roofs are made of layered vegetation mats — the spongy moss-analogue, harvested in thick slabs and laid over frameworks of bent and lashed shrub-wood, the waxy branches of the dense crevice shrubs that are the closest thing to timber this world provides. These roofs are waterproof not by repelling water but by absorbing it so completely that they reach a saturation equilibrium — the rain passes into the mat, fills it, and then simply cannot push through to the interior because the mat is already holding all the water it can hold. The principle is counterintuitive. You stay dry by surrounding yourself with something that is already as wet as it can get. The interiors of these shelters, as a result, are perpetually damp but not dripping. The air inside smells of wet stone and green growth and the faintly sweet, decomposing-vegetable scent of the moss. Everything inside that can corrode has been oiled or waxed or wrapped in the waxy leaves of the crevice shrubs. Clothing, tools, food stores — all of it is managed against moisture as the primary adversary of daily existence.

The shelters cluster in groups of eight to fifteen, arranged not in rows or grids but in organic, terrain-following constellations that reflect the shape of the rock beneath them. Where a ledge is wide enough, three or four shelters share it. Where a crevice provides wind-shelter, shelters tuck into its flanks. Where an old quartz vein creates a raised rib of harder stone, shelters anchor to it like limpets to a pier. From a distance, a settlement looks less like a village and more like a natural feature — a crust of regular shapes clinging to the mountain, the same blue-gray as the rock, the green roofs blending with the surrounding vegetation mats, only the occasional glint of salvaged metal or the pale line of a footpath giving away the presence of intent.

---

The footpaths are important. They are the circulatory system of each settlement, and they are maintained with an attention that borders on ritual.

They are cut into the moss mats — not paved, because there is nothing to pave with, but worn and re-worn by daily passage, the vegetation trimmed back with short, heavy-bladed tools that every adult carries. Where the paths cross bare rock, the passage of generations has polished the gneiss to a dark sheen, and in wet conditions (which is to say: always) these sections are dangerously slick. Rope handlines are anchored along the steepest stretches, the ropes braided from the fibrous inner bark of the crevice shrubs — a material that is coarse, strong when fresh, and degrades in about eighteen months, necessitating constant replacement. The making and replacing of rope is one of the defining labors of life here. Children learn to braid it before they learn to swim, and they learn to swim very young.

The paths run vertically more than horizontally, connecting the different elevation zones of the settlement: the high nesting grounds, the mid-slope shelters, the cultivation terraces, the dock platforms at the waterline. Moving through a settlement means climbing and descending constantly, and the people here have bodies shaped by this — strong legs, wide feet with flexible toes that grip wet rock, pronounced calf and thigh muscles, a low center of gravity. They move on the paths with a rolling, deliberate gait, each foot placed with full attention, weight transferred smoothly. Watching them navigate a steep, rain-slicked path in a twenty-knot wind is like watching something that has been practiced ten thousand times — which it has, daily, by every person, since they could walk.

---

The cultivation terraces are where the relationship between these people and their world becomes most legible.

They do not farm in any sense a lowland agrarian would recognize. They tend. They manage. They negotiate with what the mountain already produces and try, carefully, to bend it slightly in their favor.

The primary cultivated organism is the moss-analogue itself. On the terraces — shallow, stepped platforms carved or worn into the mid-slope rock, edged with low stone walls to retain what little soil accumulates — the Bolters maintain dense beds of selected moss strains, bred (over how many generations? no one remembers the beginning) for nutritional density. The moss is not eaten directly. It is harvested, pressed to extract its water (which is saved — fresh water is abundant here but clean, filtered water is another matter), and then the compressed mass is fermented in stone-lined pits sealed with waxy leaves. The fermentation process takes roughly twenty days and produces a dense, sour, protein-rich paste that is the caloric foundation of the diet. It tastes, to an outsider, approximately like what you'd expect: pungent, vegetal, with an acidic bite and an aftertaste of minerals. To the Bolters, it is simply food. It is *the* food, the thing everything else is measured against. A good fermentation batch is a cause for quiet satisfaction. A failed batch — contaminated by the wrong microorganisms, of which there are many in this environment — is a crisis that ripples through the settlement for weeks.

The crevice shrubs provide the other vegetable component: their young leaves, their flowers (in season), and the small, hard, seed-bearing fruits they produce in late summer. The fruits are astringent and sour, rich in ascorbic acid, and eaten raw or crushed into the moss-paste to vary the flavor. The flowers are dried — a difficult process in a world where nothing dries easily — and steeped in hot water for a thin, fragrant tea that is the closest thing to a luxury the Bolters possess. Tea-making is a social ritual. It happens in the shelters, in the brief rest periods between work shifts, and it is the context for almost all important conversation: negotiation, courtship, grievance, planning.

Protein comes from the sea. This is where the real labor is.

---

The fishers are not a class or a caste but a role that rotates through the settlement's able-bodied adults on a schedule that is managed with extraordinary care, because fishing here is the most dangerous regular activity and the risk must be distributed. No one fishes every day. No one is excused from fishing unless they are injured, pregnant, or too old to swim.

The boats are small — four or five meters, open-hulled, built from frames of metal salvage and shrub-wood, skinned with layers of the moss-analogue that have been compressed and treated with fish oil until they achieve a stiff, leathery waterproofness. They look, frankly, like they should not float. They are ugly, asymmetrical, patched and re-patched, each one a palimpsest of repairs. But they are extraordinarily seaworthy in the specific conditions of this world — they flex with the confused wave action rather than fighting it, they ship water constantly but are bailed just as constantly, and their shallow draft allows them to navigate the rock shelves and submerged ridges that would gut a deeper-keeled vessel.

Fishing is done with nets and with hand-lines, and increasingly — over the last few generations — with woven traps lowered into the flooded canyons on long ropes of braided shrub-fiber. The traps are the most productive method, but they require a rope long enough to reach the canyon floors (hundreds of meters in some cases) and the labor to haul them back up by hand. Trap-hauling is a communal effort. Six or eight people on a ledge above the water, pulling hand over hand, the rope coming up black with depth-water and streaming, the trap at the end heavy with crustaceans and slow, pale fish from the deep canyons. The catch is processed immediately — cleaned, gutted, the flesh separated and either eaten fresh or packed in salt.

Salt. Salt is the other critical resource. The ocean provides it, but the ocean here is cold, and evaporation-based salt production is agonizingly slow in a world of constant rain and low temperatures. The Bolters produce salt by boiling seawater in metal vessels over fires fueled by dried (again: painstakingly dried, sheltered under multiple layers of moss-roof, weeks of patient effort to drive moisture from organic material in a world that is nothing but moisture) vegetation and fish oil. The process is fuel-intensive, time-consuming, and never produces enough. Salt is the currency of inter-settlement trade. A sealed stone jar of clean, dry salt is worth more than almost anything else that can be offered.

---

And now the question of who these people *are*, not in the material sense of what they eat and where they sleep, but in the deeper sense — what it is like to be a consciousness in this world.

They are loud. They have to be. The waterfall-roar, the wind, the wave-slap, the rain — to live here is to live inside a sound so vast it has its own geography, louder in the open channels between peaks, softer in the lee of a summit, a wall of white noise that fluctuates but never stops. The Bolters' speech has evolved in response: it is pitched to cut, like the bird-calls, with hard consonants and carrying vowels, spoken from the chest at a volume that an outsider would interpret as shouting. They do not whisper. The concept exists — they have a word for it — but it describes something done only in the most intimate circumstances, mouth against ear, in the relative quiet of a sealed shelter during a lull, and it carries connotations of extreme closeness, vulnerability, a willingness to let yourself be unheard by the world. To whisper to someone is a declaration. It means: *I will make myself quiet for you, even though quiet is the most unnatural thing I can do.*

Their language is also heavily gestural. Not a formal sign language, but a dense layer of hand signals, body postures, and facial expressions that carry meaning at distances where the voice cannot reach. A fisher in a boat fifty meters offshore can communicate the size and location of a catch, request assistance, warn of a current change, or signal distress using only their arms and the angle of their body. These signals are taught to children alongside speech, as a parallel grammar, and the most fluent signalers are valued the way a particularly articulate speaker might be valued in a quieter world — they can say more, faster, across greater distance, with nuances that less skilled signalers miss.

---

Their relationship with the water is the thing that defines them most deeply, and it is not simple.

They depend on it absolutely. It feeds them, provides their salt, carries their boats, grows the underwater meadows that anchor the entire food web. They drink it (filtered through moss beds, collected in stone cisterns). They wash in it. Their children play in it. Their dead are given to it.

And it is killing them. It is killing them slowly, the way it is killing the mountains — not through malice or even indifference, but through the same patient, geological insistence that has been at work for billions of years. The water is still rising. Not fast. Not dramatically. But measurably, over generations — a centimeter here, a centimeter there, the wave zone creeping upward, the dock platforms that a grandparent built now awash at high tide, the lower cultivation terraces abandoned, the habitable band narrowing. Every settlement has stories of lower shelters that were given up. Every settlement has a shelf of rock that is pointed to and spoken of: *that was the old dock. That was where the processing station stood. That was where the southern path began.* Now underwater. Now gone.

This creeping loss is the central psychic fact of Bolter existence. It is not acute — it does not produce panic or urgency, because it moves too slowly for that. It produces instead a kind of bone-deep, inherited melancholy that colors everything without dominating anything. The Bolters are not a sad people. They laugh — loudly, because they do everything loudly. They celebrate. They compete in boat races through the channels between peaks, reckless and joyful, spray flying. They tell long, elaborate stories during the tea-ritual, stories that are competitive in their embellishment, each teller trying to outdo the last. They fall in love with the terrifying intensity of people who know that the place they love in will not last. But beneath all of it, there is a knowledge that sits like cold water in the bones: *we are on the way down. We have been on the way down since before we arrived. The mountains were on the way down before the mountains existed.*

---

They do not have a religion, exactly, but they have a practice that functions like one.

It is called — the word is difficult to translate — something like **the Inventory**. Once per year, at a time determined by the position of certain stars (visible only rarely, through breaks in the cloud cover, and the appearance of the right stars on the right night is itself treated as a minor miracle and an occasion for tea), the settlement gathers and collectively recites everything that has been lost to the water since the last Inventory. Not just physical things — not just the lower shelter, the old dock, the southern path — but also skills, stories, songs, names. A fishing ground that has become too deep. A variety of moss that grew on a now-submerged shelf. The name of the peak that used to be visible to the southwest and has since slipped below the waves. A person who was taken by the sea — drowned, lost in a squall, pulled from a boat by a wave.

The Inventory is recited in a specific cadence, almost sung, each item introduced with a formulaic phrase that translates roughly as: *The water took [this thing] and we say its name so the water knows we noticed.* The recitation can take hours. In a bad year, it is very long. The community stands together on the highest accessible point of the settlement, in the wind and rain, facing the ocean, and they say every name. The children stand with the adults. Even the youngest children are there, held in arms, listening to the cadence before they understand the words. They will grow up knowing the rhythm of loss before they understand what has been lost.

After the Inventory, there is a second, shorter recitation: everything that has been *gained*. A new shelter built. A new terrace established. A child born. A new fishing ground discovered. A signal received from a distant peak. The gains are always fewer than the losses. Everyone knows this. The asymmetry is not hidden or softened. It is the point. The practice does not exist to create hope. It exists to create *accuracy* — an unflinching, communal awareness of the exact state of things. The Bolters believe (if "believe" is the right word for something so deeply practical) that the worst thing you can do is lose track of what you've lost. That if you stop counting, you stop being able to respond. That the water is patient enough to outlast denial, and the only defense is to be as patient and as honest as the water.

---

The inter-settlement relationships are complex and shaped by geography.

Some peaks are close enough — a kilometer or two of open water — that regular contact is possible. Boats cross between them in fair conditions, carrying trade goods (salt, metal salvage, dried fish, rope, seeds of productive moss strains), marriage partners, news, and the particular, invaluable commodity of *different perspectives on the same problems*. These close-neighbor settlements develop shared cultures, aligned calendars, mutual aid agreements. When a settlement loses a critical terrace or a dock, its neighbors send boats with food and labor. This is not charity; it is insurance. Everyone knows they will need the same help eventually.

More distant peaks — ten, twenty, fifty kilometers across open ocean — are reached only by the long-voyage boats, larger vessels crewed by eight to twelve people, built from the best salvaged metal and the most carefully treated moss-skin, provisioned for weeks at sea. These voyages are undertaken once or twice a year, in the brief periods when the wind patterns are least murderous, and they are the closest thing the Bolters have to an epic tradition. The long-voyagers are respected and slightly feared. They have seen things — other settlements with different practices, peaks at various stages of submergence, ruins of the old industrial infrastructure in states of decay that offer clues about how long ago the builders came and went. They bring back stories that reshape the community's understanding of its own position in the archipelago. They also bring back, sometimes, people — refugees from settlements that have finally gone under, their peak lost, their community dissolved into boats and hope and the memory of everything the Inventory tried to preserve.

These refugees are absorbed. Always. Without exception. It is the deepest law the Bolters have, deeper than any practical consideration: *you do not turn away people whose mountain has drowned.* The law exists because everyone knows, with that same bone-deep certainty, that someday they will be the ones in the boats.

---

The old structures — the processing stations, the docks, the signal towers — are treated with a particular kind of attention that is neither reverence nor indifference.

The Bolters did not build them. They know this. The builders were someone else, some earlier group that came to these mountains with engines and steel and a plan that did not account for deep time. The Bolters' own origin story is fragmented and debated — some settlements hold that they are the descendants of the builders themselves, diminished by centuries of attrition; others hold that they arrived later, separately, and found the ruins already old. The truth is probably somewhere in between and has been so thoroughly obscured by time that it no longer matters in any practical sense. What matters is the metal.

The old structures are mined. Carefully, methodically, with a sustainable-harvest ethic born of the knowledge that there will never be more metal than there is right now. A settlement will spend months extracting a single steel beam from a ruin, cutting it with chisels and cold hammers (there is no fuel to spare for cutting torches, if cutting torches ever existed here), carrying the pieces up the mountain on human backs, and re-forging them over fish-oil fires into the tools and fasteners and boat-fittings that daily life requires. The metallurgists — they do not call themselves that; the word translates more accurately as **the people who remember what metal wants to be** — are the most skilled artisans in any settlement. They can assess an alloy by the sound it makes when struck. They know which old structures yield steel that can be re-worked and which yield steel so corroded it crumbles. They maintain, in memory and in quiet, meticulous oral tradition, a catalogue of every significant ruin in their settlement's range and its current state of salvageability.

The signal towers, though, are treated differently. They are not mined. Even when a settlement is desperate for metal, the signal towers are left alone. This is not rational — the lattice steel in a tower is high-quality, exactly the kind of alloy the metallurgists value most. But there is a feeling, not quite articulated, not quite a rule, more like a shared flinch — that the towers are still *listening*. That they might yet receive something. That to dismantle them would be to close a door that might, impossibly, still open. So the towers stand, rusting, on their quartz anchors, their empty instrument housings facing the sky like cupped hands waiting to be filled. And sometimes, during the Inventory, someone will add: *the tower on the north peak is still standing. The tower on the east ridge is still standing.* And this is counted among the gains.

---

The children.

The children are loud and fearless and wet. They are wet at all times, because there is no state of dryness available to them, and they have never known anything else, and so the concept of "wet" as a distinct condition does not exist in their experience — there is only the world, and the world is water. They swim before they walk with confidence. Not shark-sleek competitive swimming but a practical, energy-efficient, head-up paddle that gets them from rock to rock and shelter to shore and back, their small bodies adapted to the cold water in ways that would alarm a physician from a drier world — subcutaneous fat deposits, efficient peripheral vasoconstriction, a metabolic tolerance for sustained immersion. They play in the tidal pools that form on the lower rock shelves, catching amphipods and small fish with their hands, building tiny dams of pebbles that the waves destroy and they rebuild and the waves destroy and they rebuild, rehearsing without knowing it the central activity of their adult lives.

They are educated communally. Not in a school — there is no structure designated for this — but in the fabric of daily work, attached to adults who are doing things and explaining things and expecting the children to do them too, in simplified form, as soon as they are able. A child of six can braid rope. A child of eight can identify every edible organism in their settlement's range. A child of ten can crew a fishing boat in moderate conditions. A child of twelve can participate in the Inventory and is expected to remember everything recited. The education is not gentle, exactly — there is no margin for incompetence in this world — but it is patient, and it is collective, and it carries an undertone of fierce, unsentimental love: *we are teaching you this because you will need it. We are teaching you this because someday we will be gone and you will be the ones who remember.*

---

And the old ones. The elders.

In a world this physically demanding, old age is not a given. Those who reach it have survived decades of fishing in turbulent water, climbing slick paths in gale-force wind, hauling trap-ropes, cutting stone, enduring cold that never relents. Their bodies show it: swollen joints, hands permanently curled from years of gripping rope and rock, skin thickened and weathered to a texture not unlike the gneiss itself. They move slowly. They cannot fish or climb as they once did. In a purely utilitarian calculus, they are a burden.

They are not treated as a burden. They are treated as the Inventory made flesh.

The elders remember. That is their function, their labor, their contribution. They remember which fishing grounds were productive twenty years ago and are now too deep. They remember the last time a signal was received from the eastern peaks. They remember the exact wording of the Inventory from their childhood, which included losses that the current generation has never seen. They remember the names of peaks that are now fully submerged — peaks that, to anyone under forty, are simply not there, are open water, are nothing. The elders insist they were something. They sit in the shelters during tea-time and they say: *there was a mountain there. It was called [this]. It had [these] terraces and [this many] shelters and the fishing on its eastern shelf was the best in the range. The water took it in my mother's time.* And the younger people listen, and nod, and add it to their own internal map of a world that extends, through memory, below the waterline.

This is the function of the Inventory writ large. The elders are living Inventories, carrying in their minds a version of the archipelago that is bigger than the current one — an archipelago that includes the ghosts of drowned peaks, the echoes of lost settlements, the accumulated knowledge of generations who watched the water rise and said: *we noticed. We say its name.*

---

There is one more thing.

On the clearest days — rare days, when a break in the cloud cover aligns with a lull in the rain and the light suddenly sharpens, going from flat diffusion to something almost bright — you can see, from the highest ridgelines, a great distance across the ocean. And on those days, the long-voyagers say, if you look in the right direction, you can see something at the extreme edge of vision: a dark shape on the horizon. Not a mountain — too low, too flat, too regular. Something else.

No one has ever reached it. The distance is too great, the open ocean crossing too dangerous, the winds and currents between here and there too chaotic. Boats that have tried have turned back or have not returned. But the shape is there. It appears on clear days. It has appeared on clear days for as long as anyone can remember, and the elders say their elders spoke of it too.

Some Bolters believe it is another island — a lower one, a remnant of a different part of the drowned continent, possibly inhabited, possibly not. Some believe it is the remains of something built — something so large that even this ocean could not swallow it completely. Some believe it is an illusion, a trick of refraction and distance and the desperate human need to see something solid on the horizon. Some believe it does not matter what it is, because whatever it is, it is also drowning, at the same rate, under the same patient rain, and to reach it would only be to find another version of the same problem.

But they look. On the clear days, they climb to the high ridgelines and they look. And sometimes a child, held up by an elder, will be pointed toward it and told: *that is there. We don't know what it is. We say it is there so it knows we noticed.*

And then the clouds close in, and the rain resumes, and the sound of the waterfalls fills the basin between the peaks again, and the Bolters go back to the work of living on mountains that are still, imperceptibly, slipping away beneath them. They braid rope. They tend moss. They haul traps from the deep canyons. They bolt steel to quartz and expect it to hold for a while.

Not forever. They have never expected forever.

Just: a while longer. Just: long enough to say every name.
