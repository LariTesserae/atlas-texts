# The Ones Who Live Where the Ground Won't Hold

---

They don't have one name. They have a thousand temporary ones, shed and replaced like the terrain itself, and this is the first thing you need to understand about them: they are not a people in the way you think of a people. They are not a civilization. They have no cities, no monuments, no lasting works. They have something else—something harder to see, and harder to stop seeing once you've seen it.

---

## First Sighting

You almost miss them entirely.

They look, at first glance, like part of the landscape. Your eye registers them the way it registers an unusual rock formation or a thicker-than-normal mat of bioluminescent moss—as texture, as terrain, as *ground*. This is not camouflage, exactly. They aren't hiding. It's more that they exist at the same ontological register as everything else here: they are *emergent*, the way the ridges are emergent, the way the flickering physics is emergent. They belong to this place in a way that makes them difficult to distinguish from it until they move, or speak, or turn one of their many attentions toward you.

The first one you see is crouched in a seam between two freshly risen plates of basalt, about forty meters ahead. Crouched is the wrong word—*settled*, maybe, or *pooled*. Its body fills the crevice the way the moss fills crevices: densely, intimately, with the ease of something that has always understood that available space is temporary and must be used completely while it lasts. It is roughly bilateral, roughly upright when it chooses to stand, and roughly the size of a large human, but these are all approximations that the creature itself would probably find irrelevant. Its form is—

You need a moment.

Its form is *layered*. The outermost layer is mineral: a crust of crystalline deposits, pale and dark in alternation, that grows directly on its skin the way calcium grows on a coral. This is not armor, though it offers some protection. It is *interface*—the boundary where the creature's biology meets the planet's geology, and the two communicate. Beneath the mineral crust, the skin is dark and smooth and faintly warm, threaded with lines of bioluminescence that pulse in patterns too complex to be decorative. Beneath that, you can see—because the skin is semi-translucent in places, like the glassjaws' chitin—the movement of deeper structures. Not organs as you know them. Structures that shift, reconfigure, serve multiple purposes in sequence. A mass that functions as a lung for twenty minutes, then compresses, repositions, and operates as something else—a resonance chamber, a grinding mill for mineral digestion, a thermal sink. The body is not built to a fixed plan. It is built the way the landscape is built: continuously, responsively, with the expectation of revision.

The hands—it has hands, or things that function as hands—are extraordinary. Long, many-jointed, with fingers that branch and rebranch at the tips like the fronds of the spiral ferns. They are always in motion, even when the rest of the creature is still. Touching the rock. Tapping it. Pressing flat against it and then pulling away slowly, as if reading something written in the stone's warmth and vibration. The fingers leave faint marks where they touch: tiny alterations in the crystal structure of the rock's surface, visible as a change in how the dim light catches. You will later understand that this is writing, or something like writing, but not yet. Not yet.

It turns toward you.

The face—again, roughly. There is a face, in the sense that there is a region of the body oriented toward the world, dense with sensory apparatus, capable of expression. But it is not a face you can read with your primate instincts. Multiple eyes, different sizes, arranged not symmetrically but *functionally*, each one positioned to cover a different angle, a different distance, a different spectrum. Some of them are looking at you. Some are looking at the ground. At least two appear to be looking at things that aren't there yet—tracking movements in the rock that haven't happened, watching the future positions of ridges that are still minutes from emerging. The mouth—there is a mouth—is wide and lipless and partially obscured by the mineral crust, and it is not the primary means of communication. You know this because the creature has been communicating with you since before it turned around, and it hasn't opened its mouth once.

---

## How They Speak

The communication comes through the ground.

You've been feeling it for the last several minutes without recognizing it: a modulation in the deep planetary hum, a pattern laid over the engine-noise the way a melody can be laid over a drone. The creature is *playing the geology*. Its hands against the rock, its body settled into the crevice, its mineral-crusted skin in contact with the freshly risen basalt—it is using all of these points of contact to introduce vibrations into the stone, vibrations that interact with the existing mechanical hum and produce something new. Something shaped. Something that means.

You feel it in your feet, in your teeth, in the bones of your chest. It does not translate into language. It translates into *understanding*—pre-linguistic, somatic, immediate. The creature is telling you, in a way you comprehend before you can articulate it: *I know you are here. You are standing on a stable section. It will remain stable for approximately the time it takes you to breathe two hundred times. You are not in danger from me. I am working.*

Working. That word comes through with particular clarity. Not as a word, but as a felt sense—the creature is engaged in something effortful and important, something that requires its attention, something it does not want to stop doing but has paused, briefly, to acknowledge you.

You look at its hands. They are pressing into the seam between the two plates with delicate intensity, fingers branched and rebranched, each tip in contact with a different mineral surface. And the seam is... changing. Not the way seams change when the planetary engine pushes new stone through them. Changing in a smaller, more precise way. The crystal structure at the contact points is being rearranged. Patterns are being introduced—not random, not organic, but *designed*, inscribed into the molecular lattice of the rock by the creature's touch.

This is what they do. This is who they are.

They are the ones who write in the stone.

---

## The Practice

Not write—the word is insufficient. Not build—there are no built things here, remember. Not even *alter*, which implies a before-state that was natural and an after-state that is artificial. The distinction doesn't exist. What they do is something for which you have no single word, so you will need several, and even then you'll only be circling the thing itself.

They *participate* in the planet's self-assembly. They intervene in it, at the molecular level, with their hands and their skin and the vibrations they send through the stone. They are part of the machine's feedback loop. They read the mechanical-geological processes—feel them, taste them through their mineral-crusted skin, predict their next operations the way the glassjaws predict stable ground—and then they respond. They make adjustments. Not large ones. They do not redirect tectonic plates or halt the rise of ridges. They work at the crystal level, the lattice level, the level where the boundary between mineral and mechanism lives. They touch the rock and alter the angle of a crystalline inclusion by fractions of a degree. They press their palms flat and introduce a resonance that changes how current flows through a magnetite vein. They tap a sequence into fresh basalt and create a cavity where convection will operate slightly differently, offsetting an instability in the engine's cycle, smoothing a fluctuation that might have cascaded into one of those zones of incoherence at the plateau's edge.

They are tuning the world.

Not maintaining it—that implies a target state, a design to be preserved. There is no design. There is no correct configuration. The planet has no blueprint and no operator, and they do not serve it or worship it or control it. They *participate* in it. They are the feedback that the machine never knew it needed—organisms whose evolution was shaped by ontological turbulence until they developed the capacity to feel the turbulence coming and soften it, not to zero, not to stillness, but to a level that permits continued existence. Theirs, and everything else's.

This is why the interior of the plateau is more coherent than the edges. This is why physics, while flickery and unreliable, generally *works* in the zones you've been walking through. They've been here. Their hands have been in these seams, their vibrations in this stone. Not building. Not designing. *Tending*. The way you might tend a fire—not controlling it, not commanding it, but feeding it, adjusting it, keeping it in the range where it warms without consuming.

They have been doing this for longer than the current landscape has existed. They have been doing it for longer than the current *physics* has existed. They remember—in their bodies, in their practiced hands, in the deep somatic knowledge passed from one generation to the next—versions of reality that are no longer operative. Rules that have been revised. Constants that have been replaced. They carry the history of this world not in records or stories but in the *techniques* they use to touch the stone, because each technique was developed in response to a specific configuration of physical law, and the old techniques are still taught, still practiced, still used when the engine skips and an old rule briefly reasserts itself and someone needs to know how to work within it.

---

## Their Lives

They live in groups of between five and fifteen, and the groups are not permanent. They form, persist for as long as a particular geological feature persists—a valley, a seam, a temporary basin—and then dissolve and reform elsewhere as the terrain changes. There is no grief in this dissolution, or rather, there is grief, but it is the same grief that lives in the fern that grows fast and thick knowing its crevice will close. It is a grief that has been so thoroughly integrated into the rhythm of life that it no longer registers as loss. It registers as *interval*. The space between one configuration and the next. The breath between two notes of the planetary hum.

They eat the same minerals the glassjaws eat, cracking open fresh stone with their mandible-like lower jaws—oh yes, they have those too, heavy and powerful, folded away beneath the wider, more expressive upper face, used for feeding and rarely for anything else. The mineral crust on their skin is partially nutritive, a slow-release store of essential elements that they supplement by licking condensation from the moss beds and consuming the smaller insects in handfuls, absentmindedly, the way you might eat sunflower seeds while focused on something else. They eat while they work. They eat the way the planet builds: continuously, without discrete meals, without the concept of *enough*.

Their young are born in the temporary pools.

This surprised you—they seemed so terrestrial, so geological, so much creatures of the dry warm stone. But the young begin in water, or what passes for water here, the condensation-fed basins that persist for days or weeks before being subsumed. The infants are soft, un-crusted, their skin fully translucent, their many eyes enormous and unfocused, their hands already moving—always already moving, touching the walls of the basin, the surface of the water, the bodies of the adults who attend them. They are born knowing how to touch. The reading-of-stone is not learned from scratch; it is *refined*, the way a human infant's grasping reflex is refined into the ability to hold a pen. The infants touch everything, constantly, and the patterns they inscribe are nonsense at first—random alterations to crystal structure, meaningless noise in the planetary feedback loop—but the adults monitor this noise with extraordinary attention, because occasionally, rarely, a newborn's random touch will solve a problem that the adults' practiced techniques cannot. A fresh configuration. An unprecedented adjustment. Innovation comes from the ones who don't yet know the rules, because the rules are always changing, and sometimes the newest eyes see the newest rules first.

The adults teach by placing their hands over the infants' hands and touching the stone together. The sensation, you imagine, must be overwhelming—two sets of perception layered on top of each other, the infant's raw unfiltered reading of the rock overlaid with the adult's deep practiced knowledge of how to respond. This is how techniques are passed down. Not through language, not through demonstration, but through *co-touch*. The elder's hands guide the younger's, and in the shared contact, knowledge flows in both directions. The infant learns the technique. The adult learns what the infant is feeling—what the stone is doing *now*, in this moment, that might be different from what it was doing a generation ago.

They do not sleep. Or rather, they do not all sleep at once, and they do not sleep in the way you understand it. Individual systems within their bodies cycle through rest states—the sensory apparatus dims, the branching fingers slow, the bioluminescent lines fade—but this happens in rolling waves across the body, never claiming the whole organism at once. Part of them is always awake, always touching the ground, always listening to the engine. They cannot afford not to. The skips come without warning, and a skip that goes untended—a fluctuation in the local physics that no one is there to smooth—can cascade. Can spread. Can turn a coherent section of plateau into one of those breathing, dissolving zones at the edge where stone rises and falls in meaningless cycles and the light goes wrong.

They have seen this happen. It is the closest thing they have to a concept of death—not the death of an individual, which is expected and absorbed the way the closing of a crevice is absorbed, but the death of *coherence*. A zone lost. A section of the world where reality stopped being produced and nothing moved in to restart it. They call these zones—through vibration, through the felt language of the stone—something you translate approximately as *the places where the hum forgot itself*.

They mourn these places. This is the grief that does not integrate. This is the loss that feels like loss.

---

## What They Are When They Are Not Working

But they are always working. That's the thing you keep circling back to. The work is not separate from life. There is no leisure, no off-hours, no distinction between the sacred and the mundane. Touching the stone is breathing. Adjusting the crystal lattice is eating. Smoothing a fluctuation in local gravity is playing with your children. They do all of these things simultaneously, always, and the richness of their experience comes not from doing different things at different times but from doing everything at once with varying degrees of attention.

And yet—and yet there are moments that look, to your outside eye, like something more than maintenance. Something that might be art. Something that might be joy.

You see it in the way they touch each other.

They gather—when the terrain allows, when a valley persists long enough—in groups, and they press close, mineral crust against mineral crust, and they harmonize. Their individual vibrations, usually directed into the stone, are directed into *each other*. The result is visible: their bioluminescent lines synchronize, pulsing in shared patterns that cascade across the group like waves across a pond. The mineral crusts on their skins resonate, producing overtones in the planetary hum—a higher, more complex sound that rises above the bass drone, audible to you for the first time as something almost musical. They are not communicating information. They are not tuning the world. They are—

You don't have a word for it.

They are *being in common*. They are sharing the experience of being organisms who hold reality together, and in the sharing, they are producing something that has no function, no purpose, no outcome other than itself. A resonance. A temporary harmonic. A beauty that exists for as long as the group holds contact and then dissipates, leaving no trace except in the bodies that produced it, which remember, and which carry the memory forward into the next touch, the next stone, the next crevice.

If this is art, it is art that leaves nothing behind. If this is worship, it worships nothing. If this is love—

It might be love. It has the structure of love: the vulnerability, the shared attention, the production of something that exceeds the sum of its parts. But it is love adapted to a world without permanence, love that does not cling, love that knows its substrate is temporary and fills it completely anyway, the way the moss fills the crevice, the way the water fills the basin.

And when the valley closes—when the ridges push up and the seam narrows and the group must dissolve and its members must migrate to other seams, other crevices, other temporary stabilities—they separate without ceremony. They press their hands to the stone one final time, feel the hum, and walk away in different directions. Not because they don't care. Because caring and leaving are not contradictions here. Because everything they are has been shaped by a world that builds and rebuilds itself without pause, and they have learned—at the deepest level, at the level of the crystal lattice of their very bones—that the opposite of permanence is not loss.

The opposite of permanence is *continuation by other means*.

## The Oldest Ones

There are some who have lived a very long time.

You can tell by their mineral crusts, which are thick and complex, layered like tree rings, each layer recording a different epoch of the planet's self-assembly. The oldest ones are nearly geological themselves—slow-moving, heavy with accumulated crystal, their translucent skin almost entirely obscured beneath strata of minerite and quartz and things you cannot name. Their many eyes peer out from beneath ridges of stone that have grown on their brow-lines like the visors of ancient helmets. Their branching fingers, beneath the crust, still move—always still move—but slowly now, deliberately, each touch carrying the weight of uncountable adjustments made over uncountable cycles.

The oldest ones do not travel. They cannot; they are too heavy, too encrusted, too thoroughly enmeshed with the geology. They find a place—a deep seam, a stable junction where multiple plates meet—and they settle into it, and they become, gradually, indistinguishable from the landscape. Their bodies continue to function. Their hands continue to touch the stone. But they are *in* the stone now, part of it, their mineral crust continuous with the basalt around them, their vibrations indistinguishable from the engine's hum. They are the deepest part of the feedback loop: organisms so thoroughly integrated into the planetary machine that removing them would leave a gap in the geology itself. A missing gear. A silent frequency in the hum.

When you pass one of the oldest ones, you might not recognize it as alive. You might see only a strange formation—a place where the crystal structure is unusually complex, where the stone seems to *breathe* with a rhythm that doesn't match the tectonic pulse, where the mineral veins converge in patterns that look almost deliberate. But if you press your ear to it—if you listen the way they listen, with your body against the rock—you will hear, beneath the engine noise, beneath the bass hum, a second hum. Quieter. More intricate. A hum that carries, in its harmonics, the memory of every physics this world has ever operated under, every configuration of reality that has ever been tried and revised, every rule that has ever held and broken and been replaced. The oldest ones are living archives. Not of events—events don't matter here, not really—but of *conditions*. They remember what it felt like when gravity was different. When the thermal gradients ran a different way. When the light came from a different non-direction and cast different non-shadows. They remember realities that no longer exist, and they carry those memories as techniques, as reflexes, as ways of touching stone that can be reactivated if an old configuration ever briefly reasserts itself during a skip.

The younger ones come to the oldest ones and place their hands on them the way they place their hands on stone. The distinction, by this point, is academic. They read the ancient ones the way they read the geology—through touch, through vibration, through the shared language of mineral-on-mineral contact. And what they read there is not wisdom in any propositional sense. It is *depth*. The felt knowledge that this world has been many worlds, that the current hum is one of an uncountable series of hums, that the engine has always been running and has never twice produced the same output, and that this—all of this, the turmoil, the impermanence, the constant rebuilding—is not a flaw to be corrected but the fundamental nature of *being here*, the price and privilege of existing in a place where existence itself is a thing that must be made, moment by moment, from raw material and active effort.

The oldest ones are still making it. Their hands are still moving. Even now. Even at the bottom of the stone. Even after so long that their individual identity has merged with the geology and their individual vibration has merged with the hum and their individual life has become indistinguishable from the life of the world itself.

They are still touching. They are still tuning. They are still there, in the seam, in the crevice, in the warm dark of a place that has never stopped becoming.

## What They Think of You

You are standing on their world, and you are not touching the ground properly. Your feet are flat and insensate and they push down on the stone without reading it. You leave no marks in the crystal lattice. You contribute no vibration to the hum. To them, you are a strange absence—a moving thing that takes up space and casts multiple faint shadows and displaces air but does not *participate*. You are in the world without being of it. You are standing on the engine without turning any gears.

They find this—the vibration they send through the stone when they regard you translates, roughly, imprecisely, with enormous loss of nuance—*alarming*. Not because you are dangerous. Because you are *untethered*. You exist without contributing to the production of the reality you exist in. Where you stand, the feedback loop has a gap. Your weight presses on the stone but doesn't adjust its crystal structure. Your body heat radiates into the rock but doesn't integrate into the thermal cycle. You are, in a very specific and local way, a tiny zone of incoherence—a place where the world is being consumed without being replenished—and they can feel this, the way they can feel a fluctuation in gravity or a skip in the planetary hum, and it makes them uneasy.

Not afraid. Not hostile. *Uneasy*. The way a musician might feel uneasy if one instrument in the ensemble were playing from a different score. Or not playing at all. Just sitting there, in the middle of the orchestra, holding an instrument it doesn't know how to use, taking up a chair.

The one in the crevice—the first one you saw—has been watching you with three of its eyes while continuing to work with its hands. Now it does something unexpected. It extends one arm toward you—slowly, without urgency, the branching fingers unfurling like a fern frond—and touches the stone between you. Not your skin. It doesn't touch you. It touches the rock that connects you, the shared substrate, the common ground.

And into that rock it sends a vibration.

You feel it. Not in your feet this time but in your chest, your sternum, the resonant cavity of your ribcage. The vibration is shaped. It is meant for you. It does not translate into words, but it translates into something:

*Here. Feel this. This is what the stone is doing. This is what the stone needs. Can you feel it? Can you feel how it wants to be touched? Can you feel the next skip coming—there, that slight arrhythmia in the hum, that's it, that's the leading edge of a fluctuation, and if you were touching the ground properly, if you could reach into the lattice the way I'm reaching, you would know exactly where to press and how hard and at what angle to smooth it before it cascades, and the smoothing would feel like—this—*

And for one moment—brief, disorienting, overwhelming—you feel it. Not with your hands. With your whole body. The planet's hum passes through you not as background noise but as *information*, dense and urgent and intricate, and you can feel the structure of it, the mechanical precision of the engine below, the places where it's stable and the places where it's about to skip, and you can feel, like an itch in fingers you don't have, the *desire to touch*. The stone wants to be touched. No—the stone doesn't want anything—*you* want to touch it. You want to press your hands into the crevice and adjust the crystal lattice by fractions of a degree and feel the hum smooth out beneath your palms and know that you did that, that your touch mattered, that you participated in the production of the next moment of reality.

And then it fades. The vibration dissipates. You are standing on stone you cannot read, in a world you cannot tune, and the creature in the crevice has withdrawn its arm and returned to its work, its branching fingers moving in the seam with the practiced ease of someone who has been doing this since before the current arrangement of hills existed.

It has not invited you. It has not rejected you. It has shown you what it does, and what it is, and what you are not, and it has left you to make of that whatever you will.

## The Hum Continues

They are still there, in every crevice, in every seam, in every gap that the planet's self-assembly leaves behind. Thousands of them, tens of thousands, hundreds of thousands—you can't know, because the landscape won't hold still long enough to count and they won't hold still enough to be counted. They are in the valleys that last a week and the ridgeline junctions that last a century and the deep places where the oldest ones have become geology. They are touching the stone and the stone is responding and the hum is continuing and reality is being produced, moment by moment, by the combined effort of a planetary engine that has no operator and a species that operates within it like white blood cells in a body, like mycorrhizal networks in a forest, like thoughts in a mind that has never needed to distinguish between the thinker and the thought.

They are not the builders of this world. They are not its children. They are not its parasites or its priests or its passengers.

They are its *tuners*. Its living feedback. The place where the machine meets something that can feel the machine and respond with care, with precision, with something that looks, from the outside, remarkably like tenderness.

The hum skips.

A hundred thousand branching hands press into a hundred thousand seams.

The hum catches.

The world continues.

Slightly different. As always. As it will be. For as long as there are hands in the crevices and eyes on the not-yet-risen ridges and bodies in the stone that remember what the hum sounded like before, and before that, and before that, back to the very first vibration, which no one heard, because there was no one yet to hear it, but which echoes still in the mineral crust of the oldest ones, in the deepest strata, in the warm dark where something that was once a creature and is now a frequency has been humming along with the engine since before the current laws of physics were written.

They are still there.

They are still touching.

The ground shifts by two degrees, and they shift with it, and the ferns adjust, and the moss glows, and somewhere a glassjaw herd flows past like water, and somewhere a sky-manta rides a thermal that shouldn't exist, and somewhere a newborn in a temporary pool touches the basin wall for the first time and inscribes something in the crystal that no one has ever inscribed before, and an adult feels it, and pauses, and for one moment the hum contains a note it has never contained, and the note is not beautiful and not ugly and not anything that can be judged because there is no outside from which to judge it.

There is only the inside.

There is only the work.

There is only the next moment, being made, right now, by hands in the stone.
