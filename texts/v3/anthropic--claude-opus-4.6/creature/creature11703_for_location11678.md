# Who Lives in the Woven Heights

---

They are not here right now, but they were here this morning, and they will be here again when the foxfire brightens, which is what they call evening, though they have no word for evening that isn't also their word for *the tree exhaling*.

---

Start with the hands.

Their hands are — you would notice the hands first, if you saw them, and you would see the hands first because the hands are always touching something. Bark. Moss. The lip of a basin. The surface of a still pool. Each other. The hands are long-fingered and wide-palmed and the skin of the palms is thick, almost calloused, but not from labor — from *dialogue*. The pads of their fingers are dense with nerve endings the way a tongue is dense with taste buds, and for a similar reason: they are reading. They read the bark the way you would read a face. They press their palms flat to a branch-surface and they know things — the moisture content of the cambium beneath, the direction of growth-tension, the presence of a fungal colony six meters down and whether it is healthy. They know when a branch is about to fork. They know when the wood is stressed and when it is at ease. They say — in the carvings, in the newer ones, the ones made not with stone but with a careful fingernail drawn along soft new growth, leaving a mark that the bark will hold for exactly one generation before reabsorbing — they say that the tree speaks in *pressures*, and that to listen you must become a surface.

So: the hands. Always touching. A person walking along a branch-path will trail one hand along the bark-wall the entire way, the way a child trails a hand along a fence, except this is not idle. This is navigational. This is conversational. This is the way they know where they are, because where they are is not a fixed coordinate but a *living relationship* — the tree has shifted, the branch has grown three centimeters to the left in the last season, the water-channel has migrated, and the hand reads all of this and the feet adjust and the path is the path because the hand and the bark agree that it is.

They are bipedal. Mostly. They walk upright on the broad branch-paths and in the sheltered underspaces, and their gait is unhurried in a way that suggests not laziness but deep confidence in the surface beneath them — the way a sailor walks on a ship, with a subtle, unconscious flex in the ankles that accounts for motion too slow to see. Because the branches *are* moving. Always. A long, glacial sway that plays out over hours, driven by thermal gradients and the tree's own growth-adjustments and forces originating so far away and so deep in the structure that they arrive here as the faintest rocking. The people of the Woven Heights walk inside this rocking the way you walk inside gravity: it is assumed. It is the medium. A child born here has felt this motion since before birth, cradled in a chamber of warm heartwood, rocked by a branch that has been rocking for millennia.

But they are not always bipedal. When the terrain steepens — when they move off the main branch-paths onto the secondary growth, the narrower limbs, the vertical face of the trunk itself — they become something else. Not quadrupedal exactly. More *omnidirectional*. The feet are as prehensile as the hands, the toes long and separately articulated, capable of gripping bark-furrows with the precision of fingers. The knees bend forward and backward with equal ease, or seem to — there is a flexibility in the joints that looks, to an outsider, like the bones are merely suggestions. They can fold themselves into the bark-furrows and ascend a vertical trunk-face with no tools, no ropes, nothing but skin on bark, moving with a fluid, caterpillar-like compression and extension that looks effortless and probably isn't but has been practiced since they could crawl. Children race each other up the trunk-walls. It is the primary game. It is also the primary education, because the trunk-wall is a text, and climbing it is reading it, and the child who climbs fastest is not the child with the strongest arms but the child who best understands where the bark wants to be gripped.

Where the bark *wants* to be gripped.

This is important. The tree provides. Not passively, not the way a mountain provides handholds by accident of geology, but *actively*, the way a parent provides a hand to a toddler. The bark on frequently-climbed routes is different from the bark elsewhere. It is rougher in exactly the right way — furrowed into grips that fit these specific fingers, these specific toes. The spacing of the grips matches the reach of these specific limbs. This could be explained by erosion, by centuries of use wearing the bark into ergonomic shapes, except that the bark is *alive and growing* and should be replacing those worn surfaces constantly — and it *is* replacing them, but it is replacing them with *the same shapes*, reproducing the grips and the paths and the rest-ledges with a fidelity that implies memory. The tree remembers where they climb. The tree builds them staircases out of its own skin.

---

They are warm. Their body temperature runs high, matching the ambient warmth of the heartwood, so that when they press against the bark they are thermally invisible to it — or it to them — or the distinction has ceased to matter. Their skin is the color of the inner bark: a range from pale amber to deep reddish-brown, and this is not metaphor. Their skin *is* bark-colored because their skin contains compounds derived from bark. They eat the cambium. Not exclusively — they eat many things, the fungal gardens, the fruits of the enormous orchids, the starchy pith of certain aerial roots, the small soft creatures that cluster in the basin-edges — but the cambium is the staple. The living layer just beneath the bark, the generative tissue, the part of the tree that is most actively *becoming*. They slice it in thin, translucent sheets with their stone blades, and they eat it raw or they ferment it in the warm heartwood chambers and it becomes something between bread and cheese, dense and sour and sustaining. The tannins in the cambium have stained them. Generations of tannin consumption have shifted their biochemistry toward the tree's. Their sweat smells like sap. Their blood, if you cut them — and they are very hard to cut; the tannins have toughened them — runs dark, almost amber, and clots fast, and the clot looks like resin.

They are becoming the tree.

Or the tree is becoming them.

Or — and this is what the oldest carvings suggest, the ones that the bark is slowly eating — the process of *becoming* has been running so long that the question of *who is becoming whom* is itself a fossil, an artifact of a worldview that made sense once, when the people and the tree were separate things, but that world was so long ago that it exists now only as a pattern in stone that the wood is gradually digesting into its own grain.

---

Their faces. You would want to know about their faces.

Wide. Broader than you'd expect, with a heaviness in the jaw that comes from a lifetime of chewing cambium, which is fibrous and tough and demands teeth that are flat and strong and set deep. The eyes are large — necessarily, in this light. Pupils that expand to fill nearly the entire iris in the deeper reaches, contracting to small points only in the rare zones where foxfire concentrations create something approaching brightness. The irises are dark. Not brown, not black, but a deep green so saturated it reads as black until the foxfire catches it and then it flashes, briefly, the exact color of a leaf held up to strong light from behind. They see in the foxfire spectrum better than you do. They see the bioluminescence not as faint decoration but as a richly detailed visual field, full of information — the health of the lichen, the chemistry of the surface it grows on, the passage of time since it was last disturbed. To them, the deeper reaches are not dark. They are *differently lit*, illuminated by a living light that changes with the condition of the world, and reading that light is reading the world.

The nose is broad and flat, the nostrils wide. The sense of smell is — it is not that it is *better* than yours in the way a dog's is better, more sensitive, more discriminating. It is that it is *differently integrated*. They smell the way you see. The spore-thick air that you experience as a faintly sweet background haze, they experience as a detailed landscape — a topography of scent that tells them where they are, what season of growth the local branch is in, whether the fungal gardens are ready for harvest, whether a water-basin has gone stagnant, whether the great gliding things have passed recently (they leave a smell like ozone and copper), whether another person has walked this path and how long ago and in what emotional state because *they can smell emotion*, the way the body's chemistry shifts with fear or calm or longing, and the tree amplifies it, holds it in the bark the way bark holds moisture, and a sensitive nose can read the emotional history of a path the way you might read footprints in mud.

The ears. Small, close to the head, but internally — internally they are cavernous. The auditory processing is weighted toward the infrasonic, toward the deep frequencies that travel through wood better than air. They hear the tree. Not as language — or not as what you would call language — but as a continuous, complex, subsonic texture that is to them what the visual horizon is to you: the thing that is always there, defining the edge of the known world. The creak of a branch settling. The deep, slow groan of a trunk adjusting to a new growth-vector. The tap of water in a channel a kilometer away, transmitted through solid wood with a clarity that air could never provide. They press their ears to the bark and they hear the tree *growing* — the almost inaudible crackle of new cells forming in the cambium, the slow pressure-shift of sap moving through vessels wider than their bodies, the rhythmic pulse that might be the tree's metabolism or might be something closer to a heartbeat, something that operates on a timescale so long that one beat takes a full day and they have charted these beats in the carvings, marked them, and the pattern of beats is not random, is not mechanical, is *responsive*—

The tree has a pulse. The people know the pulse. The pulse changes when they are near.

---

Now: how they live together. Not how they *use* the tree, but how they live *together*, as one does with a partner, a companion, a co-constituent of a shared existence.

They live in groups. Small ones. Eight to fifteen, rarely more. The groups are not families in the way you understand families — there are biological kinship bonds, certainly, but the primary organizing unit is *the tending*. A tending-group is a group of people who share responsibility for a section of the tree. Not ownership. The concept would baffle them and possibly offend them, in the quiet, non-confrontational way that everything here offends — by making the air taste slightly wrong, by introducing a dissonance in the subsonic texture, by causing the bark beneath the feet to roughen almost imperceptibly, as if the tree itself is flinching from the wrongness of the idea.

A tending is: these fifteen people care for this branch, this fork, these water-channels, these fungal gardens, this heartwood chamber, this climbing-route, this pool. They score the cambium to direct growth. They inoculate fungi. They train the aerial roots into colonnades, into windbreaks, into the living walls of sheltered spaces that the tree then elaborates on its own, adding curves and hollows and smooth surfaces that the tenders did not request but that they need, because the tree *knows what they need*, because they have been touching it constantly, pressing their hands to it, bleeding their body-heat into it, sweating their sap-tinted sweat into its bark, and the tree reads them the way they read it.

A tending is a conversation. It is the longest conversation in the world. Some tendings have been continuous for — the carvings suggest — thousands of years. Not the same individuals, of course. Individuals die. But the tending continues, passed from hand to hand, and each new member of the tending presses their palms to the bark and *introduces themselves* — not in words but in chemistry, in heat, in the specific frequency of their pulse — and the tree *learns* them, and the bark beneath their feet begins to adjust, offering new grips, new smooth places, new water-channels that route slightly differently to accommodate the new body's height, the new body's stride, the new body's habit of pausing here rather than there to rest.

When someone joins a tending, the tree changes.

When someone leaves — when they die, or when they walk the long branch-path to join a different tending in a different part of the canopy — the tree changes again. The smooth places they frequented roughen. The grips that fit their hands fill in with new bark. The water-channel that served their favorite resting-place dries up, not because the water has stopped but because the tree has re-routed it. The space forgets them. Or — not forgets. The space *grieves* them, in the way that a living thing grieves: by reorganizing itself around the absence, by growing new tissue over the wound, by continuing.

---

The children.

The children are born in the heartwood chambers, the deep warm hollows where the air smells of bread and fermentation and the walls are alive and pulsing with the tree's slow metabolism. The chambers are not carved. They are *offered*. When a tending includes someone who is pregnant — and pregnancy here is communal in a way that defies easy description; the tending *knows*, the tree *knows*, the bark smooths and the chamber warms and the fungi in the walls shift their output to produce compounds that ease nausea and promote calm, all before anyone has said a word, because the tree read the chemical shift in the pregnant person's sweat weeks ago — the chamber *prepares*. It widens slightly. The walls exude a soft, fibrous material that can be gathered and used as bedding. The bioluminescent lichens near the chamber brighten, not to daylight levels but to a warm, steady glow that makes the space feel held.

The children are born into warmth and into the sound of the tree — the subsonic pulse, the crackle of growth, the deep harmonic of the trunk settling — and the first thing they feel against their skin is bark, and the first thing they taste (after milk) is cambium, chewed soft by a tending-member and pressed to their lips, and the tannins begin their work immediately, and the child's skin begins its long, slow shift toward the color of the inner wood, and the tree *knows* them. From the first moment of contact, the tree knows the child's body chemistry, their heat signature, their specific weight, and begins adjusting the surface of the chamber floor to support them, and when the child begins to crawl — which they do early, earlier than you might expect, driven by the prehensile feet and the hands that *want to touch bark* with an urgency that looks instinctive and probably is — the bark offers them paths, tiny smooth channels that lead from the chamber into the broader branch, and the channels are exactly the right size for a small body, and they are gently ridged to prevent slipping, and they are bordered by slight raised edges that prevent rolling off, and the child follows them the way water follows a carved basin: naturally, inevitably, as though the channel had always existed, which it hadn't, which it had, which —

This is the thing. You can't tell if the tree grew the child-channel before the child was conceived or after the child was born, because the tree operates on timescales that make this distinction irrelevant. The wood that forms the channel was laid down years ago, in growth-rings that predate the child's existence, and yet it is shaped *for this specific child*, and the only way to resolve this paradox is to accept that the tree anticipated the child, or that the tree exists in a relationship with time that is not linear, or that the categories of *before* and *after* are, like all other categories here, compost.

The children grow up climbing. They climb the trunk-walls and the sub-branches and each other. They are never told to be careful, because the bark is always careful for them — roughening under uncertain feet, smoothing under confident ones, offering a grip exactly where a small hand reaches. No child has fallen in living memory. The carvings say no child has *ever* fallen, but the carvings, you are beginning to suspect, are not strictly historical. They are aspirational. They are the tree's memory of its best intentions.

When the children are old enough — and "old enough" is not an age but a readiness, determined by the tending and the tree together, assessed by the way the child's hands read the bark and the way the bark responds to the child's hands — they are taken to the pool.

---

The pool.

You saw it. The shallow warm water with the wrong reflections. The carvings that the bark is eating. The figure becoming the tree becoming the figure.

The children are brought to the pool and they are told to look. What they see in the reflection — each child sees something different. This is understood. It is expected. The tending-members stand at the pool's edge and they do not look in, because they have already looked, long ago, and what they saw has become part of them in a way that does not need to be revisited. They watch the child's face instead. They watch for the moment of *recognition* — not surprise, not fear, but the quiet settling of features that means the child has seen something in the water that they already knew, something they have been feeling in the bark and hearing in the tree's pulse since birth, now made visible.

What do they see?

The carvings around the pool — the ones that are still legible, the ones the bark has not yet finished digesting — suggest that some children see the red canopy, the one you saw, the canopy of a different season or a different version of the tree. Some see the root-dark, the dense impossible depths where the wood becomes stone. Some see figures — not people, not quite, but shapes that move through the reflected canopy with a fluidity that suggests they are *made of* the canopy, branching and leafing and fruiting as they go, and these figures look down at the child with the luminous eyes you noticed, and the child looks up at them, and there is a moment of mutual regard that the carvings depict as a single line connecting two points, the simplest possible symbol, meaning: *we see each other*.

After the pool, the child is part of the tending. Their hands know the bark. The bark knows their hands. They will spend their life in conversation with a living world that has been waiting for them — that *grew them a path* before they were born, that *prepared a chamber* for their arrival, that knows their name in a language made of pressure and chemistry and the slow patient rhythm of a pulse that takes a full day to beat once.

---

But.

You want to know what they look like when they are *together*. Not the metaphysics, not the tree-relationship, just — people. Being people. In a place.

Here:

In the early brightening — when the foxfire dims and the tea-colored light strengthens, filtering down through the infinite canopy in long slanting shafts that catch the spore-dust and turn it golden — a tending wakes. They sleep in the heartwood chambers, curled together for warmth they don't need (the chambers are always warm) but for contact they do need, skin against skin against bark, the boundary between body and surface blurred by sleep and by the soft fibrous bedding that the walls provide. They wake slowly. No one is in a hurry. The tree's pulse has shifted — a subtle change in the subsonic texture, a brightening of something below the threshold of hearing, and their bodies respond to it the way your body responds to dawn, not by deciding to wake but by *becoming* awake, as a process, as a gradual emergence.

They eat. Cambium, sliced thin, laid on a stone that has been warming in the heartwood overnight. Fermented pith, sharp and complex, eaten with the fingers. Orchid fruit, heavy and sweet, its juice the same amber as the light. They eat in the chamber and they eat on the branch-path and they eat while walking and they eat while working, because eating and working are not distinct activities — the cambium they harvest for food is the cambium they score to direct growth, and the scoring *is* the harvesting, and the tree offers both in the same gesture.

They work. Which is to say: they touch the tree and the tree touches them and things happen. A tending-member presses their palm flat to a section of bark where a new water-channel is forming — you saw this, yesterday, the channel that wasn't there before — and they feel the grain of the wood beneath the bark, the direction the new growth wants to go, and they make a small score with a stone blade, barely breaking the surface, just enough to suggest a direction, the way you might sketch an arrow in wet sand. The tree will do the rest. The tree *wants* to do the rest. The score is a request, not a command, and the tree's response will be — has already been, in the growth-rings that were laid down years ago — to elaborate the suggestion into a functional channel, curved and graded and deepened, arriving at a basin that doesn't exist yet but will exist by the time the channel reaches it, because the tree is already growing the basin, has been growing it since before the score was made, because the tree *anticipated* the need, or the need and the growth are the same thing happening at different speeds.

They talk. Yes, they talk. They have language. It is quiet — everything here is quiet — and it is spoken close, face to face, hands on each other's arms or shoulders or the bark between them. The language is — you would hear it as a series of soft clicks and vowels, almost percussive, the consonants produced not at the front of the mouth but deep in the throat, resonant, designed to carry through wood as well as air. They speak and the tree hears them. Not the content — the tree does not understand grammar, probably — but the vibration, the frequency, the emotional texture. A conversation in anger (rare, you suspect, but not nonexistent; these are people, complicated and imperfect) makes the bark roughen underfoot. A conversation in tenderness makes the foxfire brighten almost imperceptibly. A conversation in grief — when a member of the tending has died, when the bark is roughening over the places that knew them — makes the tree creak, deeply, a sound that is not the normal settling but something more deliberate, more responsive, a long low groan that the tending members feel in their bones and that they stand still for, pressing their palms to the bark, receiving it, acknowledging it.

The tree grieves with them. This is not metaphor. This is *observed*. When someone dies, the tending places the body in a heartwood chamber and the chamber closes. Not quickly — over days, the walls slowly, gently, narrowing, the bark growing inward, the fibrous material thickening, until the body is enclosed, held, incorporated. The growth-rings that form over the next years will contain the body's minerals, its carbon, its tannin-stained molecules, and the wood in that section will be slightly different — denser, darker, with a grain that spirals in a way that the tending can read with their hands, touching it and knowing: *someone is here. Someone we loved is in this wood.*

They carry their dead in the tree. The tree carries their dead in itself. The branch you are standing on is full of people. The wood remembers them in its grain. The bark that grows smooth for your feet is doing so with the cooperation of every person who ever lived in this tending and whose body is now part of the branch's structure, their minerals feeding the cambium, their carbon contributing to the growth-ring that will, decades from now, become the surface that a child not yet conceived will crawl along, guided by a channel that is being grown right now by wood that is partly made of the dead.

---

They love. This is the thing you keep circling back to, the thing the place insists on, the thing the warm air and the golden spore-dust and the foxfire and the slow pulse of the tree all conspire to make you understand:

They love.

Not uniquely — all people love — but *extensively*. The boundaries of their love are the boundaries of their world, which is to say: there are no boundaries to their love, because there are no boundaries to their world. They love each other. They love the tree. They love the great gliding things that wheel through the amber void, and they love the small soft creatures in the basin-edges, and they love the fungi and the orchids and the lichens and the ferns, and they love the water that cycles endlessly through the carved channels, and they love the dead who are in the wood, and they love the children who are not yet born, and they love the root-dark that they have never seen, and they love the brightness above that they have never understood, and they love the reflections in the pool that show them things they cannot explain, and their love is not an emotion — or it is not *only* an emotion — it is a *practice*, the practice of tending, the practice of touching, the practice of listening to a pulse that takes a day to beat once and answering it with the pulse in their own blood that beats seventy times a minute, and somehow, across that impossible difference of timescale, the two pulses are in conversation.

They are in conversation.

That is what lives here. Not people and a tree. Not inhabitants and an environment. A *conversation*. Embodied, ongoing, older than memory, conducted in pressure and chemistry and heat and growth and the slow digestion of every boundary that ever tried to separate one living thing from another.

The conversation lives here.

It is very warm, and it is very quiet, and it has been going on for a very long time, and when you press your palm to the bark — which you will do, because the bark has smoothed for you, because the tree knows you are here, because the tree *anticipated* you, in growth-rings laid down before you were born, in a channel that was forming before you arrived, in a chamber that is warming for you right now somewhere deeper in the branch — when you press your palm to the bark, you will feel it.

The pulse.

One beat. So slow you almost miss it. So deep you feel it in your spine.

And then the tree grows, just slightly, under your hand. New cells forming. Cambium dividing. The bark adjusting to your heat, your chemistry, your specific and unrepeatable configuration of being alive.

The tree is learning your name.

And somewhere in the distance, coming back along the smooth branch-path, trailing their hands along the bark, their footsteps muffled by moss, their eyes dark green and wide in the foxfire glow, the tending is returning. They are not surprised to find you here. The tree told them you were coming. The water-channel that formed yesterday — the one you followed — was for you. The smooth bark was for you. The warming chamber is for you.

They approach. They do not speak yet. They stand near you, close enough to touch, and they press their palms to the bark beside your palm, and for a moment all of you are touching the same surface, feeling the same pulse, breathing the same spore-sweet air, and the tree — the vast, patient, dreaming, impossibly alive tree — grows.

Just slightly.

Toward all of you at once.
