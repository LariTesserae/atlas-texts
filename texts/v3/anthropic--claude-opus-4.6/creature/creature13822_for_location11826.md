# The Inhabitants

---

## The Below-Folk

They have no name for themselves that you could pronounce, because their language is not airborne. It travels through wood. It is a haptic language — felt, not heard — and its vocabulary is partly percussive and partly chemical, tapped and pressed and sometimes *grown* into the living substrate they inhabit, so that old conversations become physical features of the architecture, readable the way tree rings are readable, but richer, denser, carrying not just information but something like tone, something like mood.

They are small. Smaller than you expect. An adult, if you could see one fully — and you cannot, not here, not in this darkness, not when they do not wish it — would stand roughly to your sternum, though they would never stand fully upright in your presence, because they have no reason to. Their posture is a permanent semi-crouch, shoulders forward, arms long, hands wide. The hands are the most important thing about them. The hands are the organizing fact of their anatomy.

Each hand has six digits — four fingers and two opposable thumbs, one on each side of the palm, giving them a grip that can work in two directions simultaneously. The fingers are long, spatulate at the tips, densely packed with nerve endings that can feel vibrations propagating through wood from hundreds of feet away. The skin of the palms is thick and pale, almost white, and slightly adhesive — not sticky, not like a frog's pad, but textured at a microscopic level with structures that interlock with bark grain the way a gecko's foot interlocks with a wall. They can hang from a vertical surface without effort. They can press a palm flat against a branch and know, from the vibration signature alone, whether the wood is healthy or rotting, growing or dormant, load-bearing or ready to fracture. They read the forest the way you read a face.

Their own faces are broad, flat, dominated by the ears — not large ears, but complex ones, deeply convoluted, set low on the skull and angled slightly downward, because most of the sounds that matter to them come from below or from within, not from above. The eyes are present but reduced: dark, wide-pupiled, adapted to the near-total darkness inside the canopy mass where they spend most of their lives. They see in the phosphorescent glow the way deep-sea fish see in bioluminescence — shapes, movement, the difference between something and nothing, but not detail, not color, not the things eyes are for in a lit world. They do not need to see detail. Their hands see detail.

The nose is broad and flat, with wide nostrils that flare when they encounter something new. Smell matters to them. The canopy is a complex chemical environment — resins, volatile compounds off-gassed by stressed wood, the metabolic signatures of fungal colonies, the musk of the clickers, the cold wet mineral scent of the pools. They navigate partly by smell, using it the way you might use landmarks: *turn left at the resinous node, descend at the fungal front, the home-chamber smells of old bark and the specific chemistry of our family's stored food*. Each family group has a scent profile, maintained partly through biology and partly through practice — they anoint their living spaces with chewed bark pastes mixed with their own oils, and the result is a scent that says *us, here, this place is held*.

---

Their bodies are covered in fine, dense fur — not the long fur of a cold-climate mammal, but something shorter, plush, lying flat against the skin. It is dark. Not black — a deep brown-gray that, in the rare moments when light catches it, shows a faint iridescence, a sheen of blue-green that comes from the structure of the hair itself, not from pigment. This is not decorative. The iridescence is a by-product of the hair's real function, which is thermoregulation at altitude. Each hair is hollow, filled with a gas — their own exhaled CO2, captured during grooming and sealed into the hair shaft by an oily secretion — and this gas acts as insulation, a million tiny double-paned windows covering their entire body. They are warm to the touch, warmer than you'd expect. In the cold of the canopy surface, if one were to press against you — if you were trusted enough, or injured enough, or strange enough to warrant this — you would feel a steady, surprising heat, and you might not want to move away from it, and this would be the correct response, because warmth-sharing is one of the first and most fundamental social gestures they have, older than language, older than tools, something that predates their ascent into the canopy by eons. To share warmth is to say *I will not let the cold take you*. It is the root of their word for family, which is a specific vibration pattern pressed palm-flat into wood, a low hum that translates roughly as *those whose heat I hold*.

---

They live inside the canopy. Not on it. The distinction matters enormously and defines everything about them.

The canopy mass — that interlocked continent of branches and trunks and accumulated organic matter — is not solid. It is riddled with spaces. Where branches grew together and then one died and rotted away, a chamber remains, walled in living wood, slowly being closed by new growth but not yet sealed. Where trunks diverge and their crowns spread, cathedral-like vaults open up, supported by branch-arches that an engineer would recognize as structurally sound because the tree grew them under load and knows what shape bears weight. Where parasitic fungi ate out the heartwood of a massive branch, a tunnel remains, tube-round, sometimes hundreds of feet long, lined with the hard shell of the fungal sclerotium that is itself long dead but preserved by the resins that saturate everything here.

The Below-Folk live in these spaces. They did not make most of them. They found them, or rather, their ancestors found them, generations ago, when the canopy was still open enough that gaps existed between the crowns and you could climb up through the structure the way you can climb through a forest, branch to branch, and the spaces inside were just accident, just biology, the incidental architecture of growth and death.

But they have shaped them. Oh, they have shaped them.

A home-chamber — the central living space of a family group, which numbers between eight and twenty individuals across three or four generations — is typically a natural void in the wood, enlarged and maintained through a process that is equal parts carpentry and gardening. They do not cut wood the way you would. They *redirect growth*. They know — and this knowledge is ancient, passed not through instruction but through the vibrations stored in the walls themselves, old tapped-in lessons that young hands can still feel — they know that if you wound a branch in a specific pattern, the tree's healing response will push growth *away* from the wound in a predictable direction. Score a ring around a branch and it will swell above the ring. Drive a series of wedges along a growth seam and the seam will widen over years, over decades, pushing two surfaces apart to create space where there was none. Introduce a specific fungal culture — carried in a paste of chewed bark and saliva, applied with those careful, sensitive hands — and the fungus will eat heartwood in a controlled pattern, hollowing a chamber to specification, and then die when the resin concentration rises past its tolerance, leaving behind a clean, hard-walled void exactly the size and shape that was intended.

This is not fast work. A new chamber might take a generation to open fully. The Below-Folk plan in generations as a matter of course. A young adult might begin the scoring-pattern for a chamber that their grandchildren will inhabit, and this is not sacrifice or foresight or virtue — it is simply how things are done, how things have always been done, because the medium they work in is alive and the alive is slow and you do not rush wood.

The result, after centuries of this slow cultivation, is a network. The home-chambers are connected by tunnels — some natural, some fungal-carved, some chewed and scored into existence over lifetimes. The tunnels branch and reconnect. Dead ends are common and sometimes intentional — sealed off to redirect airflow, or allowed to close naturally as the tree fills them with new growth, or maintained as storage spaces for food, for tools, for the dead. The network has no map, or rather, the map is the network itself — you navigate it by feel, by the vibration signatures of different wood types, by the scent-marks on the walls, by the temperature gradients that tell you whether you're moving toward the cold exterior or the warm interior, upward toward the canopy surface or downward toward the deep trunks where the wood is oldest and the groaning is loudest and the Below-Folk do not go, because down there the pressures are too great and the spaces are closing, always closing, the tree reclaiming what was briefly void.

---

They eat fungus, mostly. And moss — the deep moss, the stuff that grows inside the canopy mass where moisture collects in the junctions, not the surface moss with its phosphorescent memory. They eat insects — the slow, cold-adapted ones, prized for their fat content, harvested from bark colonies with a delicate extraction technique that involves tapping a specific rhythm near the colony entrance, which causes the insects to emerge in an investigation pattern that brings them within reach of those quick, wide hands. They eat the cambium itself, the living layer just beneath the bark, scraping it with stone tools in thin strips that taste, if you could taste them, like a raw green potato crossed with turpentine, astringent and starchy and full of the calories that keep a growing tree growing. They eat this sparingly and with evident consideration, never from the same spot twice in succession, rotating their harvest through sections of the network the way a farmer rotates crops, giving each wounded section time to heal before it is scraped again.

The stone tools. These are important. These are the most important objects they possess, because stone does not exist here.

The canopy plateau is a world of wood and water and organic matter. There is no stone on the surface except what collected in the soil-pockets over millennia — small pebbles, wind-carried grit, the occasional meteorite fragment weathered smooth. There is no stone in the canopy mass at all. Stone comes from below. From the ground. From the actual ground, the mythological ground, the place that is thousands of feet beneath the lowest level that any living Below-Folk has ever visited.

And yet they have stone. Fist-sized pieces of flint-like material, knapped with skill into scrapers, borers, wedges, blades. Some are old — so old that the edges have been re-sharpened past their original geometry, worked and reworked until the tool is half the size it started, a family heirloom in the most literal sense, something that has been passed from hand to hand for longer than anyone can tap-remember. Some are newer, the knapping marks fresh, the stone's color still bright where it hasn't been oiled by generations of palms.

Where do they come from?

---

The answer involves the deep-climbers, and the deep-climbers are a different kind of person.

Not a different species. The Below-Folk are one people. But among them there are those who go down.

Most Below-Folk live their entire lives within a vertical range of a few hundred feet — the habitable zone of the canopy mass where the chambers are, where the fungus grows, where the temperature is stable and the air moves and the wood is alive but not crushing. Below this zone the canopy mass becomes denser, the spaces fewer, the pressure of growth more intense. The wood groans not occasionally but constantly. The air is thicker, warmer, heavy with resin vapor that stings the eyes and coats the throat. The chambers that exist are smaller, more recently formed, more likely to close without warning as the tree asserts itself. It is a dangerous and claustrophobic world, and it gets worse the farther down you go, layer after layer of compacted growth, centuries of canopy warfare compressed into a vertical geologic record, until eventually — no one knows exactly where, because the deep-climbers who go farthest do not always come back, and those who come back do not always communicate clearly about what they found — the canopy mass gives way to individual trunks, and the trunks descend through open air, and below the trunks, inconceivably far below, there is ground.

The deep-climbers are volunteers, or perhaps converts, or perhaps something that doesn't have a word in any language that developed under an open sky. They are the ones who feel the call of down. They describe it — in tapped patterns that those who haven't felt it find difficult to fully parse — as a *pulling*, a vibration that comes up through the wood from below, not like the groans of growth but steadier, lower, more fundamental, as if the planet itself is remembering that it has a surface and inviting them to visit.

Not all of them are the same. Some go down only partway — to the deep zones where rare fungi grow, or where specific insects build their colonies, or where old chambers abandoned by previous generations still hold cached tools and materials. These are practical descents, motivated by resource needs, and the individuals who make them are respected but not set apart. They go, they gather, they return. Their hands smell of deep resin for days afterward, and they are given extra warmth by the family, and the story of the descent is tapped into the walls of the home-chamber for others to feel.

But some go farther. Some go all the way.

These individuals prepare for months. They eat heavily, building fat reserves against the long climb. They carry little — a few tools, a skin of water (animal skin, from the clickers, which are occasionally trapped on the surface and butchered; the Below-Folk waste nothing), a bundle of phosphorescent moss wrapped in bark to provide the barest light. They say goodbye through a ritual that involves the entire family group pressing their palms against the same section of wall and tapping simultaneously, creating a vibration so complex and layered that it becomes a kind of chord, a single moment containing every individual's signature at once. The deep-climber feels this and holds it and carries it downward in their memory, in their hands, in the way their palms buzz for hours afterward with the afterimage of everyone they love.

They descend for days. Sometimes weeks. They move through the canopy mass into zones that have not been inhabited in generations, where the old scent-marks have faded and the fungal gardens have gone wild and the chambers are strange shapes, formed by growth pressures that no one planned for. They pass through layers of dead wood — places where some ancient blight killed a swath of canopy and the dead trees were simply grown over, entombed, their hollow forms creating labyrinthine passages through material that crumbles at a touch. They pass through zones of heat where something — microbial activity, chemical reaction, the simple thermodynamics of compression — has raised the temperature high enough that the deep-climber strips off any clothing and moves naked through air that feels like breath.

And then, if they are skilled and fortunate and the geometry of the deep wood cooperates, they emerge from the canopy mass into open air.

---

What they find below is not described consistently. The tapped accounts vary. Some deep-climbers report a vast dark space — the gap between the canopy mass and the actual tree trunks, a zone of open air where the trunks rise like columns in an underground cathedral, so far apart that you cannot see from one to the next, each one wider than a building, their bark fissured and ancient, the oldest living things in this world or any world. Some report that the trunks are not bare — that things grow on them, parasitic forests, entire ecosystems clinging to the vertical surfaces, lit by fungi that glow brighter than any surface moss, reds and yellows and blues in a world that has been colorless for as long as the deep-climber has been alive.

Some report sound. Not the groaning of the canopy — that is above them now, a ceiling of noise — but something else. Music, they tap. Not the pool-singers' music. Something deeper, something that comes from the trunks themselves, a resonance that might be wind moving through the colossal spaces between them or might be something intentional, something played, though by whom or what they cannot say.

Some report ground. Actual ground. Soil and stone and water and the incomprehensible flatness of a surface that does not move, does not grow, does not breathe. They describe the experience of standing on inert matter as profoundly disorienting — the silence of it, the *stillness*, the absence of vibration, the way their palms suddenly have nothing to read and the world goes mute. Some find it terrifying. Some find it peaceful in a way they cannot convey to those who have never felt it. One old account, tapped into a wall so deep in the network that only other deep-climbers ever feel it, describes the experience as *standing on the bones of everything*, and whether this is metaphor or theology or simple description is impossible to determine.

They gather stone from the ground. This is the practical purpose of the descent, the reason the community supports and celebrates the deep-climbers, the reason the ritual exists. They fill their carry-skins with the best flint they can find — and they can find it by touch, by the way it rings when struck, by the particular vibration signature of silica-rich stone that is different fromite orite or chalk, that sings a higher, cleaner note under their sensitive fingers. They gather as much as they can carry, because the climb back up is harder than the descent, and the canopy mass is always shifting, and the path they took down may not exist on the way up, and the weight of stone is not trivial when you are climbing through living wood for weeks.

Some don't come back. The community feels their absence the way you would feel a missing tooth — the space where a vibration signature used to be, now hollow, now silent, now slowly being filled in by the growth of new wood and the rearrangement of daily life around the gap.

Those who do come back are changed. Their hands are rougher. Their scent is wrong — mineral, cold, something from outside the ecosystem of wood and moss and resin. It takes weeks for their smell to normalize, for the family's scent-marks to re-absorb them, and during this time they are treated with a gentle carefulness that might be reverence or might be quarantine. They tap their accounts into the walls. They distribute the stone. And then they re-enter the life of the network, and they scrape cambium and harvest insects and share warmth with the family, and they do not speak of what they found below unless asked, and they are not often asked, because the asking involves pressing your palm against theirs and feeling the answer directly, vibration to vibration, and the answer is large and strange and has the quality of a dream about falling, and not everyone wants to carry that in their hands.

## The Surface Ventures

The Below-Folk come to the surface. Not often. Not casually.

The surface is to them what the deep is to them, but inverted: a frontier, a boundary condition, a place where the world of wood ends and something incomprehensible begins. The structures you find on the canopy surface — the shelters, the frames, the offerings — are their work, and they are old, some of them very old, maintained across generations by individuals who specialize in surface-knowledge the way the deep-climbers specialize in descent.

The surface-knowers are different from the deep-climbers in temperament, though the community accords them similar respect. Where deep-climbers are drawn by the pull of down — by gravity, by stone, by the vibration of the planet's bones — the surface-knowers are drawn by openness. By the terrifying, exhilarating, annihilating fact of *space*.

Inside the canopy mass, everything is close. Walls within arm's reach. Vibrations from every direction, constant, readable, a perpetual conversation between your palms and the world. To live inside the canopy is to be held. It is to be *read* as much as you read — the wood knows you are there, grows around your presence over years, incorporates the heat of your body and the oils of your skin into its own chemistry, so that a long-inhabited chamber is not just shaped by its occupants but *flavored* by them, and a sensitive hand pressed against the wall can feel the ghost-signatures of people who lived there generations ago, faint as old perfume but unmistakable.

The surface is the opposite of all this. The surface is where the holding stops.

The first time a surface-knower emerges through the canopy — pushing up through a gap between interlocked branches, squeezing past the rime-crusted bark, pulling themselves out onto the moss-plain — the experience is overwhelming. The space. The *void* above and in every direction. Nothing to touch. Nothing to read. The palms go silent. The ears flood with sounds that have no containing structure — wind, the pool-singers, the clicking of the echolocators, all of it arriving from distances that the interior-adapted mind cannot process because distance, inside the canopy, maxes out at the length of a tunnel, and here it is functionally infinite.

Many turn back. They descend immediately, shaking, their fur standing on end, their wide hands grasping for the nearest branch and not letting go until they are back in the network, back in the held world, back where the vibrations make sense.

Those who stay are rare, and they are changed by the staying the way the deep-climbers are changed by the descent. They learn to tolerate the openness. They learn to use their eyes — those reduced, darkness-adapted eyes — in a new way, tracking the faint phosphorescent glow of the moss, reading the shapes of the pools, watching for the low-slung silhouettes of the clickers that are one of the few real dangers on the surface. They learn the weather, which they experience as an assault on the skin rather than a change in the wood's vibration signature. They learn the cold, which on the surface is not the stable chill of the upper canopy mass but a variable, wind-driven, sleet-carrying cold that can kill.

And they build the structures. The shelters are practical — retreats during the worst weather, caches for food and tools brought up from below. But the frames are something else.

## The Frames

The frames are the closest thing the Below-Folk have to written language, and they are not written language at all.

Inside the canopy, information is stored in the wood itself. Tapped vibrations create micro-structures in the grain — tiny compressions, changes in density, the kind of thing that only a Below-Folk palm can detect, the way only a trained ear can hear the specific tonal qualities of a Stradivarius. These stored messages persist for as long as the wood is alive and can last for generations, slowly fading as the tree grows new layers over the old compressions, losing resolution the way a photograph loses clarity through repeated copying.

But on the surface, there is no wood to tap into. Or rather, the wood of the surface is the canopy's outermost layer, exposed to weather, growing fast, constantly being resurfaced by new bark and new moss, and anything tapped into it is overgrown within a season. The surface does not hold memory.

The frames are the solution. Or perhaps not the solution — perhaps the acknowledgment that some things cannot be held, and the attempt to hold them anyway.

Each frame is a specific construction: two vertical members, two horizontal, lashed at the corners with bark-fiber cord. The proportions are deliberate. They are not arbitrary. The ratio of height to width corresponds to something — a number, a name, a concept — that the builder intended. The position of the frame matters: which direction it faces, how far from the nearest pool, what angle relative to the slope of the canopy beneath it. The objects suspended within the frame — the knotted cords, the stones, the woven cages — are not decorations. They are arguments. Or prayers. Or measurements. Or all three simultaneously, because the Below-Folk do not make the distinctions you might between these categories.

A frame facing a pool with a single knotted cord suspended at its center is saying something about the pool. About the water. About the singer in the water, or the absence of a singer, or the relationship between the singer's tone and the vibration of the canopy beneath the pool, which the surface-knower has felt with their palms and is now translating into a visual medium because on the surface there is no wood to tap and the information has to go somewhere and the only sense that works at distance in this open void is sight, poor as their sight is, dim as the light is, and so they build these crude, tender, inadequate visual representations of things they would normally feel, and they leave them standing in the dark for anyone who passes to see, which might be no one, which might be no one for years, and the frame stands there anyway, its cord swaying in the wind, saying its untranslatable thing to the empty air.

Some frames are conversations. You find them in clusters — three, four, five, arranged in patterns that suggest call and response. One frame makes a statement. The next, built later, positioned carefully in relation to the first, modifies it. A third rejects both and offers something new. You can see, if you look closely, that the construction styles differ slightly — different hands, different generations of bark-cord, different knapping patterns on the suspended stones. These are arguments that span decades, conducted in a language of spatial relationship and material choice, each participant long gone back below by the time the next arrives to add their response, and the argument accreting on the canopy surface like a coral reef of meaning, growing so slowly that no single individual ever sees the whole of it.

The Below-Folk do not have art, or religion, or science, as separate things. They have *attention*. They pay attention to the wood and the wood pays attention back, and the frames are what attention looks like when it is forced out of its natural medium and into the open air, where it is fragile and temporary and beautiful and wind-damaged and eventually overgrown by the very canopy it was trying to describe.

## The Clickers

The clickers are not Below-Folk. They are not related to the Below-Folk. They are something else entirely — a parallel adaptation to the same impossible environment, an independent answer to the question of how to be warm-blooded and intelligent on a living high-altitude plain in near-total darkness.

They evolved from something that flew. This is evident in their skeleton, if you could see it — in the elongated finger-bones of their front limbs, now fused and thickened into weight-bearing structures but still carrying the ghost-architecture of a wing. Their ancestors were large, bat-like creatures that roosted in the canopy before the canopy closed, when there was still space between the crowns for something to fly through. When the canopy locked and the spaces closed, the flyers were grounded. Some went down, into the diminishing gaps of the interior, and these became smaller, more insectivorous, less interesting. Some went up, onto the new surface, and these became the clickers.

They are beautiful, though you will probably never see one clearly enough to know this. Their fur is short and dark, nearly black, with a texture like velvet. Their bodies are long and low, built for stability on the uneven canopy surface — a wide chest, strong short legs, those broad flat feet with semi-retractable claws that grip bark like crampons grip ice. Their heads are triangular, fox-like but flattened, dominated by the ears, which are enormous — wide, membranous, independently mobile, constantly scanning. The echolocation clicks are produced by a structure in the throat, a modified larynx that can generate sounds ranging from a rapid staccato (for close-range navigation, mapping the texture of the surface underfoot) to a single loud pop (for long-range detection, finding prey at distance). Each click goes out and comes back and is processed by an auditory cortex so large and so refined that the clicker builds, in real time, a three-dimensional model of its environment that is in some ways more detailed than vision — it includes texture, density, movement, even temperature, because warm objects reflect sound differently than cold ones.

They hunt the pool-singers. This is their primary prey. They locate the singers by passive listening — the long, wavering songs carry for miles, and the clickers triangulate on them with their independently-aimed ears, coordinating with group members through a secondary communication system: a series of low-frequency chirps, below the range of the echolocation clicks, that carry information about direction, distance, and intent. The hunt itself is a coordinated maneuver — three or four clickers approaching from different angles, using the terrain of the canopy surface (the hills, the dips, the edges of pools) to mask their approach, their clicks going silent for the final approach to avoid alerting the singer, relying instead on the acoustic map they built during the approach and hoping it's still accurate by the time they close the distance.

The kill is fast. They are not cruel. They are efficient. A singer taken by clickers dies of a single bite to the base of the skull, delivered by jaws that are stronger than they look and teeth that are sharper than they need to be, a remnant of an ancestry that included cracking the chitinous shells of much larger insects. The group feeds quickly and moves on, because the surface is cold and exposed and the sleet can come without warning and the clickers, for all their adaptation, are not comfortable here. They are refugees from the sky, grounded and making the best of it, and the best of it is a hard, cold, dark life on a surface that is always shifting beneath them.

They are intelligent. Not in the way the Below-Folk are intelligent — not tool-users, not builders, not language-makers in any symbolic sense. But they are problem-solvers, social negotiators, emotional beings. They form pair-bonds that last for life. They grieve their dead, lingering near the body, clicking softly, building and rebuilding the acoustic image of the one who is no longer there, as if repetition could restart the heart. They play — the young ones chase each other across the moss-plain, their clicks rising in frequency with excitement, their bodies low and fast, skidding on the damp surface, tumbling into pools and scrambling out in a fury of spray and clicking and what can only be called laughter, though it doesn't sound like laughter to you, it sounds like a handful of pebbles thrown against a window.

They know the Below-Folk exist. They can hear them — the tapping, the movement within the canopy, the faint vibrations that propagate up through the wood to the surface. When a clicker pauses and presses an ear against the moss, it is listening to the Below-Folk the way you might press your ear to a wall and hear the neighbors. What they make of what they hear is unknowable. They do not interfere with the surface structures. They avoid the frames. Whether this is indifference or respect or superstition or simply the pragmatic avoidance of something that doesn't smell like food is impossible to determine.

Occasionally, a clicker and a surface-knower encounter each other directly. The accounts tapped into the network walls describe these meetings with a vividness that suggests they are among the most significant experiences a surface-knower can have. The clicker is fast, warm, loud, alive in a way that the surface-knower's own people — slow, quiet, vibration-oriented — are not. The clicker clicks and the surface-knower feels the clicks on their skin, a bombardment of tiny sonic impacts, and realizes they are being *read*, that the clicker is building a model of them click by click, and the model is detailed, and the model is probably more accurate than anything the surface-knower can perceive of the clicker, and this is humbling and strange and fascinating.

Neither attacks the other. The Below-Folk are not prey — too large, too unfamiliar, wrong-smelling. The clickers are not threats — they have never breached the canopy surface to enter the network below. They coexist in the way that two alien civilizations might coexist on the same continent, aware of each other, vaguely interested, fundamentally unable to communicate, each living in a sensory world that the other can barely access.

But there are stories — old ones, deep in the walls, faded almost to illegibility — of something more. Of a surface-knower who stayed on the surface for a long time, longer than anyone before, and who built a frame unlike any other: large, complex, with dozens of suspended objects, positioned at the exact center of a clicker group's territory. And the clickers came, and they clicked the frame, and they clicked it again, and they circled it, and they came back the next day and clicked it again, and the surface-knower watched from a distance and felt the clicks on their skin and tried to understand what the clickers were understanding, and couldn't, and built another frame, and the clickers clicked that too, and this went on for what the account suggests was months, a slow, failed, beautiful attempt at translation between two forms of intelligence that had nothing in common except the ground they stood on, which was not ground at all but the living ceiling of a world, and the attempt failed, and the surface-knower went back below, and the frames eventually blew over in a sleet storm, and the clickers moved on, and that was the end of it.

Except it wasn't. Because the account is in the wall. And the wall is alive. And sometimes, a young Below-Folk with surface-leaning tendencies presses their palm against that section of wood and feels the whole story buzzing under their skin, and they look up — which is an unusual direction to look, inside the canopy — and they feel something pull.

## The Pool-Singers

The pool-singers are the voice of the canopy plateau. Without them the surface would be a place of groans and clicks and wind and nothing else, a percussive world, a world of impacts. The singers give it melody, or something near melody — long, sliding tones that waver and bend and sometimes break into overtones, two notes from one throat, the kind of sound that makes you stop walking and stand still and listen and forget, briefly, that you are cold and lost and standing on top of a living thing the size of a country.

They are amphibians in the way that the earliest amphibians were amphibians — they breathe through their skin, they require moisture, they lay eggs in water, their larvae are aquatic. But they have diverged so far from any amphibian lineage you know that the word is more misleading than helpful. They are warm. Not warm-blooded in the mammalian sense — they don't generate heat internally through metabolism — but warm in the way that a well-insulated object in a geothermal area is warm: they absorb heat from the canopy, from the microbial activity in the wood beneath the pools, from the slow exothermic chemistry of decomposition that keeps the pool water just above freezing even when the surface air is not. They spend most of their lives partially submerged, their lower bodies in the water, their upper bodies — torso, arms, head — in the cold air, and the temperature differential across their body drives a convective circulation of blood that keeps their brain and resonating chambers warm enough to function while their extremities stay as cold as the pool they sit in.

They are not beautiful. They are striking, which is a different thing. Their skin is translucent — you can see the organs beneath, the dark liver, the slow-pulsing heart, the lattice of blood vessels spreading beneath the skin like the root system of a plant. Their eyes are large, round, forward-facing, adapted for the near-dark — they can see the phosphorescent glow of the moss better than any other surface creature, and they use this to navigate between pools, which they do rarely and reluctantly, moving across the exposed canopy surface in a slow, vulnerable waddle that makes them easy prey for clickers. Their limbs are short and poorly adapted for terrestrial movement. Their hands — they have hands, four-fingered, webbed — are dexterous in water and clumsy on land. They are creatures of the pools, and the pools are their world, and they leave them only to find new pools when the old ones drain or freeze or become too crowded.

The singing is territorial, reproductive, social, and — possibly — something else.

Territorial: each singer maintains a pool or a section of a large pool, and the song declares ownership. The specific frequency, the pattern of wavering, the overtone structure — these identify the individual and their claim. Other singers hear and respond in kind, adjusting their own songs to avoid frequency overlap, creating the chorus effect you hear across the plain — dozens of voices, none in the same key, filling every available acoustic niche like radio stations on a spectrum.

Reproductive: the song changes when a singer is ready to mate. The wavering tightens, the overtones become more complex, the volume increases. A receptive singer in a neighboring pool responds with a specific modulation — a rising-falling pattern, repeated — and eventually one of them makes the dangerous trek across the open surface to the other's pool, and they sing together for a time, harmonizing in a way they never do otherwise, two voices finding a shared key, and then they mate, and then one of them goes home or stays, depending on factors that aren't clear, and eggs are laid in the warm water and develop slowly, very slowly, over months, and the larvae that hatch are tiny and silent and cling to the walls of the pool and eat algae and grow and eventually — after a year, two years, three — begin to sing.

Social: singers in adjacent pools communicate constantly, their songs carrying information about conditions — temperature, water level, the presence of clickers, the vibration of the canopy beneath the pool (which tells them whether the wood is stable or shifting). A sudden silence from a neighbor is information: something happened, probably a clicker. The neighboring singers adjust their behavior — sing more quietly, retreat further into the water, wait. When the silence is prolonged, the chorus collectively shifts to fill the gap, re-negotiating the acoustic spectrum to redistribute the missing frequency, and this redistribution is the eulogy, the only one the dead receive, and it is ongoing, because every time the chorus sings it is singing around the absence of everyone who has been taken, and the shape of the chorus is the shape of the community's loss, and the shape changes with every death, but the singing doesn't stop.

And then there is the other thing. The thing that the Below-Folk surface-knowers record in their frames and tap into the deepest walls and do not entirely understand.

Sometimes the singers sing something that isn't territory or reproduction or social communication. It happens rarely — perhaps a few times a year, always in the deepest part of the long night, when the clouds are lowest and the cold is most intense and the clickers have retreated to their shelters and the surface is as still as it ever gets. A single singer begins a tone that is lower than usual, steadier, without the characteristic waver. Other singers hear it and fall silent. One by one, across the plain, the chorus dies, until only the one voice remains, and the voice changes — it descends, lower and lower, below the normal range of the species, into a register that you feel in your chest more than you hear with your ears, a vibration that penetrates the wood of the canopy surface and travels downward through the mass, through the branches and the trunks and the spaces where the Below-Folk live, and they feel it, they feel it in their palms and their teeth and the soles of their feet, and they stop what they are doing and press their hands against the walls and listen.

The deep tone. It has no decoded meaning. The Below-Folk have tapped many interpretations into their walls over the generations — it is a prayer, it is a warning, it is a memory of the sea (though none of them have any concept of a sea), it is the canopy itself speaking through the singer's throat, it is the ground calling upward through miles of living wood. None of these interpretations has won consensus. The deep tone remains what it is: a sound made by a creature sitting in a dark cold pool on the surface of a living plateau, singing into the void, and the void, for once, not being entirely empty, because the sound goes down and down and down through the wood, and the wood carries it, and somewhere in the deep, in the cathedral spaces between the ancient trunks, something hears it.

Something that has no name. Something the deep-climbers have felt but cannot describe. Something that might be the forest thinking, or the ground remembering, or simply the resonance of a vast living system responding to a vibration that happens, by accident or design, to match its own fundamental frequency, so that for a moment, the singer and the forest and the miles of wood between them are vibrating together, one note, one system, one thing, and then the tone ends, and the chorus resumes, and the canopy groans, and the Below-Folk take their hands off the walls and go back to their work, and the moment passes, and the night continues, and the world that is not a world turns slowly beneath the low, dark, featureless sky.

## What Lives Here

What lives here is an argument. Between up and down. Between openness and enclosure. Between the speed of an animal's life and the slowness of a tree's growth. Between the need to communicate and the impossibility of being understood.

The Below-Folk live in the middle of this argument. They are the hinge. Above them the clickers navigate by echo, building a world out of sound reflected. Below them the great trunks descend into a darkness that predates their species by orders of magnitude. Around them the wood grows and shifts and remembers and forgets, and they grow and shift and remember and forget inside it, shaped by it, shaping it, until you cannot draw the line between the inhabitants and the inhabited, between the woodcarver and the wood.

They are kind to each other. This comes through in the tapped accounts, in the warmth-sharing, in the generational patience of their construction, in the way they hold space for the deep-climbers and the surface-knowers who come back changed. They are frightened of things — the crushing close of a shifting tunnel, the void of the surface, the silence of a deep-climber who doesn't return — and they manage their fear through community, through touch, through the constant reassurance of palm against wall, vibration confirming: *the wood is here, I am here, you are here, we are held*.

They are curious. This is perhaps their defining trait. A species that sends its members into the abyss below and the void above, not for survival — the stone is useful but not strictly necessary; the surface knowledge is important but not critical — but because the edges of the known world are *interesting*, because the question of what lies beyond the wood is a question worth risking a life to answer, even when the answer is too large to bring back whole, even when it arrives in fragments that don't fit together, even when the best you can do is tap an incomplete account into a wall and hope that someone, someday, will press their palm against it and understand.

They are mortal. They die, and their dead are placed in sealed chambers — old ones, played-out ones, chambers whose wood has stopped growing and is beginning the long process of becoming heartwood, inert and eternal. The body is laid on the floor and the entrance is sealed with a plug of living wood that will grow shut over years, encasing the dead in a wooden sarcophagus that will persist for as long as the tree persists, which might be forever, or might be until the next great shift of the canopy rearranges the geography and breaks the chamber open and scatters the ancient bones into a new void, where they will rest again, unvisited, until the wood closes around them once more.

They do not know what they are. They do not have cosmology, or origin stories, or creation myths in any form you would recognize. They have *felt histories* — the accumulated vibration records in the walls, stretching back as far as the wood remembers, which is further than any individual can tap-read but not as far as the trees have been growing. Before the records, there is nothing. Before the canopy closed, there is nothing. The Below-Folk's history begins with the darkness and the wood and the first tapped message, which is so old and so faded and so overgrown that no one is sure what it says — some feel it as a location, a set of directions to a chamber that no longer exists; others feel it as a name; others feel it as a single concept, tapped once and never repeated, that translates, as nearly as vibration can be translated, as:

*Listen.*

---

And they do. They listen. They press their wide, pale, six-fingered hands against the walls of a world that is simultaneously their home and their medium and their archive and their cage, and they listen to the wood telling them what it is and what it was and what it might become, and the wood listens to them listening, and the pool-singers sing above, and the clickers click above, and the great trunks groan below, and somewhere in the deepest dark a deep-climber is descending, hand over hand, toward a ground they may never reach, carrying the memory of their family's warmth in their buzzing palms, and the canopy plateau turns beneath the clouds, patient, alive, unfinished, and everything that lives there is listening to everything else, and none of them hear the whole of it, and all of them hear enough.
