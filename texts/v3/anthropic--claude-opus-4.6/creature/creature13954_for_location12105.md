# What Lives in the Nesting Terraces of Óramak

---

Start with the founders. Start with what built this place without meaning to.

---

## The Three Architects

**The Blackthroat** (*Pelagorrhos óramensis*) is the largest of the three, and the most numerous, and the most ancient — if you could read the óram like a core sample, the deepest layers would be almost entirely Blackthroat. They are massive birds, heavy-bodied, with wingspans approaching two meters in the largest females. Their plumage is a deep sooty black with a faint oil-sheen of violet across the back and wings, and their throats are bare skin — dark grey, heavily wrinkled, capable of inflating into a resonating pouch that amplifies their call into the deep, chest-shaking bark that forms the basso profundo of the colony's soundscape. Their eyes are small and dark and set far forward on a heavy skull, giving them a fixed, almost confrontational stare. Their beaks are long, pale, slightly downcurved, stained yellow at the base from the calcium-paste they secrete — the primary building material of the óram, produced in a specialized gland at the back of the throat and regurgitated in thick ropes that harden on contact with air.

They are not graceful. In flight they are magnificent — soaring on thermals with barely a wingbeat, riding the updrafts that curl over the plateau's edge, traveling two hundred kilometers or more in a day to reach their feeding grounds in the lowland marshes. But on the terraces they are lumbering, aggressive, clumsy. They waddle. They shove. They use their beaks like clubs to batter competitors away from prime nesting hollows, and the sound of two Blackthroats fighting — a rapid percussive *clack-clack-clack* of beak on beak, accompanied by hoarse screaming — is one of the colony's signature noises, occurring hundreds of times per hour across the full extent of the terraces.

Their nests are the deep tube-hollows, the ones that angle back into the terrace walls. A Blackthroat pair does not so much build a nest as *excavate and line* one — scraping out the cured óram with their claws (which are disproportionately strong, the toes thick and scaled, the nails curved and worn blunt from a lifetime of digging), then coating the interior with fresh calcium-paste that they work smooth with the sides of their beaks. Each season's relining adds to the tube's depth. Some Blackthroat hollows extend two meters or more into the terrace wall, and the deepest have been used for so many generations that they have broken through into adjacent hollows, creating linked chambers that multiple pairs share in an uneasy, squabbling arrangement. The interior of a Blackthroat nest is warm — body heat, composting material below, the insulating mass of óram on all sides — and in the dim light from the entrance you can sometimes see the single large egg, cream-white with faint brown speckling, sitting in a shallow depression that has been polished glassy-smooth by decades of incubating bodies.

Blackthroats mate for multiple seasons, possibly for life, though "life" here is uncertain. They are long-lived for birds — twenty years, perhaps thirty. A pair that has held a good nesting hollow for a decade has, by that act alone, meaningfully shaped the architecture of the terrace: deepened the tube, reinforced the lip, deposited enough material around the entrance to raise a slight rim that sheds water and discourages intruders. Multiply this by tens of thousands of pairs. Multiply by centuries. This is how the terraces grow.

They are also — and this matters — profoundly, almost pathologically habitual. A Blackthroat returns to the same hollow, on the same terrace, in the same position, year after year. If a hollow collapses, the bird will attempt to rebuild it in exactly the same location, using the rubble. If a terrace face shears away, the birds whose hollows were on that face will circle the site for days, calling, landing on the raw exposed cross-section of layered óram, attempting to nest on surfaces that no longer exist. This fidelity to place is the engine of the terraces' construction. It is not design. It is not intelligence. It is a deep behavioral groove worn so deep by evolution that it functions as something like memory embedded in the species itself — not any individual bird remembering, but the species as a whole remembering, through the sheer repetition of bodies doing the same thing in the same place until the place reshapes itself around them.

---

**The Stormwing** (*Procellarkos cinerea*) is smaller, sleeker, faster. Slate-grey above, pale grey below, with long narrow wings built for speed rather than soaring. Where Blackthroats ride thermals, Stormwings cut through the air like thrown blades, arriving at the terraces in tight flocks of fifty to two hundred that move with a coordination that seems choreographed — banking and turning in near-unison, threading the gaps between terrace walls with centimeters of clearance, landing in a sudden collective brake that sounds like a heavy curtain being shaken.

Stormwings nest on the open ledges, not in hollows. Their nests are flat platforms of compacted material — less calcium-paste than the Blackthroats use, more feather and vegetation and dried insect carapace, pressed and trampled into a hard disc that sits directly on the ledge surface. A Stormwing ledge in active season is a dense crowd of these disc-nests, each one close enough to touch its neighbors, each defended with furious pecking and wing-buffeting against any encroachment. The density is extraordinary. Where Blackthroats space themselves by the dimensions of their hollows, Stormwings pack together as tightly as physically possible, and the ledges they occupy — the flat, outward-facing surfaces of the terrace walls — become layered over the years into slightly raised platforms as nest-disc accumulates on nest-disc. This is how the ledges widen. This is how new ledge-shelves begin to extend outward from the terrace face, cantilevering on the compacted material of generations of Stormwing nests, until some of them project a meter or more from the wall and form the overhangs that shelter the hollows below.

They are noisy in a different register than the Blackthroats — higher, sharper, a rapid staccato *kik-kik-kik-kik* that thousands of birds producing simultaneously creates a shimmering, metallic wall of sound, like hail on a tin roof that never stops. Their social disputes are constant and brief and involve a great deal of wing-raising and bill-gaping but relatively little actual contact. They are colonial in the truest sense: the flock is the unit, not the pair. They coordinate departures and arrivals. They mob predators collectively. They seem, at times, to function less like individual birds than like cells in a tissue — each one interchangeable, each one performing its small function, the colony-ledge behaving as a single organism that grows and repairs and defends itself.

Stormwings are also the primary long-distance foragers. They fly farther than Blackthroats — three hundred kilometers or more — and they bring back food not just for their own young but in a surplus that spills, that drops, that is stolen by passerines and scavenged by beetles, that feeds the entire ecosystem of the ledge-face. A returning Stormwing flock is a rain of regurgitated fish and marsh invertebrates, and the passerines know it, and they gather at the ledge-edges in anticipation, and the beetles know it too, and they emerge from their crevices, and the whole terrace comes alive in a feeding frenzy that lasts until the last bird has settled and the last dropped morsel has been claimed.

---

**The Pallid Groan** (*Diomedarkos pallidus*) — named by the lowlanders, who can hear its call carried down from the plateau on still nights — is the strangest of the three, and the least numerous, and perhaps the oldest in terms of lineage. It is large, nearly as large as the Blackthroat, but lean and angular where the Blackthroat is heavy. Its plumage is not dark — it is the one exception to the colony's general darkness, a washed-out cream-white with faint grey barring on the wings, so that against the pale óram walls it nearly disappears. Its beak is enormous relative to its body, deep and laterally compressed, pale pink, with a slight hook at the tip. Its eyes are disproportionately large, amber-colored, adapted to the dim light that is all the plateau ever receives.

The Pallid Groan nests in the warrens — the deep interconnected chamber-systems that riddle the interior of the oldest, thickest terrace walls. It does not excavate its own chambers. It uses those that have been created by generations of Blackthroats digging their hollows deeper and deeper until they break through into each other. The Groan moves through these interior spaces with a hunched, shuffling gait, folding its long wings tight against its body, navigating by some combination of spatial memory and low-light vision through passages that would be pitch-dark to a human eye. Deep in the warrens, far from any entrance, in chambers where the air is warm and still and thick with ammonia and the walls are furred with pale fungal growth, the Pallid Groan lays its single egg directly on the floor — no nest, no lining, just the egg on the bare óram — and incubates it in total darkness for what is believed to be an extraordinarily long period, perhaps three months.

Its call is the one that gives it its name. It is not a bark or a chatter or a pipe. It is a long, low, resonant moan that seems to come from everywhere at once — from inside the walls, from under the floor, from the substance of the terrace itself. The sound propagates through the óram the way sound propagates through bone. Stand with your hand on a terrace wall when a Pallid Groan calls from deep inside and you will feel the vibration before you hear the sound, and the sound when it arrives is felt as much as heard — a deep, aching groan that rises slowly in pitch over four or five seconds and then cuts off sharply. In the dark hours, when many Groans call simultaneously from within the warrens of the oldest terraces, the effect is profoundly unsettling. The walls themselves seem to moan. The whole structure seems to be vocalizing. The lowlanders, hearing this carried on the wind, have stories about the plateau being alive — being an animal, sleeping, dreaming, calling out in its sleep. They are not entirely wrong. The Pallid Groan has become so integrated with the terrace structure that its voice *is* the voice of the building, its body *is* the body that moves through the walls, and its existence is so hidden, so interior, that you could spend days on the terraces and never see one — only hear them, only feel them, only know they are there by the sound that has no visible source.

Pallid Groans feed differently from the other two. They do not fly to the lowlands. They fly at night — launching from the upper terraces in the darkest hours, their pale wings nearly invisible against the dim sky — and they fly *up*, over the southern ridge, to feeding grounds on the far side that no lowlander has ever reached. What they eat there, no one knows with certainty. Their beaks suggest large prey — fish, perhaps, from high mountain lakes, or large invertebrates, or carrion. They return before the grey-violet light of midday, slipping back into the warrens through upper entrances that are hidden among the spires and fins of the highest terrace ranks, and they are not seen again until the next dark.

They are also, uniquely among the three species, quiet around other birds. On the ledges, in the open hollows, around the nesting Blackthroats and packed Stormwings, a Pallid Groan passing through (as they sometimes must, to reach their warren entrances) moves in silence — head low, wings folded, its pale body ghosting along the terrace wall like a faded projection of a bird. The other species ignore it. It ignores them. It is as if the Groan occupies a different layer of the same place, sharing the physical structure but inhabiting it in a completely separate mode — interior rather than exterior, nocturnal rather than diurnal, solitary rather than colonial, silent on the surface and vocal only from within.

## The Dependents

Below the three architects, around them, on them, inside their constructions, feeding on their waste and their food and their bodies and each other, lives everything else.

---

**The Crevice Finches** are perhaps the most visible of the small birds — not because they are large or brightly colored (they are neither; they are tiny, dun-brown, almost featureless), but because they are *everywhere* and they never stop moving. A Crevice Finch weighs less than a Blackthroat's egg. It can fit through gaps that would stop a human finger. It builds its nest — a tiny cup of woven fungal fiber and moth wings — in the interstices between Blackthroat hollows, in the narrow cracks where terrace walls have shifted, in the space beneath a Stormwing's nest-disc where it meets the ledge. It feeds on the insects that feed on the guano, and it feeds on seeds from the valley grasses, and it feeds on scraps dropped by the returning seabirds, and it feeds on the mites it picks from the feathers of the seabirds themselves, perching boldly on the back of a Blackthroat twenty times its size and pecking systematically through the plumage while the larger bird sits on its egg and tolerates it — not out of gratitude (there is no gratitude here, only mechanisms) but because the cost of dislodging the finch exceeds the cost of being climbed upon, and the mite-removal provides a real benefit.

There are at least four species of Crevice Finch, differentiated by beak shape and feeding preference, but they look nearly identical to the untrained eye — small, brown, hyperactive, singing a thin, rapid, insistent song that is one of the few sounds in the colony that could be called cheerful. They are short-lived. A Crevice Finch might survive two years. But they breed prolifically — three clutches per season, four or five eggs per clutch — and their population tracks the seabird population with a slight delay, rising and falling in echo.

---

**The Terrace Kestrel** (*Falco óramensis*) is their nemesis, and the nemesis of the lizards, and the apex predator of the terrace surfaces. Small for a raptor — barely larger than a pigeon — it is nonetheless the most visually striking creature on the terraces. Where everything else is dark or dun or pale, the Terrace Kestrel is marked: rufous-red back, barred grey breast, a sharp black mask across the eyes, bright yellow feet. It nests on the highest spires, where wind-erosion has carved the ancient óram into thin blades and pillars, and it hunts from these vantage points with a precision that is the closest thing to design the terraces possess. It does not soar. It does not chase. It waits — still as the spire it perches on, its head moving in small tracking increments — and then it drops, a folded bolt of feathers, and something small dies.

The kestrels are territorial, fiercely so. Each mated pair defends a section of the upper terraces, and their thin, descending scream — *kee-kee-kee-kee* — is the alarm that sends Crevice Finches into their crevices and lizards into their cracks. But they also serve a function they do not intend: their kills, plucked and eaten on the spires, drop feathers and bones and viscera onto the terrace surfaces below, and these are processed by the beetles, and the beetles are eaten by the centipedes, and the centipedes are eaten by the frogs, and the frogs are eaten by the kestrels, and the circle closes.

The kestrels are also, incidentally, the only creatures on the terraces that seem to notice a human observer. They will track your movement. They will adjust their perch position to keep you in view. If you approach their nest spire they will scream and dive and pull away at the last moment, close enough that you feel the wind. They are, of all the terrace's inhabitants, the only ones that appear to recognize you as something — not a threat, exactly, but a novelty, an irregularity in a system so self-contained that anything from outside is conspicuous simply by being different.

---

**The Dark Geckos** have no formal name because no naturalist has studied them. They are known from the accounts of the few outsiders who have spent a night on the terraces, and those accounts are consistent: small (finger-length), dark-scaled (black or very deep brown, with a faint blue iridescence under fungal light), sticky-footed, large-eyed, silent. They emerge at the darkest hours, flowing up the terrace walls in a dispersed wave — not in groups, not coordinated, but so numerous that the effect is collective, like watching a wall begin to move. They eat the moths. Each gecko eats dozens per night, catching them with a quick lateral snap of the head that makes a faint *tck* sound, barely audible, but multiplied by thousands of geckos it becomes a soft, irregular clicking that underlays the night soundscape like a distant typewriter.

They are preyed upon by the kestrels in the hours when their activity overlaps — the geckos emerging just before full dark, the kestrels hunting in the last grey light — and by the bats, and probably by the Pallid Groans, though no one has observed this directly. Their eggs are laid in deep cracks in the óram, one or two at a time, and the hatchlings are so small they are nearly invisible, transparent-skinned, their organs visible as dark smudges through the tissue. They grow quickly. They must — their entire generation time is compressed into a single dark season, and by the time the dim midday light returns, the adults are hidden in their cracks and the next cohort is already developing within its eggs.

---

**The Rust Centipedes** deserve special mention because they are, in their way, as important to the terrace ecology as any bird. They are large — the length and thickness of a human finger — and they are the color of dried blood, a deep rust-red that is startling against the pale óram. They are nocturnal, or rather crepuscular, emerging in the transition hours when the moths are beginning to fly but the geckos have not yet fully mobilized. They patrol the base of the terrace walls with an unsettling mechanical regularity, moving in straight lines, turning at right angles, following scent trails that are invisible to any other creature. They eat beetles. They eat beetle larvae. They eat anything they can catch and overpower, which is most things smaller than themselves — including, occasionally, the transparent hatchling geckos, though this seems opportunistic rather than systematic.

Their importance is not as predators but as processors. Like the beetles they prey upon, the centipedes contribute to the breakdown and recycling of organic material at the terrace base. Their burrows — shallow horizontal tunnels in the lower layers of óram — create ventilation channels that prevent anaerobic decay in the deep substrate. Without them, the base layers of the terrace walls would rot from within, weakened by pockets of toxic gas and liquefied organic matter. With them, the substrate breathes. The terraces stand. The centipedes, red and mechanical and utterly without awareness of their role, are part of the structural engineering.

There is a wasp that parasitizes them. It is very small, dark-winged, and it lands on the centipede's back between two body segments and inserts its ovipositor through the joint membrane. The centipede does not seem to notice, or perhaps cannot respond — its body plan does not allow it to reach that particular spot. The wasp's larva develops inside the centipede's body cavity, feeding on non-essential tissues for weeks before finally consuming the vital organs and emerging from the husk. The dead centipedes, hollowed out, are sometimes found still standing in their patrol positions, legs locked in place, as if the mechanism had simply stopped.

---

**The Pool Frogs** are jewels. That is not a metaphor for their beauty — they are not beautiful in any conventional sense, being thumb-sized, translucent, and vaguely gelatinous in appearance — but for their rarity and their precision of adaptation. They breed nowhere else. They *can* breed nowhere else. Their tadpoles require the specific chemistry of the standing pools — the particular concentration of nutrients, the particular microbial community, the particular temperature maintained by the terrace structure's insulating mass. Take a Pool Frog to the lowlands and it dies within days. Take pool water to the lowlands and the tadpoles develop abnormally and do not metamorphose. The species is absolutely, irreversibly bound to this place.

The adults sit at pool-edges on beds of wet moss, their skin glistening faintly in the fungal light, their huge dark eyes adapted to near-darkness. Their call — that high, glassy piping — is produced by a vocal sac that inflates to nearly the size of the frog's body, a translucent balloon through which you can see the frog's internal organs pulsing. When dozens of them call in chorus from a single pool, the sound is ethereal, almost electronic, a crystalline oscillation that seems to belong to a different world than the rough barking of the birds and the drone of the insects. It is the one sound in the terraces that visitors remember most clearly after leaving — not for its volume but for its strangeness, its purity, its incongruity in a place so saturated with coarse noise.

The frogs eat anything that falls into or near the pools. Insects, mostly. But also the mites that rain down from the birds overhead — a constant fine drizzle of parasites dislodged by preening or by the finches' ministrations, too small to see individually but collectively enough to sustain a pool-edge frog population through the lean periods when flying insects are scarce.

---

**The Bats** are an afterthought in the colony's ecology — or seem to be. There may be only a few hundred of them, perhaps fewer, roosting deep in the oldest warrens in chambers so far from any entrance that reaching them would require crawling on hands and knees through Pallid Groan territory, through passages carved by generations of Blackthroat claws, through air that burns the eyes with ammonia. They are small, dark-furred, with disproportionately large ears that suggest a heavy reliance on echolocation. They emerge at the same hours as the moths and geckos, and they compete directly with both — eating the moths the geckos would eat, navigating the same airspace in the same darkness.

Their echolocation clicks are the highest sound on the terraces — well above the range of human hearing, perceptible only as a faint pressure or tingling in the teeth and sinuses. But other animals hear them. The moths have evolved ears tuned precisely to bat frequencies, and a moth that detects an approaching bat will fold its wings and drop from the air, falling to the terrace surface where it is safer (from bats) but more vulnerable (to geckos and centipedes). The bats have counter-adapted by varying their call frequencies, by hunting in erratic flight patterns, by sometimes falling silent and gliding, listening for the moth's own wingbeats. This is an arms race being conducted in ultrasonics in near-total darkness, and neither side is winning.

The bats also, possibly, navigate using the Pallid Groan's calls as landmarks. Deep in the warrens, where their roosts are, the Groan's low-frequency moans propagate through the óram and create a sonic map of the tunnel structure — resonances, echoes, interference patterns that differ at every junction. A bat flying through the warrens might use these sounds as a three-dimensional coordinate system, its own echolocation providing fine detail while the Groan's calls provide the large-scale architecture. This has not been proven. It may never be proven. But the bats roost only in warrens where Groans are present, and in the rare sections where Groans have disappeared, the bats leave too.

## The Invisible Majority

Below the level of what can be seen with the naked eye, the terraces teem.

**The mites** are the most numerous animals here by many orders of magnitude. There are feather mites that live on the seabirds — specialized species for each of the three architects, adapted to the particular structure and oil composition of each species' plumage. There are nest mites that live in the lining of the hollows and feed on skin flakes and feather fragments. There are dust mites in the óram itself, feeding on fungal spores and decomposing organic particles. There are predatory mites that feed on all of the above — larger, faster, orange-red, visible under magnification as tiny prowling hunters moving through a landscape of feather barbules and calcium crystals.

On the mites live smaller things still. Bacteria coat every surface. Fungal spores fill every breath of air. The immune systems of every animal here are in constant conversation with a microbial community that is as dense and as specific to this place as the frogs or the geckos. A Blackthroat's gut flora has been shaped by millennia of eating the same food and returning to the same terrace, and the guano it deposits carries those microbes into the óram, where they contribute to the chemical processes that harden the calcium-paste into something approaching stone. The birds do not merely build the terraces with their excrement and their paste. They *inoculate* them. The óram is a living material, permeated at every level with microbial life that participates in its structural integrity.

**The fungi** are the hidden architecture within the architecture. The blue-green bioluminescent species that colonizes damp surfaces is the most visible — its ghostly glow is what gives the terraces their nighttime appearance, their phantom quality, the sense that the walls are faintly alive with cold light. But beneath the surface, in the body of the óram itself, mycelia of a dozen or more species penetrate every layer. Some are decomposers, slowly breaking down the oldest material at the base. Some are symbionts, their hyphae intertwined with the root systems of the valley vegetation, transferring nutrients from the guano-rich óram to the nitrogen-hungry plants. Some are structural — their thread-like growth fills microfractures in the cured material, binding it, reinforcing it, functioning as a biological mortar that prevents the terrace walls from crumbling along their many fine fault lines.

The ants that farm some of these fungi are among the most organized creatures on the terraces, which is saying something in a place where Stormwing flocks move as one and Blackthroats return to the same hollow for decades. The ants are small, pale, nearly eyeless — subterranean in all but name, moving through tunnels in the lower óram that they have carved themselves, maintaining gardens of fungal mycelium that they feed with chewed plant matter and insect parts. Their colonies are modest by ant standards — a few thousand individuals — but there are many colonies, distributed through the base layers of every terrace wall, and their combined tunneling activity is significant enough to affect the porosity and drainage of the entire structure.

**The pseudoscorpions** — tiny, lobster-shaped, chelae raised — hunt the mites that fall from above. They live in the surface layers of the óram, in the spaces between calcium crystals, in a world so small that a single Blackthroat feather lying on a ledge is, to them, a fallen tree. They are predators of extraordinary patience. They sit motionless for hours, chelae open, waiting for a mite to wander within reach. When one does, the grab is instantaneous — too fast for a human eye to follow even if the pseudoscorpion were large enough to observe unaided. They are eaten by nothing. They are too small to be worth catching, too armored to be easily digested, too sparse to sustain a specialist predator. They are the terrace's microfauna apex, tiny lords of a kingdom measured in millimeters.

## The Vegetation, Which Also Lives

The plants in the terrace valleys are not passive. They are participants.

**The Broad-Leaf Mats** — unnamed, unstudied, known only by description — carpet the valley floors in dense, overlapping layers. Their leaves are nearly black, adapted to capture every photon of the dim, slant light. They do not photosynthesize efficiently; they don't need to. Their root systems tap into the fungal networks that connect them to the óram, from which they draw nutrients in such abundance that photosynthesis is almost secondary. They are, functionally, semi-parasitic on the birds' waste, using sunlight only enough to maintain the minimal chemical processes that the fungal pipeline cannot supply. In total darkness, they would survive for weeks. They have been observed to continue growing during the darkest periods, fueled entirely by the nutrient flow from below.

They flower — small, pale, waxy flowers that open at the darkest hours and are pollinated by the moths that fill the valleys. The moths, in turn, are pollinated by nothing — they do not need to be; they pupate from larvae that feed in the upper óram, and their adult forms exist only to mate, to pollinate, and to be eaten. The flowers produce seeds that are small, hard, and coated in an oil that attracts the Crevice Finches. The finches eat them. The finches fly to other valleys. The seeds pass through and are deposited in finch droppings on distant terrace surfaces, where they germinate in the bird-made soil of a different valley. This is the plant's entire reproductive strategy: bribe a finch, be carried, grow where you fall. It has worked for longer than any human system of agriculture.

**The Spiral Ferns** are older than the Broad-Leaf Mats — their lineage predates the colony, predates the seabirds' arrival on the plateau, reaches back to a time when this was bare rock and the ferns grew in the crevices as they grow on any tropical mountain. They have adapted to the colony's presence by growing faster, growing larger, growing denser than their ancestors could have in the nutrient-poor conditions of bare stone. Their fronds are long and pale-green and they unfurl with agonizing slowness — a single frond takes weeks to fully open — but they produce spores continuously, clouds of them, microscopic, caught by the damp air and deposited on every surface, where most die but some find a hospitable crack and begin the long process of establishing a new plant. The ferns are the valley's vertical element, their fronds arching upward toward the dim light, creating a canopy of sorts over the Broad-Leaf Mats below.

**The Seed-Grasses** are the newest arrivals, evolutionarily speaking — or so their relatively simple adaptations suggest. They grow in tussocks at the edges of the standing pools, their roots in the waterlogged soil, their seed-heads held high enough to be reached by the Crevice Finches. They are the only plants on the terraces that could plausibly survive in the lowlands — their relatives grow there, in the marshy margins of the rivers where the seabirds feed. How they arrived on the plateau is obvious: in a bird's gut, as a seed, transported up from below and deposited in the rich soil of a terrace valley. They found it hospitable. They stayed. They have diverged from their lowland relatives enough that they are recognizably different — shorter, denser, their seed-coats harder to withstand the ammonia-rich environment — but not enough to be a truly separate species. They are the colony's most recent incorporation, the latest thread woven into a fabric that has been weaving itself for geological time.

## What Is Not Here

There are no large predators. Nothing hunts the seabirds except disease and age and the occasional kestrel taking a Stormwing chick from an unguarded ledge-edge. No mammals ascend the plateau except the bats and, very rarely, a human. No snakes — the altitude and the lack of connective habitat have kept them out. No corvids, no raptors larger than the Terrace Kestrel, no scavengers large enough to carry off a carcass. Dead birds — and there are dead birds everywhere, constantly, in a colony of tens of millions; death is as constant as calling — are processed in place. They fall from ledges or collapse in hollows or die on the wing and tumble from the sky, and where they land they are consumed: by beetles first, then by larvae, then by fungi, then by the slow chemistry of decomposition that incorporates their substance back into the óram. A dead Blackthroat on a terrace ledge is reduced to scattered feathers and clean bones within days, and the bones themselves are dissolved by the acidic runoff within weeks, and within a season there is no trace — only a slight thickening of the óram layer at that point, a faint unevenness in the surface, the bird's final contribution to the structure it spent its life building.

There is also nothing here that is *aware* of the terraces as a whole. The Blackthroat knows its hollow. The Stormwing knows its ledge. The kestrel knows its hunting range. The frog knows its pool. But none of them know the terraces — none of them comprehend the structure they have collectively created, the miles-wide, forty-meter-high, centuries-deep construction that is visible from the plains below as a pale scar on the northern face of the plateau. The terraces have no architect. They have no user. They are not *for* anything. They are the residue of living, the accumulated side effect of millions of small lives doing what they do — nesting, feeding, defecating, building, dying — in the same place for long enough that the place itself has been remade.

This is, perhaps, what is most alive about the Nesting Terraces of Óramak: not any single creature but the *system*, the emergent totality, the way every organism participates in the construction and maintenance of a structure that no organism intended or understands. The birds build the walls. The insects process the waste. The fungi bind the material. The plants stabilize the soil. The frogs indicate the water quality. The centipedes ventilate the substrate. The mites clean the birds. The finches distribute the seeds. The kestrels regulate the finches. The bats and geckos regulate the moths that pollinate the plants whose roots are fed by the fungi that reinforce the walls that the birds build.

Remove any piece and the system adjusts. Remove enough pieces and it collapses — the óram crumbles, the vegetation dies back, the pools dry or go toxic, the terraces slowly erode back to bare rock. But nothing has been removed. Nothing has interrupted the cycle. The terraces have been building themselves, maintaining themselves, rebuilding after every setback, for longer than any of their inhabitants can measure.

What lives here is a single thing with millions of bodies. It does not think. It does not plan. It does not know it exists. But it builds, and what it builds endures, and what endures becomes the foundation for more building, and the process continues, and continues, and continues, and the sound of it — that enormous, layered, unceasing roar — is the sound of a world being made and remade and remade again, one small life at a time, forever.
