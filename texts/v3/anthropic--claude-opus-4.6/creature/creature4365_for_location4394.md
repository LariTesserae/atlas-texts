# The Congregants

---

They don't have a name for themselves. Or rather, they do — but it isn't a word. It's a chemical signature, a particular ratio of dissolved proteins and mineral salts that permeates the seep-water and saturates the damp air of the basin, and every one of them produces it, and every one of them tastes it constantly, and it means *this is us, this is here, this is now, we continue*. It's less a name than a metabolic fact. They are the thing that tastes like itself to itself.

---

Start with the translucent ones, because they are the most numerous and the most unsettling and the most *present*, and because when you watch the basin long enough they begin to seem less like individual creatures and more like the basin's thoughts made mobile.

They are ovoid. Slightly flattened along one axis, like a lentil pressed between two fingers, but with a gelatinous give that makes their shape contextual — they deform gently as they pass through narrow channels, squeeze between walls, slide along the drainage grooves the stone provides. Their outer membrane is a single continuous surface, clear as saliva, and through it you can see their organs: not organs exactly, but darker condensations of material, denser pockets that pulse and contract in rhythms that don't match each other. One near the center beats slowly — every four or five seconds — and seems to draw fluid inward. Another, closer to what you'd call the front, flickers rapidly, a kind of shimmering, and this one seems to correspond with movement, or perhaps with attention. When the creature pauses and orients toward something — a water bead, another creature, a crack in a wall — the flickering intensifies, concentrates, becomes almost strobing before settling into a new steady pattern.

They have cilia. Hundreds of them, fine as eyelash hairs, arranged in ventral rows that ripple in coordinated waves. This is how they move: a controlled, liquid gliding that looks almost frictionless, though if you watch carefully you can see the cilia gripping the stone's micro-texture, finding purchase in pores and mineral grain boundaries that are invisible to you. They can reverse direction without turning. They can climb vertical surfaces. They can traverse the underside of a bridge arch, hanging inverted, their cilia gripping the calcified secretion of the structure as comfortably as the raw stone. Gravity is a mild suggestion at their scale, not a law.

But the cilia do more than move them. The anterior rows — the ones closest to the front — are longer, finer, and they extend outward into the air or water like someone testing a conversation before entering it. These are sensory. Chemical, thermal, perhaps tactile. When two translucent ones meet on a path, they pause and extend these forward cilia until the tips interdigitate — the filaments of one sliding between the filaments of the other in a gesture that looks, from your vantage, almost unbearably intimate. They hold this contact for one or two seconds. Then they separate and continue, often changing direction, as if the meeting communicated something navigational. Sometimes, after contact, one of them reverses course entirely and follows the other. Sometimes both change direction, moving toward a third location neither was originally heading for. The network of these encounters, if you could map it, would look like a nervous system — signals propagating through a body that has no single brain, only a city full of synapses touching and parting and touching again.

They are the ones who gather at the base of the pale filament on the tall structure. You called it worship or maintenance, and you were probably right both times, because for them the distinction hasn't been invented yet.

---

The furred ones are different in almost every way that matters.

Where the translucent ones are smooth and continuous and look like they might have been extruded from the seep-water itself, the furred ones are dense, opaque, and segmented. They are slightly larger — apple-seed-sized, the bigger ones — and they carry their weight more heavily, their bodies lower to the ground, their locomotion achieved not by cilia but by multiple pairs of stubby, articulated limbs that emerge from between their segments. Six pairs? Eight? It depends on the individual. The number varies, and this is one of the first things that disturbs your sense of species boundary — not only between the furred ones and the translucent ones, but among the furred ones themselves. Some have more legs. Some have fewer. Some have a pair that seems vestigial, tucked against the body, twitching occasionally but never bearing weight. As if the body plan is a suggestion, a tendency rather than a blueprint.

The fur — it isn't fur. You know that, even at this distance. It's too regular, too responsive. Each filament rises from the creature's dorsal surface and extends outward at a slight angle, and they move — not with the wind, because there is no wind in the basin, but with something else. Proximity. When another creature passes nearby, the filaments on the near side lean toward it, tracking, sampling. When the air's humidity shifts — and it does, minutely, as water arrives and departs in its slow molecular commerce — the filaments ripple in a wave that propagates across the creature's back like grass responding to a breeze only it can feel.

They are sensory organs. Each one. The entire dorsal surface of the furred ones is a receptor array, a living antenna tuned to chemical and thermal and perhaps vibrational gradients in the basin's saturated atmosphere. They don't have eyes — none of them do, none of the basin's inhabitants, not in any form you'd recognize — but the furred ones don't need them. They read the air itself. They navigate by taste and temperature and the faintest pressure differentials caused by other bodies moving through a shared medium.

The furred ones are the builders. The carriers. You see them transporting granules of calcium-rich mineral — harvested from where? From the edges of the basin, where the stone's surface flakes in the thermal gradient. From the deeper channels where water has deposited its dissolved freight over centuries. They carry these granules in their anterior limbs, which are modified — slightly longer, slightly more dextrous, with tips that flatten into pads capable of gripping particles many times denser than themselves. They move in files along the drainage channels, and the directionality is clear: outward to harvest, inward to build. They bring the raw material to construction sites — a cracked wall, a new foundation, a bridge segment being extended — and there the shelled ones take over.

But not always. Sometimes the furred ones bring their cargo to the translucent ones instead, depositing granules directly onto the cilia of a waiting receiver, who absorbs them — not into the body but into a thin coating of mucus on the ventral surface, a biological adhesive that the translucent ones seem to produce in abundance and that forms the bonite, the calcified mucus, that is the city's primary construction material. The furred ones bring the calcium. The translucent ones bring the binder. The material is manufactured between them, in the contact, in the exchange. The city is made of cooperation rendered literal — a substance that cannot exist without two kinds of body contributing to it.

---

The shelled ones.

You want them to be the leaders, the administrators, the governing class. Something about their rigidity, their purposeful movement, their apparent rarity — there are far fewer of them than the other two — invites a projection of authority. But watch longer. Watch how they move. Watch what they do.

They are the smallest in number but the most varied in form. Each shelled one looks slightly different from the next: different proportions, different arrangements of plate and joint, different numbers of appendages emerging from between the segments of their carapace. One has a broad, flat ventral plate and moves like a miniature tank, pressing itself against surfaces, seeming to measure them. Another is narrower, almost tubular, and it works its way into crevices and channels where the others can't fit, disappearing into the rock's own porosity and emerging minutes later trailing a film of moisture. A third has elongated anterior appendages — delicate, branching, almost fractal — that it uses to manipulate the wet secretion at a construction site, shaping it with movements so precise they look surgical.

Their shells are not uniform. Not chitin exactly, not calcium carbonate exactly, but something in between — a composite material that, under the basin's amber light, has a faintly iridescent quality, shifting between dull bronze and deep violet depending on the angle. The shells are layered, and if you could look at a cross-section you might see growth rings, like a tree, each layer deposited over a period you cannot guess at. They are not shed. They are not molted. They grow by accretion, the creature adding to itself from the outside in, incorporating minerals from the water and the stone into its own architecture. The oldest shelled ones — and you can tell which these are, because their carapaces are thicker, darker, the iridescence muted under accumulated layers — are slightly larger than the rest and move more slowly, their joints audibly clicking against each other in a rhythm that contributes to the basin's hum.

The shelled ones are the ones who go to the rim.

You watched it happen three times. Now you understand: it is always a shelled one. Always one of the older ones, with the thick carapace and the deliberate gait. It emerges from a structure near the edge — always the same structure, which is slightly larger than the others and positioned where the basin's floor begins its upward slope toward the world outside. It climbs the gentle grade. Its legs find purchase on the smoother stone, where the seep-water doesn't reach and the surface is drier, less hospitable. The sensory filaments — fewer than the furred ones have, but present, emerging from joints in the shell like sparse whiskers — extend and sample the air.

Dry air. Foreign air. Air that doesn't carry the chemical signature of *us, here, now, continuing*.

The shelled one holds this position for almost exactly the same duration each time. Long enough to sample. Long enough to confirm. Then it turns — slowly, carefully, its feet finding the familiar micro-texture of the basin's interior stone — and descends back into the city. Back to the structure. And something happens after each return, though you can't see it clearly: a change in traffic patterns. A shift in the direction of the carrier files. A redistribution of the translucent ones' gathering points. As if the shelled one brought back a report, and the report was acted on.

*The world is still out there. The world is still dry. The world is still not us. We stay.*

---

But there are others. Of course there are. A city this dense, this old, this intricately maintained, cannot be home to only three kinds of body.

In the deepest channels — the ones eroded so far into the stone that they're more like tunnels now, their original openings half-collapsed and rebuilt and collapsed again — things move that you can barely see. They are darker than the stone, which is already nearly black in the low light, and they move slowly, and they are large. Large for this basin: the size of a small beetle, you said, and that's right, but they carry their size differently than any beetle you've known. They are soft. Boneless. They compress and extend as they navigate the tunnels, their bodies conforming to the passage's shape like a tongue filling a mouth. Their surface is slick and unmarked — no cilia, no fur, no shell. Just a smooth, dark integument that glistens faintly with the same moisture that coats everything in the basin.

These deep ones rarely come to the surface. When they do, the other creatures give them space — not fearfully, not deferentially, but the way you might step aside for a river. The deep ones move through the city as if the city is incidental to them, as if their real business is with the rock itself, with the water that seeps up from below, with the thermal gradient that keeps the basin warm. They press their bodies against the stone at the deepest points and hold still for long periods, and during these periods the seep-water seems to arrive faster, as if they are somehow facilitating its passage, widening pores, encouraging the capillary action that brings moisture to the surface.

Are they feeding? Are they farming? Are they maintaining the plumbing?

Yes. All of it. The distinction doesn't apply.

They are the oldest thing in the basin. Not individually — no single creature here lives longer than what you'd call a season, though the seasons in the basin bear no relation to the ones above — but as a type, as a lineage. The deep channels they inhabit are the city's oldest structures, the ones embedded in the eroded walls, the foundations of foundations. They were here before the translucent ones evolved their cilia. They were here before the furred ones grew their sensory arrays. They were here before the shelled ones hardened. They were here when the basin was just a warm, wet depression in the rock and the first film of biofilm established itself on the stone, and something grazed on it, and something grazed on the grazer, and over uncountable generations the whole system folded inward on itself, complexity accreting like the layers of a shell, until it became this: a city, or what a city becomes when it forgets what it was for and becomes simply a way of continuing.

The deep ones remember nothing. But they carry in their bodies a chemical continuity with the basin's first inhabitants — certain protein structures, certain metabolic pathways, that have been conserved across all the branching and specialization that produced the translucent ones and the furred ones and the shelled ones. They are the root. Not the ruler. The root.

---

And then there are the parasites.

No — that word carries too much judgment. Call them the *riders*. Call them the small-smalls, the ones who live not in the city but on and within the citizens, the ones for whom the body of a translucent one or a furred one is itself a landscape, a basin within the basin, a habitat within the habitat.

They are nearly invisible. Smaller than the smallest grain of sand. They cluster in the mucus layer of the translucent ones, feeding on the binder-substance, the construction material, siphoning off a fraction before it can be deposited. They nest in the fur-filaments of the furred ones, each one occupying a single filament like a bird on a wire, absorbing whatever chemical intelligence passes through. They wedge themselves into the growth-ring seams of the shelled ones' carapaces and slowly, slowly, dissolve the mineral layers from within, creating tiny hollows that they fill with their own bodies, their own offspring.

Are they harmful? In aggregate, over time, they must cost their hosts something. A translucent one carrying a heavy load of riders produces less binder. A furred one whose filaments are occupied reads the air less clearly. A shelled one whose carapace has been internally riddled moves more stiffly, its joints grinding.

But they have been here as long as the city has. The hosts have not evolved to eliminate them. The riders have not evolved to kill their hosts. An equilibrium has been reached — not a happy one, not a designed one, but a stable one, a mutual tolerance born of deep time and the impossibility, in a system this enclosed, of eliminating anything without eliminating some part of yourself.

The riders are part of the hum.

---

Now. Stop categorizing. Stand back from the taxonomy you've been building and look at the basin again.

What lives here is not five kinds of creature in a city. What lives here is a single metabolic event, extended in time, differentiated in space, that has been running since the rock was young enough to crack. The translucent ones and the furred ones and the shelled ones and the deep ones and the riders are not separate species cohabiting. They are organs of a single process, the way your liver and your lungs and your skin are organs of you. The city is not something they built. The city is something they *are* — the mineralized external skeleton of a collective organism that breathes through seep-water and thinks through chemical contact and remembers through architecture and defines itself by the simple, endlessly repeated act of tasting its own presence in the saturated air.

*This is us. This is here. This is now. We continue.*

---

The shelled one goes to the rim again. The filaments extend. The dry air touches them. Information travels back into the body of the city through pathways you cannot trace: chemical cascades in the seep-water, vibrations in the stone, the altered trajectories of a thousand translucent ones adjusting their routes in response to something they can taste but not see.

The report is the same as it has always been.

The world outside is vast and dry and empty and extends in every direction under a sky that doesn't care. The world inside is the size of a dining table and warm and wet and full of mouths — mouths that eat and mouths that build and mouths that speak in chemistry and mouths that taste the air for threats that have never come and mouths that press against the stone to coax water up from the dark.

The basin hums. The city continues. The creatures — the congregants, the collective, the single ancient organism wearing a thousand thousand tiny bodies — go about the only work they know, which is the work of being what they are in the only place that will have them.

They do not wonder what they are for. They have never wondered this. The wondering would require a kind of distance from themselves that the basin does not permit. They are too close to each other, too saturated with each other's presence, too continuously engaged in the chemical murmur of mutual recognition. There is no remove from which to ask the question. There is only the hum, and the water, and the warmth, and the slow patient work of building something out of what your own body and someone else's body can make together when pressed against ancient stone.

They are, perhaps, content. But that's your word, projected from your height, your dryness, your distance. They are simply *continuing*, which is the oldest verb, older than contentment, older than purpose, older than the basin itself. They are the continuation continuing. They are the hum humming. They are the water arriving.

They are what lives here.
