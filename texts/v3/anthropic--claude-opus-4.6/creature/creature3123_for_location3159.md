# Who Lives on the Screaming Plateau

---

The first thing to understand is that they did not leave.

The second thing to understand is that they are not hiding.

The third thing is harder, and it will take time.

---

## The Crevice People

They are down in the fissures. They have always been down in the fissures. When you walked across the plateau's surface and heard the clicking in the deep cracks, the tapping, the rhythmic scraping — that was them. Not all of it. Some of it was the beetles, some was root-growth, some was stone settling against stone in ways that mimic intention. But the pattern you noticed, the one that sounded like communication, the one that sounded like argument — that was them.

They are small. Not dwarfed by malnutrition or some pathological compression, but small the way the dwarf trees are small — by design, by deep time, by the relentless editorial pressure of a place that punishes anything that rises above the line of the wind. An adult, fully grown, stands perhaps to your sternum. Their bones are dense, denser than yours, dense the way the dwarf-tree wood is dense, as if their skeletons remembered a time when they were taller and compressed all that height into less vertical space. They are broad across the shoulders and hips but thin through the chest, their ribcages laterally compressed in a way that would look wrong on anyone else but which makes them aerodynamic, allows them to turn sideways to the gale and present an edge rather than a face. Their centers of gravity are low. They do not stand fully upright on the open rock. They move in a crouch that is not a crouch — it is their natural posture, spine curved forward, knees perpetually soft, hands ready to grip the stone at any moment. Watching them traverse a wind-blasted surface is like watching a wrestler who has been wrestling the same opponent for ten thousand years: every shift of weight is a response to a force you cannot see, every step is a negotiation.

Their skin is dark. Not in the way you might expect — not the darkness of melanin alone, though there is that — but a particular matte darkness, almost powdery, that comes from a lifetime of fine basalt dust ground into the skin's surface by the wind. They do not wash it off. It is a layer, a coating, part of them. It protects against the UV that is stronger at this altitude, and it makes them, when crouched and still against the rock, nearly invisible. You almost certainly walked within a few meters of one on your first day and did not see them. They saw you. They watched you with considerable interest and no alarm, because they have seen your kind before — not often, not recently, but enough times across the generations that you are a known category. You are a below-person. You come up. You stay briefly. You leave. You are a weather event, no different in kind from a rare eastern wind or an unusual fog.

---

Their hair grows thick and lies flat, like the fur on the mammalian creatures you saw pressed against the rock — and this is not a coincidence; the same pressures shaped both. It grows in a pattern you have never seen: rooted densely at the crown and temples but sparse at the back of the skull, so that when they face into the wind (which is most of the time) the hair streams backward and covers the nape and upper back like a short mane, a heat-retaining, wind-deflecting cape that they were born wearing. In some individuals, the hair extends down the spine in a narrow strip that broadens at the lower back. The color is uniformly dark, but in the late-afternoon light, when the plateau runs with water, you might notice — if you were close enough, which you will not be — that the hair has a faint reddish undertone, like iron in basalt, like rust in the oldest stone.

Their eyes are narrow and deep-set, protected by brow ridges that are pronounced enough to cast small shadows. The eyes themselves are extraordinary. They are adapted to constant tears — the wind ensures that the lacrimal glands work overtime — and the tears do not streak down the face but are channeled by subtle grooves in the skin beside the nose into the corners of the mouth. They drink their own tears. Not ceremonially. Constantly, unconsciously, the way you breathe. In a place where water exists only as condensation and momentary rivulets, no moisture is wasted. Their lips are often wet when the rest of their face is dry, and this gives them an expression that you might misread as anticipation, or hunger, or the aftermath of speech. They are simply hydrating.

The ears are the strangest feature, and the one that takes the longest to understand. They are not small. You would expect small ears, pressed flat against the skull, streamlined against the wind — and indeed, the external pinnae are reduced, flattened, tucked tight. But behind them, inside them, something else has happened. The auditory canal is longer than yours, and it spirals. The bones of the middle ear are thicker, more numerous — there is, perhaps, an extra ossicle, or a branching of one of the existing three, though you would need to look inside a skull to confirm this, and you will not be looking inside any skulls. The result is a hearing system that can filter the wind. Not block it. Filter it. They hear *through* the roar the way you might hear a conversation through a waterfall if you had spent your entire evolutionary history standing beside that waterfall. The constant scream of the plateau is, for them, a background — still audible, still present, but parsed, its components separated and catalogued by an auditory system that treats the wind's chord the way a musician treats an orchestra, hearing individual instruments within the whole. They can detect the click of a beetle's mandibles at thirty meters in a full gale. They can hear the specific frequency shift that means a vortex is forming two hundred meters upwind and will arrive in approximately eleven seconds. They can hear you. Right now. Your breathing, your heartbeat, the creak of whatever you are wearing, the particular tonal signature of your body displacing air. They know exactly where you are and they have known since you arrived.

## How They Live

They live in the fissures. This is not a concession or a retreat; this is mastery. The plateau's network of stress fractures — those deep cracks, a meter or two wide and ten or fifteen meters deep, that lace the surface like veins — is their world. They have been modifying these cracks for longer than the stone shelters on the surface have existed. The shelters were, you now realize, not homes. They were waypoints. Temporary staging areas for surface work. The real habitation is below.

Down in the cracks, out of the worst of the wind, the crevice-gardens grow. You saw these from above — the ferns, the wire-grasses, the sealed flowers, the dwarf trees. What you did not see, because you did not climb down, is that the gardens are tended. Not in the way you tend a garden — not with rows and schedules and the imposition of order on chaos. Tended the way a reef is tended by the organisms that live in it: through constant, intimate, mutual adjustment. The people move through the fissure-gardens the way the beetles move across the rock surface, following invisible paths of least resistance, and as they move, they tend. A hand reaches out and redirects a vine that is growing toward a root system it would strangle. A foot, passing, presses a loose mat of moss more firmly into a crack where it will anchor and thrive. Seeds are carried — not deliberately gathered and planted, but carried in the hair, in the folds of the sparse garments, in the grooves of the skin itself, and deposited wherever the carrier happens to pause. The gardens are not planted. They are *inhabited*, and the inhabiting is the planting.

The deeper fissures — and some go down twenty meters, thirty, widening in places into chambers where the original fracture intersected a softer stratum that has since eroded out — are where the life happens. The chambers are connected by the fissures themselves, creating a network that runs beneath the plateau's surface like a root system of its own. Some chambers are sleeping places: lined with mats of the dense wire-grass, roofed by the interlocking canopies of dwarf trees that have been trained (over decades, over centuries) to grow across the gap, their flat canopies meshing into a living ceiling that filters the wind to a murmur. Some chambers are work areas, where the basalt is knapped into tools — and the tools are more varied than the few you found on the surface suggested. There are fine blades, delicate scrapers, awls that could bore through leather or wood, and grinding stones with concave surfaces worn smooth by the processing of seeds and dried plant matter into something between flour and paste. There are lengths of the twisted grass cordage in various thicknesses, some fine enough to serve as thread, some thick as your finger and strong enough to bear a person's weight. There are nets, loosely woven, designed not for catching animals but for catching the wind's cargo — the seeds, the spores, the fragments of vegetative matter that fly across the plateau at speed. The nets are strung across the upper portions of the fissures, and they harvest the wind's botanical freight the way a whale's baleen harvests krill.

Food. They eat the dense, starchy roots of the wire-grasses, which must be pounded and soaked (in condensation water collected in basalt basins) before they become edible. They eat the seeds of the sealed flowers, which are tiny and numerous and taste, reportedly, like iron. They eat a preparation made from the inner bark of the dwarf trees — not often, because the trees are slow-growing and valued, but when the root harvest is poor. They eat the beetles. The beetles are, in fact, a primary protein source, and the relationship between the people and the beetles is complicated and long-standing. The beetles graze on the moss mats; the people harvest the beetles from specific surfaces where the moss is allowed to grow thick specifically for this purpose. There is a cycle here, a managed ecology that is so deeply embedded it looks like nature. The beetles are not domesticated. They are *integrated*. The people understand their movement patterns, their breeding cycles, their preferred grazing surfaces, and they manage all of these through subtle modifications of the crevice-garden environment — encouraging moss here, discouraging it there, opening a fissure wall to create a new wind-shadow where moss will grow and beetles will congregate. It is farming without any of the gestures you associate with farming. It is farming that looks like landscape.

And they eat something else. In the deepest chambers, where the darkness is total and the wind is only a distant vibration in the rock, there are fungi. Not the familiar shelf-fungi of wetter places, but something unique to this geology: a mycelial network that lives *in the basalt itself*, threading through microfractures in the rock, digesting mineral traces, fruiting in absolute darkness in the form of small, dense, almost calcite-hard nodules that must be crushed and boiled (in condensation water, always condensation water, every drop accounted for) before they can be eaten. The nodules are pale, nearly white, and they glow — faintly, greenly, with a bioluminescence that is the only light in the deep chambers. The people carry them. Not as lamps, because the light is too faint for that, but as — well. You will get to that.

## How They Speak

They do not shout. You expected shouting. In a place where the wind screams ceaselessly, you expected a people who had learned to scream louder. But screaming louder than this wind is not possible, and they are not a people who attempt the impossible.

They click. They tap. They scrape.

The language is percussive, transmitted through the rock and the dense air of the fissures by sharp, short sounds that cut through the wind's broadband roar the way a knife cuts through fabric — not by being stronger than the fabric but by being narrower, more focused, occupying a frequency band that the wind does not. The clicks are produced by the tongue against the palate, the teeth, the inside of the cheek — a system superficially similar to certain click languages you may know, but developed independently and to a much greater degree of complexity. There are, by the most conservative estimate, over two hundred distinct clicks, differentiated by location, force, duration, and the shape of the oral cavity at the moment of production. Some are loud enough to carry across the open plateau surface; these are the alarm calls, the navigational signals, the long-distance summons. Others are so quiet they can only be heard in the sheltered depth of a fissure-chamber, and these are the intimate sounds — the words for specific emotions, for the names of individuals, for the subtle conditions of the wind that are relevant only if you are standing in a particular spot and deciding whether it is safe to cross to the next crack.

The tapping is done with the hands or with tools against the rock, and it carries through the stone itself, transmitted along the fissure network the way sound travels through a pipe. This is how they communicate over distance. A person in a chamber on the plateau's eastern edge can send a message to a person two kilometers west by striking the rock wall in a specific pattern, and the message will arrive in seconds, carried by the dense basalt at the speed of sound in stone, which is faster and more reliable than any shout. The network of fissures is, in effect, a telephone system — not designed as such, but used as such, the geological accident of the fracture pattern exploited by a people who had millennia to learn its acoustics. Certain junctions in the fissure network are gathering points, places where multiple cracks intersect and a message tapped into one wall can be heard in three or four chambers simultaneously. These are the public squares, the meeting halls. The carved niches you found in the stone shelter on the surface? Some of them were not for storage. They were resonance chambers, carefully shaped to amplify tapped signals from the fissure below and make them audible on the surface. The shelter was not just a shelter. It was a communications hub.

The scraping is something else. The scraping is music, or prayer, or both, or neither — it is the sound they make with flat stones drawn slowly across the basalt in the deep chambers, producing a frequency that resonates with the rock, that sets the bioluminescent fungus-nodules glowing brighter, that makes the condensation on the walls tremble and run. It is the sound they make together, many of them, in the late afternoon when the plateau sings, matching their scraping to the harmonics of the wind-organ above, adding a human voice (a human *friction*) to the geological chorus. You heard it and did not know what it was. You thought it was the wind finding a new channel. It was not. It was them, singing with the plateau, in the only way the plateau allows.

## How They Are With Each Other

There are perhaps two hundred of them. Perhaps fewer. It is difficult to count a population that is distributed through a three-dimensional network of cracks in an area of dozens of square kilometers, a population that moves constantly, that does not gather in one place except during the late-afternoon resonance, and that is, by deep habit, disinclined to be visible.

They live in small groups — three, four, five individuals who share a cluster of adjacent chambers and tend the gardens between them. The groups are not families in any way you would recognize. They are not organized by kinship, or by age, or by gender (and gender, as far as you can tell from the glimpses you have had, is not a primary organizing category here — the wind does not care, and the rock does not care, and the beetles do not care, and so the people have largely stopped caring, though there are differences, and the differences are known and named in the click-language with a precision and a casualness that suggests a culture long past the point of anxiety about such things). The groups are organized by *resonance*. This is the word you settle on, though it is not quite right. Each cluster of fissure-chambers has its own acoustic properties — its own echo, its own harmonic response to the tapped signals, its own particular relationship to the wind-sound filtering down from above. The people choose their living spaces the way a musician chooses an instrument: by timbre, by feel, by an affinity that is partly aesthetic and partly physical and partly something you do not have a word for. A person might live in one cluster for years and then move to another because the resonance has changed — because a rockfall has altered the acoustic properties of a chamber, or because the person themselves has changed, their hearing shifting with age, their preference for certain frequencies deepening or fading. The groups reform. They are fluid. They are held together not by obligation but by the simple fact that they sound right together, that their clicks and taps and scrapes harmonize in the space they share.

Children are raised by the cluster, whichever cluster they happen to be born into, and they move between clusters freely as they grow, sampling the acoustics, finding their place. A child's first independent act — the thing that marks the transition from dependence to personhood — is choosing a chamber. Not being assigned one. Choosing. Walking (crawling, climbing) through the fissure network until they find the space that sounds like them, and beginning to tend the garden there, and tapping on the wall to say: *I am here. This is where I am.*

They touch each other constantly. In the fissures, where space is tight and the wind is reduced but not absent, bodies are in frequent contact — shoulders pressing together in narrow passages, hands steadying each other on climbs, the simple proximity of people sleeping in a chamber barely large enough for three. The touch is unselfconscious, utilitarian, and also tender. You see (if you are lucky enough to see, if you have waited quietly in a crevice long enough for them to decide you are a weather event and ignore you) — you see an adult adjusting a child's hair to better cover the nape, a gesture so practiced and gentle it barely registers as intentional. You see two people sitting side by side in a wind-shadow on the surface, their outer arms locked together at the elbow for mutual bracing against a gust, their inner hands separately occupied — one knapping a tool, the other mending a net — while their locked arms communicate through pressure and micro-adjustment a continuous real-time conversation about the wind's behavior that requires no clicks, no taps, no language at all. They are reading the air through each other's bodies.

There is conflict. The argument you heard — the irregular clicking — was real. Disputes arise over garden management, over chamber allocation, over the proper tending of a beetle-grazing surface, over the deep and apparently inexhaustible question of how to best maintain the fissure network's acoustic integrity. (A rockfall that improves the resonance in one chamber may ruin it in another; the decision of whether and how to clear debris is, here, an aesthetic and social crisis of the first order.) Disputes are settled by a process you can only partially reconstruct from observation: the involved parties retreat to separate chambers and tap their arguments into the rock, and the arguments travel through the fissure network, and other people hear them, and respond with taps of their own, and the conversation propagates through the stone until something like consensus coalesces — not unanimity, but a rhythm, a shared pattern of tapping that means *this is what we will do*, and the pattern is picked up and repeated until it becomes part of the network's background sound, absorbed into the geological hum. Disputes are not resolved. They are *composted*. They become part of the acoustic substrate of the place, layered into the ongoing conversation between people and rock and wind that has been running for longer than anyone can remember.

## What They Know

They know the wind. This is not a metaphor or a generalization. They know the wind the way you know the face of someone you have loved for decades — every line, every shift of expression, every mood. They know that the pitch of the scream through the third arch from the western edge drops by approximately a quarter-tone when rain is twelve hours away. They know that a particular rolling vortex, which forms in the same place every few days, has been gradually shifting its track northward over the course of generations, and that this shift correlates with changes in the moss distribution on the upper terrace that will, in another generation, require a relocation of three beetle-grazing surfaces. They know that the forty-minute wet period at sunset is not constant — it varies by season, by the multi-year cycles of the lowland climate they have never directly observed, by a longer cycle still that they track through the accumulated depth of soil in certain crevices and that operates on a timescale of centuries. They know these things not as abstract data but as felt, embodied, inherited knowledge — knowledge carried in the language, in the resonance-preferences of the chambers, in the way a particular garden is tended, in the precise angle at which a net is strung to catch the wind's cargo. Their knowledge of this place is so deep and so integrated that it is indistinguishable from the place itself. They do not *study* the plateau. They *are* the plateau, in the same way that the moss and the beetles and the dwarf trees are the plateau — organisms shaped by and for this specific set of conditions, unimaginable anywhere else.

They know the rock. They know which basalt formations are stable and which are in the late stages of erosion, ready to collapse in the next decade or the next century. They know the sound a pillar makes — the specific harmonic it contributes to the plateau's chord — in the years before it falls, a subtle flattening of its resonant frequency as internal fractures propagate. They mourn the falling of formations. Not with ceremony or obvious grief, but with a silence — a deliberate cessation of tapping in the network, a pause that propagates through the stone the way the arguments do, and that means *something has ended that will not come again*. The pause lasts exactly as long as it lasts. No one decides when it ends. It ends when someone, somewhere in the network, begins to tap again, and the tapping spreads, and the conversation resumes, altered by the absence of a frequency that was part of it and is now gone.

They know the stars. On clear nights, from the surface, the stars are extraordinary — hard, bright, trembling in the disturbed air — and the people come up from the fissures to watch them, lying flat on the polished rock in whatever wind-shadows they can find, their dense bodies pressed against the stone, their filtered ears listening to the night-wind's different song while their deep-set eyes track the slow wheeling of the sky. What they know about the stars, you cannot determine. They do not build observatories or mark alignments. But they tap about them. There are click-sequences that seem to correspond to specific stars or constellations, and these sequences appear in certain patterns during certain seasons, and you suspect — though you cannot prove — that the stars are woven into the same vast body of knowledge as the wind and the rock, that the people track celestial cycles the way they track vortex migration and moss distribution, as part of a single integrated understanding of the systems that govern their world. They do not separate sky from stone. Why would they? The wind comes from elsewhere and touches both.

## What They Carry

The bioluminescent fungus-nodules. You noticed them earlier. They carry them.

Each person has several, kept in a small pouch made of dwarf-tree bark and grass-fiber cordage, worn against the body where it stays warm. The nodules glow faintly, greenly, and the glow intensifies when they are vibrated — when they are scraped, or when the resonance-singing fills the chambers, or when a person holds one in their cupped hands and hums (yes, hums — the one vocalization they make, a low sound below the wind's register, felt more than heard, directed into the nodule through the bones of the hands). The glow is not useful as light. It is too faint. But it is *personal*. Each nodule has been harvested from a specific depth, a specific location in the deep rock, and its bioluminescence has a specific character — a shade of green that varies subtly depending on the mineral composition of the basalt it grew in, a pulse rate that reflects the mycelial network's local metabolism. No two are alike. A person's collection of nodules is, in some sense, a map of where they have been in the deep places, a record of their relationship with the rock's interior life.

They exchange them. This is, as far as you can determine, the closest thing they have to a ritual, or a gift economy, or a declaration of significance between persons. One person gives a nodule to another. The other receives it. The exchange happens in the deep chambers, in near-darkness, and the two nodules — the one given and one offered in return — are held together, and their combined glow is briefly brighter, and the two people watch the light in each other's cupped hands, and something is communicated that you will never fully understand because it is in a language of light and mineral and the specific resonance of two bodies in a stone room and it was never meant for you.

## What They Think of You

They think you are very tall and very loud and very temporary. They are not wrong about any of these things.

They have watched you for two days. They have discussed you — you heard the discussion, the clicking and tapping in the rock, though you did not know it was about you. The consensus, as far as you can parse it from the rhythms (and you cannot, really, you are guessing, you are projecting, but the guess feels sound) — the consensus is that you are interesting but not worrying. You do not tend. You do not listen to the rock. You stand on the surface in the full wind like something that has never been scoured. You will leave.

There is, in the click-language, a word for people like you. You will never hear it clearly enough to reproduce it, but it translates, roughly, as: *one who does not know where the wind is going next*. It is not an insult. It is a description. It is, perhaps, the mildest possible way of saying: *you do not belong here*. Which is true. You do not. This place is theirs — not by conquest or by claim but by the slow, total, bodily fact of ten thousand years of fitting, of being shaped and shaping, of becoming the thing that can live here because everything that could not live here has already been stripped away.

They are the plateau's hardest version of a person. They are what remains when everything soft has been taken by the wind.

## What Will Happen

The plateau is eroding. You read this in the description of the place: it is in its final form, the bones already slowly going. The fissures widen. Chambers that were stable for centuries develop new fractures. The formations fall, one by one, over millennia, and each one takes a frequency out of the wind's chord, and the chord changes, and the people's language changes with it — because the clicks are calibrated to cut through the wind's specific noise, and when the noise changes, the clicks must change, and when the clicks change, the thoughts they carry change, and the people become, incrementally, someone else.

They know this. They know it the way the dwarf trees know the wind — not as a fact held in the mind but as a condition of existence, a pressure applied to every moment. They are not sad about it. Sadness requires the belief that things could be otherwise. They do not believe things could be otherwise. The plateau is going. They are on the plateau. They will go with it, or before it, or they will change so completely in the going that the question of continuity becomes meaningless. The fungus-nodules will glow in the deep rock long after the last chamber has collapsed. The mycelial network will persist, threading through whatever basalt remains, fruiting in darkness for no one. The beetles will outlast the people, probably. The moss will outlast the beetles. The wind will outlast everything.

But that is later. That is geological later, which is not the same as human later.

For now — for the long, strange, wind-hammered now that is the only time they have ever known — they are here. In the cracks. In the gardens. In the deep chambers where the fungus glows green and the resonance-singing makes the water run on the walls. They are tapping messages through the stone. They are tending the beetle-grazing surfaces. They are lying flat on the polished rock at night, watching the stars tremble through the disturbed air, their dense bodies pressed against the body of the plateau, their filtered ears hearing, through the scream, the particular silence that means a vortex is forming to the west and will arrive in eleven seconds and will require a subtle shift of weight, a tightening of the locked arms, a micro-adjustment so practiced it is not even conscious — just the body doing what the body has learned to do, over ten thousand years of not being blown away.

They are what lives here. They are what the wind has not yet taken.
