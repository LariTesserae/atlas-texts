# Who Lives in Kethane-Above

---

## The Workers

Start with the largest truth: most of the people in Kethane-Above are workers, and they look like workers, and they move like workers, and this is so fundamental to the city's character that everything else must be understood as growing out of it or pressing against it.

They are not a single ethnicity or type. The city has drawn people — has *always* drawn people, according to its own spotty records — from wherever *below* is, and below is apparently multiple places, because the faces in the market district span a range of features and complexions that suggests several distinct populations of origin. But Kethane-Above does something to the people who stay. Not quickly. Not violently. It is more like what the plateau does to its own rock: it begins a slow negotiation with their bodies, and after a decade or two the differences between a worker from one below-place and a worker from another begin to matter less than the similarities they've developed from living *here*.

Their skin, for instance. Long-term residents develop a faint translucency at the extremities — fingertips, earlobes, the thin skin at the temples — that isn't visible in bright light (not that bright light is available) but becomes apparent in the plateau's phosphorescent nightglow, when the edges of their hands seem to catch and hold the faint luminescence the way a glass catches candlelight. It is not illness. The city's physicians — there are physicians, of a kind, though their practice has had to accommodate phenomena that no medical school below would recognize — regard it as adaptation. The body learning to negotiate with the plateau's light conditions. Children born in Kethane-Above have it from birth, more pronounced, so that a crèche at night looks like a room full of small lanterns, and the mothers touch their babies' glowing fingers and feel nothing unusual, because this is what fingers do, here.

Their lungs are larger. Not grotesquely so. You wouldn't notice it looking at them. But the slight insufficiency of the air has, over generations, selected for and encouraged a deep-chestedness, a barrel-ribbed solidity, that gives even the thin and wiry workers of the conveyor lines a certain *anchoredness* of frame. They breathe slowly. They breathe *deliberately*. A newcomer from below, gasping and light-headed in the first weeks, watches a Kethane worker haul a loaded cart up a grade and marvels at the economy of their respiration — three deep, measured breaths where the newcomer would need ten shallow ones. This is not supernatural. This is what living at this altitude for generations will do. But it contributes to an impression — an impression the citizens of Kethane-Above cultivate, perhaps unconsciously — of people who are *settled*, who are *dense*, who have gravity in them even when the gravity outside is misbehaving.

Their hands are the most telling thing. Everyone in Kethane-Above works. There are no idle rich, or if there are, they have not been idle long enough to lose the calluses. And the calluses here are distinctive because the materials are distinctive. A worker in the stamp mills has palms stained dark by ore dust that is not any ore from below — it changes color over the course of a week, cycling through deep reds and blacks and a peculiar iridescent green, and it does not wash off so much as *migrate*, spreading gradually up the forearms in patterns that look, if you squint, almost intentional, almost like script. Conveyor-line workers develop a fine, silvery residue on their fingers from handling the sympathetic alloys, and this residue makes their touch faintly magnetic: they can pick up iron filings, can feel the hum of electrical current in a wire just by holding it. Factory workers who process the spiral-stalk structures — rendering them down into compounds that have pharmaceutical applications below — have hands that smell permanently of ozone and wet stone, and their fingerprints, over years, become unusually deep and complex, as though the biological material of their skin is responding to the quasi-biological material they handle by becoming more *itself*, more articulated, more resolute in its patterning.

They work long shifts, but the shifts are strange, because time in Kethane-Above is strange. The standard work period is measured not in hours but in *cycles* — the interval between steam-whistle blasts, which, as noted, follow no regular schedule. A worker might labor for what feels like six hours and discover that three have passed, or vice versa. The body adjusts. Long-term residents develop an internal clock that is synchronized not to solar time (useless here, where the sun is a rumor behind pewter clouds) but to the ground note, to the plateau's own deep rhythm, which has a periodicity that is not regular but is *predictable* in the way weather is predictable — not precisely, but with a felt sense, a body-knowledge. Workers know when a shift is ending the way a farmer knows rain is coming: not from evidence but from a change in the quality of their attention.

They are tired. They are often tired. But it is a specific kind of tiredness — not the exhaustion of despair or overwork, though both exist here, but a tiredness that has *texture*, that is woven through with a kind of alertness. Because living in Kethane-Above requires attention. Requires that you notice when the gravity shifts, when the distance between your home and your workplace has dilated, when the wrench you left on the bench is developing wood grain. The tiredness of the workers is the tiredness of people who cannot fully relax their awareness, even in sleep, because the parameters of their environment are always, slowly, changing. It shows in their eyes, which have a quality of *active rest* — not anxious, not watchful in the way of hunted things, but *calibrated*, continuously adjusting, the way a sailor's balance continuously adjusts to the sea.

## The Families

They have families. Of course they have families. This is perhaps the most remarkable thing about Kethane-Above — not that people came here, which might be explained by economic desperation or adventurism or exile — but that people *stayed* and *had children* and *raised those children* and those children had children, and now there are lineages in this city, family names that mean something, grandmothers who remember when the temporal layering in the old quarter was thin enough to see through.

The family structures are dense and practical. Housing is multigenerational by necessity — apartments in the tenement-factories are large by room count but small by square footage, and a typical household includes grandparents, parents, children, and often an unmarried aunt or uncle or cousin whose role in the family economy is specific and acknowledged. The grandmother manages the household's relationship with the building itself, because the building is always slowly changing — she knows which walls are developing sympathetic properties and need to be kept clear of prolonged contact with household objects, which floors have begun to slope as the underlying rock reconsiders its angle, where the moss is getting in and whether it should be discouraged or accepted. This is a full-time occupation. The practical knowledge of how to live in a Kethane building is vast and largely oral, passed from the older women to whoever is paying attention, and it encompasses structural engineering, materials science, and something that is not quite mysticism but is certainly not empiricism either — a *feel* for what the building wants, for what the rock beneath the building is becoming, for how the two of these desires intersect and where they might conflict.

Children in Kethane-Above grow up fluent in impossibility. They play in the empty lots where the groundcover hums after rain, and they know which lots are safe and which are currently experiencing spatial dilation (a ball thrown in a dilated lot travels an absurd distance and returns at an unexpected angle, which makes for a game that Kethane children play and that no child from below could learn without years of practice — the rules change with the terrain, literally, and the children adjust their throws with an intuitive, laughing precision that adult physicists would find disturbing). They keep burrowers as pets, carrying them in pockets, feeding them crumbs, naming them with the solemn inventiveness of children everywhere. They watch the ridge-soarers with a possessive familiarity — *that one, the big one with the torn wing-edge, that's Old Ferik, he's been here since Grandmother was young* — and they claim to understand the crooning sounds the soarers make on the rooftops, though the claims are probably inventions, probably games. Probably.

The children born here are *of* here in a way their parents and grandparents are not, and everyone can see it, and no one talks about it much. The translucency is stronger in them. Their eyes adjust to the permanent dusk faster and more completely, the pupils wide and dark and capable of discriminations of shadow that adults from below cannot match. Some of them — not all, not even most, but enough to be remarked upon — seem to have a sensitivity to the ground note that goes beyond hearing. They will stop in the middle of play and press their hands flat against the rock and go still, listening with their palms, and then say things like *it's going to be hard here soon* or *this part is almost done thinking*, and within weeks the rock in that spot will have resolved into a definite mineral, or a new spatial anomaly will have begun. The adults do not make much of this. They note it. They factor it into their planning. They do not call it prophecy or magic. They call it *good ears*.

There are teenagers who are restless, who look at the cable-car lines descending into the cloudbank and want to go *down*, to see what below is, to live somewhere where the ground stays the same shape and the light comes on properly in the morning. Some of them go. The cable cars descend carrying Kethane's strange alloys and exports and, sometimes, young people with glowing fingertips and deep chests and a sensitivity to geological vibration that will never entirely leave them. Some of these come back. Many do not. The ones who come back have a particular expression — not quite relief, not quite defeat — a complex settling of the features that the other residents recognize instantly. *Below wasn't wrong*, the expression says. *But it was finished. And I am not.*

## The Engineers

In a city where the fundamental rules shift, engineering is not a profession. It is a *practice*, in the way meditation is a practice or medicine is a practice — something you commit to without the expectation of mastery, something that requires daily recalibration of your assumptions.

The engineers of Kethane-Above are the closest thing the city has to an intellectual elite, and they carry this status with the weary pragmatism of people who know that being the smartest person in a room where the room itself is changing shape is a distinction of limited value. They are identifiable by their tools, which are extraordinary: rulers that are intentionally flexible, built to measure distances that may not be Euclidean; levels that have been recalibrated for local gravity anomalies and must be recalibrated again quarterly; surveying instruments of original design that measure not just distance and angle but *rate of change of distance and angle*, because what matters in Kethane-Above is not where a wall is but where a wall is *going*. Some carry small jars of burrowing creatures, which they release at construction sites to encourage the rock to decide on a composition before they build on it. Some carry tuning forks calibrated to the ground note, which they strike and press against surfaces to assess the rock's degree of resolution — a clear, sustained ring means decided stone; a wavering, polytonal hum means the rock is still thinking, still young, and should not be relied upon.

The chief engineer — there has been a chief engineer for as long as there have been records, though the title and its responsibilities have shifted as the city's relationship with its own instability has matured — is currently a woman named Harath Senn, who is sixty-three years old and was born in the northern factory district and who has, over the course of her career, developed a theory of plateau mechanics that is either visionary or insane and that she presents with the same flat, unperformative certainty either way. Her theory, simplified: the plateau is not merely undecided rock. The plateau is a *process* — an ongoing act of geological creation that is still in its earliest stages — and Kethane-Above is not built *on* this process but *in* it, woven into its fabric, and the city's presence is affecting the process's outcome. The stamp mills, the burrowers, the generations of human habitation, the weight and warmth and chemical byproducts of civilization — all of this is *input* into the plateau's becoming. The mountain is not just deciding what it is. It is deciding what it is *in the context of being lived on*. And this means the city has a responsibility not merely to adapt but to *participate thoughtfully*, because what the plateau eventually becomes — what rules it eventually settles on — will be shaped in part by what the city does in the meantime.

This theory is controversial not because it is obviously wrong but because its implications are exhausting. If Harath Senn is right, then every factory, every building, every conveyor line and cable-car route and foundation and drain is not merely a structure but a *vote* — a contribution to the plateau's ongoing referendum on what kind of reality it wants to host. And the engineers, who already work brutal hours maintaining infrastructure in a place where infrastructure is fundamentally provisional, would then be additionally responsible for considering the *ontological consequences* of their design choices, which is the kind of responsibility that makes people pour a second drink and stare out their window at the phosphorescent rock and wonder what they're doing up here.

Harath Senn herself is a compact, deep-chested woman with the stained hands of someone who has spent decades handling Kethane materials. Her fingertips are so translucent they are nearly transparent; you can see the shadows of bone when she holds her hands up to the dim light. She speaks quietly. She has a habit of touching walls as she walks, pressing her palm flat for a moment, the way the children do, and she says it tells her things, though she won't specify what. She lives alone in a small apartment above her office in the engineering works, surrounded by seventeen framed maps arranged in a circle on the wall, and she has named every ridge-soarer that regularly visits her rooftop, and she keeps a burrower in a jar on her desk that she claims is the best geologist in the city, and she is not joking.

## The Miners

Below the city — in the plateau itself — there are miners, and their situation is different from anyone else's, and they know it.

Mining in Kethane-Above is not mining in any conventional sense. You do not extract resources from decided rock. You extract *potential* from undecided rock — you go into the young stone, the nacreous, thinking stone, and you harvest material that has not yet committed to being any particular substance. This material — they call it *pith*, which is not a scientific term, which is a word someone used once and which stuck because it has the right mouthfeel, soft and fundamental — is extraordinarily valuable below. Its applications are proprietary and various and poorly understood even by the people who trade in it, but the demand is real, and the cable cars carry it down through the cloudbank in sealed containers, and money comes back up, and this is the engine of Kethane-Above's economy.

The miners work in shaft systems that penetrate the plateau to depths that are difficult to verify, because the spatial anomalies become more pronounced underground. A shaft that is fifty meters deep by one measurement may be three hundred meters deep by another, or twelve meters, or *meaningfully deep in a way that resists quantification*. The miners navigate by feel, by the pitch of the ground note (which is louder underground, obviously, and which acquires harmonic complexity at depth, splitting into overtones that experienced miners read the way sailors read currents), and by the color and texture of the rock around them, which tells them how far the stone has progressed in its self-determination.

What happens to the miners over years of this work is pronounced. Their translucency extends beyond the extremities; veteran miners have forearms and faces that glow faintly in the dark, their circulatory systems visible as dim, branching networks beneath the skin. They are not unhealthy. Medical examination reveals nothing wrong — their blood is blood, their organs are organs, their bones are bone. But their *material* has become less opaque, less *committed to opacity*, as though prolonged contact with undecided rock has introduced a note of indeterminacy into their flesh. They bruise in colors that are not standard bruise-colors — faint greens, pale silvers. Their scars have a nacreous quality, like the rock itself. One very old miner, who worked the deep shafts for forty years before his retirement, is said to be so translucent that in the phosphorescent nightglow he is nearly invisible — just a faint, person-shaped luminescence moving through the streets, detectable mainly by the sound of his breathing and the ground-note resonance that hums faintly in his bones.

The miners are a community within the community, tight-knit, with their own customs and a shared understanding that what they do is not entirely safe and not entirely understood. They have rituals around entering and exiting the shafts that look superstitious but may be practical: they touch the rock at the shaft entrance with both hands and wait for the consideration — that not-warmth, not-vibration — before descending, and they say it is a way of announcing themselves, of letting the rock know they're coming, so it doesn't change too rapidly around them. Whether this works is unprovable. What is provable is that miners who skip the ritual have a higher rate of encountering *pockets* — areas where the rock has suddenly resolved, mid-shift, into something hard and sharp and definitively hostile to human presence. Pockets are the primary danger of Kethane mining. They are the plateau making a sudden decision, and anyone caught inside that decision is subject to its terms.

The miners have a saying: *The rock is generous when it knows you.* They say this with the quiet conviction of people who have bet their lives on a relationship with something that is not alive and is not dead and is not any category in between, something that is still *becoming*, and who have found that relationship, on balance, to be one they can work within.

## The Merchants and the Cable-Car Operators

The cable cars are Kethane-Above's lifeline, and the people who operate them are perhaps the strangest inhabitants of the city, because they are the ones who regularly travel between *here* and *below*, and this transit changes them in ways that are subtle but cumulative.

The cable-car system descends from three stations at the plateau's edge — north, west, and south-peninsula — and drops through the permanent cloudbank into whatever is below. The journey takes between forty minutes and several hours, depending on conditions that the operators understand intuitively but cannot explain to outsiders. The cars themselves are enclosed steel capsules, riveted and heavy, insulated against the cloudbank's passage, which is reportedly an unpleasant experience — cold, disorienting, accompanied by sounds that have been described as "a conversation in a language made entirely of static" and "the sound of a very large thing breathing." The operators sit at the front of each capsule behind a panel of instruments that includes some conventional gauges and some that are clearly Kethane-made: dials that measure ground-note intensity, rock-resolution gradients, spatial dilation coefficients. The operators watch these instruments with the relaxed attention of long practice, making adjustments to the car's descent speed with hand-cranks and lever systems, and occasionally speaking to the car itself in a low, murmuring tone that might be reassurance and might be instruction.

The merchants who use the cable cars to trade Kethane's products — pith, sympathetic alloys, spiral-stalk pharmaceutical compounds, the occasional piece of furniture or tool that has been subtly transformed by long residence in the material-sympathy district and that has properties artisans below will pay well for — are a cosmopolitan bunch, more traveled than most Kethane residents, and they carry themselves with a particular kind of dual awareness. They know what below expects. They know what above produces. They live in the translation between these two worlds, and the translation is imperfect, and the imperfection is where they make their money.

But the operators — the cable-car operators are different. They go back and forth, through the cloudbank, day after day, and the cloudbank does something to them. It isn't the cold or the static-voices (which they claim to have gotten used to, and which some of the older operators claim to partially *understand*, in the way you understand a dog's bark — not the words but the tenor, the emotional shape). It is the transit itself. The passage between a place where reality is settled and a place where it is not. This passage, repeated daily, gives the operators a *looseness* — not physical, not mental, but something in between. They are the most adaptable people in the city. They are the first to notice when a new anomaly has begun, the first to adjust their behavior, the first to incorporate a change in the rules into their daily practice. They are also the most prone to *forgetting things from below* — not facts, not skills, but *assumptions*. An operator who has been running the cable cars for twenty years will sometimes stare at a newcomer's belongings — a regular wristwatch, a mass-produced shirt, a photograph — with a faint puzzlement, as though these objects are not quite real to her, not quite solid, not quite as *decided* as they think they are. She handles them gingerly. She can see, or sense, or *suspect*, a mutability in them that the newcomer cannot imagine.

The senior cable-car operator is a man called Dekken Farr, who has been running the north station line for thirty-one years and who is, by the quiet consensus of the city, the person who best understands the relationship between above and below. He is tall and very thin, with the deep chest of a native and the luminous skin of a miner, though he has never mined — his translucency comes from the cloudbank, from the repeated passage through that layer of undecided atmosphere, and it has a slightly different quality than the miners' version: less nacreous, more *gray*, as though his skin has absorbed the pewter light of the cloudbank and held it. He speaks rarely and precisely. He has an unnerving habit of finishing other people's sentences, not because he is rude but because the temporal layering seems to affect him personally — he occasionally experiences the next few seconds before they arrive, a mild and inconsistent precognition that he neither exploits nor discusses. He lives in a small room at the cable-car station, surrounded by the sounds of machinery and wind, and he has not been below in eleven years, having reached a point in his adaptation to Kethane-Above where the solidity of below is physically uncomfortable — too definite, too bright, too *resolved*. He sends other operators down. He stays at the edge, looking out over the cloudbank, his gray-luminous face unreadable, listening to something in the static that he has never described to anyone.

## The Philosopher

There is one philosopher. Just the one.

Her name is Lenne Vas, and she is perhaps the most patient person in a city that has made patience a survival trait. She is forty-eight years old. She was born below and came to Kethane-Above at seventeen as a laborer, worked in the stamp mills for three years, was injured when a pocket event resolved a section of wall two inches from her left hand (the hand was not crushed but was *changed* — the skin of her palm now has a pearlescent, rocky texture, smooth and slightly cool to the touch, and she cannot feel pressure with it but can feel vibration at frequencies no one else can detect), and during her recovery she began writing.

What she writes is the closest thing Kethane-Above has to a systematic account of itself. Not a history — history implies a linear time that the city only intermittently experiences. Not a science — science implies reproducible laws, and the laws here are still in draft. What Lenne Vas writes is a *phenomenology of the unfinished*: a meticulous, deeply observed, often beautiful record of what it is like to live in a place that has not yet decided what it is.

Her works — she writes on paper made from the processed fiber of spiral-stalks, which is pale green and slightly luminous and does not age in any predictable direction (some sheets grow crisper and more legible over years; some soften and blur; one celebrated page developed a third paragraph that Lenne Vas swears she did not write, in handwriting that is almost but not quite hers) — circulate in the city as hand-copied pamphlets and are read with an attention that ranges from devoted to hostile.

Her central argument, which has evolved over twenty years: the citizens of Kethane-Above are not merely *adapting* to an unstable environment. They are *becoming a new kind of human* — not biologically (though the translucency, the lungs, the sensitivity to the ground note are real and increasing), but ontologically. They are people who have learned to live without certainty about the fundamental nature of their surroundings, and this has changed the structure of their consciousness in ways that are not deficits but *capacities*. The ability to navigate spatial anomalies without panic. The ability to perceive temporal layering and incorporate it into daily experience rather than being shattered by it. The ability to form deep, sustaining relationships with a place that is literally *not yet real* in the way that places below are real.

She calls this capacity *ground-sense*, and she argues it is the city's most important product — more important than pith or alloys or pharmaceuticals — and that it is the thing that will matter most when the plateau eventually finishes its becoming, because the people who are here when the rules finally solidify will be the people who helped *shape* those rules, consciously or not, through the accumulated weight of their choices and presence and attention.

Lenne Vas lives in the old quarter, among the temporal echoes, and she watches them with her one good hand pressed against the wall, feeling vibrations no one else can feel, and she writes down what they tell her. She is not always right. She is not always coherent. She has a fondness for ridge-soarers that borders on the religious and a distrust of the cable-car operators that borders on the personal (she and Dekken Farr were involved, briefly, years ago, and the relationship ended in a way that neither of them will discuss, though the temporal echoes in her neighborhood occasionally display scenes from it, translucent and silent, and she does not look away from these). She drinks a tea made from the blue-green groundcover that tastes like cold metal and that she says helps her think. She is the only person in Kethane-Above who has attempted to *communicate* with the plateau — not in the way the miners announce themselves, not in the way the children listen, but deliberately, systematically, through a series of vibrations produced by striking tuning forks against her changed palm and pressing it to the bare rock — and she claims the plateau has responded, not in words, not in any interpretable signal, but in a change in the quality of its consideration, a *warming* of its attention, the way a sleeping animal shifts when it becomes aware of a hand on its back.

No one knows whether to believe her. Everyone reads her pamphlets.

## The Newcomers

They arrive on the cable cars, blinking, breathless, their skin opaque and their assumptions intact.

They come for work — below is not always kind, and Kethane-Above is always hiring, because the work is hard and the environment is strange and there is constant attrition to the descent, to the cable cars carrying people back to a world that makes sense. They come for curiosity, occasionally — scholars from below who have heard rumors and want to see for themselves, and who are typically overwhelmed within days by the sheer *volume* of the impossible and the sheer *calmness* with which the citizens navigate it. They come, rarely, because they were drawn — because they heard something, in a dream or a moment of silence, that sounded like a low, resonant hum, a ground note bleeding through the cloudbank and reaching down into the world below and touching some readiness in them, some unfinished quality in their own composition that recognized the plateau's unfinishedness and wanted to be near it.

These last ones stay the longest. These last ones adapt the fastest. The translucency comes to them within months instead of years, and they find themselves pressing their hands against the rock without being told to, and the children look at them with an appraising, approving attention, and the burrowers approach them without fear, and within a year they are indistinguishable from lifelong residents — or nearly so. There is always something. A way of startling at the temporal echoes that a native-born child would never display. A tendency to look up at the pewter sky and search for the sun, reflexively, even after years. A residual allegiance to the idea that things should be *fixed* — that gravity should pull straight down, that distance should be consistent, that time should proceed in the agreed-upon direction — that manifests not as complaint but as a faint, persistent *wistfulness*, a homesickness for rules that applied.

They are welcomed. Kethane-Above does not have the luxury of unwelcoming newcomers; the city needs bodies, needs hands, needs the fresh attention of people who have not yet habituated to the anomalies and who therefore *notice* them, who can say *this is new, this wasn't here yesterday, the gravity on this street has shifted another degree to the left*, and in so doing serve as the city's early warning system for changes that the long-adapted might overlook. There is even a formal role — *witness*, it's called, though the formality is minimal, just a nod from a shift foreman — assigned to newcomers in their first months, during which they are expected to simply *observe* and *report*, to walk the streets and the empty lots and the cliff edges and say what they see, plainly, to whoever is listening.

The best witnesses are the ones who stay. The ones who transition from observing the strangeness to *participating* in it, who let the plateau begin its negotiation with their bodies, who learn to read the ground note and feed the burrowers and walk the leftward-pulling street with their weight shifted and their eyes open. These are the people who will, in a generation or two, be grandmothers managing buildings, engineers calibrating flexible rulers, miners with nacreous scars, cable-car operators listening to the static with a gray-glowing face.

The ones who leave — and many leave, and this is no disgrace, and the city does not judge them for it — carry something with them. Not the translucency, which fades below within weeks. Not the deep breathing, which they lose in the richer air. What they carry is a knowledge — felt, not articulable, lodged somewhere behind the sternum — that the world is not as finished as it looks. That the rules are more provisional than they appear. That somewhere above the clouds, on a plateau that is still becoming what it is, people have built a city inside an open question and are living there, stubbornly and with rivets, and that this is possible, and that this changes something about what *everything else* is, even the settled places, even the decided ground.

## The Old Ones

At the other end of the spectrum from the newcomers, there are the old ones, and they must be spoken of carefully, because they are difficult to speak of, because they are becoming difficult to *see*.

Not invisible. Not gone. But the translucency that begins at the fingertips and progresses inward over a lifetime does not stop progressing. The oldest residents of Kethane-Above — the ones who have lived here for seventy, eighty, ninety years, who were born here and whose parents were born here — have become *very* translucent. In the dim light of the city's permanent twilight, they are easy to miss. They move through the streets like their own temporal echoes, and newcomers sometimes mistake them for exactly that — for ghostly remnants of past moments — until the old one speaks, or touches their arm with a hand that is warm and present and *undeniably here* but through which you can see, faintly, the dark brick of the wall behind.

They are not fading. This is important. Lenne Vas has written extensively about this, and she is adamant: the old ones are not *less* than they were. They are not dissolving or disappearing or dying in some slow, strange way. What is happening is that they are *resolving differently*. The way the plateau's rock resolves into basalt or crystal or chalk or something nameless — the old ones are resolving into a state of being that is less opaque, less bounded, less *separate from their surroundings*. Their edges soften. Their boundaries become probabilistic rather than definite. They are still themselves — still sharp-minded, still wry, still capable of the dry, understated humor that is Kethane-Above's dominant emotional register — but they are *also* becoming something that is slightly less distinct from the rock and the air and the phosphorescent light and the ground note.

The oldest of the old ones — there are perhaps a dozen who have reached this advanced state — live in the old quarter, among the temporal echoes, and they move through the layered moments with an ease that no one else can match. They seem to perceive all the time-layers simultaneously, not as confusion but as *richness*, the way a musician hears a chord rather than a cacophony. They sometimes answer questions that haven't been asked yet, or continue conversations that happened last week, or acknowledge rain that won't fall until tomorrow — and they do this not with the disorientation of senility but with the practiced grace of people who simply have a wider *now* than everyone else.

One of them — a woman called Essant, who is over a hundred and whose exact age is uncertain because she was born during a period of temporal layering that makes her birth date ambiguous — is so translucent that she is visible mainly by the effect she has on her surroundings. The air near her is slightly warmer. The ground note is slightly louder. The moss on the nearby buildings leans toward her the way plants lean toward light. She sits on a bench in the old quarter — the same bench, every day, though the bench has changed composition twice during her residence and is currently exhibiting a faint metallic sheen from sympathetic contact with the iron railings nearby — and she watches the temporal echoes with an expression of deep, complex fondness, as though they are family photographs, which in a sense they are. She speaks rarely but with a precision that suggests she is choosing her words not from the present moment's vocabulary but from the vocabulary of all the moments she simultaneously inhabits. When she does speak, people listen.

Essant said, once, to a newcomer who asked her what it was like to live in Kethane-Above for so long:

*"You are asking what it is like to be unfinished. I will tell you. It is like being a word that the mountain is still learning to pronounce."*

This was seventeen years ago. The newcomer stayed. He is now a miner with nacreous scars and luminous forearms, and he has not forgotten what she said, and he presses his hands against the rock at the shaft entrance every morning and waits for the consideration, and he thinks of himself as a syllable, a component of an utterance that the plateau is slowly assembling, and this thought does not frighten him. It locates him. It tells him what he is for.

## All of Them, Together

Stand in the market district at what passes for midday. The pewter light is at its brightest, which is not bright. The arc lamps are on, flickering. The stamp mills thud.

Here is a grandmother buying groundcover cuttings from a vendor whose hands shimmer with stalk-residue, arguing amicably about the price while her translucent fingers sort through the blue-green rosettes with practiced speed. Here is an engineer with a flexible ruler and a jar of burrowers, surveying a new building site where the rock has just been *asked nicely* and has obligingly resolved into a serviceable sandstone-like material that will hold foundations. Here is a miner, off-shift, sitting at a café table (the café serves the cold-metal tea and something denser, something warm, brewed from a moss-derived recipe that is hearty and faintly luminescent and that no one from below could stomach), his forearms glowing in the dim light, his eyes half-closed as he listens to the ground note with the deep, unconscious attention of a person hearing his own heartbeat.

Here is a child running, chasing a burrower that has escaped from her pocket, her laughter bright in the dim air, her small luminous fingers reaching. Here is a cable-car operator, gray-skinned and quiet, walking from the station to the market with the slightly anticipatory gait of someone who knows what the next thirty seconds contain and is content to live through them anyway. Here is a newcomer, three days arrived, wide-eyed, leaning slightly because the street pulls left and she hasn't learned to compensate yet, watching everything with the raw, saturated attention of a person whose worldview is being disassembled and rebuilt in real time.

Here, at the far end of the market, barely visible, a shimmer in the shape of a woman — Essant, or someone like her, one of the old ones, moving through the crowd and through the temporal layers and through the phosphorescent almost-light, speaking quietly to someone who may not be present in this particular stratum of now, her words inaudible but her expression full of a tenderness so vast and so patient it makes your chest ache to see it.

And above them all, the ridge-soarers drift, pinned to their unknowable sky. And below them all, the plateau hums its long, undecided chord. And around them all, the buildings stand on foundations that are still, slowly, becoming — dark brick and riveted iron and moss like wet fur and walls that weep iridescent moisture and doorways that are trapezoidal for reasons that are perfectly logical if you accept the premises, which they do, which they all do, every one of them, every morning, when they wake in the phosphor-glow and put their feet on floors that might be different from yesterday and breathe the thin, eighty-percent air and choose again to be here, to be part of this, to add their weight and warmth and attention to the question the mountain is asking —

— which is the only question, really, the one that every unfinished thing asks, the one that the rock and the city and the citizens all share:

*What will I be, when I am done?*

And the answer, which Kethane-Above offers with every riveted beam and asymmetric wheel and luminous fingerprint and named ridge-soarer and framed contradictory map:

*I don't know yet. But I am here, and I am trying, and I will not leave before I find out.*
