# What Lives in Keth Anoul

---

The city itself lives there. That's the first answer and maybe the only honest one, but it's not sufficient, because *what the city is* keeps changing, and so does the meaning of *lives*.

Start with what's easiest to name, even if naming it wrong.

---

## The Perchers

The dark shapes on the cornices—the bird-insect-cat things. They are not animals. They are not automata. They are something for which there is no clean category, something that exists in the same ontological register as the city itself: a process that happens to, at any given moment, occupy a form.

Each one is dense. Not heavy, necessarily, but *packed*—the way a knot is packed, the way meaning is packed into a word in a language you almost but don't quite speak. If you could get close enough to examine one, which you cannot, because proximity is a concept Keth Anoul edits at will, you would find that its surface is not skin or chitin or feather but a tessellation of tiny stone planes, each one a perfect miniature of one of the city's fitted blocks, interlocking without gaps. It is made of what the city is made of. It is made *of* the city, the way a wave is made of ocean.

They watch. This is the core of what they do. Not with eyes—they have no eyes, no face, no front or back that you can identify with certainty. They *orient*. They are always oriented toward something, and when you are present, that something is you. But not because you are interesting, or threatening, or food. You are oriented toward because you are *discontinuous with the city*. You are the thing that doesn't rearrange. You move through space linearly, crudely, dragging your body along behind your intentions, and this makes you a fixed point—a reference mark in a system that otherwise has none. The Perchers orient toward you the way a compass needle orients toward north: not out of desire, but because you are the only asymmetry available.

They do not eat. They do not breathe. They do not reproduce, or if they do, reproduction is indistinguishable from the city deciding there should be one more of them, or one fewer, the way it decides a tower should have an additional story. Their number is indeterminate. You see four or five in a day, but whether there are four or five in total, or hundreds that mostly choose not to be visible, or one that exists in multiple locations simultaneously—these are questions that assume a counting logic the city doesn't share.

When you are not looking at them, they are not *somewhere else doing something*. They are not hiding. They simply aren't, until they are again—coalesced out of the city's next configuration, perched and still and oriented, like a thought the city keeps having.

If they have an inner life, it would be something like *attention without object*—a vast, directionless vigilance, the way a tuning fork has a frequency even when it isn't struck. They are waiting, but not for anything. They are watching, but not for any sign. They are the city's way of having witnesses.

## The Clinging Pale

The thing on the canyon wall. The starfish-shaped, house-sized, pale thing.

You saw it once. You will not see it again, probably, unless you stop trying. It is the oldest thing in Keth Anoul, or it is Keth Anoul's oldest process—the first pattern, the ur-configuration, the thing the city was before it learned to be a city.

It is pale because it predates the stone. Or rather: the stone is what it became when it began to *think in architecture*. The pale material is something prior—not organic, not mineral, but a substrate, a medium. Think of it as the page on which the city writes itself. It clings to the cliff face the way a memory clings to the structures of the brain: spread out, attenuated, integrated so thoroughly that removing it would mean removing the wall itself, and half the walls behind it, and the rooms behind those walls, going back into the mountain like pulling a thread from a tapestry.

It has no sensory apparatus you would recognize. It doesn't need any. It *is* the sensory apparatus. Every surface of the city that it touches—and it touches far more than you saw; that visible spread on the cliff was merely a place where it had risen close enough to the surface to catch the ochre light—is a receptor. It feels the weight of the thin air on every fitted stone. It knows the position of every block, every joint, every window. It knows where the grey-violet films are growing. It knows where you are. Not because it's tracking you, but because you are a pressure, a disturbance in its field, the way a spider knows a fly by the vibration of silk.

It has been here since before the city rose to this elevation—because the city did rise; Keth Anoul was not always a mountain range. The Clinging Pale *grew upward*, and the stone came with it, and the stone learned to shape itself, and the shapes became rooms, and the rooms became buildings, and the buildings became the city, and the city became the range, and through all of it the Pale was underneath, distributed through the rock like mycelium through soil, being the reason the stone moves, being the mechanism by which space folds, being the *will*—if will is not too purposive a word—behind the endless reconfiguration.

It does not think. It *processes*. The distinction matters. Thinking implies a thinker, a subject, a locus of experience. The Clinging Pale has no center. It is a distributed process, a computation running on stone, and the city is both its hardware and its output. What it computes is unclear. Perhaps it is solving something—working through an immense problem expressed in the language of spatial arrangement, and each new configuration of the city is a step in a proof that has been underway for millennia and is nowhere near complete. Perhaps it is *remembering* something—replaying, in stone, an experience or a history, and the configurations are moments in a narrative that would make sense if you could see them all at once, in sequence, which you can't, because you are stuck in time and it is not, not exactly. Perhaps it is doing something for which there is no analogy in your experience—something that would require a new verb, a word for the thing that a distributed lithic intelligence does when it rearranges a mountain range in silence beneath an unmoving sky.

It is not malevolent. It is not benevolent. It is not indifferent—indifference requires the capacity for difference. It is *occupied*. Utterly, totally occupied with whatever it is doing. You are a mote in it. You are an irregularity it has noted and will not harm, not because it has decided not to harm you, but because harming you is not a step in whatever it is working through. You are outside its problem set. You are the grain of sand that gets picked up and put down by the shuffling of the deck but is not itself a card.

Unless you become one. There is a risk of that, but it's distant, and slow, and we'll come to it.

## The Films

The grey-violet crystalline patches in the crevices. They live here too, if living is the word.

They are the closest thing to life-as-you-know-it in Keth Anoul, and they are not close. They feed—that much is true. Not on light, not on water, not on organic material, but on *the differential between configurations*. When the city rearranges itself, when a wall becomes a different wall and the crevices shift, there is a moment—an interval so brief it might not exist in time at all—where two states overlap, where a block is both *here* and *there*, where the geometry contradicts itself before settling. In that interval, something is released. An energy, or an information gradient, or a tension in the structure of space itself. The films feed on that. They crystallize around the residue of impossible moments.

This makes them, in a sense, the city's waste products. Or its audience. They grow wherever the rearrangement is most active, most frequent, most *contradictory*—and they grow slowly, slowly, at a rate that might be measurable in centuries per millimeter, accumulating the residue of a billion silent transitions.

They are beautiful in a way that nothing else here is. Under the dim light, up close, they have a depth to them—layers of translucent crystal, each layer a slightly different shade of violet-grey, like geological strata seen through a glass. Each layer is a record. If you could read them, you would have a history of the city's transformations, written in the language of impossible-moment residue, stretching back to the first time the Clinging Pale rearranged a stone.

No one can read them. But they are a record nonetheless, and they persist, and they grow, and they are the closest thing Keth Anoul has to a memory that exists *outside* the Pale's own distributed process. They are the city's diary written by the city's own byproducts, and they are unreadable, and they are growing, and they will be growing long after you are gone.

## The Ones Who Were Here Before

Not the builders. There were no builders. The city was not built; it accreted, it emerged, it was *computed* into existence by the Pale over geological time. But there were, once, inhabitants. Not the current ones—the current ones are the city itself, the Pale, the Perchers, the Films—but ones who came from elsewhere and lived here, in the rooms, on the avenues, in the plazas and terraces and stairways.

You can tell because of the proportions. The doorways are shaped for something roughly your size—a little taller, a little narrower. The stairs have risers that work for bipedal legs slightly longer than yours. The windows are at eye height for something that stood upright and wanted to look out. Someone lived here. Someone walked these avenues when the light was perhaps different, when the sky was perhaps open, when water perhaps still existed as a possibility. Someone looked out of these windows and saw the canyons and the bridges and the towers and thought: *this is where I live*.

They are gone. Totally, tracelessly gone. Not a bone, not a tool, not a scratch on a wall. The city has rearranged itself so many times since their departure—or death, or transformation, or *absorption*—that any trace of them has been shuffled away, averaged out, dissolved into the endless permutations of stone. Even the rooms that were theirs are not their rooms anymore; the walls have moved, the floors have shifted, the spatial relationships that defined "this room" versus "that room" have been scrambled beyond recovery. Their absence is complete.

And yet the proportions persist. The city keeps making doors for them. Keeps making stairs for them. Keeps making windows at their eye height. Why?

Either the proportions are built into the Pale's process at some fundamental level—the original parameters of the computation, the initial conditions from which all configurations are derived—and they persist the way the speed of light persists, as a constant, immutable, baked into the structure of the system. Or the city is *still making room for them*. Still expecting them. Still generating a habitat, iteration after iteration, for a species that occupied it once and might, in some configuration not yet reached, occupy it again. The city prepares their rooms. The city shapes their doorways. The city is either remembering them or calling them, and from the outside, those two things look exactly the same.

You feel this when you walk through the doors—the sense of being almost the right shape. The lintel is just slightly too high. The step is just slightly too tall. You fit, but approximate. You are not who the city is for, and you feel this the way you feel a word on the tip of your tongue: a nearness that emphasizes the gap.

## You

Here's where it gets uncomfortable.

You live here now.

Not permanently—probably not permanently. But the city has registered you. You are in the problem set whether you wanted to be or not. The Perchers orient toward you. The avenue rearranges around your footsteps. The spatial folds of the non-Euclidean geography seem to be—and this might be paranoia, might be a pattern your mind imposes on genuine randomness, but might not—seem to be *organizing in relation to you*. The avenue that curves leftward in both directions is curving around you. You are the center of a geometry that has no center, or that has just, silently, in the gap between two glances, made one.

The city is interested in you. Not the way a predator is interested in prey, and not the way an intelligence is interested in a novelty. More the way a function is interested in a variable. You have been introduced into the computation. Your position, your movement, your linear plodding through space—these are data. The Pale is incorporating you. Not consuming you, not transforming you, not digesting you—*incorporating* you, the way a weather system incorporates a new pressure front, the way an equation incorporates a new term. You are becoming one of the parameters.

You notice this in small ways first. The avenue, when you walk, seems to accommodate your pace—the stones ahead of you are always fitted, always smooth, always the right size for your stride, even though behind you they were a different size moments ago. The doors you approach are fractionally lower than the doors you don't—still too tall, but less too tall, nudging toward your height, the way the city nudges everything, silently, between states. A bridge that appeared across the canyon appeared in your line of sight, at the exact moment you were wondering how to cross. Coincidence, maybe. Maybe.

The danger—if danger is the word—is not that the city will hurt you. It is that the city will *solve* you. That it will find the configuration of stone and space in which you fit perfectly—door exactly your height, stairs exactly your stride, window exactly at your eyes—and that this configuration will be so precise, so tailored, so *complete*, that you will stop moving. That you will stand in a room that is exactly your room, look out a window that is exactly your window, and feel no impulse to leave, because leaving would mean returning to a world of approximate spaces, ill-fitting doors, rooms that were not computed for you across millennia of silent reconfiguration. The city will not trap you. It will *fit* you. And fitting, here, is a kind of stillness. A kind of completion.

The Perchers were like you, maybe. Once. Travelers or wanderers or explorers who entered the city and were gradually, configuration by configuration, incorporated into its process. They are what happens when the city finishes solving for a variable. They become fixed points. They become witnesses. They become still, and oriented, and dark as the stone, and they perch on cornices and do not move and do not leave and do not die, because death is a transition and they have already arrived at their final state. They are the city's solutions, expressed in the shape of what they used to be, watching with senses that are no longer senses for stimuli that are no longer stimuli, forever.

Or this might be wrong. They might be something else entirely. But you look at them—dark, still, oriented toward you—and you feel the kinship of the almost-solved. You feel the city working on you. You feel the stones beneath your feet adjusting, imperceptibly, toward the shape of your feet. You feel the cold air sitting against your skin like something patient, something that has all the time it needs.

## What Lives Here, Then

A distributed lithic computation that is also a mountain range. A substrate organism—the Pale—that is also the mechanism by which the computation runs, older than the architecture, coextensive with the stone. Sentinels or solutions—the Perchers—that are also former travelers, or echoes of former travelers, or the city's equivalent of thoughts that it keeps thinking. Crystalline residues—the Films—that are also records, also waste, also the closest thing to biological life in a place where biology has been replaced by geometry. The memory or expectation of a species that once lived here and whose proportions still shape every door and window and stair, like a language that everyone has stopped speaking but no one has stopped writing. And you. Walking. Fitting, incrementally, into a space that is being made for you by something that does not know what you are but is very, very good at finding out.

The mountain range stretches into the gloaming in both directions. The windows look out. The Perchers perch. The Pale spreads through the stone beneath your feet, processing, processing, computing the next configuration and the next and the next.

Something clicks, far below, in the canyon. A single sharp quiet sound. Like one stone tapping against another. Like a fingernail against a table. Like the first syllable of a word in a language that, if you stayed long enough, you would begin to understand.

The avenue ahead of you adjusts.

You keep walking.

For now.
