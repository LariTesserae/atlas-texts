# The Tenders

They are the thing you almost see. The shape that resolves just past the point where you've already looked away. They move through the root-corridors with a familiarity so deep it resembles absence—the way your own tongue moves inside your mouth without your awareness, the way blood knows its channels. You do not encounter them. You realize they have been there the entire time.

---

There are perhaps three hundred of them in the basin. Perhaps four hundred. Counting is difficult because they are rarely all visible at once, and because when they are still—which is often—they become indistinguishable from the architecture they have spent their lives creating. Their skin has the quality of wet bark. Not metaphorically. Not "bark-like" or "bark-colored." It has the actual quality of it: a grey-green surface, cool to the touch, slightly yielding, with a texture of fine ridges and shallow furrows that maps closely to the surface of the root-walls they press against. This is not camouflage in the way a moth mimics bark. It is *convergence*. They have become like this place because they have lived inside it for so long, and the place has become like them, and the two have been growing toward each other for longer than anyone has bothered to measure.

They are not human. But they are closer to human than to anything else you'd reach for, and that closeness is what makes them strange. The body plan is recognizable: bipedal, upright or mostly upright, two arms, a head, bilateral symmetry. The proportions are wrong, though—wrong in a way that nags rather than startles. The torso is too long and the legs too short, giving them a low center of gravity, a settled quality, as though they were designed to crouch and stay crouched. The arms are long, longer than the legs, and the hands are extraordinary. Each hand has six fingers—no, not fingers. Five fingers and something else, a sixth digit that emerges from the wrist rather than the palm, opposable to the others but longer, thinner, more prehensile, tapering to a pale soft tip that is constantly in motion, constantly touching things: bark, moss, the growing tips of roots, the surfaces of still water. This sixth digit is their primary tool. It is the thing that speaks to the roots.

Their faces are broad and flat, almost concave, with wide-set eyes that have no visible whites—the entire exposed surface of each eye is a deep amber-brown, like tannin-stained water with light behind it. The pupils are large, perpetually dilated for the dim under-canopy world. They blink rarely and when they do, the motion is slow, a deliberate closing and opening, more like a shutter than a reflex. Their noses are minimal—two slits, close-set, that flare and contract rhythmically, tasting the saturated air. The mouths are wide and thin-lipped, and you will almost never see them open. When one does open its mouth—to eat, to drink, to perform whatever private function requires it—you glimpse teeth that are flat and numerous and pale green at the gumline, stained by a lifetime of chewing moss and the soft inner bark of roots. They eat the city. The city grows faster than they consume it. This is the arrangement.

They have no hair. The tops of their heads are smooth and slightly ridged, and in the cold damp air, condensation beads on their scalps and runs down behind their ears—their ears, which are the most alien feature: not external structures at all, but shallow depressions on either side of the head, lined with fine cilia that wave gently, independently, sampling vibrations in the air and water. These cilia are pale, almost translucent, and watching them move is like watching something underwater even though the Tenders are standing in open air. They hear as a place like this demands—not the sharp directional hearing of a predator or prey animal, but a diffuse, omnidirectional sensitivity to texture. The drip of condensation, the creak of a growing root, the faint hydraulic sigh of water moving through sapwood: these are not background noise to the Tenders. These are language. Or close enough to language that the distinction doesn't matter.

## How They Work

You will see one at a root-wall that is still incomplete, where two buttress roots are reaching toward each other across a gap of perhaps six inches. The Tender crouches—and crouching is their natural posture, their resting state; they sit on their heels with their long arms draped forward and their six-fingered hands resting on whatever surface is nearest, and they can hold this position for hours without visible discomfort. The Tender crouches at the gap and extends both hands, and the sixth digits—the wrist-fingers, the speaking-fingers—reach out and touch the growing tips of both roots simultaneously.

What happens next is slow. Too slow to watch in real time. If you stood and observed for an hour, you might see... a slight curve begin to develop in one root tip. A faint inclination toward the other. The Tender's fingers press and stroke and make minute adjustments, and the root responds—not immediately, not mechanically, but the way a conversation partner responds, considering, then shifting. The Tenders do not command the roots. They do not force or manipulate. The closest analogy is that they *suggest*, and the roots, being alive and growing and possessing whatever vegetable intelligence guides the tropisms of all plants, take the suggestion under consideration and, more often than not, accept it. Sometimes they don't. Sometimes a root curves away from the intended path, and the Tender simply adjusts, follows, offers a new suggestion. The architecture of the basin is not planned in the way a human plans a building. It is *negotiated*. Every wall, every arch, every platform is the result of a slow conversation between a Tender and a tree, conducted through touch, over months and years.

The speaking-fingers secrete something. You can see it if you look closely—a faint glistening on the root tips after a Tender has been working, a thin film of something that is neither sap nor saliva but shares qualities of both. This secretion is part of the conversation. It carries chemical signals—hormones, growth factors, something analogous—that influence the root's direction and rate of growth. But it is not a simple chemical instruction. The Tenders vary the composition depending on what they want to communicate. A root that needs to thicken receives a different touch than a root that needs to branch. A root that is being asked to fuse with a neighbor receives something that softens the bark at the contact point, encouraging the grafting process. This is sophisticated biochemistry, but the Tenders do not experience it as chemistry. They experience it as touch. As the particular quality of pressure and moisture between their finger and a growing root. They have been doing this for so long that the knowledge is not learned but *inhabited*—it lives in their hands the way balance lives in the inner ear, below the threshold of conscious thought.

They work alone. This is important. Despite living in close proximity—three or four hundred bodies in a space that, while large, is also densely packed—the Tenders work on their individual sections of the city in solitude. Each one tends a territory, a neighborhood, a cluster of trees and root-structures that they know the way you know your own body. They know which roots are growing fast and which are slow. They know where water is collecting too deeply and where it is draining too quickly. They know the health of every trunk, the progress of every arch. They do not share this knowledge with each other, not because they are secretive but because it is unnecessary. Each Tender's territory is a world sufficient unto itself, and the territories fit together not through coordination but through the same emergent coherence that allows the cells of a body to form organs without any single cell understanding the body's plan. The city works because each part works, and each part works because one Tender is paying complete, lifelong attention to it.

## How They Live

They do not sleep as you would understand it. They slow down. In the deepest hours of night—and night here is merely a darkening of the already dim light, a thickening of the grey-green air into something closer to black-green—the Tenders fold themselves into the root-structures, finding the niches and hollows that have grown to accommodate their bodies. Some of these sleeping-hollows are clearly shaped for the purpose: concavities in root-walls, just large enough for a crouched Tender, lined with the thickest, softest moss, oriented to drain so that the sleeper does not lie in standing water. The Tenders settle into these hollows and go still, and their breathing slows, and their skin cools until it is indistinguishable in temperature from the root-wall around them, and the moss begins to grow over them—not metaphorically, but actually, tiny tendrils of moss extending across their surfaces during the hours of stillness, beginning the slow patient work of colonization that would, if the Tender never moved again, eventually absorb them entirely into the wall. But they do move again. They rouse, slowly, and the new moss growth breaks away or is absorbed into their skin—it is not clear which—and they unfold from their hollows and resume.

They eat throughout the day, constantly but in tiny amounts. A pinch of moss pulled from a wall and pressed between flat teeth. A strip of soft inner bark peeled from a root with the speaking-finger and chewed slowly. The small white flowers of the watercress-like plant, gathered from the channels and eaten one at a time. They drink by pressing their faces into the still pools, their mouths barely breaking the surface, drawing water in through a slow sustained inhalation that barely dimples the water's skin. They do not cook. They do not store food. The city feeds them moment by moment, and they tend the city moment by moment, and the exchange is so continuous and so finely balanced that neither party accumulates surplus or deficit.

They defecate rarely, and what they produce is nearly indistinguishable from the humus of the forest floor—a dark, fibrous, odorless mass that they deposit in specific locations, always in the shallow water of the channels between root-structures, where it dissolves and disperses and feeds the very plants they will later eat. The cycle is closed. Tight. Almost mechanical in its efficiency, except that nothing about it is mechanical. It is all alive, all breathing, all responsive, all engaged in the slow conversation of growth and consumption and return.

## How They Are With Each Other

They are gentle. This is the word that keeps surfacing, the word that the place demands. They are gentle with a completeness that goes beyond behavior into something structural, something that has to do with the way their bodies are built and the way they move through space. They cannot be loud. Their bodies are not made for it. They cannot be fast. Their physiology doesn't support it. They cannot be violent—not because they are morally superior to violent creatures, but because they lack the equipment. No claws. No significant teeth. No musculature for striking or grasping with force. Their hands are made for touching root tips. Their bodies are made for crouching and waiting. Their entire organism is a specialized instrument for patience, and this has made them, incidentally and without any particular effort, gentle.

When two Tenders meet in a corridor—which happens regularly, since the corridors are narrow and the territories adjacent—they pause. There is no greeting that you could identify as such. No vocalization, no gesture, no change in facial expression. They simply pause, and their ear-cilia orient toward each other, and for a moment—ten seconds, thirty seconds, sometimes several minutes—they stand close together in the narrow wet corridor and feel each other's presence through whatever senses those cilia provide. Vibration. Body heat, what little there is. The chemical signature of another living Tender. Then one turns sideways to let the other pass, and they continue on their respective ways. That is the interaction. That is the full depth and extent of Tender sociality in its most common form. It would be easy to call it minimal, impoverished, lonely. It is none of those things. It is *complete*. What passes between them in those moments of stillness is sufficient. You can see it in the way they move afterward—not changed, exactly, but confirmed. Settled more deeply into themselves.

There is no speech. This must be said plainly because it is the fact that most fundamentally separates their world from yours. They do not communicate through sequential symbolic utterance. They have no words, no grammar, no stories, no names. They do not name themselves, or each other, or the places in the city, or the trees they tend, or the frogs that sit on their root-walls watching them with gold-rimmed eyes. They are not pre-linguistic, as though language were a destination they haven't reached. They are *a-linguistic*. They have taken a different path entirely. What they have instead—and it is not less, only different—is the sense-world of the cilia, the touch-world of the speaking-fingers, and the vast, slow, chemical-tactile conversation with the roots that occupies most of their waking attention. They think. They clearly think. You can see it in the way a Tender will crouch at a difficult junction—a place where three roots need to be guided into a load-bearing arch—and remain motionless for a long time, speaking-fingers resting lightly on the root surfaces, not acting, just... considering. Feeling the problem with their hands and thinking about it with whatever organ or process serves them as thought. Then they begin, and the solution they arrive at is elegant, and it is clearly the product of intelligence, and it was conducted entirely without words.

## The Young

You will find them in the central structure. Not hidden, not protected in any fortress sense—the Tenders have no concept of threat that would require fortification—but *located* there, in the oldest and most complex part of the city, because that is where the growing is most intense and most instructive.

The young are small. Obviously, inevitably small, but the smallness of a young Tender is striking because it is the only proportional difference between them and adults. They are not pudgy or round-faced or soft in the way mammalian young tend to be. They are simply scaled-down versions of the adults, complete with the sixth digit already present and functional from what appears to be a very early age. Newborns—if that word applies; the reproductive process is something you will not witness, something that happens in the deepest root-chambers, in water, in the dark, and involves biology you would not immediately understand—are the size of a human hand, and they are immediately, from the first moments, touching things. The speaking-finger moves. It reaches for root surfaces, for moss, for the walls of the birth-pool. It begins its conversation before the rest of the body has fully figured out how to move.

The adults who attend the young—and it is never a specific adult, never a parent in the possessive mammalian sense; whichever Tender is nearest will crouch beside a young one and share the space—do not teach. They tend. They tend the young the way they tend the roots: with patience, with presence, with the gentle sustained application of attention. A young Tender will place its speaking-finger on a root tip and the root will not respond, because the young one's secretions are not yet calibrated, not yet carrying the right chemical vocabulary. The attending adult does not correct or demonstrate. It simply places its own finger nearby, on the same root or on an adjacent one, and works. The young one watches. Feels the vibrations in the root, perhaps, or smells the adult's secretions, or detects something through its own nascent cilia. It tries again. The root does not respond. It tries again. Over days, weeks, months—the root begins, faintly, tentatively, to respond. This is how they learn. Through immersion. Through the kind of knowledge that can only be absorbed, never transmitted.

There are perhaps thirty or forty young in the basin at any time. They grow slowly. A young Tender will spend years in the central chambers before beginning to move outward, claiming or being absorbed into a territory, beginning its own section of the endless construction. By the time it does, it is indistinguishable from the adults in everything except the depth of its root-knowledge, which will continue to deepen for the rest of its life, which is long. How long is unclear. The oldest Tenders—the ones who work the most ancient structures at the very center, whose skin has become so bark-like that moss grows on them even when they are moving, whose speaking-fingers are calloused to the point of being nearly wooden themselves—these may be very old indeed. Centuries, possibly. Their slowness has increased with age until they move at a pace that is difficult to distinguish from the growth of the roots themselves. They are becoming the city. The city is becoming them. The distinction, which was never sharp, is dissolving.

## The Other Creatures

The frogs know them. This is evident in the way the frogs behave around the Tenders—which is to say, they do not behave differently at all. A frog sitting on a root-wall will not move when a Tender's hand passes inches away. A frog in a pool will not dive when a Tender's face descends to drink. The frogs have incorporated the Tenders into their model of the world as a feature of the landscape, something as fixed and predictable as the root-walls themselves, and the Tenders have done the same in reverse. They do not interact. They coexist, which is both less and more.

The salamanders are bolder. They will climb onto a Tender's arm or shoulder and remain there, riding along as the Tender moves through its territory, absorbing body heat—what little there is—or perhaps tasting the Tender's skin secretions with their own sensitive skin. The Tenders do not react. A salamander is a minor, temporary additional weight, barely noticeable, and its presence on the body is not categorically different from the presence of moss or moisture or the slight tug of a spiderweb encountered in a root-lattice doorway.

The birds avoid them, mostly. The small dark birds that hop along the root-walls will flutter away when a Tender approaches—not in alarm, just in casual avoidance, the way they'd avoid a falling branch. The heron-like wader ignores them entirely, secure in its size and its own ancient patience.

The creature you glimpsed—the brown, rounded, dog-sized mammal moving through the corridors—is something else. It is a root-eater, like the Tenders, but it is not a Tender. It is native to the basin but separate from the building project, a herbivore that feeds on the same mosses and soft bark that the Tenders eat. It has a wombat-like body, dense and low, with thick fur that sheds water and small dark eyes. There are several dozen of them in the basin. They move through the root-corridors on their own paths, which sometimes coincide with the Tenders' paths and sometimes don't. When a root-eater and a Tender meet in a corridor, the root-eater pauses, the Tender pauses, and then one or the other steps aside. There is no dominance in this interaction. No hierarchy. Just two bodies in a narrow space working out, moment by moment, how to pass.

The pale long-bodied thing in the central pool does not interact with the Tenders at all, as far as you can observe. It circles. It does not surface. The Tenders who work in the central chamber do not look at it or acknowledge it. It may be important. It may be incidental. The Tenders do not explain, and there is no one to ask.

## What They Are Building

This is the question that sits at the center of the central pool, as deep and as cold and as unanswerable as the water itself.

They are building the city. That much is obvious. But *why*? What is the city *for*? It is not for shelter—the Tenders need minimal shelter, and the basin provides it without architecture. It is not for defense—there is nothing to defend against. It is not for storage—there is nothing to store. It is not for display or status or worship or commerce or governance or any of the functions that human cities serve. The city has no function that you can identify beyond its own existence.

But watch them. Watch a Tender at a root-junction, speaking-finger pressed to the growing tip, face still, eyes half-closed, the full weight of its attention focused on the infinitesimal movement of living wood responding to its touch. Watch how the architecture becomes more complex, more refined, more *itself* as it progresses from the basin's edge to its center. Watch how the oldest Tenders, the ones who are becoming the city, work with a subtlety and precision that the younger ones cannot yet match. There is a trajectory here. The city is getting *better*. Not bigger—though it is also getting bigger—but better. More intricate. More responsive to the flow of water, the fall of light, the needs of the countless organisms that live within it. Each generation of growth incorporates the lessons of the previous generation, not through instruction but through the accumulated wisdom embedded in the living structure itself.

The city is a practice. That is the closest you will come to understanding it. The Tenders build because building is what they do, the way the roots grow because growing is what they do, and the building and the growing are the same activity, the same slow patient conversation between life and life, and the product of that conversation is not the city but the *ongoing quality of attention* that the building demands. The city is a by-product. An extraordinarily beautiful, astonishingly complex, living by-product of the act of paying complete and sustained attention to the growth of roots. The Tenders are not architects. They are contemplatives. The city is their meditation made visible, and it is still being made, and it will go on being made for as long as the roots grow and the water rises and the cold grey light falls through the canopy and there is a single Tender crouched in a narrow corridor with its speaking-finger pressed to the pale growing tip of a root, listening with its whole body, waiting for the next infinitesimal movement, and then the next, and then the next.

## At Evening

The light dims. The already-grey air takes on a deeper tone, and the green world goes quiet in a way that makes you realize it was not truly quiet before—there were layers of sound so subtle you registered them only as texture, and now even those layers are thinning. The frogs descend from their perches on the root-walls and slip into the pools, where they float with just their eyes above the surface, amber jewels in the darkening water. The birds tuck their heads under their wings. The salamanders retreat under root-ledges and into the gaps between fused bark plates. The root-eaters settle against warm root-walls in ones and twos, their dense fur beading with evening condensation, and close their eyes.

The Tenders slow. Their movements, already barely perceptible, diminish further. One by one—no, not one by one, that implies sequence, and this is not sequential; it is simultaneous, a distributed settling, like sediment falling through water—they find their hollows. They fold their long arms, tuck their speaking-fingers against their chests where the warmth is greatest, lower their broad flat faces, and let their skin cool. The moss advances over them in the slow hours. Their ear-cilia continue to move, even in the deepest stillness—sampling, sampling, never fully switching off.

In the central chamber, the last light catches the surface of the deep pool and then fades. The pale thing below continues its circling, a white shape turning in black water, purposeful and unexplained. The ancient Tenders who tend this place are already indistinguishable from their hollows, from the walls, from the roots that built the walls. You would not know they were there. You would think this was a place without inhabitants—a ruin, perhaps, or a natural formation that only resembled architecture. You would be wrong.

The city breathes. The water rises, a fraction of a fraction of an inch, as it does every night when the air cools and the ground contracts and the water table responds. The roots grow. In the dark, in the silence, in the cold, the roots grow. A tenth of a millimeter. A hundredth. The city expands by the width of a cell, and then another cell, and then another.

Somewhere in the corridors, a speaking-finger twitches in sleep—a reflex, a dream of growth—and against its calloused tip, a root responds.
